# Nvidia's Board Rules Out Huang Return as Earnings Soar

Nvidia's board said on Wednesday it does not intend to reinstate Jensen Huang as chief executive, a day after the company reported record third-quarter revenue of $115.8 billion, according to Al Jazeera. Chair Harvey Jones said the directors had unanimously accepted Huang's resignation, which took effect on November 12, and the search for a permanent successor, covering internal and external candidates, continues.

## Performance review: Q3

Taken as a set of operating results, the quarter would earn the corporate equivalent of a standing ovation. Revenue rose 103% from a year earlier, and net profit was $64.9 billion, up 109%, according to Al Jazeera. Data-center revenue reached $107.6 billion, up 110% year on year and 21% from the previous quarter, Kiplinger reported. Adjusted earnings were $2.68 a share against $2.47 expected, and gross margin was 74.9%.

The outlook was equally robust. Nvidia guided fourth-quarter revenue to $130 billion, plus or minus 3%, with gross margin of 71% to 72%, and said the guidance assumes no data-center compute revenue from China. Its purchase commitments stand at $390 billion, and a $150 billion buyback is due to be completed by the end of fiscal 2028.

On every line a manager could audit, then, the leadership transition has cost the company nothing yet. The stock, which closed down 2.89% at $150.96 on Wednesday, according to Reuters, has not been so generous. Over five days it is off 13.11%.

## The name that was not spoken

Interim chief executive Colette Kress, who retains her role as chief financial officer, led her first call without, according to Kiplinger, mentioning Huang once. Her prepared remarks were confident. "Vera Rubin is off to an explosive start," she said, adding that the company's roadmap is on track and its ambition has never been greater.

It is a familiar technique. When a founder leaves abruptly, the remaining executives tend to talk about the product, the roadmap and the customers, and to leave the vacancy to the analysts. The analysts obliged. Oppenheimer's Rich Schafer described a leadership vacuum, and Seaport's Jay Goldberg said he did not know that any internal candidate could run the whole company.

The company's own filing is notably spare. The Form 8-K, dated November 12, says Huang resigned as president, chief executive and director with immediate effect, names Kress as interim chief executive, and appoints Jones as the company's first board chair. It gives no reason. Al Jazeera reported that Jones tied the departure to personal conduct that conflicted with the company's code of conduct, and that the circumstances had drawn scrutiny, details that Nvidia's filing does not contain and which this publication has not independently confirmed.

Will Rhind of GraniteShares offered the sunniest reading, telling the press that the earnings were very strong and were just what the market needed to shake off some AI-related jitters. The market, as it turned out, shook off the earnings instead. Both comments can be true in a single trading day, which is a useful reminder that a stock price is a view on the future and an earnings release is a record of the past.

## The campaign for his return

Huang himself has not gone quietly. Al Jazeera reported that he said on X he would be honoured to return as chief executive if the board decided so, and that he owns his mistakes completely. Nearly 200,000 people have signed an open letter urging his reinstatement, the outlet said, including Elon Musk and Tim Sweeney.

The board's answer, delivered by Jones, was to decline. Kiplinger separately reported that the board has hired a consultant and does not want to hurry the search. Goldberg of Seaport said a return remained possible, "unlikely but possible", a phrase worth keeping for the next time anyone asks a board for a timeline.

## Forensic note

A rule of thumb applies to executive-transition announcements: the stronger the financial results, the fewer questions the audience will tolerate about governance. Wednesday inverted the pattern. A company that earned more profit in one quarter than most firms see in a lifetime spent its earnings week answering questions about an empty chair.

The market's verdict was a 2.89% decline. The boardroom's verdict was a consultant. In McKinsey terms, which are the only terms in which the author is qualified to speak, that is a "phase one" with no phase two.
