# Palo Alto Beats Estimates, Guides Higher and Pays $4.5 Billion in Stock

Palo Alto Networks reported first-quarter fiscal 2027 results on November 18 that beat analyst estimates, raised the outlook for its second quarter and, on the same day, agreed to acquire the AI start-up Applied Compute for $4.5 billion in stock. The review of an employee who exceeds expectations and then asks for a very large budget follows.

## Performance against objectives

On the numbers, the quarter is difficult to fault. According to Newsquawk, adjusted earnings per share were $1.05 against a consensus of $0.97, and revenue was $3.44 billion against $3.31 billion expected. Adjusted net income came in at $881 million, ahead of the $816.7 million analysts had pencilled in. This is a straightforward beat by any standard, and the manager should note that it was delivered without apparent drama.

The guidance is, if anything, stronger. For the second quarter Palo Alto projected adjusted EPS of $1.01 to $1.03, compared with a consensus of $1.01, and revenue of $3.53 billion to $3.55 billion, compared with $3.42 billion expected. A company that guides revenue about $120 million above the analysts' figure at the midpoint is a company that wishes the analysts to feel slightly behind.

One line of the results deserves the attention of any forensic reader. Newsquawk lists net income of $482 million, and operating income of $96 million, against adjusted operating income of $1.1 billion. The gap between the last two figures is roughly a billion dollars. That is the sort of difference that footnotes were invented to explain, and that the phrase "adjusted" exists to describe without discussing.

## The budget request

The expensive part of the day came after the results. Palo Alto agreed to acquire Applied Compute, which the LDS aggregator describes as a platform for post-training and inference of open-weight AI models, for $4.5 billion in an all-stock deal, subject to adjustments. The transaction is expected to close in Palo Alto's fiscal third quarter of 2027, subject to regulatory clearance and customary conditions. Applied Compute's co-founder and chief executive, Yash Patil, is to become Co-Chief AI Officer after closing.

The title is revealing. A company that appoints a Co-Chief AI Officer has concluded that the AI portfolio is too important for one executive, or that the incoming executive is too important to be given anything less. Nikesh Arora, Palo Alto's chairman and chief executive, is paraphrased by the aggregator as linking the platform to the company's Unit 42 Frontier AI Assessment, though the sources reviewed for this article give no verbatim quotation.

Paying in stock has a particular logic. It spares the company's cash, and it lets the target's owners share in the future of the combined business. It also depends on the buyer's shares being worth what the buyer thinks they are. On November 5, according to TIKR, Palo Alto shares fell 10% in a single session, and Okta fell 11%, as software stocks were sold after Democrats won both houses of Congress, in TIKR's account. The same article says the stock had been up almost 90% for the year before that day. The sources reviewed here do not report the share reaction on the day of the earnings and the deal, so no claim is made about it.

## Assessment

The deal terms carry an obvious risk. When the currency of an acquisition is the acquirer's own stock, the price is a fixed number of dollars on the day of signing and a moving number of shares by the day of closing, which is expected a few quarters away. If the shares rise, the target is cheap in retrospect; if they fall, the target is expensive in retrospect. The "subject to adjustments" language is designed to describe the second case without admitting it.

Meanwhile, the stock market in which the shares trade has had a difficult month. Technology shares have sold off sharply this week, and any acquirer whose paper is denominated in them is making a decision about the future of its own valuation. The most tactful reading is that the board believes its shares are undervalued, in which case paying with them is an act of confidence. The less tactful reading is that it is the only currency on hand that does not need to be earned this quarter.

The manager's file should also record that the same announcement contained both the good news and the request, a sequencing familiar to anyone who has ever asked for a promotion on the day of a strong quarter.

Overall rating: exceeds expectations. Recommended action: continue to monitor the expense, and review it in fiscal Q3.
