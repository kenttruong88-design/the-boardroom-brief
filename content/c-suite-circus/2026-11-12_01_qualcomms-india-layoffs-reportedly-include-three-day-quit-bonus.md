# Qualcomm's India Layoffs Reportedly Include a Three-Day Quit Bonus

Qualcomm began laying off staff at its Bengaluru and Hyderabad offices on Monday, November 9, with the cuts expected to spread to Delhi-NCR and Chennai, according to Business Standard. The company has not disclosed how many positions are affected. Reports put the global restructuring at about 7% of its roughly 52,000 employees, and the Indian operation, which employs around 20,000 people, could see 1,000 or more jobs go, Business Standard said, citing reports.

The company's own description is that the exercise is "aimed at strengthening its performance culture." Chief Executive Cristiano Amon announced the global reduction on October 20, in terms that tied it to the company's annual performance review process.

This is the sort of sentence that deserves a close reading. In most employment reviews, performance culture is something a company has. Here it is something a company is strengthening by removing some of the people who are in it, an approach to cultural improvement that has the same logic as repairing a bridge by taking out some of the planks.

Note the geography. India accounts for around 20,000 of the company's roughly 52,000 staff, or close to two in five. Applying the reported 7% evenly would imply about 1,400 Indian jobs; the estimate of 1,000 or more is consistent with a somewhat lighter touch, or with a rounding error in a spreadsheet nobody has published.

## The three-day clock

The detail that gave the story its legs concerns the exit terms. According to Financial Express, as relayed by Business Standard and others, affected employees are offered severance of three months' salary, scaled by tenure, and an additional month's pay if they sign their relieving documents within three days. Qualcomm has not confirmed these terms.

If accurate, the structure deserves study. A severance package with a speed bonus is an incentive scheme, and incentive schemes encode what the sponsor cares about. A company that wanted a thoughtful exit would offer a long window in which to consider it. A company that pays more for signing within three days values the signature itself, and values it more highly in the first seventy-two hours than the third week. Three months of severance is a figure; one more month for speed is a lever. The additional month is not generosity. It is the price of not having to read the second page of a legal document with a lawyer.

The conventional counsel runs the other way. The standard advice to anyone handed a separation agreement is to take time before signing. The three-day bonus converts that advice into an expense, and the expense is one month of pay.

## Who goes first

The first round reportedly affected senior engineers with several years of experience, according to Financial Express, and another round could follow in January. Senior engineers are the people whom the semiconductor business has historically described as its core asset. They are also the people with the highest salaries, which is a different property but not an unrelated one.

The financial backdrop explains the motion, if not the vocabulary. Qualcomm's revenue for the September quarter fell 8% to $10.32 billion from $11.27 billion a year earlier, and handset revenue of $5.14 billion came in below analysts' estimate of $5.25 billion, according to figures reported alongside the layoffs. Handsets remain the largest part of the business, and the company has spent several years trying to persuade investors that automotive chips, PCs and data-center products will eventually matter more. The quarter did not end that argument.

## The review

If Qualcomm were reviewed the way its management reviews its staff, the assessment would be mixed. Objective: reduce costs in a quarter when revenue fell. Achieved, on a plan that has not yet been fully disclosed. Objective: protect the technical franchise. Under review, the first round having reportedly taken out experienced engineers. Objective: communicate. Here the company has done well, in that it has chosen a phrase, "performance culture," that can be applied to any outcome whatever. A layoff that goes well strengthens the culture. A layoff that goes badly reveals why the culture needed strengthening.

Development: restructuring programs commonly take more than one round. The January date reported by Financial Express suggests that employees who survive this one will have a few weeks to reflect on the culture that has been strengthened, before being asked to demonstrate it again.
