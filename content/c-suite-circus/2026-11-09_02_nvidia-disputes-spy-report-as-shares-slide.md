# Nvidia Disputes Spy Report as Shares Slide on CEO Scrutiny

Nvidia has responded to a New York Times report about Chief Executive Jensen Huang with a statement that is best read slowly. The company's spokesperson told The Hill that "the diversion allegations against this regional cloud were thoroughly investigated and debunked last year". On Monday, Reuters headlined a 3.4% slide in the company's shares as Huang's future came "under scrutiny", per investingLive.

## What the report says

The Times report, published on 29 October and summarised by The Hill and Tom's Guide, says US intelligence agencies found that Alice Huang, an executive at the Singapore-based data-centre company Megaspeed, passed information to Beijing while building what the Times called a close relationship with Jensen Huang. Officials reportedly saw the two together on visits to Taiwan in 2024 and 2025. The Times also reported that President Trump was briefed on the findings about a year ago and took no action, and that Jensen Huang never received a defensive briefing.

Tom's Guide stresses that there is no public evidence that Jensen Huang knew about or took part in any operation. Megaspeed has reportedly retained outside counsel and said it is investigating the allegations. The Chinese Embassy in Washington rejected the claims as unfounded, according to Tom's Guide.

## A close reading of the statement

Corporate denials are written with the care that people usually reserve for wills. Nvidia's contains five or six distinct claims, and it is worth separating them.

First: the diversion allegations "were thoroughly investigated and debunked last year". This is a statement about a prior investigation into whether chips were diverted. The Times report, as summarised by both outlets, concerns a different question: what an executive may have passed to Beijing, and who knew. A denial that answers the first question is not a denial of the second.

Second: Huang's interactions with the executive were "not unusual or unexpected", and, the company added, he speaks with thousands of customer executives every year. That is a fair point about volume. It is also a point about frequency, whereas the Times reported on the character of the relationship.

Third: Nvidia said its regional cloud customers receive ordinary business information, not trade secrets. That may well be true. It is a claim about what Nvidia shared, not about what Megaspeed's executive did with anything she already had.

Fourth: Nvidia told The Hill it is confident the US government would let it know if it believed any person was a threat to American industry. The Times reported that the administration did not act on its findings for about a year, and that Huang was not warned. The sentence is therefore a statement of confidence in a warning system that, on the Times's account, did not issue one.

Fifth: the company noted that China's block on US AI chips had caused what it called the largest write-offs in US tech history. This is a legitimate argument that Nvidia has lost more than it gained in China. It is also, strictly speaking, a different topic.

There are two further details in the coverage that the statement does not address. Tom's Guide reports that, according to the Times, officials found Nvidia equipment at a Malaysian data centre still boxed rather than installed. And The Hill reports that anonymous sources told the Times that companies tied to Alice Huang have continued to buy Nvidia chips. Both are the Times's account, relayed by secondary outlets, and Nvidia has said it is confident in its own process. They are, however, the questions an auditor would ask next.

## The accountant's footnote

None of this suggests Nvidia is hiding anything. The statement is the work of a company that has taken legal advice and is aiming to say only what it can prove. The White House, for its part, did not immediately respond to The Hill's request for comment.

What the exercise reveals is the gap between a denial and a defence. A denial says the allegation is false. A defence says the allegation, even if true, does not matter. Nvidia's statement leans on the second without committing to the first, which is a perfectly orthodox approach to a crisis in a company that cannot afford to be wrong on the details.

Investors appear to have drawn their own conclusions about what remains unanswered. After a 9.3% fall on 5 November, per the AP, Monday's slide of 3.4% suggests a market that is not waiting for the footnotes. In corporate communications, as in audit, the date on the sentence matters. "Last year" is carrying a great deal of weight.
