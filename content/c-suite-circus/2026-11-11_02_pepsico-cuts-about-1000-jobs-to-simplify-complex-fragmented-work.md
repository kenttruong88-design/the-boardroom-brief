# PepsiCo Cuts About 1,000 Jobs to 'Simplify' Work It Calls Fragmented

Performance review: PepsiCo North America, current year. Objective: simplify. Method: reduce about 1,000 people. Rationale: "too much of how we work today remains complex and fragmented." Rating pending, as the organisation has not yet explained which of the 1,000 were the complex ones.

![Corporate headquarters building exterior](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/c-suite-circus/2026-11-11-02-pepsico-cuts-1000-jobs-simplify-memo-hero)
*Photo: Francesco Ungaro via Pexels*

The Wall Street Journal first reported the cuts on Monday. Reuters, relayed by BNN Bloomberg, and Bloomberg, relayed by Transport Topics, followed the same day, and Fast Company covered the memo on Tuesday. About 1,000 roles in North America's food and beverage business are affected, according to the reports. Reuters said it could not independently confirm the number, and we found no formal PepsiCo press statement, so the account rests on a memo and the reporting around it.

The memo came from Ram Krishnan, chief executive of PepsiCo North America, and is dated November 9. In it, according to Bloomberg and Fast Company, he wrote: "We have opportunities to simplify our structures, clarify accountability and make decisions faster." He added: "Over the coming week, we'll make changes across parts of our organization to address this." The goal, he said, is "to move faster, execute better, and invest behind the opportunities that will drive our growth."

## Close reading

Three phrases deserve attention. First, "complex and fragmented". This is the standard diagnosis for any organisation whose results have disappointed, and it has the useful property of being true of every organisation. Second, "clarify accountability", which in practice means deciding which named person will be unavailable to the next series of questions. Third, "over the coming week", a timeframe that tells employees there is a spreadsheet and it is already open.

The company has about 306,000 employees worldwide, including roughly 125,000 in the United States, as of December 27, 2025, according to its annual report as cited by Reuters. Set against that, 1,000 is a small fraction of the total, though a large one for the 1,000.

## The numbers behind the memo

The cuts follow a mixed quarter. In October, PepsiCo reported third-quarter revenue of $25.27 billion against analyst expectations of $24.96 billion, and adjusted earnings per share of $2.34 against $2.29 expected, Fast Company reported. Operating profit rose 19%. That is the part of the review the company will quote.

The part it will quote less eagerly is that North American food volume was flat, beverage volume fell 2%, and PepsiCo cut its full-year guidance for core earnings-per-share growth from 5-7% to 2.5-3.5%. A beat on the quarter and a cut to the year is a combination that looks, to an investor, like an organisation that can sell a lot of product in a month and expects to sell less over the next several.

The share price tells the same story. PepsiCo closed at $141 on Monday, according to Fast Company, down 1.1% for the year while the S&P 500 is up more than 10%. Its 2026 high was above $170. Anyone who has owned the stock this year has watched the index make money that the shares did not.

*Photo: cottonbro studio via Pexels*

Two other parties are in the room. The first is Elliott Investment Management, the activist fund that took a stake of roughly $4 billion about a year ago, according to Reuters. Activists do not usually take $4 billion positions to admire the packaging. The memo says nothing about Elliott, and the timing is a coincidence in the way that most corporate timing is coincidence: technically available as an explanation.

The second is Coca-Cola. Bloomberg reported that PepsiCo recently lost its US KFC contract to its rival, a loss that is difficult to describe as fragmented or complex. It is a customer who left.

## Overall assessment

The case for the cuts is easy to make. A company with a lagging stock, a reduced profit forecast and an engaged activist is expected to find savings, and a memo about simplicity is a more pleasant way to say so than one about margins. If the restructuring really makes decisions faster, shareholders will be pleased, and some of the 1,000 will even concede the point.

The case against is also easy. Simplification programmes have a long history of adding a new layer, a new committee, and eventually a new transformation office to simplify the simplification. Anyone who has worked in a large company has seen one.

Rating: meets expectations on candour, below expectations on specificity. Next review: when Elliott's next letter arrives, or the next memo, whichever is simpler.
