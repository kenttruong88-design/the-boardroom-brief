# Dell Doubles Revenue to $54.9 Billion and Calls Itself New

Dell Technologies reported fiscal third-quarter revenue of $54.9 billion on Tuesday, November 24, up 103 percent from a year earlier, and its vice chairman and chief operating officer, Jeff Clarke, used the occasion to observe the obvious. "We are now a different company," he said in the company's earnings release, "with Infrastructure Solutions Group (ISG) driving the vast majority of our profit and growth." As performance reviews go, it is rare for the subject to announce a change of job title in the self-assessment section.

## Performance review: Dell, fiscal third quarter

Net income was $5.7 billion, up 266 percent, according to the company's filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission. Non-GAAP diluted earnings per share were $9.45, up 265 percent; on a GAAP basis they were $8.82, up 287 percent. The quarter ended October 30. Chief Financial Officer David Kennedy called it "record revenue of $54.9 billion" and said the team "executed exceptionally well." The record is not in dispute.

The record has a source, and it is not the one printed on the business card. Revenue at ISG, the infrastructure unit, rose 198 percent to $42.0 billion. Within that, AI-optimized servers brought in $22.0 billion, up 291 percent, and traditional servers and networking added $15.0 billion, up 235 percent. Storage, the quiet colleague in the corner, generated $4.9 billion, up 24 percent. ISG's operating income was $7.1 billion, a margin of 17.0 percent.

## The department formerly known as Dell

Client Solutions Group, which sells the PCs, reported revenue of $12.9 billion, up 3 percent, with operating income of $704 million, down 6 percent. A unit that grows 3 percent while the firm grows 103 percent is not failing. It is merely being outperformed by a colleague on a different floor. The review notes write themselves: reliable, steady, and best not discussed in the same meeting as the servers.

ISG now produces the overwhelming share of the profit that Clarke referred to. By the company's own numbers, the infrastructure unit earned $7.1 billion in operating income against $704 million from the PC business, a ratio of about ten to one.

## The backlog, which is the point

Dell booked $60.2 billion of AI orders in the quarter and ended it with a record AI backlog of $132.3 billion, according to the release. For comparison, a company blog post from Dell's September 2 second-quarter report put the backlog at $95 billion and the quarter's AI orders at $60.9 billion. The backlog has therefore grown by roughly $37 billion in a quarter while orders were broadly flat. Shipments, which are how a backlog becomes revenue, were not disclosed in the release.

Clarke added that "demand continues to accelerate and broaden globally, with more than 7,500 customers deploying Dell AI Factories." The phrase "AI Factories" is the kind of product name that a marketing department invents and a finance department then has to book. In this case, the booking has been generous.

## Guidance: raised, with enthusiasm

Dell raised its fiscal 2027 outlook. Revenue is now expected to be $202.0 billion, up 78 percent, from a prior forecast of $192.0 billion. AI server revenue is projected at $78.0 billion, up 216 percent, from $74.0 billion. Non-GAAP earnings per share are expected to be $29.68, up from $25.50. The fourth-quarter revenue guide is $56.3 billion.

A forecast that moves by $10 billion in a single quarter is, in corporate terms, either extraordinary confidence or an extraordinary absence of caution. Given that backlog, the former seems more plausible, and the second is not mutually exclusive.

Dell also returned $4.9 billion to shareholders in the quarter, a record, and authorized a new $25.0 billion share repurchase program. Operating cash flow was $4.5 billion in the quarter and $10.8 billion for the year to date, which Kennedy called a record. A company that is sitting on a record backlog and authorizing a new $25 billion buyback at the same time has decided that its best investment is itself, a view that its employees, at least, are in a position to endorse.

## The market's verdict

None of this stopped the share price from having an eventful week. According to Que.com, Dell fell about 12 percent in Tuesday's regular session, to $295.28, after rising 20 percent on Monday, then rose roughly 2 to 3 percent in after-hours trading on the results. I did not find a confirmed Wednesday reaction, so that is where this account ends. Wall Street has been assessing a company whose reported results have a pronounced gap with its day-to-day price behavior, and has not yet decided which of the two is the outlier.

## Closing remarks

Self-appraisals are usually characterised by modesty, or at least the performance of it. Dell's is characterised by accuracy. The firm has told investors that it is a different company from the one they thought they owned, and the numbers agree. The PC division is still there, growing at a sedate three percent, and has presumably been told that it is valued for its culture. The servers will be getting the corner office. The laptops get the parking space.
