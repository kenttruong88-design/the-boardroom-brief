# Verizon CEO Says Firing 13,000 Was About Showing Things Were Different

Dan Schulman took over as chief executive of Verizon in October 2025. About a month later the company cut roughly 13,000 jobs. In an interview published by Fortune on November 11, Schulman explained the decision: "I really needed to show that this was different."

It is a valuable sentence, because it states the function of the layoffs without mentioning their size. Verizon cut 13,000 positions, according to Fortune, and now employs about 93,300 people. The purpose, as the CEO describes it, was communication. The audience was the company, the market and, presumably, the 13,000 recipients of the message.

## The scorecard

Judged by what Fortune reports, the turnaround has results. Verizon added a net 406,000 phone lines in the third quarter, compared with 44,000 in the same quarter a year earlier, plus 137,000 customer accounts. It cut promotional spending by 41% while doing so. It had been in a different position: Verizon's consumer business lost a net 414,000 postpaid phone lines over the first three quarters of 2025.

A forensic reading of those figures requires context, and Fortune supplies some. In 2024, T-Mobile added 3.1 million net postpaid phone lines and AT&T 1.65 million, while Verizon added under 900,000. Verizon's quarter of 406,000 is therefore a large improvement on its own recent past and an unremarkable number against its competitors' annual totals. The story is about direction rather than distance, and the direction is the one the new management was hired to produce. Reviewers of this sort call it "meets expectations, trending positive," which is the rating given to employees whom nobody quite wishes to promote or to fire.

Reading the two numbers together, in fact, produces the sharpest line in the piece. A company reduced its payroll by about 13,000 and cut promotional spending by 41%, and then reported more customers. There is a way to describe this that makes it sound like efficiency, and a way that makes it sound like the first phase of a discount-and-layoff cycle. Fortune's reporting does not settle which, and neither does one quarter.

## The management philosophy

Schulman's interview supplies the philosophy as well as the numbers. On the subject of leadership and turnover he said: "Heaven and hell are the same place. It's just heaven for some and hell for others." Which side of the severance one sits on, on this theory, determines the weather.

It is an economical formulation of a position held by many chief executives and rarely stated so openly: that the same restructuring is a rescue for the people who stay and a loss for the people who go, and that the loss is the price of the rescue. It is also, to a forensic accountant, a statement that the cost has been allocated and the benefit assigned.

Another anecdote from the interview has the virtue of arithmetic. Schulman recalled a meeting in which a manager proposed raising the sales close rate from 15% to 25%. His response, as he tells it: "So, are you saying at the end of that, we are losing 75% of the time?" The anecdote is told to illustrate the discipline of asking what a target actually means. Applied to the company's own announcement, the same method produces the question of what "different" means when the headcount is 13,000 lower. It is a figure with no failure rate attached.

## The competition

The most revealing quote concerns not the employees but a rival. Schulman, discussing the possibility of SpaceX entering the wireless market, said on the October earnings call that "it's very hard to throw a punch against something that doesn't exist." It is a calm view of a possible competitor, and it is also the CEO's view of a lot of things: the future threat is not yet a threat, and the present cost reduction is.

## Verdict

The review, then. Objective: change the company's trajectory. Partly met, with a quarter of improved subscriber numbers after a year of losses. Objective: demonstrate a break with the past. Met with unusual clarity, because the CEO has said so. Objective: preserve the base of talent that wins customers. Not measured in any figure that Fortune reports.

Management concludes, with its customary calm, that this was different. It will be interesting to see whether the next 13,000 are needed for the same demonstration.
