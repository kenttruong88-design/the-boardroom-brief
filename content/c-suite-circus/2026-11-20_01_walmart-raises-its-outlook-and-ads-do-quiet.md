# Walmart Raises Its Outlook, and Ads Do Quiet Heavy Lifting

Walmart reported third-quarter revenue of $188.8 billion on Thursday, November 19, up 5.2% from a year earlier and ahead of the $187.25 billion analysts expected, according to Stocktwits. Adjusted earnings came in at $0.65 a share against an estimate of $0.64. It then raised its full-year outlook, which is the sort of thing a company does when it would like its performance review to be uneventful.

## The scorecard

Enterprise sales rose 4.4% in constant currency, above the 3% to 3.75% guidance, even with a drag of about 100 basis points from the timing of Flipkart's Big Billion Days sale, according to Investing.com's summary of the call. Walmart U.S. comparable sales rose 2.9%, or 3.6% excluding a 125-basis-point hit from "Maximum Fair Pricing" in health and wellness. U.S. e-commerce grew 23%, the eleventh straight quarter above 20%, and global e-commerce grew 17%.

The guidance moved up. Walmart now expects constant-currency sales growth of 4.9% to 5.2% for fiscal 2027, up from 4% to 5%. Operating income growth is forecast at 8.0% to 8.6%, up from 7% to 8.5%, and adjusted EPS at $2.84 to $2.89, up from $2.80 to $2.87. The arithmetic implies fourth-quarter EPS of $0.73 to $0.78. In performance-review language, the employee has exceeded expectations and promised to exceed them again, which is how every manager learns to fear a strong December.

## Where the margin comes from

The line item that deserves a second read is advertising. U.S. advertising grew 37%, including the acquired Vizio and Vibe businesses, and U.S. marketplace net sales rose 49%, per Stocktwits and Singju Post's transcript. Global membership fee revenue rose 17%.

Then came the remark that explains the rest. Asked by Morgan Stanley about the profitability of e-commerce, Chief Financial Officer John David Rainey said it is "pretty close to break even without advertising." That is a candid thing to say about a business growing 23%. It means that the part of Walmart that investors find most exciting is, absent the ad revenue, roughly a hobby. Rainey also told UBS that the business is "fundamentally changing," which is correct and which describes the company's own income statement rather neatly.

CEO John Furner, on the call, said e-commerce was the primary driver of the quarter again. Both statements can be true at once. The sales come from e-commerce, and the money comes from the people paying to appear in front of the shoppers e-commerce attracts.

## The bundle and its accounting

The quarter also carried a small lesson in how a company can move profit around itself. Walmart launched a bundled Walmart+ and Sam's Club Plus membership on October 20 at $120 a year, against up to nearly $400 if bought separately, per Investing.com. Furner said Sam's Club added "a couple million new members," and bundled members' online spend with Walmart rose more than 80%.

Sam's Club U.S. operating income fell 15.8%, a drop that Stocktwits and Singju Post both attribute to the bundle's accounting, while Walmart U.S. operating income rose 9% to $6.3 billion. The profit did not vanish. It was recorded in a different department, which is the corporate equivalent of a team getting credit for work that was done two floors away.

Walmart also flagged more than $2 billion in incremental diesel and transportation costs this year, per Investing.com, a reminder that a company can grow its ad business at 37% and still be undone by the price of a truck.

Elsewhere in the report, Sam's Club e-commerce grew 30%, and management said the quarter's U.S. comparable sales would have been stronger without the health and wellness pricing hit. Rainey told analysts the company aims to grow operating income faster than sales, a goal that is easier to meet when a growing share of revenue is advertising rather than groceries. Investors were listening for that, and the raised guidance suggests management knows it.

## The verdict

Shares were down about 1.5% in pre-market trading and up about 14% for the year, according to Stocktwits. The market, it appears, had already given the company full marks. Walmart's story is that of a grocer that discovered a media business inside its aisles and is now trying to explain the media business to shareholders without alarming the grocers.

The outcome for next year is not complicated. If shoppers keep coming and advertisers keep paying, the company will beat again. If either stops, the question of what Walmart is will be settled in a hurry.
