# Adobe Expects $13 Billion Black Friday, Beating Its Own Forecast

US shoppers spent $9.7 billion online on Black Friday through 6:30 p.m. Eastern, up 12.8% from a year earlier, Adobe said, according to a Reuters report published on 27 November. The company expects the final total for the day to land between $13 billion and $13.4 billion.

## The forecast, reviewed

Adobe's own forecast, issued in September, was for a rise of 9.2% to $12.9 billion. The day is therefore ahead of plan, at least on the arithmetic offered so far. The $13 billion to $13.4 billion range implies a final result above the September number, though the final tally was not available when the report was filed, and an expected total is not a total.

For a performance appraisal, this is a clean result: the forecaster beat the forecast. Nobody asked how the forecast was calibrated, because nobody ever does.

Adobe expects Cyber Monday spending of $15.1 billion, up 6.2%, and projects $47.5 billion for the whole stretch from Thanksgiving through Cyber Monday, a 7.4% increase, Reuters reported. It said it expected US holiday online spending to grow at a pace similar to last year's.

## The rival headline

Salesforce, which tracks a different slice of commerce, published its own numbers. In the first half of Black Friday, it said, sales reached $5.9 billion in the United States and $30.7 billion globally. It projects $18.7 billion for the full day in the United States, up 4%, and $83 billion globally, up 5.2%, according to the same article.

Set side by side, Adobe's $13 billion-plus and Salesforce's $18.7 billion refer to the same country and, broadly, the same day. The report does not explain the gap, and this publication will not guess at the methodologies. The reasonable inference is that each company counts something different, which is the sort of detail that tends to appear in a footnote, if at all.

The growth rates differ too. Adobe's early-evening figure shows 12.8% growth, while Salesforce projects 4% for the US. A boardroom trying to decide whether Black Friday was strong would be entitled to ask which of the two it is meant to believe.

## Whose number is it?

This is the part that interests the forensic accountant. Adobe sells marketing and analytics software. Salesforce sells customer software. Each has a commercial interest in being the authority on how consumers behave, and each publishes consumer data as a convenient demonstration of its reach. Neither is dishonest. Both are marketing.

That does not make the figures wrong. It makes them a particular kind of right: accurate to the sample, silent on the margin. Retailers who quote them in quarterly calls are borrowing a number whose definition they did not set. The temptation, in an earnings presentation, is to choose whichever benchmark flatters the quarter. The discipline is to say which one was chosen and why.

Management teams will, over the next fortnight, cite one or other of these figures as evidence that consumers are healthy, cautious or fond of discounts. The consumer is presumably all three, depending on the slide.

There is also the matter of what the forecasts imply for the holiday as a whole. Adobe's $47.5 billion projection for Thanksgiving through Cyber Monday, up 7.4%, assumes that the pace set so far continues through the weekend. Salesforce's global figure of $83 billion for Black Friday, up 5.2%, assumes that shoppers beyond the United States behave in a similar fashion. Each assumption is plausible. Each is also an assumption, a word that appears less often in press releases than in the notes behind them.

Retail chief executives, meanwhile, will take the cue. Investors will ask on earnings calls whether online demand is holding, and the answer will draw on whichever of these figures is most helpful. Management teams have long understood that the best statistic is the one that arrives on the day the question is asked.

## Rating

Black Friday online spending shows no sign of exhaustion. Adobe's early numbers ran ahead of its own forecast, the day's total is expected to exceed $13 billion, and the forecasters have already booked the Cyber Monday figure at $15.1 billion. Employee is meeting expectations, in the sense that the expectations were set by the employee.

Development areas: reconcile the two numbers, specify the denominators and agree, before the quarterly calls, on a single answer to the question of how big a day it was. Failing that, retailers are invited to choose the larger figure, as is traditional.
