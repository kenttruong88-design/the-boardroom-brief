# Disney Reportedly Eyes Thousands of Job Cuts and 20% Savings Targets

Disney is weighing "thousands of job cuts" and has asked some managers to target 20% in annual cost savings, according to a Bloomberg report dated 2 November that cited people familiar with the matter. The report, as relayed by the fan site MickeyVisit, said part of the savings would be reinvested in film and TV programming, technology, and the theme-parks and cruise businesses. Disney has not confirmed the plan in anything this correspondent has read.

## The performance review

Subject: Josh D'Amaro, chief executive since 18 March, when he succeeded Bob Iger, per Variety. Review period: eight months.

Objectives set: reduce costs while remaining the world's pre-eminent storyteller. Results to date: MickeyVisit counts this as the fifth round of cuts since D'Amaro took over, listing earlier rounds in marketing, Nat Geo, Pixar and ESPN, tech and HR, and Walt Disney World Imagineering. Those labels come from links on the site, and I have not independently confirmed each round, but the pattern is consistent with how the company has described itself. In an August shareholder letter, quoted by MickeyVisit, D'Amaro and Chief Financial Officer Hugh Johnston wrote: "We remain highly focused on reducing costs across the enterprise."

Manager's comment: consistent. Where an employee might be assessed on delivering a reorganisation, this one is being assessed on delivering the same reorganisation repeatedly, in instalments.

## A memo with a familiar shape

The April cuts, which Variety reported on 9 April as an expected 1,000 positions, many in marketing, came with a staff memo. The text, as published by The Statesman, spoke of the need to "streamline operations" and to build an "agile and technologically-enabled workforce". It added that the decisions were "not a reflection of their contributions", which is the sort of reassurance that arrives only when the contributions have already been counted.

Nothing in these phrases is new. A restructuring memo is a genre, and it has conventions: the verb is passive, the beneficiary is the future, and the people affected are described as colleagues until the third paragraph. What is notable is not the wording but the cadence. Variety put Disney's workforce at 231,000 full- and part-time employees, about 76% of them full-time. Cutting 1,000 roles from that base is a rounding error. Cutting thousands, at 20% savings targets for some teams, is a strategy.

## The reviewer's notes

D'Amaro's background is not in dispute. Per Variety, he joined Disney in 1998 at Disneyland, rose through consumer products and the parks, and was chairman of Disney Experiences before the board, ten members plus Iger, voted unanimously to make him chief executive. A parks executive taking over a media conglomerate is a story about faith in operations. Operations, as any consultant will tell you, is where cost lives.

The Wall Street Journal first reported the April plan, per Variety, and on 3 November UPI ran a headline saying Disney would see another round of layoffs. MickeyVisit says Dana Walden is now president and chief creative officer. Disney's peers are not exempt: Variety noted that Sony Pictures Entertainment had confirmed plans to cut hundreds of positions in the spring, and The Statesman reports that Paramount Skydance has cut about 2,000 jobs since David Ellison's takeover. The industry is in the middle of a collective performance review, and nobody is rated above "needs improvement".

## Reading the target

The 20% figure deserves forensic attention, because it is a target, not an outcome. According to MickeyVisit's account of the Bloomberg report, some managers were asked to aim for it. A cost target handed down to managers without an operating plan is a request for each manager to decide whose job counts as the cost. It is efficient only in the sense that it moves the decision to someone who will not have to sign the memo.

Disney has done this before at scale. Variety noted that the company's last large cuts were in 2023, around 7,000 positions, after Iger returned. The Statesman puts that earlier round at roughly 8,000 and dates it to 2022. The two accounts differ, so the precise figure is better left to Disney. Either way, the arithmetic of a company shedding thousands every few years and then hiring a new strategy is not unfamiliar.

The unanswered question is the one that the reinvestment language is meant to cover: if savings fund programming, technology and parks, which of those will also have fewer people working on them? Bloomberg's sources do not say. Neither, in the pieces I could read, does Disney, which declined to comment when Variety asked about the April plan.

## Conclusion

Overall rating: meets expectations, if the expectation is cuts. Development area: a plan whose second paragraph is not "again". Next review: shortly.
