# CEO Misconduct Exits Hit 17 This Year, Up From Eight

Seventeen chief executives left their jobs through September 2026 over allegations of professional misconduct, compared with five in all of 2025 and eight in 2024, according to data from the outplacement firm Challenger, Gray & Christmas, as reported by HR Dive on November 12. Two further departures this year were tied to allegations of sexual misconduct. The year is not over, which is a sentence boards should read slowly.

## The arithmetic of leaving

The numbers carry a caveat that the headlines usually trim. The 2026 figure covers nine months, while the comparison years are full calendar years. Even so, 17 is more than double the 2024 total and more than three times the 2025 total, which means the partial-year count is already ahead of both full years. A performance review would call this "exceeding last year's results", though not in the way anyone intended.

Challenger has also tracked a narrower category since 2016: chief executives who lost their jobs specifically over inappropriate, though consensual, workplace relationships. That count stands at 14. Across all categories of harassment or misconduct, 42 CEOs have been removed since 2016, according to the same data as relayed by HR Dive and its sister publication ESG Dive. The second outlet is by the same author and the same wire, so this desk counts the two as one source.

## A question of judgment

Andy Challenger, the firm's chief revenue officer, framed the trend not as a moral story but as a competence one, saying it is "a question of judgment." He also said the standard for company behavior comes from the top, and that every board in America should be paying attention, in a remark connected to the recent Nvidia situation. This desk has not independently verified the circumstances of that company's leadership change beyond what is already public, and Challenger's own research arm titled a November 11 post "Nvidia Probe Puts CEO Relationships Back in the Spotlight." What can be said is that the firm sees the topic as live.

Challenger's point is the useful one. For most of corporate history, a chief executive's private conduct was treated as a private matter until it became a public one. Boards now appear to be treating conduct as a governance issue from the start, which is to say that the code of conduct is no longer something written for the people who report to the person who signs it.

## What employees already know

The article also cited separate research on the working environments that surround these departures. In that study, 79% of U.S. employees in toxic workplaces blamed bad leadership. Some 39% of those who saw toxic behavior did not report it, and 45% did not trust human resources or leadership to help. Those figures come from a separate study referenced in the piece, and this desk has not reviewed its methodology.

Taken together, the two data sets describe a familiar gap. The people who work near the problem tend to know about it, the channels meant to receive their reports are not trusted, and the first official acknowledgment arrives when the board announces a departure and thanks the executive for their contributions.

## A note on process

What happens after an allegation is the part boards rarely describe. Some exits are immediate, some follow an investigation, and some are announced as retirements, so a count of departures is also a count of how candidly companies choose to word a press release. Challenger's tally relies on public announcements, which means it is a floor rather than a ceiling. An allegation that never reaches a filing never reaches a spreadsheet either, and the board's silence is itself a data point, though a notoriously hard one to chart.

## The forensic reading

Reading the 17 as evidence that executives are behaving worse is the weakest conclusion available. An equally plausible reading is that boards are acting sooner, that employees are reporting more, and that investors have grown less tolerant of leaders who generate their own risk disclosures. The data cannot separate those explanations, and Challenger's counts are a tally of announced exits rather than a measure of conduct.

What the numbers do show is that the cost of ignoring a conduct problem at the top has risen, and that boards are now behaving accordingly. The annual training module on workplace behavior has, at last, found its audience, and the audience is the one that signs off on the budget for it.
