# Bureau Veritas Shares Jump 6.6% on Advent Report, Then It Says It Is Not in Talks

Bureau Veritas, the French testing, inspection and certification group, spent Wednesday, November 25, in the unusual position of having its stock rise and then having to certify that nothing had happened. Shares closed up 6.6% at €31.80, after touching an intraday record of €33.31, following a Bloomberg report that private-equity firm Advent was weighing a bid. By 3 p.m. Paris time the company had issued a statement.

Bloomberg reported on Tuesday, November 24, that Advent was considering an offer and working with advisers. The report described the process as early-stage and said it might not lead to a deal. Advent declined to comment, according to Reuters. Bureau Veritas's market value was about $14.76 billion, per LSEG data, and the shares were up around 10% for the year before the move.

## The statement

The company said it "wishes to clarify that it is not engaged in discussions with the private equity firm Advent". It added: "As a matter of principle, Bureau Veritas does not comment on market rumors." Readers will notice that the second sentence arrives immediately after the company commented on a market rumour.

This is a perfectly good statement, and also a narrow one. Its subject is whether Bureau Veritas is in discussions. It does not say whether Advent is studying an offer, which is what Bloomberg reported. A company can be un-approached and still be a target, in the way that someone can be unaware they are being measured for a suit. The shares, notably, did not fall much on the denial.

## Performance review: Bureau Veritas, rumour management

The employee is, by profession, an auditor. Its business is to check that other companies' claims match reality, and here it has found a claim, the Bloomberg report, that it declares partly non-compliant. The audit was quick: a few hours from shares jumping to a corrective statement. Response time deserves a high score.

The wording is another matter. "Not engaged in discussions" is a precise formula, and the precision is the point. It does not rule out an unsolicited approach, a letter, or an offer, and it leaves room for the sentence "we have received a proposal", which would be the logical next one. The review would note that clarity was achieved at the level of the statement and not at the level of the situation.

## The interested parties

Advent has resources. It raised $30 billion last month for its buyout and mid-market funds, according to the reports. A company with a market value near $15 billion is large for a mid-market fund and manageable for a flagship one, which makes the pairing plausible, if not confirmed. Bloomberg's own report is explicit that no deal may follow.

Wendel, the French investment group that owns 21.4% of Bureau Veritas, had not commented. For any bidder, Wendel is the number that matters. A holder with a fifth of the stock cannot be ignored and need not be loud, and its silence on a day of this kind is a position in itself, if not an announcement.

The company has also been through this before in a different guise. Merger discussions with SGS, a rival in the same line of work, ended in January 2025. A sector with a small number of large players is one in which the available transactions are, by design, repeated, rearranged and eventually revisited. Private equity, which can bid for a certifier without the competition-law complications of a rival, has an obvious interest in the asset.

## The forensic reading

What do we actually know? A newswire reported that a firm was weighing a bid. The firm did not comment. The target said it was not in discussions. The share price rose 6.6% on the day and, as far as this correspondent can establish, retained most of it. The market has read a denial of talks and concluded that it is a denial of talks, which is a different thing from a denial of interest.

The lesson for executives is a familiar one. The most dangerous sentence in a rumour is the carefully correct one. A flat denial of the whole premise would have been more useful to shareholders and more risky for the company if later contradicted. The narrow denial carries less risk for the company and passes some of it to the shareholder. Whether that counts as stewardship is, like so much in this field, a matter of how the review is written.
