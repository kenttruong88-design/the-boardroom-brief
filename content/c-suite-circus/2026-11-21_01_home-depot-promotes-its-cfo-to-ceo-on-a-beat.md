# Home Depot Promotes Its CFO to CEO on a Quarterly Beat

Home Depot announced on Tuesday 17 November that Ted Decker will step down as president and chief executive after four years, remaining as chairman, and that chief financial officer Richard McPhail will succeed him. The change takes effect on 23 November. The company announced it alongside third-quarter results, which it said exceeded its own expectations, and shares fell about 2 percent in premarket trading, according to IBTimes.

What follows is a performance review of the transition, since it is the closest thing to one that shareholders will receive.

## Outgoing: Ted Decker

Decker has held the top job for four years. He recently returned from a temporary medical leave that began in August, IBTimes reported. Home Depot did not give a reason for the departure and did not attribute it to its financial results, which is, in fairness, a reasonable thing to withhold on the day those results arrive.

His record, on the evidence of the quarter just reported, is respectable. Revenue was $43.4 billion, up 4.9 percent and ahead of the roughly $42.8 billion analysts expected. Comparable sales rose 2.2 percent. Net earnings were $3.7 billion, against $3.6 billion a year earlier, and diluted earnings per share were $3.72 against $3.62. Adjusted earnings per share of $3.86 compared with $3.74 a year earlier and analyst expectations of $3.84, per IBTimes. Quarterly beats of a cent or two are the sort of performance on which an executive team can build a case for continuity.

Decker, speaking as chairman in the company's release, said he had tremendous confidence in his successors. It is difficult to argue with an endorsement from the person whose job they are taking, although it is also the only kind of endorsement the form permits.

## Incoming: Richard McPhail

McPhail has been with Home Depot since 2005 and has been chief financial officer since September 2019. He joins the board with the new role. During Decker's leave, IBTimes reported, McPhail oversaw finance and the company's professional-business subsidiaries, while Ann-Marie Campbell ran day-to-day retail operations. The board therefore appears to have promoted the officers who were already doing the jobs, which is the least adventurous version of succession and, for that reason, the safest.

Campbell, who has spent 41 years at the company, becomes president and chief operating officer. Jordan Broggi, a 13-year veteran who previously ran interconnected retail, becomes executive vice-president and CFO. The combined tenure of the new leadership team is, by the standards of American public companies, closer to a pension than to a turnaround.

Independent lead director Greg Brenneman said in the company's statement that McPhail and Campbell were "the ideal leaders to continue building on the momentum we're seeing in the business". The brief, in other words, is continuity.

## The outlook section

Succession announcements prefer that the numbers be read last. The updated guidance for the full fiscal year is a mixed document. Total sales growth is now expected to be about 4.5 percent, the top of the earlier range of 2.5 to 4.5 percent. Comparable sales growth is expected at about 1.4 percent. Adjusted earnings per share growth is expected at about 2 percent. Adjusted operating margin is expected at about 12.8 percent, near the low end of the prior range.

The revenue line therefore improved while the margin line settled at the lower end, which is the sort of detail that a market notices even when a press release does not highlight it. It may explain the 2 percent drop in the shares. It certainly makes the incoming CEO's first inherited metric a margin figure he has been forecasting, as CFO, for several years.

The transaction count tells a similar story. Customer transactions were about 392.7 million, against 393.5 million a year earlier, a small decline. Revenue growth was therefore driven by the average ticket, which rose to $93.09 from $90.39. Customers visited slightly less often and spent slightly more each time. In a business that sells ladders, this counts as either pricing power or a very long hold on the ladder.

McPhail's own comment in the earnings release was that third-quarter results exceeded the company's expectations. The line is useful because it is the CEO-designate describing the CFO's forecast.

## Overall rating

Meets expectations, with a note on development. The handover is orderly, the bench was prepared, and the numbers are decent. The company has not said why the change happens now, and in succession planning, silence is a policy as well. Investors will therefore have to wait for the first earnings call under the new management to learn what, if anything, has changed.

Until then, the best available evidence is the one fact the company chose to lead with: the quarter was good, the margin guidance was not, and the person who produced both is now the one explaining them.
