# Target Posts Best Sales Growth in Five Years, With Refund Help

Target reported on Wednesday 18 November that comparable sales rose 5.6 percent in the third quarter, its strongest growth in five years, and that earnings per share reached $2.65. Of that, 50 cents came from a pretax tariff refund of $301 million. The company raised its full-year outlook, and the Associated Press had Target shares up 5.8 percent by 10am Eastern, even though they had dipped in premarket trading.

Because the quarter arrives with a note attached, it is best read the way an employee's year-end review should be read: first for what was achieved, then for what was helped along.

## Objectives achieved

The results for the quarter ended 31 October are strong by any method of accounting. Net sales were $27.1 billion, up 7.1 percent. Comparable sales rose 5.6 percent against a consensus of about 3.8 percent, according to the trade press coverage. Traffic grew 5.0 percent, which matters because it implies customers rather than prices are doing the work. Store comparable sales rose 4.6 percent and digital comparable sales 9.8 percent. Non-merchandise sales rose 19.3 percent.

Operating income was $1.7 billion, a 6.2 percent margin, and net earnings were $1.21 billion. Gross margin was 30.5 percent. The company bought back $251 million of stock. Earnings per share of $2.65 compared with $1.51 on a GAAP basis and $1.78 on an adjusted basis a year earlier, and with analyst expectations of about $2.07, according to Fast Company.

These figures describe a retailer that has fixed something. Chief executive Michael Fiddelke, who took over in February, said in the company's release that the results reflected continued progress against its strategic priorities. Fiddelke told the AP that the driver was the cumulative impact of changes across the business. In performance-review language, this is "consistently exceeds expectations through cross-functional execution", and for once the evidence is on the page.

## Development areas

The asterisk is the tariff refund. Target said it recognised $301 million before tax, equal to 50 cents a share and $228 million of net earnings. Without it, earnings per share would have been $2.15, which still beats the $2.07 consensus by 8 cents, as Fast Company noted. The refund therefore did not create the beat; it made a good beat look like a spectacular one.

The guidance leans on it harder. Target now expects net sales growth of about 6 percent for the full year, a percentage point above the prior range and well above the 5 percent it initially forecast. It expects an operating margin of about 6.2 percent, including about 1.1 percentage points from tariff refunds. And it expects earnings per share of $10.65 to $11.40, including about $2.14 from refunds recognised to date. The guidance excludes any future refunds.

A reader with a calculator can remove the $2.14 and arrive at a range of $8.51 to $9.26 for the underlying business. That arithmetic is this publication's, not the company's, and Target has not presented it that way. The point is only that roughly a fifth of the top of the range comes from a source that the company itself describes as recognised to date rather than recurring.

The management case for the quarter, in other words, can be made in two ways. Excluding the refund, the retailer beat comfortably and grew quickly. Including it, the retailer beat spectacularly and raised guidance. Both versions appear in the earnings release, which is the correct approach to disclosure, and also the one that tends to produce two different headlines.

## The sector

The refund was not unique to Target. InvestingLive reported that Lowe's gross margin fell by 124 basis points to 32.95 percent in its quarter, even with about $250 million of tariff refunds. The comparison is flattering to Target: a similar tailwind, a very different result.

Investors, for their part, rewarded the traffic. The AP showed Target up 5.8 percent and TJX up 9.9 percent in mid-morning trading, while Lowe's was down 0.6 percent. Fast Company noted that Target shares had already risen more than 60 percent this year, and speculated that the premarket dip reflected profit-taking or doubts about how long the refunds would last. That is the analyst's inference, not the company's, and the afternoon rally was the market's own answer.

Capital spending is the other signal. Target said 2027 capital expenditure would be $5 billion to $6 billion, about $1 billion more than in 2026. Companies that expect refunds to vanish do not usually plan to spend more.

## Overall rating

Exceeds expectations, with a footnote. The footnote is $301 million. The retailer has returned to growth, the customer is visiting more often, and the chief executive has a story to tell that does not depend on a single line of the income statement. What remains unclear is the story for next year's review, when the refund is either repeated, or must be explained.
