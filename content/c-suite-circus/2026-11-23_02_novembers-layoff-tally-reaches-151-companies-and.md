# November's Layoff Tally Reaches 151 Companies and 65,000 Jobs, Tracker Says

The layoff tracker LayoffHedge counted 151 companies and 65,000 job cuts in November as of its latest update on November 22, led by BT Group's 7,731 positions, or 8.6% of its workforce, announced on November 5. The tracker does not list sources for individual rows, and the review below treats the total accordingly.

## The top of the table

According to LayoffHedge, the next largest cut after BT is KLM's 5,000 positions, or 15.1%, also on November 5. Schaeffler follows with 3,460 on November 3, and Fiserv with 3,000 on the same day. Boeing's 2,500, recorded for November 9, completes the top five. The sectors are a telecoms group, an airline, an automotive supplier, a payments company and an aircraft maker. It is a fair cross-section of the economy, which is the one respect in which it is reassuring.

Added together, the five largest rows come to 21,691 positions, which is a little over a third of the tracker's headline total. The remaining 146 companies on the list therefore account for the other two thirds, at an average of roughly 300 jobs each, assuming the total is right. That arithmetic is the strongest argument for the tracker's general shape: the long tail of modest cuts is as much a feature of November as the headline names, and it is the part that rarely makes a press release.

The percentages are more revealing than the headcounts. KLM's cut of 15.1% is large for any company, and BT's 8.6% is not small. A reduction of that size does not describe pruning; it describes a decision that the previous structure was wrong, communicated in the language of a decision that it was right.

## The caveats the tracker does not add

A forensic reader will note that the 65,000 figure should not be read as an audited total. The tracker's table repeats some companies on separate rows, including Qualcomm, Amazon, Mueller Water Products and The Children's Place, and its categories, such as "cost cut," "restructure," "weak demand," "merger" and "AI-driven," are the site's own. No row cites a source. A reasonable reading is that November has been a heavy month for job cuts, and that the exact size of it is unknown.

Other counters offer less tidy but more traceable numbers. As of November 18, Stocktwits reported, citing Layoffs.fyi, that 149,158 tech employees at 364 companies had been laid off so far this year. SalesforceBen cited an earlier figure from the same tracker of 142,004 at 346 companies. Eighteen companies and about 7,000 tech jobs, in other words, passed between the two readings. That is the sort of statistic that a reader must treat as an approximation, but the direction of it requires no footnote.

## The language of departure

The announcements themselves follow a recognisable form. After ServiceNow's most recent round, the company said it was "making necessary changes to how we are structured," according to People Matters, and that it was managing headcount "with discipline" to end 2026 where it started. Intel's spokesperson told Business Insider, per Stocktwits, that the company continually assesses its workforce to ensure that priorities and resources are aligned. Neither statement contains a number.

The numbers that do exist are in the filings. Intel employed 81,100 people at the end of September, against 85,100 at the end of 2025, Stocktwits reported, citing company filings. That is a reduction of 4,000 without any single announcement bearing the figure. A company can lose a school's worth of staff and still keep its press statements brief.

Microsoft, Stocktwits adds, reportedly offered buyouts to 60,000 workers last month. The word "reportedly" carries the weight of that sentence, since the company's own account is not in the source reviewed.

## Assessment

The performance-review framing is tempting here, but it fits poorly. Employees are reviewed against objectives. Layoffs are, by design, a failure to meet one's own objectives, reallocated to those who did not set them. The usual management defence is that the company is "investing in AI," which is a sentence with the useful quality of being both true and unfalsifiable.

The honest observation is that, by any count, November has been a month in which a very large number of large employers decided that they employed too many people. The less comfortable observation is that most of them had said, in earlier quarters, that they employed exactly the right number.
