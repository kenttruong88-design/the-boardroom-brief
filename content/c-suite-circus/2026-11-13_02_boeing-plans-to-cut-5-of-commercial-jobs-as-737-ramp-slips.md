# Boeing Plans to Cut 5% of Commercial Jobs as 737 Ramp Slips

**Performance review: Boeing Commercial Airplanes, Q4 workforce planning. Reviewer: Miles Bancroft. Overall rating: Below Plan.**

Boeing Commercial Airplanes will cut about 5% of its workforce, with layoff notices starting in December, after its production plans for the 737 and 787 moved later, according to reports based on a memo from Stephanie Pope, the unit's chief executive, dated November 9. TheStreet put the figure at roughly 2,500 jobs, calculated from the 50,494 employees the unit had at the end of 2025. The reductions will come through attrition and involuntary layoffs.

## Objective: align headcount with output

Pope's memo is a model of plain language, according to KOMO News, which quoted her as writing: "Adjusting our production schedules and reducing aircraft output puts us in an overstaffed position." It is the most direct sentence in the recent history of aerospace human resources. A company can usually describe a layoff as a transformation, a reallocation or a journey. Here, the reasoning is arithmetic. Fewer planes are being built than were planned, so fewer people are needed to build them.

Affected employees will receive career transition services, layoff benefits and employee assistance support, KOMO reported. Bloomberg, as cited by TheStreet, called it the unit's first significant reduction in close to two years, and the first since CEO Kelly Ortberg's October 2024 plan to cut about 17,000 jobs, or about 10% of the workforce.

## Q3 results: the context

The review for the third quarter is not a flattering one. Boeing reported third-quarter earnings on October 27 that missed expectations, and the stock fell nearly 10% that day, according to TheStreet. Through the first nine months of the year, 737 deliveries totalled 367, which leaves the 500-aircraft full-year target looking out of reach.

On the call, Ortberg was asked about the pace of the ramp. He said, as quoted by CNBC and reported by TheStreet: "I know everybody wants us to go faster, but we're going to do it right." As a statement of management philosophy it is hard to fault. As a statement of schedule, it is a notice. According to TheStreet, citing the company's quarterly filing, the 737 rate of 47 a month has slipped to early 2027, and the step to 52 a month has moved to the second half of 2027. The 787's planned rate increases were also delayed in the third quarter.

## The balance sheet, reviewed

Boeing's backlog stands at $745 billion, which is a number that suggests the problem is not demand. Its long-term debt is about $41 billion, according to TheStreet. The Commercial Airplanes division has not posted a full-year operating profit since 2018, the same article noted. Taken together, these figures describe a company with an enormous order book, an expensive balance sheet and a factory that cannot convert one into the other fast enough.

Analysts have responded accordingly. Jefferies said the revised long-term free-cash-flow guidance now trails the earlier consensus by roughly a year, per TheStreet. Morgan Stanley stressed the slower timeline. In plain terms, the profits investors were expecting in 2027 and 2028 are now more likely to be profits in 2028 and 2029, which is the sort of rescheduling that is easy to describe and unpleasant to receive.

## Manager's assessment

There is a case that Boeing's approach is the right one. A company that has been through several years of quality and safety scrutiny has an obvious incentive to build slowly and correctly rather than quickly and badly. Ortberg's phrase, "we're going to do it right," is a promise customers and regulators will want kept. The cost of keeping it is that the production line must be sized to the rate that is actually achievable, not the one that was hoped for.

The staffing consequence follows directly. Scott Hamilton of Leeham, who had predicted the cut, was cited by KOMO as calling the Renton wing factory a bottleneck. If a bottleneck is the limiting factor, adding people elsewhere in the system does not add aircraft. It adds cost.

## Development areas

Three items for the next cycle. First, align the forecast with the factory, not with the order book. Second, communicate the next production milestone only once the previous one has been met. Third, recognise that the 2,500 employees who will receive notices in December were not the cause of the delay, and that a memo using the word "overstaffed" is accurate and will land hard on the people it describes.

Recommendation: retain the leadership, retain the backlog, and revisit the headcount only when the rate has been proven rather than promised.
