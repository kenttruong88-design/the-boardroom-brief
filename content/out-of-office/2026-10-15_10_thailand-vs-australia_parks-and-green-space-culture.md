# One City Is Rationing Shade By The Square Metre. One Country Has So Much Park It Doesn't Bother Naming Half Of It.

🇹🇭 Thailand vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I once walked forty minutes through central Bangkok looking for a patch of shaded grass large enough to sit on, and found, eventually, a pocket park roughly the size of a tennis court shared with what felt like half the neighbourhood. I have also stood in an Australian suburb and been told, with a shrug, that the "local park" was actually three separate reserves that all happened to blend into one another, nobody had bothered naming the boundary.

Green space is one of those things you don't notice a country has engineered, deliberately or by sheer geographic luck, until you're desperately looking for a patch of grass and either find thirty of them or none at all.

## 🇹🇭 Thailand: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn where the nearest pocket park actually is; Bangkok's are small, scattered, and precious | Expect a sprawling Western-style park experience in the city centre; space is genuinely scarce |
| Go early morning or evening; midday heat makes even good green space unusable for hours | Assume all of Thailand matches Bangkok's park scarcity — rural and northern areas differ hugely |
| Appreciate "15 Minute Park" style pocket parks for what they are: a genuine, recent civic effort | Expect every pocket park to feel equally welcoming; quality and upkeep vary block to block |
| Use a park's shade and benches as a legitimate midday retreat, the way locals do | Treat a tiny park as a disappointment; by local standards, any accessible green patch is a win |

## 🇦🇺 Australia: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect genuinely enormous public green space as the default, not the exception | Be surprised when a "local park" turns out to be several merged reserves nobody bothered naming clearly |
| Use council-run outdoor gym equipment and BBQ facilities freely; they're built for public use | Assume all parks are equally maintained; rural and outer-suburban upkeep can vary |
| Treat a weekend park picnic or barbecue as a default, near-universal social activity | Underestimate wildlife common sense; even a city park can have genuine local hazards |
| Appreciate how much civic land is simply, unremarkably, green — it's not an accident | Compare it unfavourably to a tiny tropical city and expect anyone here to find the comparison fair |

## Thailand: Green Space As Precious, Rationed Resource

Bangkok's relationship with green space is, by the numbers, genuinely stark. Research from the Stockholm Environment Institute puts green space per resident at roughly 7.6 square metres — comfortably below the World Health Organization's own recommended minimum of 9 square metres per person, in a megacity pushing close to ten million inhabitants. That shortfall isn't abstract; it shows up as real, lived scarcity, the kind where a decent patch of shaded grass becomes something you plan a walk around rather than stumble into.

The city's response has been genuinely inventive rather than defeatist. The "15 Minute Park" initiative, launched with a target of 500 pocket parks designed to put green space within 800 metres of every resident's home, had delivered around 196 of them within its first couple of years — small by international standards (researchers note some fit into less space than a football pitch) but meaningful precisely because of how little alternative space exists. One example, a pocket park tucked beside Hua Lamphong, covers barely over a thousand square metres and still functions as a genuine neighbourhood anchor, used for everything from morning exercise to evening gatherings once the worst of the day's heat has finally broken.

What research into these pocket parks found, interestingly, complicates the easy narrative: a university study of Bangkok residents found larger community parks — the five-to-twenty-hectare range — were far more strongly associated with actual physical activity and psychological wellbeing than the tiny pocket parks, which showed no clear measurable benefit on those same fronts. In other words, Bangkok's pocket-park solution is solving an access problem, genuinely and admirably, without quite solving the deeper space problem underneath it. More parks, still not enough park.

## Australia: Green Space As Unremarkable Abundance

Australia's relationship with green space sits at the absolute opposite extreme, to the point where most Australians don't experience it as a resource at all — it's simply the backdrop, assumed and unremarked upon, the way other countries assume roads or electricity. Council-run parks come standard with free outdoor gym equipment, barbecue facilities, and enough unstructured grass that a casual Sunday gathering can relocate itself three times across an afternoon without anyone needing to book anything, because there's always more park where that came from.

What genuinely disorients visitors from genuinely space-constrained cities isn't the quantity so much as the casualness. A "local park" in an Australian suburb will frequently turn out to be several separate council reserves that have simply grown together at the edges, with nobody particularly bothered about where one technically ends and the next begins, because the question has never mattered enough to settle definitively. Ask a local the name of "that park" and you may get three different answers, all correct, none of them contradicting each other in any way that matters locally.

The flip side of this abundance, worth noting honestly, is that maintenance quality isn't uniform — outer-suburban or rural green space doesn't always get the same council attention as a flagship inner-city park, and genuine local wildlife hazards (snakes, certain spiders, occasionally more dramatic encounters) are a real consideration even in otherwise manicured public green space, a fact that newcomers from genuinely concrete-dense cities tend to find either thrilling or alarming, depending on temperament. But the baseline assumption — that green, usable, free public space will simply be there, nearby, whenever you want it — is one Australians have never had cause to question, which is precisely what makes Bangkok's careful, metre-by-metre pocket-park strategy look so foreign by comparison.

## The Verdict

Thailand, or at least Bangkok specifically, treats green space as a precious, actively managed, carefully distributed resource, measured in square metres per person and engineered pocket by pocket into a genuinely space-starved city. Australia treats green space as ambient, assumed, borderline infinite — so abundant that naming its exact boundaries has never felt urgent enough to bother with. Comparing the two isn't entirely fair, geographically speaking, but that's rather the point of putting them side by side: it shows exactly how differently "normal" gets defined depending on what a place started with.

If I had to live somewhere and actually use the local green space daily, Australia wins without much contest — abundance is simply more pleasant than scarcity, whatever clever pocket-park engineering you dress the scarcity up in. But I'll admit real admiration for Bangkok's effort here; turning a genuine, measurable shortfall into 196 small, dignified neighbourhood anchors within a couple of years is the kind of practical civic response most space-rich cities would never have bothered attempting, because they'd never have needed to.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/Bangkok — paraphrased: a resident explained that knowing which pocket parks have genuine midday shade versus which are just decorative is local knowledge that takes months to build, and that locals will happily share their personal shortlist if asked.*

> *Internations Bangkok — paraphrased: an expat described the adjustment from a green, spacious home city as "learning to treasure a strip of grass the size of a living room," and said it changed how they valued small green spaces permanently, even after later moves.*

> *expat.com Australia forum — paraphrased: several posters debated which suburb had "the best" park access, with most agreeing the real differentiator wasn't size but proximity to water, shade, and a decent coffee van within walking distance.*

## Conclusion

Bangkok will teach you to treasure a thousand square metres of shaded grass like it's a genuine luxury, because by the numbers, it is. Australia will teach you to barely notice a park exists at all, right up until you leave and discover most of the world doesn't have one within easy reach. I've sat gratefully on a Bangkok pocket-park bench and sprawled carelessly across an Australian reserve twice its size without a second thought, and the honest truth is both experiences taught me something real about what "enough" green space actually means. One place rations it carefully. The other has never had to.
