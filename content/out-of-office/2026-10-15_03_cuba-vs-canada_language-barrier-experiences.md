# One Country Makes You Learn Spanish By Force Of Silence. The Other Makes You Apologise In Two Languages.

🇨🇺 Cuba vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have been charmingly misunderstood in a Havana paladar for forty-five minutes over the word for "rare," and I have been corrected, gently but relentlessly, by a Montreal barista for ordering a coffee in the wrong official language entirely. Both experiences cost me dignity. Only one cost me wifi minutes.

Language barriers reveal less about vocabulary than about infrastructure, politics, and how a country has decided to handle the fact that not everyone speaks the same tongue. Cuba and Canada could not have arrived at more different answers.

## 🇨🇺 Cuba: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn functional Spanish before arrival — English fluency is rare outside tourist zones | Expect reliable wifi to translate anything for you on demand; connections are patchy and metered |
| Treat public wifi-park gatherings as a genuine social and linguistic lifeline | Assume a smartphone dictionary app will save you mid-negotiation at a market stall |
| Carry small cash and patience; transactions double as improvised language lessons | Get frustrated when a conversation takes four tries and a lot of hand gestures to land |
| Lean on warmth and gesture — Cubans are famously generous interpreters of bad grammar | Expect to simply "look it up" the way you would anywhere with constant connectivity |

## 🇨🇦 Canada: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn which province you're in before assuming English will do; Quebec has opinions | Call it "Canadian French" within earshot of a Quebecker — say "Québécois" or brace yourself |
| Expect government services, packaging, and signage to be bilingual by law, everywhere | Assume bilingual signage means bilingual service; rural counters may default hard to one language |
| Treat a French mistake in Montreal as a genuine social event, not a private embarrassment | Mix up "francophone pride" with hostility — most corrections come from enthusiasm, not judgment |
| Use translation apps freely; connectivity is excellent and nobody will blink | Underestimate how seriously official bilingualism is taken in federal jobs and paperwork |

## Cuba: The Barrier Is Analogue, And That's The Point

Cuba's language barrier isn't really about Spanish at all — plenty of visitors manage with a phrasebook and some patience. It's about the fact that you cannot simply outsource the problem to your phone the way you can everywhere else on earth, and that single missing crutch changes everything. Internet access in Cuba has historically been described, accurately, as fleeting and expensive; a new state telecommunications scheme lets Cubans buy home connections for roughly fifteen to over a hundred dollars a month for a fixed number of hours, numbers that would be considered a scandal anywhere with real broadband competition. Public wifi hotspots — clusters of people hunched around a single glowing screen in a park, a scene repeated across Havana — exist precisely because nobody can simply pull up Google Translate from their kitchen table.

What this produces is a genuinely old-fashioned language-barrier experience, the kind most of the world abandoned around 2010. You negotiate with your hands. You repeat yourself slowly, not because Cubans are unused to foreigners — Havana has seen every kind of tourist imaginable — but because the usual safety net simply isn't there in real time. Reader accounts collected alongside reporting on Cuba's connectivity culture note average monthly salaries hovering around twenty-five dollars, and no functioning credit card system for most residents, meaning the entire economy around tourist communication runs on cash, goodwill, and an almost theatrical willingness to mime your way through a menu.

The upside, and I say this as someone who has been linguistically humiliated on four continents, is that Cubans are extraordinarily forgiving interpreters. Badly conjugated Spanish gets met with warmth, not the faint eye-roll you'd get in, say, Paris. The barrier is real. The judgment, refreshingly, is not.

## Canada: The Barrier Is Legal, And Everyone Has Opinions About It

Canada's language situation is the precise opposite problem: not too little infrastructure, but a fully codified, federally mandated, occasionally fiercely defended bilingual system that newcomers consistently underestimate until they've accidentally committed a small diplomatic incident in a Montreal bakery. English and French are both official languages nationally, government services and product packaging are required to be bilingual, and Quebec in particular treats the French language not as a courtesy but as a matter of cultural survival, backed by provincial language laws that can, and do, generate genuine local controversy over storefront signage.

What trips up expats isn't the existence of two languages — most countries manage that fine — it's the emotional charge attached to getting it wrong. Call Québécois French "Canadian French" in Montreal and watch a perfectly pleasant conversation cool by several degrees; the distinction isn't pedantry, it's identity. Meanwhile, outside Quebec, "bilingual Canada" can be something of a polite fiction at the counter level — plenty of rural service interactions default hard to English with barely a nod to the national branding, which confuses newcomers who arrived expecting French everywhere and got none of it west of Ottawa.

The genuinely funny part, to me, is how connectivity flips the entire power dynamic from Cuba's situation. Canadians have blisteringly fast internet and will absolutely watch you fumble through Google Translate in real time rather than switch languages for your convenience, not out of malice but because the correction itself is considered a kind of gift — a chance to practice, to be corrected gently, to improve. It's language-as-ongoing-project rather than language-as-obstacle. Exhausting in its own way. Just a completely different flavour of exhausting.

## The Verdict

Cuba's barrier is infrastructural — you're cut off from your usual digital safety net and have to actually, physically communicate like it's 1998, which is either charming or terrifying depending on your relationship with eye contact. Canada's barrier is bureaucratic and emotional — the tools to translate are all there, but the social stakes of using the wrong language in the wrong province are oddly high for a country that markets itself globally as endlessly polite.

Given the choice, I'd rather be charmingly misunderstood over mojitos in Havana than correctly understood but quietly judged over a double-double in Montreal. Cuba's barrier forces connection. Canada's barrier, somehow, despite every resource imaginable, occasionally manufactures distance instead. That's the real surprise twist nobody mentions in the brochure.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/cuba — paraphrased: a traveller asked how locals manage without constant internet translation, and residents explained that wifi-park gatherings function as much as a social ritual as a technical necessity, with strangers helping translate for each other as a matter of course.*

> *Internations Havana — paraphrased: a longtime resident described learning more functional Spanish in three weeks without a phone than in three years of a language app, purely because there was no alternative on offer.*

> *expat.com Canada forum — paraphrased: several newcomers to Quebec agreed that the biggest shock wasn't the French itself, but discovering that calling it "Canadian French" in the wrong room could derail an otherwise friendly conversation entirely.*

## Conclusion

Cuba teaches you to communicate with your whole body because the wifi won't save you. Canada teaches you that having two official languages doesn't mean either one is guaranteed at the counter, and that enthusiasm for correcting your grammar is apparently a national love language in Quebec. I'd rate Cuba's barrier the more honest one — at least nobody's pretending the system works perfectly. Canada's barrier just hides behind excellent branding. Learn your "por favor" before Havana, and learn the difference between "Québécois" and "Canadian French" before Montreal does the correcting for you.
