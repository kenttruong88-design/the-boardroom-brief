# One Country Lights An Entire Subcontinent, The Other Mulls Wine Until February

🇮🇳 India 🇦🇹 Austria

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

If you want to understand a culture, don't watch how it works. Watch how it stops working, on purpose, together. India shuts down — loudly, luminously, in wildly different ways depending on which state you're standing in — for Diwali, the festival of lights, a celebration so vast in scale and so regionally varied that "Diwali" in Mumbai and "Diwali" in Kolkata are, in practice, close to different holidays wearing the same name. Austria, by contrast, commits to an entirely different register of festivity: the Christkindlmarkt, the Christmas market, a slower, colder, more deliberately unhurried tradition of mulled wine, roasted chestnuts, and wooden stalls that transforms every Austrian city and town into a six-week-long, candlelit ritual running from late November through the new year.

Diwali's scale is genuinely hard to overstate for anyone arriving without context — homes and streets strung with diyas (oil lamps) and fairy lights, rangoli patterns drawn at thresholds, fireworks that turn entire cities' night skies incandescent, and a religious and cultural meaning that shifts by region: Lakshmi puja and the worship of prosperity dominates in the north and west, while Kali puja takes precedence in Bengal, and parts of the south fold in their own distinct harvest and new-year associations. It's simultaneously a single, nationally recognised public holiday and a dozen genuinely different regional festivals sharing a name and a rough calendar date. Austria's Christkindlmarkt tradition runs on a completely different rhythm — not one explosive night but a sustained, six-week season, market stalls appearing in main squares from Vienna to the smallest Tyrolean village, selling handicrafts, spiced wine served in reusable, deposit-charged mugs, and roasted chestnuts, building steadily toward Christmas itself rather than climaxing in a single spectacular evening.

## Do's & Don'ts: India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask which regional tradition your hosts follow before assuming — Diwali's meaning and rituals genuinely shift by state and community | Assume fireworks will be a brief, contained affair — in many cities they run for hours across multiple nights, loud well past what you'd expect |
| Accept sweets (mithai) graciously when offered — gifting and sharing sweets is a core, near-universal part of the celebration | Schedule anything demanding during the days immediately around Diwali — travel, business, and even basic services slow dramatically |
| Join a rangoli-making or diya-lighting activity if invited — participation is genuinely welcomed, not just tolerated | Wear plain, muted clothing to a Diwali gathering if you have anything brighter — festival dress leans vibrant, and you won't be underdressed by going bold |

## Do's & Don'ts: Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pay the small deposit (Pfand) for your Glühwein mug and either return it or keep it as a souvenir — both are completely normal | Rush through a market visit — Christkindlmärkte reward slow, repeated evening visits across the whole season, not a single hurried stop |
| Visit smaller, local Christkindlmärkte beyond Vienna's famous ones — regional and village markets are often considered more authentic | Expect markets to run past the holiday season — most wind down by early January, so don't assume the atmosphere lasts indefinitely |
| Bundle up properly and embrace standing outdoors — the whole ritual assumes cold weather and treats it as part of the charm, not an obstacle | Be surprised by the sheer density of Austria's public holiday calendar — plan ahead, as closures cluster heavily around Christmas, New Year, and beyond |

## India: Diwali Is Not One Festival, It's Several Wearing A Trench Coat

What surprises newcomers most about Diwali isn't its scale, which every guidebook prepares you for, but its regional plurality, which almost none do. In much of north and west India, the festival centres on Lakshmi puja, honouring the goddess of prosperity, with homes cleaned and lit specifically to welcome her in. Travel to Bengal, and the dominant observance shifts to Kali puja instead, a different deity, a different tone, timed to the same lunar calendar but carrying distinct ritual weight. Parts of the south layer in harvest and new-year associations that predate or run parallel to the north Indian narrative entirely. None of this is contradiction; it's the ordinary texture of a country where "national festival" has always meant "framework flexible enough for genuinely different traditions to coexist under one name and one public holiday." The sensory experience, wherever you land, tends to share a throughline: diyas and string lights transforming entire streets after dark, rangoli patterns painstakingly drawn at doorways each morning, fireworks that, depending on city and local regulation, can run considerably longer and louder than a newcomer expects, and an obligation to share sweets — mithai — with neighbours, colleagues, and near-strangers alike, a gesture that functions as both celebration and social glue.

## Austria: A Season, Not A Night

Austria's Christkindlmarkt tradition rejects the single-spectacular-night model entirely in favour of sustained, weeks-long ritual. Vienna alone hosts a genuine circuit of markets — the famous one in front of the Rathaus, plus others scattered across the city's squares, each with a slightly different character, crowd, and stall selection — and the serious local move is to treat this as a season-long activity, returning multiple times through late November, December, and into the new year rather than ticking it off in one visit. The Glühwein mug deposit system is a small but telling detail: you pay extra for the reusable mug itself, can return it for a refund, or simply keep it, and most locals have accumulated a small, informal collection of Christkindlmarkt mugs from years of markets they liked enough to not bother returning the cup. What ties the whole tradition together is a genuine comfort with cold, dark, outdoor lingering — nobody's rushing through a Christkindlmarkt visit, and the ritual assumes you'll stand outside in proper winter clothing, mug in hand, for considerably longer than efficiency would suggest, precisely because the slowness is the point. Beyond Vienna, smaller town and village markets carry their own devoted followings, often considered by Austrians themselves to be more genuine than the capital's tourist-heavy flagship.

## The Verdict

India delivers the bigger sensory event — louder, brighter, more regionally varied, over faster, and genuinely disorienting in the best way if you land in a city whose specific Diwali traditions you didn't research beforehand. Austria delivers the longer, gentler immersion — six unhurried weeks of the same cosy ritual repeating itself across dozens of towns, rewarding patience and repeat visits rather than a single overwhelming night. If you want festival as spectacle, India wins outright. If you want festival as season, as an entire mood that colonises six weeks of your calendar, Austria's Glühwein circuit will happily, slowly, absorb you.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/india — paraphrased: a thread comparing regional Diwali traditions ran to hundreds of replies, with commenters from different states describing genuinely unfamiliar customs to each other, several noting they'd assumed their own region's version was simply "how Diwali works everywhere."*

> *Quora — paraphrased: a respondent explained that fireworks in their city typically run for several nights surrounding the main date, not just the night itself, and that newcomers should not expect a single, contained evening of noise.*

## Conclusion

India will teach you, fast, that a single festival name can hold a dozen different meanings depending purely on geography, and that the correct response to confusion is simply to ask and then join in. Austria will teach you that festivity doesn't need to be explosive to be meaningful — that six slow weeks of mulled wine in the cold, repeated market after market, can build an atmosphere no single spectacular night quite matches. Bring earplugs and an appetite for sweets to India. Bring a scarf and a tolerance for standing outdoors to Austria. Neither country is going to apologise for how seriously it takes its own calendar.
