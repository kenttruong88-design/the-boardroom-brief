# America Will Sell You Thirty Kinds of Peanut Butter and Call It Freedom

🇺🇸 United States 🇩🇪 Germany
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have pushed a trolley through a supermarket on four continents, and nowhere — nowhere — have I felt more surveilled by a piece of retail infrastructure than at a German Aldi checkout, where the cashier scans your items at a velocity that suggests she is being timed by a Stasi successor agency and will personally suffer if you fumble your bag-packing. Compare that to an American Safeway, where the checkout experience resembles group therapy: the cashier asks about your weekend, bags everything for you, and occasionally comments on your choice of ice cream like an old friend who happens to be wearing a name tag.

These two countries have built entire grocery-shopping civilisations on opposite philosophies — one treats the supermarket as a cathedral of choice, the other as a logistics exercise you'd better not slow down — and if you've relocated between them for work, you will spend your first month either overwhelmed or humiliated, sometimes both in the same aisle.

## Do's & Don'ts: United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a loyalty card app — everything is 40% off if the algorithm likes you | Expect anyone to bag your groceries quickly if you've got exact change ready |
| Accept the free sample lady's offerings graciously, even at 10am | Assume "organic" means anything coherent about the actual product |
| Budget actual time for the cereal aisle — it is a pilgrimage, not an errand | Try to buy wine in some states' regular grocery stores — it isn't happening |

## Do's & Don'ts: Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a one-euro coin for the trolley and your own bags, always | Dawdle at the register — bag-packing is a timed event you will lose |
| Save every bottle and can for Pfand return machines — it's real money | Expect Sunday opening hours; the entire retail sector observes a sabbath |
| Learn the difference between Aldi, Lidl, Edeka and Rewe fast | Linger near the till chatting to the cashier; you are holding up a queue of judgmental Germans |

## The United States: Choice as a Personality Trait

American supermarkets are not shops, they are statements of national character, and the character in question is "more, but make it confusing." A single Kroger or Safeway will stock thirty-odd varieties of peanut butter — crunchy, creamy, reduced-fat, no-stir, with honey, without honey, in a squeeze pouch for people who have given up on cutlery — and this is presented as liberty rather than what it actually is, which is a stress test. You will stand in the condiment aisle for longer than is dignified, paralysed by options nobody asked for, while a toddler two feet away screams about a specific brand of fruit snack with the conviction of a union organiser.

The size of the enterprise matters too. An American supermarket can be the size of a German village, with aisles devoted entirely to seasonal Halloween candy in August, a pharmacy counter, a Starbucks concession, and occasionally a small bank. You will push your trolley for what feels like geological time, and the muzak will follow you the entire way, a soft-rock hostage situation from which there is no escape until checkout.

Checkout itself, by contrast, is almost tender. Someone bags your shopping for you — a kindness so foreign to the German-conditioned expat that they will initially try to do it themselves and get mildly scolded for it. Payment is relaxed, chat is encouraged, and the whole affair has the unhurried texture of a country that has never once worried about holding up the person behind it. This is either deeply humane or a sign of civilisational decline depending on your mood and how long you've lived in Berlin.

And then there's the price tag theatre: sales, "buy one get one," coupons that require a smartphone app, a loyalty card, a second loyalty card for a store brand within the store — a baroque discount architecture that means the sticker price is essentially fictional, a starting bid in a negotiation nobody told you that you were having.

## Germany: Speed as a Moral Virtue

Walk into a German discount supermarket — Aldi, Lidl, Netto, Penny, the whole unglamorous pantheon — and you will be struck immediately by the absence of romance. There is no muzak, no sample table, no friendly banter, just stark fluorescent efficiency and a sense that joy has been actively engineered out of the architecture. The shelves rotate constantly: this week's socks-and-garden-hose aisle will next week hold ski gear or, inexplicably, a crate of bicycles, because German discounters have long since discovered that selling random non-food items at absurd margins subsidises the food you actually came for.

Then there's the checkout, the single most reliable way to identify a newly arrived expat in Germany: they are the person fumbling, bagless, while the cashier scans their shopping at a pace best described as "weaponised." German checkout staff are trained, implicitly or explicitly, to treat bagging slowness as a personal failing of the customer, and you will feel the hot stare of six Germans in the queue behind you as you try to simultaneously pay, find your Pfand receipt, and locate a bag you were supposed to have brought from home, because plastic bags here cost money and using one marks you as either a tourist or a disorganised disgrace.

The Pfand system itself deserves its own seminar: bottles and cans carry a deposit, returned via a machine that spits out a voucher, which Germans treat with the seriousness of a small side income rather than a mild inconvenience. And Sunday — Sunday is a void. Supermarkets close, by law, almost entirely, a nationwide commitment to rest that will catch out every newcomer at least once, usually on the one Sunday they genuinely needed milk.

## The Verdict

If you want to be coddled, confused, and mildly overcharged in a pleasant atmosphere, America wins hands down — it is retail as hospitality, choice as a love language, and nobody will judge you for standing still. If you want to get in, get out, and get on with your actual life, Germany is unbeatable, provided you've memorised the coin-for-trolley ritual and accepted that speed is now a core personal value you didn't choose but must perform daily. Neither system is wrong, exactly; they're just built for different kinds of person. America is built for someone who has never once felt rushed in their life. Germany is built for someone who finds the concept of a "browsing" supermarket trip faintly obscene. My own allegiance, after enough humiliating Aldi checkouts to last a lifetime, lies quietly with efficiency — but I will always miss the free samples.

## Conclusion

The supermarket, it turns out, is one of the purest distillations of national temperament you'll find anywhere, more honest than any corporate orientation deck. America wants you relaxed, distracted, and buying more than you need. Germany wants you efficient, silent, and gone within four minutes. Both systems work precisely as designed, and both will embarrass you at least once before you adapt — America by overwhelming you with peanut butter taxonomy, Germany by making you cry over a coin-operated trolley in front of strangers. Learn the rules of wherever you've landed, bring your own bag either way, and for heaven's sake, stop asking the Aldi cashier how her day is going. She does not have time for your warmth. She has a queue to clear.
