🇩🇪 Germany vs France 🇫🇷 — *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Berlin treats its parks like a shared backyard — walk on the grass, bring a disposable grill, drink a beer in public, nobody cares. Paris treats its parks like a museum with a lawn in it — look, admire, sit on the designated bench, and under no circumstances step on the "pelouse interdite." Both cities adore their green space. Only one of them will actually let you touch it.

I have grilled sausages on the grass in Tempelhofer Feld, a decommissioned airport turned public park, with absolutely nobody batting an eye. I have been politely but firmly whistled off a patch of grass in the Jardin du Luxembourg by a park attendant who seemed to genuinely enjoy the job. Only one of these parks wanted me there.

## The Do's & Don'ts

### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a portable grill to a park in summer — it's a beloved, near-universal weekend ritual | Assume nudity is shocking; many parks have designated FKK (free body culture) sunbathing areas |
| Walk, sit, picnic, or nap directly on the grass almost anywhere; it's genuinely encouraged | Leave litter behind — the freedom to use the grass comes with a strong social expectation to clean up |
| Visit a Schrebergarten (allotment garden) community if invited — it's a deep cultural institution | Expect every park to allow dogs off-leash; rules vary and are taken seriously when posted |
| Join the lakeside culture in summer — swimming in city lakes is a normal weekday activity | Play loud music without headphones near quiet zones; shared space still has limits |

### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use a chair or bench in a formal jardin — the seating is often intentionally portable and plentiful | Walk, sit, or picnic on grass marked "pelouse interdite"; it's enforced, sometimes by whistle |
| Visit on the specific days or hours when lawns are opened to the public, where that applies | Assume all French green space is this formal; local parcs and informal spaces are far more relaxed |
| Dress reasonably well even for a casual park visit — presentation still matters in public space | Expect grilling or alcohol to be casually tolerated in the grand formal jardins |
| Appreciate the horticultural design — French parks are landscaped as deliberate art, not just lawn | Be surprised by strict opening and closing times; most parks lock their gates at dusk |

## Germany: The Grass Is Genuinely Yours

German park culture runs on a simple, almost radical premise: the grass belongs to the public, and the public is trusted to use it responsibly. Berlin's Tempelhofer Feld — a former airport whose runways now host kite-flyers, cyclists, urban gardeners, and barbecuers by the thousand on a warm Saturday — might be the single best symbol of this philosophy anywhere in Europe. Disposable grills are sold at every corner Spätkauf through summer specifically for this purpose, and using one on open grass isn't just tolerated, it's practically a civic rite of passage.

This openness extends into genuinely surprising territory for newcomers: FKK, or Freikörperkultur (free body culture), has deep roots in German social history, and many city parks maintain clearly marked, casually accepted nude sunbathing areas, used without fuss by people of all ages. It says something about the underlying German attitude toward public green space — it's viewed less as a precious, fragile amenity to be protected from the public, and more as shared infrastructure the public is simply expected to use well and leave clean.

The Schrebergarten tradition adds another layer entirely: small, fiercely tended allotment gardens, often with tiny cabins, clustered in dedicated colonies across most German cities, inherited from a 19th-century movement to give urban workers access to green space and fresh produce. Getting invited into someone's Schrebergarten community — for coffee, cake, and a tour of their tomatoes — is a genuine honour and a window into a slower, deeply rooted German relationship with land that long predates the current wave of urban park enthusiasm.

## France: The Garden as Formal Art

French green space operates on an entirely different philosophy, one inherited directly from the grand formal garden tradition — think Le Nôtre's geometric, symmetrical designs at Versailles, scaled down and distributed throughout Paris as the jardins publics. These spaces are landscaped as deliberate works of art, with sightlines, gravel paths, and manicured lawns designed to be appreciated visually rather than trampled underfoot, and the famous "pelouse interdite" (lawn forbidden) signs aren't bureaucratic overreach so much as the logical conclusion of that design philosophy.

What softens this for newcomers, once you know to look, is the portable chair. The Jardin du Luxembourg and Jardin des Tuileries are famous for their loose, movable green metal chairs, scattered rather than bolted down, allowing visitors to drag a seat into whatever patch of sun or shade they like, facing whatever view they prefer — a small, charming act of personal freedom within an otherwise highly controlled formal space. It's a very French solution: maintain the formality of the garden itself, but let the individual compose their own little scene within it.

Beyond the grand formal jardins, French green space culture relaxes considerably. Smaller neighbourhood parcs, riverside quays along the Seine, and especially the increasingly popular informal picnic culture along the Canal Saint-Martin show a far more Berlin-adjacent looseness — blankets on grass, wine, baguettes, genuine lounging. The contrast matters: French formality isn't a national personality trait applied uniformly to every green space, it's specifically reserved for the grand historic gardens, where centuries of horticultural tradition still, quite literally, call the shots.

## The Verdict

Germany wins, unambiguously, on usability — green space here is built to be lived in, grilled on, napped on, and occasionally enjoyed without clothes, and the trust that implies is genuinely rare. France wins on sheer aesthetic ambition — nowhere else will a lunchtime walk double as a stroll through deliberate, centuries-old landscape artistry, chair-dragging included.

If you want your park to double as your back garden, Germany delivers, generously, grill and all. If you want your park to double as a living museum you're occasionally, grudgingly, allowed to sit near, France delivers that too — just read the signs before you unroll the picnic blanket, or a whistle will do the reading for you.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/expats — paraphrased: "I got genuinely whistled at in the Jardin du Luxembourg for sitting on grass that looked, to my untrained eye, completely ordinary. There was a sign. I hadn't seen the sign."*

> *Internations Berlin — paraphrased: a member described her first Tempelhofer Feld barbecue as "the most German thing I've ever experienced — total strangers sharing grill space and bratwurst opinions within ten minutes."*

> *expat.com Paris forum — paraphrased: a longtime resident recommended learning to spot "pelouse interdite" signs on sight, since ignorance is not treated as an excuse by the park staff, however charmingly they deliver the correction.*

## Conclusion

Both countries clearly adore their green spaces — they've simply built entirely opposite relationships with them. Germany trusts you with the grass and expects you to earn that trust by cleaning up after yourself. France protects the grass as art and offers you a chair instead, politely, formally, and with centuries of horticultural pride behind the gesture.

Learn which kind of park you're standing in before you sit down. One will hand you tongs for the grill. The other will hand you a whistle-based correction and a very pointed look.
