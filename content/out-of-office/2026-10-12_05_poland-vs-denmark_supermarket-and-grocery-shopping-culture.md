# One Country Judges You By Which Discount Chain You're Loyal To. The Other Just Wants To Know If You Haggled At The Market Today.

🇵🇱 Poland vs 🇩🇰 Denmark

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Grocery shopping is the most honest window into a culture you will find, precisely because nobody is performing for you. Nobody dresses up for the supermarket. Nobody rehearses their queue etiquette. What you see, at 6pm on a Tuesday among the root vegetables, is simply what a country actually is when it thinks no tourist is watching.

Poland and Denmark offer two wonderfully different answers to the question of how a nation feeds itself, and I have spent enough time in both — one haggling cheerfully over tomatoes, the other silently calculating a Rema 1000 receipt down to the øre — to report back with some authority.

## Do's & Don'ts: Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit a local targ (outdoor market) for produce; it's cheaper, fresher, and genuinely social | Assume supermarket prices and market prices for the same vegetables will be remotely similar |
| Bring your own bags; plastic bag charges are standard and expected | Expect small grocery shops to carry much beyond the basics; that's what the big chains are for |
| Learn a little Polish produce vocabulary; vendors appreciate the effort enormously | Be shy about a little friendly bargaining at a market stall, especially near closing time |
| Embrace Biedronka and Lidl as the genuine national default, not a budget compromise | Assume "discount" means low quality; both chains stock genuinely excellent Polish produce |

## Do's & Don'ts: Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pick your chain and stick with it; loyalty to Netto, Rema 1000, or Føtex says something about you | Mix up the weekly tilbudsavis (offer leaflet) planning with casual, unplanned shopping; locals plan |
| Bring your own bag and your coins for the trolley deposit; both are standard practice | Be surprised that shelf prices and checkout prices almost always match exactly, down to the øre |
| Consider a cross-border run to Germany or Sweden for bulk savings, like many Danes genuinely do | Assume an "international" grocery aisle will be cheap; imported goods carry a real premium |
| Respect the quiet, efficient, no-small-talk checkout rhythm; it is not rudeness, it's normal | Linger at the conveyor belt bagging slowly. Pack fast, move along, there is a system here |

## Poland: The Market Still Matters, Even With a Biedronka on Every Corner

Poland's grocery landscape runs on a kind of comfortable duality. On one side sit the ubiquitous discount chains — Biedronka and Lidl above all, with a store density that borders on comic, sometimes two branches within sight of each other — offering reliably good produce at prices that still make visiting Western Europeans do a small double-take at the receipt. On the other side sits the targ, the open-air market, which has not been rendered obsolete by supermarket convenience the way it has in many countries, and still functions as a genuine social institution rather than a tourist photo opportunity.

At the targ, prices are not fixed with the same rigidity as a supermarket shelf tag, and a little good-natured negotiation, particularly as a vendor nears closing time and wants to clear stock, is completely normal rather than awkward. Regulars develop relationships with specific stallholders over years; a favourite tomato seller is treated with something close to loyalty. This isn't nostalgia theatre kept alive for visitors — Polish shoppers of every age genuinely split their grocery routine between the discount chain for staples and the market for produce, meat, and anything they actually care about the quality of.

What surprises newcomers most is how little snobbery attaches to the discount end of this system. Biedronka is not Poland's "budget option" in the apologetic sense it might carry elsewhere; it is simply the default, used by every income bracket, stocked with genuinely good Polish dairy and produce. The targ isn't an upgrade from it. They're two different tools for two different jobs, and most households use both without a second thought.

## Denmark: Your Supermarket Loyalty Is a Personality Test

Denmark has turned grocery shopping into something closer to tribal identity. Which chain you default to — Netto, with its renovated wood-panelled stores and more than 500 locations; Rema 1000, Norwegian-owned and beloved by methodical planners who build their week around the tilbudsavis leaflet; Lidl, for the budget-conscious and the student population; Føtex, for shoppers happy to pay more for a warehouse-style range that includes clothing and household goods — functions as genuine shorthand for lifestyle and values, a fact The Local's Danish readers discuss with more seriousness than you'd expect from a conversation about tinned fish.

The practical culture underneath the chain loyalty is impressively consistent: shelf prices match checkout prices exactly, store layouts within a chain are often near-identical from branch to branch so regulars can shop on autopilot, and a genuinely significant portion of the population does at least some grocery shopping across the border in Germany or, in the east, via a short hop to Sweden, where a 2023 analysis estimated Danes spend several billion kroner annually chasing lower prices and a wider range.

The checkout experience itself rewards speed and punishes dawdling. Bagging is fast, impersonal, and efficient; the cashier is not there to chat, and loitering at the belt while you slowly repack your bags will earn you, at minimum, a pointed look from the next person in line. It isn't coldness. It's simply a system that assumes everyone values their time the same way the supermarket does: highly, and without sentiment.

Store opening hours reveal another layer of the contrast. Polish supermarkets, particularly the big discount chains, have historically kept generous hours, though recent Sunday trading restrictions have narrowed that somewhat and now require a bit of planning around which Sundays are "trading" Sundays. Danish retail hours are considerably more predictable but also considerably more restrictive by comparison to, say, a 24-hour culture — most shops close reasonably early on weekdays and keep shorter hours on Saturdays, with very little expectation that errands can be handled spontaneously after a long workday. Danes have simply built their shopping rhythm, and their weekly tilbudsavis planning habit, around this reality rather than fighting it. A newcomer used to late-night convenience shopping in one country or the other will need to recalibrate fast, because showing up at 7:30pm expecting a fully stocked, unhurried shopping trip is a mistake both countries will let you make exactly once before the lesson sticks.

## The Verdict

Poland keeps grocery shopping genuinely social, genuinely affordable, and split sensibly between two systems that have learned to coexist rather than compete. Denmark has turned the supermarket choice itself into an identity statement, wrapped in a famously efficient, famously low-chat checkout culture that rewards planning and punishes hesitation.

I will say this for Poland: haggling over tomatoes is more fun than calculating whether a cross-border Costco run in Malmö is worth the petrol. But I respect the Danish system's honesty about what it is — no pretense of charm, just extremely well-organised capitalism with a loyalty card.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/poland — a longtime market shopper's advice to newcomers: go to the targ right before closing on a Saturday if you want the best prices, but go early in the week if you want the best produce; timing your visit matters more than haggling skill.*

> *Discover Study Abroad — a newcomer's note on Danish grocery etiquette: pack your bags fast or feel the silent judgment of the entire queue behind you; nobody will say anything, but everybody will notice.*

> *International Staff, Aarhus University — practical guidance repeated across many new-arrival orientation notes: pick one supermarket chain near your flat and learn its layout quickly, because Danes treat "which store" as a real decision, not an afterthought.*

## Conclusion

Grocery shopping, properly observed, tells you whether a culture values negotiation or system, relationship or efficiency. Poland has kept both options alive and genuinely used, a discount chain for the mundane and a market stall for the things worth caring about. Denmark has streamlined the entire exercise into a quiet, exact, chain-loyal ritual that doubles, apparently, as a personality quiz. Both systems feed their populations extremely well. Only one of them will let you negotiate the price of a cucumber, and I know which one I'd rather do my shopping in on a Saturday morning.
