# Germany Queues in Straight Lines and Blows Its Nose at the Dinner Table. Greece Orders One Coffee and Occupies the Café Until Dusk

🇩🇪 Germany vs 🇬🇷 Greece

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Public behaviour is the part of a country no relocation pack mentions and every expat discovers within forty-eight hours. Germany runs on an unwritten rulebook so thick that the Germans themselves have stopped noticing it, until someone breaks it, at which point the entire street develops a pained expression. Greece runs on a different system entirely, one based on proximity, volume and the firm belief that a stranger is just a friend you have not shouted at yet.

Both countries think they are the sensible one. Both are partly right, which is the irritating thing about comparing them. If you are about to be posted to either, you are not choosing between rude and polite. You are choosing between two completely different definitions of consideration, and you will be judged by the one you did not grow up with.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Germany**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say what you mean the first time, because people take you at your word | Expect a host to offer the cake twice |
| Shake hands at first meetings and on birthdays | Assume a hug is the default greeting |
| Keep your voice down in restaurants | Narrate your bonus to the whole table |
| Respect a marked queue to the millimetre | Treat a rope as a suggestion |

**Greece**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Nurse one coffee for as long as you like | Feel pressured to reorder every forty minutes |
| Accept the kisses and the backslaps | Flinch at a stranger's compliment |
| Give strangers a sliver of space on the metro | Assume close means friendly |
| Keep your palm closed when you gesture | Hold up a splayed hand to mean five |

## Germany

The German public sphere has rules, and the rules are enforced by a kind of collective telepathy. The Deutschland.de piece on German manners, written by a Deutsche Welle journalist, captures the paradox neatly. Germans will queue in perfect formation when a rope tells them where the line is. Put them at a crowded bratwurst stand with no rope and the line dissolves into a scrum where whoever catches the server's eye wins. Order is a product of infrastructure, not virtue. Remove the barrier and you meet the same animal you find anywhere else.

Directness is the other pillar. Germans say what they think without the cushioning phrases English speakers rely on. The same article notes that a German host will not repeat an offer of cake. Say no once and you have said no for the afternoon, so if you were merely being polite you will go hungry and nobody will apologise. Handshakes are common at first meetings, birthdays and any moment of social uncertainty, which means the warm embrace you expected from your new colleagues will arrive as a firm grip and a nod.

Then there is the matter of bodies. An Expat.com thread about public nose-blowing is one of the funniest things I have read about German manners, because it is so earnest. An American member raised to blow her nose strictly in private was startled to find Germans of every background doing it openly, sometimes at the table. A Singapore-and-Germany veteran told her that success abroad means seeing differences without judging them, and added that nobody is ostracised for being ill. Another poster pointed out that loud, repetitive sniffling is far worse than one decisive blow, and a South African member listed what Germans find rude in visitors: loud talk in restaurants, boasting about money and dodging eye contact during a toast.

That last list is the real German rulebook. Volume is the offence, not bodily function. A restaurant in Munich can be perfectly full and perfectly quiet, because the unspoken agreement is that your dinner is not everyone's business. Reaching past a stranger for something on a supermarket shelf, the Deutschland.de author adds, bothers few Germans, which tells you where the line really sits: personal space is negotiable, personal noise is not.

## Greece

Greece will take your rulebook and hand it back annotated in marker pen. The Expat Arrivals essay on culture shock in Greece reads like a field diary from an anthropologist who has given up taking notes and joined in. Customers sit in cafés for three hours or more over a single coffee and nobody hints that the table is needed. Some older women push into taxis ahead of you, occasionally with an umbrella as a negotiating tool. Hugging, backslapping and kissing hello and goodbye are normal, teenage girls hold hands down the street, and old men outside the kafenio may stare at you with an intensity that the writer eventually decided was curiosity rather than menace.

The warmth comes with traditions that will baffle you. An older man once stopped his car to tell the writer, in Greek, that she was beautiful, and both parties ended up thanking each other. Some older women spit three times, "ftou, ftou, ftou", after a kindness to ward off the evil eye. Teenagers have asked strangers what religion they are. The writer says Greek has no real word for privacy, the nearest equivalent being the word for lonely, and that is the most useful sentence in the essay. Privacy here is not a right. It is a symptom.

And yet, plot twist, personal space exists. A Greece-based expat from Canada named Ludmila told a YouTube channel, in an interview picked up by the British press, that the lack of space people give each other surprised her, and that sitting too close to a stranger gets you labelled a weirdo. On the metro, she said, people will move to another carriage rather than sit near someone. This is the nuance that every cliché about Mediterranean physicality misses. Greeks touch their friends and keep a polite distance from strangers, which is the reverse of what newcomers expect.

The queue, finally, is a theory. Greece does queue, but it queues the way water finds a level: with enthusiasm, some improvisation and a general understanding that the person at the front will be accompanied by two friends and a cousin. Come equipped with patience and a sense of humour and you will be fine. Come equipped with a number ticket and you will be disappointed.

## The Verdict

Germany wins on predictability and Greece wins on humanity, and I resent that neither of them wins outright. If you want a day in which nothing surprises you, the Germans have built a nation for you. You will know when to queue, when to hush and how long the cake offer lasts. You will also eat many lunches in respectful silence.

Greece is more tiring and more fun. The rules are looser, the volume is higher and the hospitality arrives unrequested. You will be hugged by someone you have met twice, stared at by a man in a cafe, and fed by a woman who has decided you look thin. You will also never feel invisible, which in a posting is worth more than a rulebook.

My vote goes to Greece for everyday life and Germany for anything involving a deadline. If you must choose one for a family, pick the country whose eccentricity you can laugh at, because you will spend years laughing at it. Germany's eccentricities require a straight face. Greece's require only a coffee, and nobody will rush you.

## Conclusion

Germany expects you to behave like a guest in a very tidy house. Greece expects you to behave like a relative who has arrived without warning. Both are asking you to pay attention, which is the only universal rule of public life.

So pick your poison. If you crave order, take Germany and learn the handshake. If you crave company, take Greece and learn to take a compliment from a man in a stationary car.

Just do not hold up five fingers in either country. It is the one gesture on which Greece and I are in complete agreement.
