# Guyana Will Make You Wait In Line. Canada Will Make You Wait Online. Neither Will Apologise.

🇬🇾 Guyana · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

People love to tell you bureaucracy is a developing-world problem, usually while queuing for forty minutes at a Service Ontario kiosk to renew a health card that expired because the renewal letter went to an address they moved out of eleven months ago. I have banked in both Georgetown and Toronto, and I can report with total confidence that both systems are designed by people who have never personally had to use them, and that the only real difference is which century's paperwork technology they've settled on.

Guyana's banks run on patience, photocopies, and a faint but unmistakable suspicion that you are up to something. Canada's banks run on algorithms, call centres, and a faint but unmistakable suspicion that you are up to something. Pick your poison. I picked both, several years apart, and I am still composing the strongly worded letter.

## Do's & Don'ts: Guyana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring two forms of ID, a reference letter, and your birth certificate, even to deposit a cheque | Assume one visit will finish anything — budget for three |
| Befriend the branch manager by name; relationships move paperwork faster than policy does | Raise your voice at the teller — it adds a day, not urgency |
| Use a courier or agent for GRA (tax authority) matters if you can afford one | Expect email to be the preferred communication channel — bring a pen |
| Keep every stamped receipt forever, in a physical folder | Assume a stamp means the process is actually finished |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book your newcomer bank appointment online before you land — some banks let you start pre-arrival | Expect a Social Insurance Number same day; budget a week minimum |
| Get a Canadian cell number early — SMS verification gatekeeps nearly every financial app | Assume "newcomer package" fee waivers last beyond 12 months without reading the fine print |
| Keep your immigration documents PDF'd and ready — every institution wants a fresh copy | Call the help line expecting a human before the third menu layer |
| Build credit history deliberately with a secured card — nothing imports from your home country | Vent on hold — the hold music outlasts your patience, not the other way round |

## Guyana: Bureaucracy as Endurance Sport

Opening a bank account in Guyana is less a transaction than a character reference. You don't simply prove who you are — you prove you are known. A reference letter from an existing customer carries more weight than a payslip, which tells you everything about how trust actually moves through Guyanese institutions: personally, slowly, and via someone who already vouched for the last three people who asked.

The paperwork itself is gloriously analogue. Expect to produce your passport, a national ID or alien registration card, a utility bill that's never more than three months old, and — delightfully — sometimes a notarised letter explaining why you need the account at all, as though a bank account requires a justification beyond "I have money, you have a vault." Government offices operate on similar logic. The Guyana Revenue Authority, the Deeds Registry, and the various ministries each run their own internal weather system of queue lengths, lunch breaks, and inexplicable half-day closures that nobody posts anywhere except a handwritten sign taped to the door you're currently standing in front of.

What saves Guyana from total bureaucratic despair is the human override. Everything is, in the end, negotiable with the right person in the right mood on the right day. Guyanese friends taught me this is not corruption so much as relationship-based governance — the system assumes institutions are slow, so people build fast lanes out of goodwill instead. It is inefficient by design and somehow still faster than filing a complaint through the proper channel, which exists mostly as a formality nobody expects results from.

The oil boom has layered a strange second economy on top of all this — ExxonMobil subcontractors and Georgetown's new finance crowd move through a parallel system of expedited services and private bankers, while everyone else still waits in the same queue their grandparents waited in. It is bureaucracy with a velvet-roped VIP section bolted onto the side.

## Canada: Bureaucracy as Infinite Hold Music

Canada's trick is making you believe it's efficient because it's digital. It is not efficient. It is merely efficient at generating reference numbers. You will receive a reference number for your Social Insurance Number application, a reference number for your bank's newcomer programme enrolment, and a reference number for the complaint you file about how long it took to get the first two reference numbers.

The newcomer banking programmes — RBC, Scotiabank, and TD all run versions — are genuinely well-designed on paper: no credit history required, fee waivers, a dedicated advisor. In practice, "dedicated advisor" means a rotating cast of call-centre staff reading from the same script, none of whom can override a flag the system quietly placed on your file for reasons nobody will explain. CRA (tax) correspondence arrives exclusively through an online portal you must first prove your identity to access, which requires a document the portal itself was meant to issue. It is bureaucracy as a Möbius strip.

What Canada does brilliantly, by contrast with Guyana, is standardisation — every branch, regardless of province, follows the identical form, which means you can at least predict your own suffering. There's a strange comfort in a system that fails you the exact same way every time. And once you're in — once the SIN exists, the credit score has a pulse, the accounts are verified — Canada's infrastructure genuinely hums. Getting in is the entire ordeal; being in is close to seamless. Guyana is the reverse: getting in is a conversation, staying functional is the ordeal, forever.

## The Verdict

If I had to choose which bureaucracy to be trapped inside for a decade, I'd pick Canada's, reluctantly, because at least its dysfunction is consistent and eventually resolves into something that works. Guyana's system rewards charm and local knowledge in a way that's genuinely delightful if you have both, and genuinely brutal if you're a stranger with neither. Canada's system doesn't care if you're charming. It doesn't care about anything. That's almost restful.

But credit where it's due: Guyana's bureaucracy, for all its friction, still has a human being in it who can look at your situation and decide to help. Canada automated that human being out of the loop years ago and replaced them with a satisfaction survey you'll be asked to complete after the call that solved nothing.

## Conclusion

Here is the uncomfortable truth both countries would rather you not notice: bureaucracy isn't a measure of how developed a place is, it's a measure of what a government has decided to make you prove, and to whom. Guyana makes you prove you're known. Canada makes you prove you exist, repeatedly, to a system that keeps forgetting. Neither one is interested in your time. Pack patience, not expectations, and bring snacks — for the queue in Georgetown and the hold music in Toronto alike.
