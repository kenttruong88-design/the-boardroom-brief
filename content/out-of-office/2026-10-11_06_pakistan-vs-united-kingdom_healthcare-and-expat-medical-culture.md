# Pakistan Will Get You a Doctor This Afternoon; Britain Will Get You a GP Appointment, Eventually, After a Phone Queue That Builds Character

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Healthcare is the one topic where "efficient" and "free" refuse to shake hands in either country, just for different reasons. Pakistan's private medical system will see you same-day, often within the hour, for a price that would make an American weep with relief — but step outside the private system and the public hospitals are a different, far harder story entirely. Britain's NHS will see you eventually, for genuinely free, with a thoroughness and continuity of care that most of the world would kill for — but "eventually" has, in recent years, stretched from a mild inconvenience into a legitimate planning consideration.

I have had a chest X-ray in Lahore inside ninety minutes of walking into a private hospital, cash in hand, doctor already waiting. I have also waited seventeen days for a UK GP appointment for something that, by day four, I'd diagnosed myself via a worrying amount of internet searching. Both systems eventually got me seen. Only one of them let me set the schedule.

## Do's & Don'ts: Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use private hospitals (Shaukat Khanum, Aga Khan, Shifa) for anything time-sensitive — speed is their core product | Assume public hospital standards match private ones; the gap between the two systems is significant |
| Keep cash or a card ready — most private care is pay-as-you-go, not insurance-first like the UK | Skip travel/health insurance even short-term; private care quality comes with private care pricing |
| Ask locals for a trusted specialist recommendation — reputation travels fast and matters enormously | Expect appointment booking apps or online systems everywhere; many clinics still run on phone calls and walk-ins |

## Do's & Don'ts: United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a GP practice the moment you have an address — don't wait until you're actually sick | Expect a same-day GP appointment for a non-emergency; phone lines open at 8am and fill within minutes |
| Use NHS 111 (phone or online) for urgent-but-not-emergency guidance before heading to A&E | Go to A&E for routine issues; waits there are long precisely because it's reserved for genuine emergencies |
| Consider private healthcare insurance if speed matters to you — it exists, legally, alongside the NHS | Assume "free" means fast; the NHS trades speed for universal access, and the trade is real and ongoing |

## Pakistan: Fast, Private, and Entirely Dependent on What You Can Pay

Private healthcare in Pakistan's major cities operates on a logic that would astonish a Briton used to NHS timelines: you call, you're often seen the same day or next, imaging and lab results frequently come back within hours rather than weeks, and the entire system is oriented around patient convenience in a way that genuinely rivals private healthcare anywhere in the world. Institutions like Shaukat Khanum, Aga Khan University Hospital, and Shifa International have built reputations — deserved ones — for speed, modern equipment, and specialist access that doesn't require a referral chain six steps long.

The catch is the one everyone already suspects: this is a two-tier system, and the tier you experience depends entirely on what you can pay. Public hospitals serve the overwhelming majority of the population and face real, serious strain — overcrowding, resource shortages, and a quality gap from the private sector that is neither small nor a secret. For a well-resourced expat or returning diaspora member, Pakistan's healthcare can feel like a startling upgrade in speed and personal attention. For most actual residents, the experience is a different, far harder story that this comparison would be dishonest to paper over.

What genuinely impresses, even accounting for that gap, is the doctor-patient relationship itself: specialists here tend to spend real time with patients, explain things thoroughly, and build the kind of ongoing personal rapport that a fifteen-minute NHS GP slot structurally cannot accommodate. Word-of-mouth reputation does more work here than any ratings app — ask any Lahori which cardiologist to see and you'll get a confident, specific answer within seconds, backed by someone's actual aunt's actual recovery story.

## United Kingdom: Free, Thorough, and Increasingly a Test of Patience

The NHS remains, on paper and mostly in practice, one of the great achievements of twentieth-century public policy: comprehensive healthcare, free at the point of use, regardless of income, available to anyone who registers. For genuine emergencies, A&E departments triage ruthlessly but effectively, and for serious diagnoses, the continuity of care — a documented history, consistent follow-up, specialist referral networks — is genuinely excellent once you're inside the system.

Getting a routine, non-emergency GP appointment, though, has become a genuine rite of passage for every newcomer. The now-familiar ritual: call at exactly 8am, redial repeatedly as the line is engaged, finally get through to be told the day's appointments are already gone, try again tomorrow. Many practices have shifted to online or app-based booking, which helps, but demand still frequently outstrips same-week capacity in a way that pushes people toward either NHS 111's phone triage or, increasingly, private GP services that have quietly grown as a parallel option for anyone both insured and impatient.

What British healthcare does extraordinarily well, that Pakistan's private-pay model structurally cannot replicate at scale, is universality without a means test. Nobody in the UK avoids calling an ambulance because they're worried about the bill. Nobody delays a cancer screening because private specialists are the only fast option. That peace of mind is real, it's valuable, and it is, in its own quiet way, the actual point of the entire system — speed was never what the NHS was optimising for.

Insurance culture tells its own quiet story too. In Pakistan, private health insurance remains relatively uncommon outside employer-provided plans for white-collar workers, so most private care is paid directly, which keeps the system responsive to the person actually paying rather than a distant insurer. In Britain, private health insurance exists specifically as a bolt-on to the NHS rather than a replacement for it — nobody buys it instead of using the NHS, only alongside it, which says something quietly reassuring about how little even insured Brits actually distrust their public system, waits and all.

## The Verdict

If you can pay for it, Pakistan's private system will outrun the NHS on almost every measure of speed and personal attention, and it isn't close. If you can't pay for it — which describes the vast majority of people in any country — the NHS's universal, means-blind coverage is a genuinely different order of achievement that no pay-as-you-go system, however fast, actually replicates.

So: Pakistan wins on speed for the privileged. Britain wins on dignity for everyone. I know which one I'd rather need in an actual emergency, and it isn't the fast one.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations London — a recurring theme among newcomers: private GP membership, once dismissed as an unnecessary expense, is increasingly adopted purely to avoid the 8am phone-booking scramble, even by people who philosophically support the NHS model.*

> *r/AskUK — longtime residents frequently advise newcomers to register with a GP practice immediately upon moving, even before feeling unwell, because registration itself — not the appointment — is often the slower step.*

> *expat.com UK forum — a recurring observation from Pakistani and South Asian newcomers specifically: the biggest adjustment isn't NHS quality, which is often praised, but the unfamiliar experience of not being able to simply pay extra to be seen sooner.*

## Conclusion

Neither system is actually broken, despite what both sets of locals will tell you while queuing. Pakistan's private hospitals solved speed and personal attention by letting money do the work; the NHS solved universal access by refusing to let money do any work at all, and accepted the resulting wait as the cost. You will, inevitably, resent whichever system you're currently inside and romanticise whichever one you're not. That's not hypocrisy. That's just what it feels like to actually need a doctor in two different countries within the same decade.
