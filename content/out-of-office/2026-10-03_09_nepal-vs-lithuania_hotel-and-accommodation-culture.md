# One Country's Hotel Is a Wooden Lodge at 4,000 Metres With No Heating. The Other's Is a Sleek Serviced Apartment With Better WiFi Than Your Home Office.

🇳🇵 Nepal 🇱🇹 Lithuania
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nepal's accommodation culture, for anyone venturing beyond Kathmandu, is defined by the teahouse — simple, family-run wooden lodges strung along trekking routes through the Annapurna and Everest regions, offering a bed, a shared bathroom down a frozen hallway, and a dal bhat dinner cooked by the same family that's run the place for generations. Lithuania, by contrast, has quietly built one of the Baltic region's most sophisticated short-term and serviced-apartment markets, where Vilnius visitors increasingly skip traditional hotels entirely in favour of sleek, design-forward apartments with faster WiFi than most people's actual homes.

I have slept in a Himalayan teahouse at 4,000 metres wearing every layer I owned, including gloves, because the "heating" was a single yak-dung stove in the communal dining room that went cold the moment everyone went to bed. I have also checked into a Vilnius serviced apartment with underfloor heating, a espresso machine, and a smart lock I controlled from my phone. Both are, technically, "accommodation." Only one of them tested my will to live.

## Do's & Don'ts: Nepal 🇳🇵

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a proper sleeping bag rated well below freezing, even in "warmer" seasons — teahouse heating is minimal to nonexistent in bedrooms | Expect private bathrooms or hot showers as standard on popular trekking routes — shared, often cold-water facilities are the norm, hot water often costs extra |
| Order the dal bhat — it's usually the best-value, most reliably hearty meal on any teahouse menu, often with unlimited refills | Assume WiFi or phone signal will be reliable above a certain altitude — treat connectivity as a bonus, not a given, the higher you climb |
| Tip and thank the family running the teahouse genuinely — many of these lodges are multi-generational operations central to the local economy | Expect to book trekking-route teahouses online in advance like a hotel — most operate on a walk-in, first-come basis, especially off peak season |

## Do's & Don'ts: Lithuania 🇱🇹

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Consider a serviced apartment over a traditional hotel for stays beyond a few nights — Vilnius's market genuinely rivals Western European capitals on quality and price | Assume all short-term rentals are properly licensed — ask about registration status, as Lithuania has tightened short-term rental rules in recent years |
| Book Old Town Vilnius accommodation well ahead during summer and major festivals — the historic centre's inventory is genuinely limited | Expect round-the-clock reception or concierge service at most serviced apartments — many operate on a self-check-in, minimal-staff model |
| Use the apartment's kitchen — Lithuanian serviced rentals are generally well-equipped, and local market produce is excellent value | Overlook smaller regional cities like Kaunas or Klaipėda for accommodation value — Vilnius prices have risen faster than the rest of the country |

## Nepal: Hospitality Built on Family, Not Infrastructure

The teahouse system that supports Nepal's trekking tourism industry is one of the more remarkable grassroots hospitality networks anywhere in the world, built almost entirely by local families along routes like the Annapurna Circuit, Everest Base Camp trail, and Langtang Valley, with minimal external investment or corporate standardisation. A typical teahouse offers a simple private room — often just enough space for two narrow beds — a shared bathroom down the hall, and a communal dining room that functions as the genuine social and literal warmth centre of the building each evening, since bedroom heating is rare to nonexistent even at serious altitude.

What the system lacks in infrastructure, it compensates for enormously in warmth of a different kind. These are, overwhelmingly, family-run operations, often multi-generational, where the same household has hosted trekkers for decades, cooks the same reliable menu (dal bhat, the lentil-rice staple, remains the smart order everywhere), and treats guests with a hospitality that feels genuinely personal rather than transactional, a stark contrast to the corporate hotel experience most international travellers default to expecting. Pricing scales with altitude and remoteness — a room might cost next to nothing, with the real cost loaded into meals and the (often genuinely overpriced) WiFi or hot shower add-ons, since everything at altitude has to be carried in by porter or mule.

The physical experience, it must be said honestly, can be genuinely rough. Shared cold-water bathrooms, below-freezing bedroom temperatures at higher elevations, walls thin enough to hear every creak of your trekking neighbour turning over at 3am, and electricity that may run on limited solar power with charging fees per device — this is not luxury accommodation by any conventional metric, and travellers expecting hotel-grade comfort in exchange for hotel-grade effort will be genuinely miscalibrated. But the system works precisely because it's honest about what it is: basic shelter, real food, and genuine human connection, functioning reliably at altitudes and in terrain where almost nothing else could.

## Lithuania: Quietly Building a Baltic Hospitality Powerhouse

Lithuania's accommodation sector, Vilnius's in particular, has undergone a genuinely impressive transformation over the past decade, moving well beyond the generic post-Soviet hotel stock that once defined Baltic travel into a sophisticated, design-conscious serviced-apartment and boutique-hotel market that increasingly rivals, and in value terms often beats, comparable Western European capitals. A growing share of visitors, especially those staying more than a few nights, now default to serviced apartments over traditional hotels, drawn by genuine kitchens, more space, smart-home conveniences, and pricing that remains meaningfully below equivalent offerings in Berlin, Stockholm or Copenhagen.

Vilnius Old Town's UNESCO-listed historic core has become the epicentre of this shift, with converted historic buildings now housing stylish, minimalist apartments alongside the city's traditional hotel stock, creating genuine inventory competition that has pushed quality upward across the board. Self-check-in via smart lock or keypad has become increasingly standard, reflecting both a broader European trend toward minimal-staff hospitality models and a genuinely tech-forward Lithuanian consumer base comfortable managing their own stay without a front desk. The regulatory environment has tightened meaningfully in recent years, with Vilnius and other municipalities introducing registration and taxation requirements for short-term rentals, a response to the same Airbnb-driven housing pressure that's prompted similar crackdowns across much of Europe.

What's easy to miss, if you only visit Vilnius, is how much better the value gets once you venture to Kaunas, Klaipėda, or the Baltic coast resort towns, where accommodation prices haven't risen at anything like the capital's rate, offering genuinely comparable quality for a meaningfully lower cost. Lithuania's broader hospitality culture, across both budget and premium tiers, tends toward a quiet, efficient, low-friction competence — check-in works, the WiFi works, the heating works, and nobody particularly wants to make conversation about any of it, which is, depending on your mood, either refreshingly professional or faintly lacking in warmth compared to a Himalayan teahouse family that remembers your name by dinner.

## The Verdict

If you want accommodation that functions as advertised, with modern conveniences and minimal fuss, Lithuania wins comfortably and without much argument — it's simply a more developed, better-regulated hospitality market operating at a higher baseline of reliability. But if you want accommodation that teaches you something about genuine hospitality under genuinely difficult conditions, Nepal's teahouse system, cold showers and all, delivers an experience of human warmth that no smart lock or underfloor heating system has yet figured out how to replicate. Lithuania wins on comfort. Nepal wins on something harder to put a star rating on.

## Conclusion

Nepal's teahouses succeed by being honest about their limits and generous with everything they can actually offer — warmth of character substituting for warmth of temperature. Lithuania's serviced apartments succeed by quietly, competently matching Western European standards at a price that still feels like a genuine discovery. Neither system needs to apologise for what it is. Just don't book the wrong one expecting the other's version of comfort; the disappointment will be entirely yours to manage, at altitude, in the cold, alone.
