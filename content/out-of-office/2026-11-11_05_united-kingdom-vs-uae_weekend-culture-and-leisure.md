# Britain Invented the Sunday Roast. The UAE Moved Its Weekend So Brunch Could Survive

🇬🇧 United Kingdom vs 🇦🇪 UAE

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The British weekend is a quiet national tragedy performed in a garden centre. Saturday is for errands and football, Sunday for the roast, the newspapers and the creeping dread that Monday exists. It is cosy, rain-lashed and fundamentally unambitious, and I say that with love. The Emirati weekend, by comparison, is a place where a government changed the days of rest and a restaurant industry held its breath.

That is not hyperbole. Until 2022 the UAE worked a Sunday to Thursday week with a Friday-Saturday weekend, and the Friday brunch was an expat institution. When the country moved to a Monday-to-Friday week with a Saturday-Sunday weekend, as a news feature recorded in 2021, the great question of the age was: what happens to the brunch? The answer, it turns out, is that it adapts. Hospitality always does.

## United Kingdom

I will describe the British weekend from general knowledge, because none of my fetched pages covered it. Saturday morning is for sport and shopping, Saturday night is for the pub or a takeaway of regrettable ambition, and Sunday is a long, slow lunch followed by the discovery that you must iron a shirt. It is a weekend structured around modest pleasures: a walk with a dog, a visit to a relative, an afternoon doing nothing in a way that feels like virtue.

What defines it is predictability. Everyone knows the shape of it, and the shops close early on Sundays, which keeps the entire nation roughly in sync. The roast is not just a meal but a ritual, and the pub is the cathedral in which it is celebrated. You can criticise the climate, the traffic and the prices, but you cannot criticise the sense of collective agreement about what a Sunday is for.

There is also an entire civilisation of leisure beyond the pub: parks, markets, football grounds, village fetes, car-boot sales and the heritage railway enthusiast who appears at weekends with a flask and a sandwich. Britain is, at heart, a hobby nation. It takes its pleasures seriously and its enthusiasm quietly.

The sting for a returnee is how small it all feels after the spectacle of a Gulf city. Nothing is wrong with a pub roast, but it does not have a pool.

On the British side, one detail deserves more attention than it gets: the sheer reliability of the British weekend as a social institution. Nobody needs to organise a Sunday roast, because the pub, the menu and the newspaper all exist already. Compare that with the UAE, where the entire social calendar has to be actively constructed, booked and paid for, and where the arrival of a new weekend in 2022 forced restaurants to reinvent their week overnight. That is the quiet difference in leisure culture: Britain inherits its pleasures, while the Emirates design them. Both are pleasant, and only one comes with a deposit.

## UAE

A 2026 guide for British expats explains the structure: the working week moved from Sunday-Thursday to Monday-Friday in 2022, with the private sector working five days and the public sector finishing at lunchtime on Friday, giving a Saturday-Sunday weekend. The guide describes the Saturday afternoon all-day brunch as a pillar of expat social life. That is the sentence every Brit in Dubai quietly lives by.

The 2021 news feature written before the change took effect described the Friday afternoon brunch, nicknamed "frunch", as a staple for non-Muslim residents, and quoted restaurateurs weighing their options. Some predicted brunch would shift to Saturday or Sunday; McGettigan's, an Irish pub in Abu Dhabi and Dubai, kept Friday brunch with a later start and added a Saturday brunch and a Sunday roast. One Abu Dhabi owner expected Friday brunches to stay and grow as the opening of a two-and-a-half-day weekend. A travel guide of uncertain date insists residents attend many brunches, sometimes several on one Friday, and notes that over eighty percent of Dubai's population are expats.

The climate shapes the leisure. The guide records a midday outdoor work ban from 12:30 to 3:00pm between 15 June and 15 September, which tells you what the summer does to ambition. Weekends in July are therefore interior affairs: malls, restaurants, pools and cinemas. In winter the city moves outdoors with a speed that is almost suspicious.

The sting is cost and performance. The brunch industry rewards those who like their Saturday served on a platter and charged accordingly, and the expectation to be seen having a good time is stronger than in Britain. Leisure becomes an event to be booked, not a mood to be drifted into.

There is a family dimension that the brunch coverage rarely mentions. The public sector in the UAE finishes at lunchtime on Friday, which gives government workers and many school families a shorter day before a Saturday-Sunday weekend. For a British family used to a Friday evening that begins with commuter trains and ends in a takeaway, a half day on Friday can feel like a gift. The guide also records the summer ban on outdoor work between 12:30 and 3:00pm from mid-June to mid-September, a reminder that leisure here is shaped by heat as much as by timetables. Pools, malls and cinemas fill with families, and the outdoors waits for the evening.

## The Verdict

The UAE wins on options, Britain on atmosphere. If you want a weekend that is endlessly bookable, air-conditioned in summer and generous in winter, the Emirates deliver with a straight face. If you want a weekend that smells of woodsmoke, wet dog and gravy, Britain has no rival.

I will say this: the Emirati decision to move the weekend is the more impressive act of cultural engineering. It aligned the country with global markets, and the hospitality industry rearranged itself in a matter of months. Britain, by contrast, has argued about Sunday trading for decades and settled on a compromise nobody loves.

For a relocating executive, the question is whether you want to be entertained or restored. The UAE entertains. Britain restores, in its damp, apologetic way. I would choose the UAE for a three-year posting and Britain for the rest of my life, which tells you something about how humans actually work.

If you are choosing between postings on leisure alone, ask yourself what you do with a free Saturday. The Briton who loves a walk, a match and a pint will thrive in the UK and feel faintly overheated in the Gulf. The Briton who loves a pool, a long lunch and reliable sunshine will feel a little guilty, then enormously cheerful, in the UAE. Be honest about which one you are, because the weekend is where you will spend the other half of your life.

## Conclusion

A weekend is the purest expression of what a society thinks life is for. Britain thinks it is for rest, with gravy. The UAE thinks it is for experience, with a booking confirmation.

Take the roast and the rain if you want to feel at home. Take the brunch and the pool if you want to feel on holiday. Just check what day it is before you plan either.
