# One Country Turned Shopping Into a Negotiation. The Other Turned It Into Air Conditioning.

🇻🇳 Vietnam · 🇶🇦 Qatar
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Vietnam has never met a price tag it considered final. Every transaction is an opening bid, a small act of theatre performed daily by grandmothers who could out-negotiate a hostage crisis team, and if you pay the first number quoted, you have not just overpaid — you have failed a test you didn't know you were sitting. Qatar, meanwhile, has decided that the entire concept of "outdoor commerce" is a design flaw to be corrected with 45-degree heat and unlimited air conditioning, resulting in malls so vast they contain their own weather systems, gondola rides, and, in one memorable case, an actual indoor snow slope.

Both are shopping cultures. Neither would recognise the other as related to it in any way.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Haggle at markets like Ben Thanh or Dong Xuan — it's expected, and a fair price is usually 40-60% of the opening offer | Haggle at a fixed-price boutique or convenience store — this just confuses everyone and makes you look new |
| Smile and walk away if the price feels wrong — sellers will often call you back with a better one | Show visible excitement about an item before negotiating — the price will rise to match your face |
| Shop early morning at wet markets for the best produce and the least aggressive sales pitch | Assume every "silk" scarf or "leather" bag is what it claims to be — ask, or accept you're paying for a story |

### 🇶🇦 Qatar

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat malls as full social infrastructure — cinema, prayer rooms, ice rinks, and dinner all under one roof | Expect to haggle in malls — prices are fixed, and trying will just get you a polite, confused stare |
| Visit Souq Waqif for the one shopping experience that still runs on atmosphere over air conditioning | Wear anything overly revealing in mall common areas — modest dress is expected, not optional |
| Shop during Ramadan evenings — malls stay open late and come genuinely alive after iftar | Assume everything is luxury-priced — Souq Waqif and local souks have real bargains if you look past the postcard shops |

## Vietnam: Where Every Price Is a Starting Position

Shopping in Vietnam is not a transaction, it's a relationship, conducted at speed, usually in a language you don't fully speak, over the fate of a t-shirt. The market economy here — literal open-air markets, not the abstract kind economists argue about — still runs on a haggling culture so deeply embedded that fixed prices can feel almost rude, a refusal to engage.

Ben Thanh Market in Ho Chi Minh City and Dong Xuan in Hanoi are the tourist-facing versions, loud, chaotic, and priced accordingly for anyone who looks like they just landed. The real education happens in neighbourhood wet markets, where locals buy produce, meat, and household goods at prices that bear almost no resemblance to what a foreigner gets quoted for the same stall thirty seconds later. This isn't dishonesty, exactly. It's a pricing system with two tiers, and figuring out which tier you're in is a rite of passage every expat goes through, usually by overpaying spectacularly for a pineapple in week one.

The etiquette is specific and mostly unwritten. You never accept the first price. You counter low, not insultingly low, but low enough to signal you know the game. You're allowed to walk away — in fact, walking away is often the move that produces the real price, called out after you as you're three steps into leaving. What you don't do is haggle at a Circle K or a boutique with visible price tags; that's fixed retail, a different universe with different rules, and treating it like a wet market marks you as someone who hasn't been paying attention.

Beyond the markets, Vietnam's cities are increasingly stacked with modern malls — Vincom, Aeon — glossy, air-conditioned, fixed-price versions of Southeast Asian retail aimed squarely at the growing middle class. But the soul of Vietnamese shopping is still the haggle, the theatre, the small daily negotiation that turns even buying dinner into a performance with winners and losers.

## Qatar: Shopping as Climate Control With Extra Steps

Qatar's shopping culture exists because Qatar's climate makes the alternative unbearable for roughly eight months of the year. When the outdoor temperature regularly clears 40°C, "going to the mall" stops being a retail activity and becomes a survival strategy, which is how you end up with Villaggio Mall's indoor canal and gondola rides, or Doha Festival City's sheer, almost defiant scale — these aren't malls so much as climate-controlled small cities, complete with entertainment, prayer facilities, and enough food courts to avoid ever needing to leave.

The fixed-price culture here is total. Nobody haggles at a mall — the concept doesn't exist in that context, and attempting it produces confusion rather than a discount. What Qatar does have, uniquely, is Souq Waqif, a restored traditional market that manages to preserve some of the negotiation-and-atmosphere culture found elsewhere in the Gulf and South Asia, selling spices, textiles, falcons (genuinely, there's a falcon souq), and handicrafts in a setting that at least gestures at authenticity, even as it sits a ten-minute drive from a mall with an indoor ski slope's less ambitious Gulf cousin.

Dress codes matter more here than almost anywhere else on this list. Malls are conservative spaces — shoulders and knees covered is the unspoken standard, more strictly enforced during Ramadan, when the entire retail rhythm shifts anyway: shops close for iftar and then surge back to life late into the night, malls buzzing at 11pm the way they'd never dare during daylight fasting hours. Luxury retail is everywhere and priced to match a country with one of the highest per-capita incomes on earth, but Qatari shopping is fundamentally social — a place to be seen, to cool down, and to spend an entire evening without a single item purchased.

## The Verdict

Vietnam makes you work for every dong you spend and rewards you with a story. Qatar removes all friction, all weather, and all negotiation, and rewards you with air conditioning and an indoor gondola. If you enjoy the sport of it — the walk-away, the counter-offer, the small daily win of not getting fleeced — Vietnam is unbeatable and genuinely fun once you stop taking the haggling personally. If you'd rather your retail experience involve zero verbal combat and unlimited climate control, Qatar's malls will treat you like a VIP for the crime of simply showing up.

I'll take the haggle. A fixed price tells you nothing about the person selling it to you. A negotiation tells you everything.

## Conclusion

Vietnam's markets and Qatar's malls are both, in their way, honest reflections of the environments that produced them — one a fast-growing economy where negotiation is survival instinct and cultural inheritance both, the other a wealthy desert state that decided the best response to unlivable heat was to build indoor cities and stock them with everything money could want. Neither is wrong. But only one of them will teach you anything about the place you're in. Vietnam's haggle is a conversation. Qatar's mall is a very well-designed distraction from having one.
