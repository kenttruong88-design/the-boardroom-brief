# One Capital Is Still Fighting to Build Its First Real Park, the Other Legally Grants You the Entire Forest as a Backyard

🇸🇳 Senegal vs 🇫🇮 Finland
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Green space culture exposes a country's priorities more bluntly than almost any other urban metric. Dakar, a dense, fast-growing coastal capital, has genuinely limited formal park space relative to its population, and locals have adapted by turning beaches, roundabouts, and informal open lots into the green-space substitute the city planning hasn't fully caught up to providing. Finland, by contrast, has a legal right — jokamiehenoikeus, "everyman's right" — that grants every single person, resident or visitor, the right to walk, forage, and camp on almost any land in the country, private ownership be damned.

One country is playing catch-up on green infrastructure with real ingenuity. The other decided, centuries ago, that the forest simply belongs to everyone, and never saw a reason to revisit that decision.

## Do's & Don'ts: Senegal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Head to the Corniche or beaches for Dakar's real, functioning green-and-open-air social space | Expect Western-style municipal parks to be common or evenly distributed across the city |
| Visit Parc Forestier et Zoologique de Hann for a genuine, if under-resourced, green escape | Assume a lack of formal parks means a lack of outdoor culture — it's just relocated to the coast |
| Support and visit newer green-space initiatives as the city invests in expansion | Judge Dakar's density against sprawling capitals; land constraints here are genuinely severe |
| Go early morning or evening to the Corniche — it's when the real community activity happens | Expect quiet, empty green space; Dakar's outdoor areas are social, communal, and often lively |

## Do's & Don'ts: Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the basics of jokamiehenoikeus — you have real legal rights to roam almost anywhere | Assume "everyman's right" means you can do absolutely anything; some real limits still apply |
| Forage berries and mushrooms freely in forests — it's a genuine, widely practised national pastime | Camp or make fire too close to a private home without basic courtesy, even where legally permitted |
| Use national parks (Nuuksio, Koli) for accessible, well-maintained wilderness close to cities | Assume year-round access is identical; conditions and daylight shift dramatically by season |
| Respect nature reserves and specially protected zones — the right to roam has real exceptions | Litter or damage land you're using; the right comes with a genuinely serious cultural expectation of care |

## Senegal: A Capital Improvising Green Space Out of Necessity and Doing It With Real Style

Dakar's relationship with formal park infrastructure is honest about its own limitations in a way I find genuinely more useful than cities that pretend the problem doesn't exist. Agence Nationale de la Statistique et de la Démographie data reflects Dakar's extraordinary population density on the Cap-Vert peninsula, a geographically constrained coastal capital that's grown far faster than its formal green-space planning has kept pace with — a familiar story across rapidly urbanising West African capitals, but felt particularly acutely here given how little land the peninsula actually offers.

What's genuinely impressive is how thoroughly Dakar residents have compensated. The Corniche — the long coastal promenade wrapping much of the city's edge — functions as the de facto central park: joggers at dawn, families in the evening, informal football matches on any strip of sand wide enough to hold one, vendors, musicians, and a level of daily communal life that plenty of cities with abundant formal parkland never quite achieve in their actual designated green spaces. Parc Forestier et Zoologique de Hann offers a more traditional park experience, if a visibly under-resourced one, and it remains genuinely beloved by Dakarois despite, or perhaps partly because of, its rough edges.

The Ministère de l'Environnement et du Développement Durable has flagged green-space expansion as a real policy priority in recent urban planning cycles, acknowledging directly what any visitor notices within a day — that Dakar's formal park provision lags well behind comparable capitals, and that closing that gap matters for a city this dense and this fast-growing. Until that catch-up fully happens, though, what Dakar has built instead — a genuinely vibrant, coastal, improvised green-space culture centred on the Corniche and the beaches — deserves real credit on its own terms, not just as a stopgap.

## Finland: A Country That Simply Decided the Forest Belongs to Everyone, By Law

Finland's approach to green space starts from a premise so different from Senegal's that the two barely feel like variations on the same subject. Jokamiehenoikeus — "everyman's right" — is a genuinely old, deeply embedded legal principle granting every person, Finnish citizen or foreign visitor, the right to walk, ski, cycle, forage for berries and mushrooms, and even camp temporarily on most land in the country, regardless of who owns it, provided you don't cause damage or get too close to someone's home. The Finnish Ministry of the Environment treats this not as a quirky legal footnote but as a foundational cultural value, one that shapes how Finns relate to nature at a level most countries' park systems never approach.

The practical result is a green-space culture where the concept of a "park" almost undersells what's actually available. Metsähallitus, the state forest authority, manages a network of national parks — Nuuksio, easily reached from Helsinki, or Koli in the east — that are genuinely well-maintained and accessible, but they're really just the curated entry point to a much larger reality: something like three-quarters of the entire country is forest, and nearly all of it is, by law, yours to walk into. Foraging isn't a niche hobby here; it's a mainstream national pastime, with families making annual berry and mushroom trips as routine as anyone else's trip to a municipal park.

What Finns themselves stress, when explaining this to bewildered newcomers, is that the right comes bundled with a genuinely serious cultural expectation of care — you don't litter, you don't build fires carelessly, you respect the handful of real exceptions around nature reserves and private gardens. It's less a legal loophole and more a functioning social contract, one that's held for generations precisely because Finns collectively treat the privilege as something worth protecting rather than exploiting.

## The Verdict

Senegal is building green-space culture from genuine scarcity, and the Corniche is proof that scarcity, met with enough communal ingenuity, can still produce something vibrant and worth celebrating. Finland never had to improvise at all — it simply legislated the entire forest into being everyone's, and has spent generations quietly making sure that right gets honoured rather than abused.

Finland wins this one, and it's not really a fair fight. A legal right to roam an entire nation's forests and coastlines is a genuinely extraordinary policy that most countries wouldn't dream of granting even a fraction of. But I'll say this for Dakar's Corniche at golden hour, packed with joggers, footballers, and grilling fish smoke drifting off the sand: it has a pulse that Nuuksio's quiet, beautiful, near-empty trails don't even attempt to match, and some evenings, that pulse is worth more than acreage.

## Conclusion

Senegal and Finland have arrived at their green-space cultures from opposite directions — one improvising vibrant communal space against real land constraints, the other legislating an entire forested nation into shared ownership centuries ago and never looking back. Go to Dakar's Corniche for a green space with a genuine pulse. Go to a Finnish forest for a green space that's, quite literally, already yours. Just don't confuse the two experiences, and don't expect a Helsinki forest trail to offer you grilled fish at sunset — some things the Corniche will simply always do better.
