# Czechs Will Not Smile at You on Principle, and Uruguayans Will Hand You Their Mate Straw Without Asking If You Want It

🇨🇿 Czechia 🇺🇾 Uruguay
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Public behaviour is where a country's actual personality leaks out, unfiltered by tourist-board marketing, and few pairs illustrate the range better than Czechia and Uruguay. One is a nation that treats an unprompted smile from a stranger as either mental illness or an attempt at a scam. The other is a nation that will hand you a shared metal straw, sticking out of a communal gourd of bitter tea, within minutes of meeting you, and consider this completely normal social behaviour, not a hygiene incident.

I've lived the reserved version and the communal version, and both, I'll admit, took real adjustment. Prague taught me that silence isn't hostility, it's just efficiency. Montevideo taught me that sharing saliva via metal straw with a stranger is apparently how trust gets built here. I've made my peace with both. It took a while.

## Do's & Don'ts: Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep greetings brief and direct — "ahoj" or a nod suffices | Smile broadly at strangers on trams; you will get a confused stare |
| Give up your tram seat to elderly passengers without being asked | Make loud small talk in a quiet public space, especially on transit |
| Queue properly and expect others to do the same | Take Czech bluntness personally — it's efficiency, not rudeness |

## Do's & Don'ts: Uruguay

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the mate gourd when offered — refusing can read as cold | Say "thank you" after each sip of mate — it signals you're finished |
| Greet with a single cheek kiss, even with new acquaintances | Rush a greeting — a hurried hello reads as dismissive here |
| Bring your own thermos and yerba if invited somewhere long-term | Touch someone's mate gourd or thermos without an explicit offer |

## Czechia: Where Warmth Exists, But You Have to Earn It in Silence First

Czech public behaviour operates on a principle that took me embarrassingly long to understand: reserve is not coldness, it's a form of respect for other people's space and time. On the Prague tram, nobody makes eye contact, nobody strikes up conversation, and a stranger smiling at you unprompted is treated with the same suspicion an American might reserve for a stranger asking for their bank details. This isn't misanthropy. It's a deeply held cultural conviction that your face, your commute, and your personal business are yours, not a shared public performance.

What foreigners routinely misread as rudeness is, once you live here, revealed to be almost aggressive efficiency. Ask a Czech shopkeeper a direct question and you'll get a direct answer, with zero cushioning, zero "how are you today," and zero interest in performing friendliness it doesn't feel. I found this jarring for exactly three weeks and then found it an enormous relief — nobody's being fake at you, ever, which after years of forced customer-service warmth elsewhere feels like taking off a corset.

Where Czechs do show warmth is in structured, earned contexts — a shared beer at a pub (and Czechs take their beer with a seriousness that borders on religious observance), a proper introduction through a mutual friend, repeated encounters at the same corner shop until you're recognised. Public transport etiquette is taken unusually seriously for a Central European capital: seat-giving for the elderly and pregnant is close to a hard rule, not a suggestion, and violating it draws exactly the kind of pointed silent stare that communicates disapproval more effectively than shouting ever could. Once you understand that Czech reserve is a system, not a mood, Prague stops feeling closed off and starts feeling like the most honest city you've lived in.

## Uruguay: Where a Shared Metal Straw Is How You Say "You're One of Us Now"

Uruguayan public life runs on almost the exact opposite operating system, and the clearest proof of it is mate — the bitter, herbal tea sipped through a metal bombilla from a shared gourd, carried by an enormous percentage of the population at all times, in all settings, on the street, on the bus, at the beach, in meetings. Being offered a sip from someone's own mate, straw and all, within an hour of meeting them isn't an oversight in hygiene standards, it's one of the most sincere gestures of inclusion this culture has, and refusing it — however reasonable your germ concerns — can genuinely read as a small social rejection.

Greetings here are warm, physical, and immediate: a single cheek kiss is standard even for a first introduction, business or otherwise, and Uruguayans will maintain closer physical proximity in conversation than most Northern Europeans find comfortable. I spent my first month here instinctively stepping backward mid-conversation and watching people step forward to close the gap again, an unconscious social dance neither of us acknowledged but both kept performing.

What nobody explains is how much of Uruguayan public behaviour is built around unhurried, communal time — the rambla in Montevideo on any weekend afternoon is lined with people drinking mate in groups for hours, doing essentially nothing else, and this isn't laziness, it's a genuine cultural value placed on presence over productivity. Rushing a greeting, cutting a conversation short, or declining an invitation to "just sit a while" registers as far ruder here than it would in almost any other culture I've lived in. Uruguay's public warmth isn't loud or performative like some Latin American stereotypes suggest — it's steady, physical, and deeply unbothered by your schedule.

## The Verdict

Czechia wins if you want to be left entirely alone, respected precisely because nobody's pretending to like you before they actually do. Uruguay wins if you want to feel embraced by a culture within your first week, mate straw and all, regardless of whether you've earned it yet. I find myself missing Prague's honest silence on my loudest days and missing Montevideo's shared gourd on my most isolated ones. Neither is wrong. Both are just answering a different question about what public life is actually for.

## Conclusion

You can tell a great deal about a place by what it considers an acceptable amount of space between strangers, physical or otherwise. Czechia guards that space fiercely and rewards you with unvarnished honesty once you're inside it. Uruguay collapses that space almost immediately and asks you to trust the intimacy will hold, straw and all. Both demand you unlearn whatever your home culture taught you about polite distance. Do it quickly. The alternative is spending years looking like the confused foreigner who either smiles too much or stands too far away, and I've been that person in six different countries. It's not a good look.
