# Precision-Timed Subways vs. Trolleybuses That Run on Vibes and Diesel Backup

🇰🇷 South Korea · 🇲🇩 Moldova
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Seoul's subway system runs with an accuracy so relentless that locals set their watches, quite literally, by train arrival times, in a city where being four minutes late has social consequences. Chisinau, Moldova's capital, runs on a beloved, slightly chaotic network of Soviet-era trolleybuses supplemented by marshrutky (shared minibuses) that depart roughly when they're full rather than when a schedule says so. Both systems get millions of people where they need to go daily. Only one of them has ever made me feel like I was living inside a piece of German engineering.

## Do's & Don'ts

**South Korea**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a T-money card immediately — it works across subway, bus, taxi | Eat or drink anything with a strong smell on the subway |
| Stand to the side and let people exit before boarding | Talk loudly on the phone; quiet-car etiquette basically applies everywhere |
| Use subway transfer apps — the interchange stations are genuinely vast | Assume rush hour will be gentle; 8am Line 2 is not for the faint-hearted |

**Moldova**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry small cash — trolleybuses and marshrutky rarely take cards | Expect a fixed marshrutka departure time; "when it's full" is the schedule |
| Learn key route numbers rather than relying on printed timetables | Assume trolleybus wires never go down; backup diesel buses do exist for a reason |
| Ask locals for the current best route; informal knowledge beats apps here | Be surprised by crowding; marshrutky pack in more people than seems physically possible |

## South Korea: The Subway Is a National Point of Pride, and It Shows

Seoul's subway system is, without much competition, one of the most impressive pieces of urban infrastructure I've used anywhere on earth, and Koreans know it, and they are not shy about knowing it. Trains arrive with a punctuality that borders on the theatrical — digital displays count down arrival times to the second, and they're right, consistently, in a way that would make most European rail operators weep quietly into their delayed timetables.

The etiquette layered on top of this efficiency is its own elaborate system. Priority seating is genuinely respected, not just legally designated. Eating on the subway, especially anything pungent, draws real disapproval. Phone calls happen in hushed voices if at all, and the general expectation of quiet, orderly, self-contained commuting behaviour is enforced almost entirely through social pressure rather than any posted rule. Koreans navigate this dense social choreography while also managing genuinely brutal rush-hour crowding — Line 2 at 8:15am is not a gentle experience, whatever the countdown clock's precision might suggest.

The T-money card system deserves its own small tribute: one card, tapped consistently across subway, city bus, and most taxis, with fare calculation and transfer discounts handled so seamlessly that visitors from cities with less integrated systems tend to become mildly evangelical about it within a week. Transfer stations, particularly Seoul Station or Gangnam, function almost as underground cities in their own right — shopping arcades, restaurants, extensive signage in multiple languages, navigable once you've made the mistake of getting lost in one exactly once.

What's genuinely remarkable is how this efficiency hasn't curdled into coldness. Seoul's transit culture manages to be simultaneously highly regimented and, in its own understated way, considerate — strangers still offer seats to elderly passengers without being asked, station staff are unfailingly helpful to visibly lost foreigners, and the whole system somehow avoids the impersonal harshness that hyper-efficient infrastructure sometimes produces elsewhere.

## Moldova: The Marshrutka Runs on Faith and Fullness

Chisinau's public transport tells a completely different story — one shaped by Soviet-era infrastructure, subsequent underinvestment, and a resulting culture of informal adaptation that Moldovans navigate with a resigned, practiced competence. The trolleybus network, powered by overhead electric wires strung across the city since the Soviet period, remains the backbone of daily commuting, supplemented heavily by marshrutky — privately operated shared minibuses that fill a gap the formal system doesn't fully cover.

The marshrutka experience is genuinely something to prepare for. These minibuses depart, broadly, on a route rather than a precise schedule, and "full" is treated as a flexible, expandable concept — I have been on a fifteen-seat marshrutka carrying what I would conservatively estimate as twenty-two people, several of whom appeared entirely unbothered by their proximity to the driver's gear shift. Payment is cash, handed forward person to person to the driver with a kind of collective trust that would be unthinkable on a Seoul subway, and somehow, remarkably, it works.

What Chisinau's system lacks in punctuality it makes up for in resilience and local knowledge. Route numbers and informal timing patterns get passed along through lived experience far more reliably than any printed schedule or, frankly, transit app — asking a local which route currently runs best, and at what actual times, beats digital tools consistently. Trolleybus wire outages do happen, and when they do, diesel backup buses cover the affected routes with a shrug and minimal fanfare, treated as routine rather than crisis.

There's a real, if unglamorous, charm to the whole system, and Moldovans, particularly older residents, speak about their trolleybuses with a genuine, slightly wistful fondness that goes beyond simple functional acceptance. Modernization efforts have begun, gradually, with newer vehicles and route rationalization in recent years, but the fundamental character — improvisational, cash-based, deeply reliant on informal local knowledge — remains, for now, very much intact.

## The Verdict

South Korea wins, unambiguously, on engineering, punctuality, and sheer commuting comfort — Seoul's subway is genuinely one of the best urban transit systems on the planet, full stop. But Moldova's trolleybus-and-marshrutka network has a resilience and a human, improvisational texture that Seoul's polished efficiency has, perhaps necessarily, traded away. If I'm commuting to an actual job every day, I want Seoul's precision without hesitation. If I want a transit system that feels like it's still fundamentally run by and for the people using it, cash in hand, faith in the driver, I have a soft spot for Chisinau's beautiful chaos.

## Conclusion

Seoul's subway is what happens when a country decides infrastructure should reflect national pride down to the second. Chisinau's trolleybus network is what happens when a country keeps a Soviet-era system running through sheer collective improvisation and cash-in-hand trust. Both will get you where you're going. Only one will make you feel, mildly, judged for eating a sandwich on board. Bring a T-money card to Seoul. Bring small bills and low expectations about personal space to Chisinau.
