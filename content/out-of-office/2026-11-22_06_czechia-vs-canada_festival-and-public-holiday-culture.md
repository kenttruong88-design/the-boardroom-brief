# Czechs Eat the Christmas Carp and Call It Tradition; Canadians Eat Turkey Twice and Call It Normal

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every December, Czech households perform a small ritual that would get a person arrested in most countries: they buy a live fish, keep it in the bath, and then eat it on Christmas Eve with potato salad. Nobody is allowed to be squeamish. Presents are delivered by Ježíšek, a gender-neutral infant of undetermined duties, and everyone goes to bed full of fried fish and the quiet satisfaction of having kept a tradition alive.

Canada celebrates differently. It has a Thanksgiving in October, a Christmas in December with a turkey and a long drive, a Boxing Day devoted to sales, and a Canada Day in July at which an entire nation stands in a park and says sorry to the fireworks. The holidays are numerous, gentle and largely about food and family, and they come with a faint apology for being so pleasant. I have celebrated both, and I can tell you that the Czech version has more fish.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇨🇿 Czechia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Try the carp and the potato salad at Christmas | Announce your disgust in front of the host |
| Expect presents on the evening of 24 December | Wait for Christmas morning |
| Sample the cukroví, the Christmas cookies | Leave without trying at least three kinds |
| Treat Christmas Eve as the main event | Plan a Christmas Eve work call |

| 🇨🇦 Canada ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a dish or a bottle to a Thanksgiving dinner | Arrive empty-handed |
| Learn which holidays are federal and which are provincial | Assume every shop is closed or open |
| Join in on Canada Day with good humour | Treat it as an American holiday |
| Expect Boxing Day shopping crowds | Wander into a mall on 26 December for peace |

## 🇨🇿 Czechia

Czech Christmas is built around the evening of 24 December, and it is so specific that it is worth taking seriously. According to Expats.cz, carp, usually served with potato salad and bread, became a popular Christmas dish in the nineteenth century, thanks to the abundance of freshwater fish and the Advent fast at a time when religion played a stronger role. Ježíšek, or Baby Jesus, delivers the presents on Christmas Eve, and they are typically opened that evening. The meal ends with cukroví, a range of Christmas cookies that includes podvodnice, which rise in water rather than in an oven, as well as křehulky, perníčky and vosí hnízda.

The folk customs are even stranger and lovelier. Expats.cz lists legends that say if you fast all day on Christmas Eve you will see a golden pig in the evening, that walnut-shell boats float in a basin, and that a carp scale kept in your wallet brings financial luck. Cutting an apple should reveal a star, which means good health, or a cross, which does not. There are midnight and daytime masses in Prague, and the article notes that many people attend as a matter of custom rather than religion, which is a very Czech way to be devout.

Easter follows its own staged ritual. A 2026 Expats.cz piece, written in partnership with Tesco, which I should flag as partner content, describes Judas rolls and green foods on Maundy Thursday, sauerkraut soup on Good Friday, baking on Saturday and a meat-focused feast on Easter Sunday. Mazanec and the lamb-shaped beránek are staples, and egg decorating is a favourite. The calendar, in other words, is organised around food, and every course has a reason.

The foreign reaction is where it gets interesting. A 2021 poll of ExxonMobil staff at its Prague headquarters, drawn from more than eighty countries, found that 40 percent of respondents called carp disgusting and 60 percent disliked carp soup, while potato salad was universally loved. Huseyin Kaya, a staffer from Turkey, said the custom of killing the carp reminded him of the sheep sacrifice at Kurban bayram, and that his favourite Czech tradition is the Christmas markets. Satya Prakash Gupta, from India, found it odd that people buy live fish before Christmas Eve, but noted that carp is the same as the rohu he grew up eating and that he makes an Indian-style carp soup for the holiday. The sample size was not stated, so take it as a flavour, not a survey.

## 🇨🇦 Canada

Canada's holiday calendar is built on gentler foundations. Thanksgiving comes in October, a harvest feast of turkey, stuffing and pumpkin pie, held on a long weekend in which the leaves are an unreasonable shade of orange and everyone drives somewhere to see relatives. Christmas arrives with a tree, a family and an enormous bird, and Boxing Day follows with a sports fixture on the television and a determined assault on the shops. Canada Day, on 1 July, is the national party, with red and white face paint and a public display of national modesty.

What defines Canadian celebration is inclusivity. It is a country of immigrants, and its holidays absorb everyone's traditions without insisting on a hierarchy. You will find Diwali lights next to Christmas ones, Lunar New Year parades in the big cities and Eid dinners that are invited, not merely tolerated. Public holidays vary by province, and the rules about which shops open can confuse a newcomer, but the general mood is of a country that has decided to be pleasant on a schedule.

It is, frankly, a little bland. Where the Czech Christmas has a fish in the bath and a golden pig on the horizon, the Canadian version has a turkey and a vague sense of obligation. There are fewer rituals with a story attached, and few that make a stranger gasp. That is not a criticism. It is a design choice. Canadian holidays are meant to be easy to join, easy to share and difficult to get wrong, and they succeed at all three.

For a newcomer, the best Canadian advice is simple: accept every invitation. Thanksgiving dinners are famous for including the foreigner at the end of the table, and you will be asked to bring something, so bring something. You will be given more food than you can eat and more kindness than you expected. In a country where winter is long and the sky is grey, that is more than enough tradition.

## The Verdict

Czechia wins for drama, and Canada wins for hospitality. If you want your holidays to have texture, ritual and a story you can tell at parties, Czechia is the better country. The folk customs, the cookies, the staged Easter menu and the reverent absurdity of the carp make it a place where every date on the calendar means something. Canada, in comparison, feels like a polite nod to the idea of tradition.

But if you are an expat and you want to be welcomed rather than educated, Canada is easier. The holidays are open to everybody, nobody will judge you for skipping the fish, and the host will probably give you leftovers. My own choice would be the Czech Christmas, with a bottle of something strong and a plan to avoid the soup. Then, in October, I would fly to Canada and let someone feed me pie.

## Conclusion

Czechia will give you a fish, a golden pig and a cookie that rises in water. Canada will give you a turkey, a long weekend and an apology. Both countries want you at the table, and both will forgive you for pushing the carp around your plate. Say yes to the invitation, bring something sweet and do not, whatever you do, ask where the fish was last night.
