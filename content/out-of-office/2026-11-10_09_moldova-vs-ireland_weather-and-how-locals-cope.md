# Moldova Fights the Weather With Wine and Stubbornness. Ireland Gave Up and Bought a Better Coat

🇲🇩 Moldova vs 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Moldova has a climate that can be described as moderately continental, which is the meteorological way of saying it cannot make up its mind. Ireland has a climate that can be described as Irish, which is the meteorological way of saying it has made up its mind, and the answer is drizzle. If you are the sort of executive who judges a posting by its sunrise and its sunlight, these two countries will test you in opposite directions.

One has actual seasons: warm summers, a proper winter, a reason to own a hat for more than aesthetic purposes. The other has weather in the plural, all of it arriving in the same afternoon. This is a guide to which one will break you first. A note on sources, so you can calibrate your trust: the Moldovan material is decent and slightly contradictory, and the Irish material turned out to be one worried question about mould with no answers attached. I will tell you where I am guessing.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Moldova**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan for warm summers, with July highs around 28°C | Expect uniformly mild winters; temperatures can drop to about -5°C |
| Pack for snow, especially if you are heading to the north or east | Trust a single forecast for the whole country |
| Check the rainy months with a local before booking outdoor events | Assume the rain is spread the same way every year |
| Carry layers for the shoulder seasons | Be surprised by a heavy thunderstorm in summer |

**Ireland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a proper waterproof coat and stop apologising for it | Rely on an umbrella in the wind |
| Ask about damp and mould before you sign a lease, as the expat forum worry suggests | Assume a pretty cottage is a dry cottage |
| Treat a bright morning as a limited offer | Plan a whole day outdoors without a wet-weather option |
| Use a dehumidifier and ventilate often | Dry laundry indoors on a still, damp week without help |

## Moldova: Four Seasons, Delivered With Conviction

The two expat sources I could read agree on the broad outline and disagree on the detail. Expat Arrivals calls Moldova's climate moderately continental, with warm, long summers and snowy winters; it puts July as the hottest month with average highs around 28°C. Expat Exchange calls it temperate continental, with warm summers and mild winters, an average summer temperature of about 25°C, and winter drops to around -5°C. Snow is common in winter, it says, especially in the north and east. It is not a country that lets you forget what month it is.

The rainfall story is where the sources part company. Expat Arrivals says the country gets very little rain overall and long dry spells are common, with the heaviest falls from May to September and in October, including heavy thunderstorms. Expat Exchange says rainfall is fairly evenly distributed, with June and July the wettest months. Expat Arrivals also describes November to March as relatively mild with minimal snowfall, which sits awkwardly alongside its own phrase about snowy winters. Take that as a sign that the weather is variable enough to make fools of the guidebooks.

How do locals cope? I cannot cite a source for that, since neither page said, but the sensible reading is that a country with hot summers, cold winters and drier stretches builds its habits around seasonal rhythm: heavy cooking in winter, outdoor life in summer, garden produce preserved for the cold months. An unnamed expat quoted by Expat Exchange simply lays out the seasons in their own words, which is the right instinct. With Moldova, you do not adapt to the weather. You accept the calendar.

I want to dwell on the question of coping, because it is where national character shows. A Moldovan faced with a sharp winter responds with preparation: preserved food, warm rooms, a calendar that assumes the cold will come. An Irish person faced with a wet week responds with acceptance: a coat by the door, a kettle on, a shrug. Neither response is better. They are the two honest answers to a problem you cannot solve, which are to get ready or to get used to it. The expat usually tries a third, which is to complain, and it works for about a fortnight.

## Ireland: A Climate That Has Learned to Live With Itself

Ireland's climate is mild, maritime and wet. I would love to give you figures, but the page I fetched carried none; it was a single question from a poster who wanted to know whether a rainy climate means a lot of mould, whether drier areas exist for someone allergic to it, and whether there is a rural area with many expats and low air pollution. That is a perfectly reasonable thing to ask, and the fact that nobody answered it tells you something about how seriously the question was taken, or perhaps how tired the regulars were of it.

What I can say from general knowledge is that Ireland rarely gets extremes. Winters are mild rather than harsh, summers are rarely hot, and rain can arrive in any month. The cultural adaptation is more interesting than the meteorology. The Irish have long since stopped treating rain as an event. It is simply the medium in which life takes place, like air, or small talk. Plans are made with a built-in shrug, jackets are worn indoors, and the phrase "grand soft day" exists to describe a morning no sensible person would call grand.

For the expat, the real practical issue is the damp. A mould question on a forum is a sign of a real concern: humid air, older buildings, and heating bills that make you consider whether a jumper is a lifestyle choice. Ask about insulation, ventilation and heating before you commit to a property. Ireland's weather will not hurt you. It will quietly colonise your wardrobe.

If you are choosing a posting on climate alone, ask yourself how you deal with disappointment. Moldova offers heat that can be oppressive and cold that can be sharp, but the pattern is legible and you can plan around it. Ireland offers a gentler envelope and a more capricious interior, where a pleasant morning gives no guarantee about the afternoon. People who like to know where they stand will prefer the first. People who can shrug off a changing sky will thrive in the second. Neither type is wrong, but only one will own an umbrella.

## The Verdict

Moldova gives you drama; Ireland gives you consistency. If you prefer your weather to have a plot arc, Moldova is the better place: genuine seasons, hot summers, snow, a reason to feel the year turning. If you prefer your weather to be a gentle background hum, Ireland is gentler on the body and harder on the mood.

I would choose Moldova, because a country with a proper winter and a proper summer tells you where you are in the year. Ireland's greyness can flatten time. But I say this as someone who has survived worse than drizzle, and the rest of you may reasonably prefer a mild existence to a dramatic one. Whichever you choose, look at the sources I could not find and ask a local for the missing pieces. They will tell you for free, probably over a drink.

## Conclusion

Weather is the most democratic of cultural differences. You cannot argue with it, you cannot negotiate with it, and you cannot expense it. You can only dress for it and pretend you meant to.

So pack for Moldova as if the year were a performance, and pack for Ireland as if the performance were a rumour. In either country, find the local who owns the best coat and ask where they bought it. Then buy two.
