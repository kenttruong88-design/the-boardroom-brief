# Lahore Will Hand You A Gorgeous Park And Quietly Underfund It; Istanbul Will Hand You A Hillside And Let The City Design The Rest

🇵🇰 🇹🇷 *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Green space tells you everything about a government's priorities that it would rather you didn't notice. Pakistan builds genuinely beautiful parks — Lahore's in particular punch well above their weight — and then, per its own domestic press, routinely lets maintenance slide the moment the ribbon-cutting photographs are filed. Turkey, and Istanbul specifically, treats green space as something closer to civic theatre: parks woven into hillsides, waterfronts, and neighbourhoods with a confidence that assumes people will actually use them daily, because they do.

I have had a genuinely lovely picnic in a Lahore park with a fountain that hadn't worked in what was clearly several years. I have also sat in an Istanbul park on a Tuesday afternoon surrounded by at least four generations of the same extended family, all of whom seemed to consider this completely ordinary.

## Pakistan: Beautiful Bones, Inconsistent Upkeep

Pakistan's parks, where they're good, are genuinely very good. Lahore in particular has a horticultural legacy running back through Mughal garden traditions, and Graana's own survey of the city's best green spaces reads like a genuinely enviable list — Jilani Park's Mughal-era bones, Racecourse's sprawling lawns, Jallo's lake and forest combination on the city's edge. The problem, consistently flagged by Pakistan's own domestic press rather than foreign complaint, is maintenance consistency. The Daily Times has reported directly on the Parks and Horticulture Authority's efforts, and the subtext of that reporting is clear: good intentions, inconsistent execution, and a maintenance budget that visibly strains once the initial landscaping investment fades.

Islamabad's experience is more pointed still. ProPakistani's reporting on the capital's parks and playgrounds being "neglected" except in the run-up to a major diplomatic summit — when the Capital Development Authority suddenly found funding to spruce up public green space ahead of international visitors — captures something that locals already knew: green space investment here tracks political visibility, not year-round civic use, more closely than anyone in government would admit on record.

What survives all of this, remarkably, is the actual social function. Even an under-maintained park in Lahore or Karachi fills, reliably, in the early morning and evening cool, with cricket games, family picnics, and courting couples maintaining the kind of careful, chaperoned distance that's its own small cultural lesson. The infrastructure may be inconsistent; the appetite for using it never wavers.

Lovin Lahore's own roundup of the city's best parks is worth reading precisely for what it reveals between the lines: nearly every entry praises the landscaping and history while gently flagging a specific maintenance gap — an unlit path, a closed section, a water feature "currently undergoing repairs" that locals know, from experience, means indefinitely. It's a pattern distinctive enough that longtime residents have developed their own informal park-quality grapevine, swapping which green space is "good this month" the way other cities swap restaurant recommendations, because the answer genuinely changes with the seasons and the budget cycle.

## Turkey: Green Space As Daily Civic Infrastructure

Istanbul approaches green space from a fundamentally different starting assumption: that people will use it constantly, daily, for hours, and the design should accommodate that rather than merely look good in photographs. The Culture Trip's own roundup of the city's best parks reads less like a list of discrete destinations and more like a description of a city deliberately woven through with green threads — Gülhane's palace-adjacent gardens, Emirgan's Bosphorus-side tulip displays, Yıldız's forested hillside sprawl, each integrated into the urban fabric rather than isolated behind gates.

The genuinely distinctive cultural layer here is the çay garden — the tea garden — which blurs the line between "park" and "café" in a way that has no clean Western equivalent. Turkish green-space culture isn't primarily about jogging laps or structured recreation; it's about settling in, for hours, with tea, with family, with friends, in a way that academic planning literature on Taksim and Maçka parks has specifically studied as a distinct model of urban green-space "use intensity" worth its own research papers.

This daily-use intensity does come with real strain. Istanbul's explosive growth has created meaningful disparities between its well-resourced historic and waterfront districts and newer, denser peripheral neighbourhoods where green space hasn't kept pace with construction — a gap that planning researchers have flagged as an active policy concern, not a settled success story.

The academic literature on Taksim and Maçka specifically — published through Istanbul Technical University's own research channels — treats these two parks as a genuine case study in "evolution and use intensity," tracking how a single green space absorbs dramatically different functions across a single day: morning dog-walkers and commuters cutting through, midday office workers eating lunch on benches, evening tea-garden crowds settling in for hours, and late-night couples using the same paths for an entirely different pace of activity. It's treated, seriously, as urban infrastructure doing several distinct jobs simultaneously, which is a considerably more sophisticated framing than "a nice park" suggests.

## The Verdict

Turkey wins on consistency and daily civic function — Istanbul's parks are built to be lived in, for hours, by everyone, and mostly deliver on that promise regardless of political calendar. Pakistan wins on raw horticultural ambition and historical pedigree, when the funding actually shows up, which — per its own domestic press — is depressingly tied to whichever international summit is coming to town. If you want a park you can trust to be maintained every single day of the year, go to Istanbul. If you want a genuinely gorgeous Mughal-era garden that might, on any given visit, have a broken fountain, Lahore remains undefeated on charm, if not consistency.

The deeper comparison, though, is about who a park is actually designed to serve. Istanbul's çay-garden model assumes the park is where ordinary daily life happens — tea, gossip, courtship, childcare, all folded into the same green space, funded as though it matters every single week, not just during diplomatic season. Pakistan's parks, gorgeous as the good ones are, still carry a faint air of civic ornament — something built to be admired and photographed as much as genuinely, reliably lived in, which is precisely why the maintenance slips the moment nobody important is watching.

## The Parting Shot

Lahore will give you the more beautiful photograph; Istanbul will give you the more reliable Tuesday afternoon. Bring a picnic blanket to both, but only pack low expectations for the fountain in one of them. And if anyone tells you a park is "just a park," hand them a return ticket to either city and let them explain that sentence again once they've actually sat in one for an afternoon.
