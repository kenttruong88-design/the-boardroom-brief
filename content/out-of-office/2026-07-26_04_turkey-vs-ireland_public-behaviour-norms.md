# One Country Queues Like It's a Suggestion, the Other Apologises Its Way Through Everything

🇹🇷 Turkey vs 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Istanbul, a queue is a rough, collectively understood approximation rather than a binding contract — people merge, gesture, negotiate their way toward the front with a confidence that would read as outrageous elsewhere but is simply how things get done, loudly, physically, and without apparent hard feelings afterward. In Dublin, I once apologised to a lamppost I walked into, and the lamppost, had it been capable of speech, would almost certainly have apologised back.

Turkey's public behaviour runs warm, physical, and unapologetically direct — personal space contracts, voices rise, opinions get shared with strangers unprompted, and none of it is considered remotely rude. Ireland's runs on a dense, near-invisible code of politeness and indirectness so thorough that actual disagreement often gets buried under three layers of soft qualification before anyone says what they mean. Both systems work. Neither makes immediate sense to an outsider.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇹🇷 Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect closer physical proximity in conversation and queues | Take a firm hand on your shoulder to move past as rude |
| Accept tea when offered — refusing repeatedly causes real offence | Assume raised voices signal genuine anger — often they don't |
| Greet shopkeepers and elders with visible respect | Rush a greeting — it's treated as socially significant |
| Expect strangers to offer opinions on your business unprompted | Be surprised by direct questions about age, salary, marital status |

### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Queue properly and wait your turn without exception | Take "that's grand" at face value — it can mean anything |
| Soften disagreement with humour or indirect phrasing | Be too direct too fast — it can read as aggressive |
| Say sorry reflexively, even when it's not remotely your fault | Assume Irish friendliness on the street means deep intimacy |
| Match the self-deprecating tone in casual conversation | Take blunt honesty as an insult — rare, but not unheard of |

## Turkey: Warmth That Doesn't Wait to Be Invited

Turkish public behaviour operates on a fundamentally different assumption about strangers than most Western cultures — namely, that a stranger is simply a friend you haven't had tea with yet. Conversations start easily, opinions get offered freely and often loudly, personal questions about age, income, or marital status arrive within minutes of meeting someone, all without any of it being considered remotely invasive. It took me an embarrassingly long time to stop flinching at questions I'd been raised to consider deeply private.

Physical space contracts significantly too. Queues bend and merge in ways that initially read as chaos before revealing themselves as a genuinely functional, if informal, system everyone understands and nobody actually resents — a hand on your shoulder guiding you slightly aside isn't rudeness, it's simply how bodies move through shared space when the culture doesn't demand the same buffer zone Northern Europeans consider sacred.

Hospitality carries real, non-negotiable weight. Refusing offered tea, especially repeatedly, isn't a minor social misstep, it registers as a genuine, if usually forgiven, breach of the basic warmth the entire culture runs on. I learned to simply accept the tea, every time, regardless of whether I wanted it, because the offer itself was never really about the beverage. Volume, too, deceives newcomers — raised voices in an argument between shopkeepers or family members frequently signal engagement and passion rather than actual hostility, and the same conversation often ends in laughter and an embrace minutes later, leaving confused foreigners wondering what, if anything, had actually happened.

## Ireland: Politeness as a Load-Bearing Wall

Irish public behaviour runs on an almost architectural level of politeness — apologies distributed constantly and reflexively, even for things that are clearly, obviously, not your fault, queues respected with something close to reverence, and disagreement, when it exists, wrapped so thoroughly in humour and indirect phrasing that visitors sometimes miss it happened at all. "That's grand" can mean genuinely fine, mildly not fine, or catastrophically not fine, and working out which requires tone, context, and considerably more time in the country than most newcomers get before making an assumption.

Queueing here isn't merely practical, it's near-sacred. Cutting a line, even accidentally, draws a level of quiet, withering disapproval that somehow feels worse than open confrontation would — nobody says anything directly, but the silence communicates plenty. I discovered this personally, and the memory still stings faintly enough that I check twice before joining any line in Ireland to this day.

What's genuinely disarming, though, is how this politeness coexists with real warmth rather than replacing it. Strangers strike up conversation easily on buses, in shops, waiting for anything at all, and self-deprecating humour functions as the primary social lubricant, deployed constantly, expected in return. The trick, which took real time to absorb, is learning that Irish friendliness on the street doesn't necessarily signal deep intimacy, it's simply the ambient social temperature — pleasant, genuine, but distinct from the slower, harder-won closeness of actual friendship.

## The Verdict

Turkey's public behaviour is warm, loud, and physically close, built on the assumption that directness and hospitality matter more than personal space. Ireland's is warm too, but filtered through layers of politeness and indirection so dense that genuine meaning sometimes requires real decoding effort.

I found Turkish directness easier to navigate once I accepted it wasn't hostility, and Irish indirectness harder to fully crack even after months of trying. Suki's verdict: Turkey wins for anyone who wants clarity delivered loudly and immediately, but Ireland wins for anyone who enjoys a genuine, ongoing puzzle — because working out what "that's grand" actually means, every single time, never entirely stopped being fun.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Turkey — a newcomer described being asked their salary by a taxi driver within the first two minutes of the ride, and admitted "I've never answered a personal question that fast in my life."*

> *Reddit r/ireland — someone recounted apologising to a door they'd walked into, then apologising again to the person who witnessed it, calling it "a fully involuntary reflex at this point."*

> *Internations Istanbul — a longtime expat warned that refusing tea a third time "will genuinely worry your host," and advised just accepting it, cold, if necessary.*

## Conclusion

Turkey and Ireland have built entirely different public grammars around the same basic goal — making shared space bearable, even pleasant, for strangers forced to occupy it together. Turkey does it through warmth loud enough to fill a room. Ireland does it through politeness quiet enough to bury an actual complaint six layers deep. Move to Istanbul and accept the tea. Move to Dublin and learn to listen for what's underneath "that's grand." Neither country is being difficult. They're just being themselves, loudly or quietly, exactly as intended.
