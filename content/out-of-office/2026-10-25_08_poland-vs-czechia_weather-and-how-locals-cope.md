🇵🇱 **Poland** vs 🇨🇿 **Czechia**
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Poland and Czechia share a climate that is, charitably, hostile for roughly five months of the year — grey, damp, short on daylight, and committed to making you question every life choice that led you to Central Europe in November. What differs is the coping mechanism each culture has built around it. Poland treats winter as a moral endurance test, something you simply push through with vodka, hot tea, and a slightly performative toughness. Czechia treats it as an excuse for elaborate, unhurried comfort — pubs, saunas, and a national commitment to being cosy that borders on competitive.

Both approaches work. Neither will stop you from developing a complicated relationship with 3pm sunsets. I've spent winters in both, and I now own more candles than any adult should need to justify.

## Do's & Don'ts: Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in proper winter boots and a real coat early — the cold arrives suddenly and stays | Underestimate how dark it gets by 4pm in December; plan your day around the light |
| Take vitamin D supplements through winter — nearly everyone does, for good reason | Expect summer plans to wait; Poles book lake and seaside trips the moment it's warm enough |
| Embrace hot tea with raspberry syrup or honey as a genuine cold-weather ritual, not just a cliché | Assume indoor spaces will be as warm as you'd like — many run cooler than Western Europeans expect |
| Join in on a sauna or thermal bath trip if invited — it's a real winter social institution | Complain openly about the weather for too long; locals have heard it and have moved on |

## Do's & Don'ts: Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect pub culture to intensify in winter — it's where a huge amount of social life relocates | Expect the famous Czech love of sitting outside to pause for cold; heated terraces run all year |
| Try a proper Czech sauna or spa day — the ritual is taken seriously, not treated as a luxury indulgence | Rush the sauna etiquette — there's a proper rhythm of heat, cooling, and rest expected |
| Appreciate "babí léto" (Indian summer) as a genuinely cherished seasonal moment, not a throwaway term | Assume Czechs dislike winter; many genuinely love it, provided there's beer and a warm pub involved |
| Dress in proper layers — Czech winters swing between damp cold and sharp frost | Expect summer to last; Czechs know exactly how short the warm window is and plan accordingly |

## Poland: Endurance, Vitamin D, and a Strong Cup of Tea

Polish winters are long, grey, and genuinely tough on anyone whose body isn't used to five months of limited daylight. The cultural response leans toward quiet stoicism rather than elaborate comfort rituals — you dress properly, you take your vitamin D (a near-universal habit, openly discussed, because seasonal low mood is taken seriously as a biological reality rather than a personal weakness), and you get on with it. Complaining about the cold exists, but mostly as dry, shared humour rather than genuine distress broadcast to the room; there's a mild social expectation that you absorb the hardship with some dignity, the same unspoken toughness that shows up in plenty of other corners of Polish culture.

What compensates for the grim stretch is how seriously Poles treat the payoff: the moment real warmth arrives, usually May onward, social life explodes outward toward lakes, forests, and the Baltic coast with an intensity that reads, to newcomers, as almost frantic. Hot tea with raspberry syrup, honey, or a splash of something stronger is a genuine daily ritual rather than a tourist-brochure detail, and visiting a thermal spa or sauna with friends or family is a real winter social event, not an indulgence reserved for special occasions. The overall posture is: winter is something you survive with a specific, inherited set of small comforts, and summer is something you then spend as hard as possible, because everyone remembers exactly how long the grey stretch lasted.

Light therapy lamps have become a genuinely mainstream purchase in Polish households over the past decade, sold in ordinary electronics and pharmacy chains rather than treated as a niche wellness product, which says something about how openly seasonal low mood is acknowledged rather than quietly endured. Christmas markets and the run-up to the holidays also do real emotional work here, giving the darkest stretch of the year a specific, anticipated social calendar — mulled wine, outdoor stalls, lights strung through town squares — rather than leaving December to simply be endured as undifferentiated grey. The toughness newcomers notice isn't really stoicism for its own sake; it's stoicism with scheduled relief built in at fairly predictable intervals.

## Czechia: Make It Cosy, Then Make It Social

Czechia gets a similarly brutal, damp, grey winter, but the cultural response leans harder into comfort as an active project rather than quiet endurance. Pub culture, already central to Czech social life year-round, intensifies in winter into the primary venue for basically everything — catching up with friends, watching sport, simply getting out of a cold flat. The famous Czech habit of sitting outside with a beer doesn't stop for cold weather either; heated terraces keep that ritual running through the depths of winter, treated as close to non-negotiable.

Sauna and spa culture carries real weight here too, inherited partly from a broader Central European wellness tradition, and taken with specific etiquette — a proper rhythm of heat, cool-down, and rest that locals follow without needing instruction. Where Czechia diverges most from Poland is in attitude: there's less of the stoic-endurance framing and more of an actual fondness for the season, provided the right comforts are in place. "Babí léto," the Czech term for the brief, golden Indian-summer stretch in early autumn, is treated with real affection, a last pocket of warmth savoured specifically because everyone knows the grey is coming. The underlying message of Czech winter coping isn't "push through it" so much as "arrange your life so the cold is simply the backdrop to something cosy and social," a subtly different emotional stance even when the actual weather, and the actual survival tactics, look nearly identical from the outside.

Christmas markets function similarly here, though with a more unhurried, less performative energy than some neighbouring countries' versions — mulled wine and roasted chestnuts consumed slowly, over long conversations, rather than rushed through as a tourist checklist item. Czechs also lean heavily on a dense network of cottages and countryside retreats, with many urban families maintaining a weekend "chata" specifically for escaping into nature even during winter, treating a frozen countryside walk followed by a warm cottage fire as one of the actual highlights of the season rather than something to merely survive until spring.

## The Verdict

Poland copes with winter through grit, vitamin D, and a clear-eyed plan for summer's eventual arrival. Czechia copes through deliberate cosiness, pub culture, and sauna rituals that turn discomfort into a social occasion. Both nations are, frankly, equally miserable by February and equally unwilling to admit it out loud — the difference is just which comfort ritual they've built around that misery.

Given the choice, I'd take Czechia's approach: if I'm going to be cold and grey for five months, I'd rather be cold and grey in a heated pub terrace with a beer than cold and grey while being quietly, stoically tough about it. Comfort, chosen deliberately, beats endurance every single time.

## Conclusion

Neither Poland nor Czechia is pretending the winter is pleasant; they've just built completely different emotional infrastructure around the same five grim months. Poland's is about proving you can take it. Czechia's is about making sure, whatever happens outside, the inside stays warm, social, and faintly festive. Pack the vitamin D either way. You'll need it regardless of which coping ritual you adopt.
