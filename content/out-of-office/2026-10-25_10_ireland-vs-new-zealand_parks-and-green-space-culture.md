🇮🇪 **Ireland** vs 🇳🇿 **New Zealand**
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ireland and New Zealand both trade heavily on a green, pastoral self-image, and both genuinely deliver on it — just at completely different scales and with completely different levels of commitment. Ireland's green space is urban, manicured, and woven into city life: proper Victorian parks, walking routes, a strong culture of the local green as a social commons. New Zealand's green space is simply the entire country, with "going for a walk" meaning something closer to "driving forty minutes to a national park and climbing a mountain before lunch."

I've strolled through Dublin's Phoenix Park feeling very pleased with myself. I've then had a New Zealander describe their "little local walk" and realised it was a glacier.

## Do's & Don'ts: Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat city parks like Phoenix Park or St Stephen's Green as genuine social commons, not just scenery | Expect every green space to be wild or dramatic — much of Ireland's charm is in the tamed, manicured version |
| Join a local walking group if you want both exercise and an easy way to meet people | Assume good weather is guaranteed for any outdoor plan — always have a backup |
| Explore beyond Dublin — the real dramatic landscapes are coastal and rural, not urban | Expect vast, untouched wilderness minutes from a major city the way you might elsewhere |
| Appreciate the smaller scale — a good park walk here is a genuinely complete, satisfying outing | Compare every outdoor experience unfavourably to bigger, wilder countries — it misses the actual charm |

## Do's & Don'ts: New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in proper outdoor gear early — hiking and tramping are default weekend activities, not niche hobbies | Underestimate a "walk" a local casually suggests; ask about distance and terrain first |
| Learn basic bush safety and the Department of Conservation hut booking system if you want to tramp seriously | Head into more remote tracks without checking weather and conditions — it changes fast |
| Treat green space as a genuine lifestyle anchor — many locals plan their whole week around outdoor access | Assume city parks are the main green-space experience; the real culture is the national parks beyond |
| Respect Māori cultural and environmental significance tied to specific landscapes when visiting them | Treat sacred or culturally significant sites as purely recreational backdrops |

## Ireland: The Civilised, Social Park

Irish green space culture runs through genuinely excellent, well-used urban parks — Phoenix Park in Dublin, one of Europe's largest enclosed city parks, functions as a proper social commons: joggers, dog walkers, deer herds, Sunday families, all sharing the same manicured green without much friction. This is green space designed for regular, almost daily use rather than occasional adventure, and it shows in how embedded it is in ordinary routine — a lunchtime walk, a weekend stroll, a casual meetup, rather than a planned expedition requiring gear or forethought.

The scale is genuinely smaller than Ireland's reputation sometimes implies, and that's not a criticism so much as the actual design. The dramatic, wild landscapes Ireland is famous for — the west coast, the cliffs, the rural countryside — exist mostly outside the cities, requiring a proper trip rather than a stroll out the front door. What Ireland does exceptionally well instead is the tamed, social, deeply walkable urban green space: parks that function as genuine community infrastructure, places you run into neighbours, where dog owners form informal daily cliques, where a short walk counts as a complete, satisfying outing rather than a lesser substitute for something bigger. Newcomers who arrive expecting constant proximity to wilderness sometimes undersell this; the charm is really in how functional and social the green space is, not how dramatic it looks on a postcard.

Allotments and community gardens add a quieter, more participatory layer to this green-space culture, particularly in cities, where waiting lists for a small plot can run years long — a reflection of just how seriously ordinary people take having a patch of growing space, even a modest one, tucked into dense urban living. Coastal walks just outside the capital, like Howth or the cliff path at Bray, function as a kind of hybrid between the tame urban park and the wilder west-coast landscapes, giving city dwellers a middle option that doesn't require a full day trip to feel like a genuine escape.

## New Zealand: Green Space Is the Whole Country

New Zealand's relationship with green space operates on an entirely different scale, because there's functionally no separation between "the outdoors" and "normal weekend life." Tramping — the Kiwi term for serious hiking, often multi-day, with huts booked through the Department of Conservation — is treated as a mainstream, widely accessible pastime rather than a specialist hobby, and even casual weekend plans frequently involve what other cultures would classify as a genuine hike: real elevation, real distance, real gear. The sheer availability of dramatic, accessible wilderness, often within an hour or two of a major city, means the baseline expectation for "going outside" sits dramatically higher than almost anywhere else in the world.

This isn't purely recreational either — green and natural space in New Zealand carries real cultural and spiritual weight, particularly through Māori connections to specific landscapes, mountains, and waterways, treated with a seriousness that goes well beyond scenery. Newcomers are expected to understand, at least at a basic level, that some sites are not simply backdrops for a nice photo but carry genuine cultural significance that should be respected accordingly. On the practical side, the culture assumes a level of outdoor competence many newcomers underestimate: proper gear, weather awareness, and basic bush safety aren't optional extras, they're treated as basic adult literacy, the same way knowing how to drive might be elsewhere. The reward for meeting that bar, though, is access to some of the most dramatic, least crowded wilderness on the planet, treated not as a rare holiday indulgence but as a completely ordinary Saturday.

This culture starts remarkably young, too — school trips and outdoor education programmes routinely include genuine tramping, camping, and water-safety components as a standard part of the curriculum, not an optional extracurricular for the unusually sporty. By the time most New Zealanders reach adulthood, a baseline comfort with multi-day hikes, river crossings, and basic navigation has simply been absorbed as general life competence, the same way other cultures absorb swimming or cycling. Newcomers raising children here often remark that their kids adapt to this expectation far faster than they do themselves, treating a weekend tramp as entirely unremarkable within a single school term.

## The Verdict

Ireland offers green space as civilised, social, woven into daily routine — genuinely lovely, genuinely accessible, genuinely modest in scale. New Zealand offers green space as an entire lifestyle built around proximity to real wilderness, demanding more preparation and competence but delivering a scale of natural drama Ireland simply doesn't have on offer within city limits.

If I want a pleasant Tuesday walk, Ireland wins without a contest. If I want to feel genuinely small in front of something ancient and indifferent to me, New Zealand wins every time — just don't call it "a little walk" unless you mean it.

## Conclusion

Both countries have built their national identity partly around greenery, and both have genuinely earned the association — they've just scaled it to match what's actually available. Ireland made the park social and the scale human. New Zealand made the wilderness accessible and the scale humbling. Neither is the "real" green-space culture and the other a lesser version; they're just two completely different answers to the same marketing claim, and both happen to be true.
