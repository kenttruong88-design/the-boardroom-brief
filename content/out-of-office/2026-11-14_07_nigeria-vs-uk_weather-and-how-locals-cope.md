# Nigeria Has Two Seasons and One Is Wet. Britain Has One Season and It Is Grey

🇳🇬 Nigeria vs 🇬🇧 UK

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The British talk about the weather because it is the only subject on which the entire nation agrees. It is also the only subject on which an incoming Nigerian will have no preparation whatsoever. You can read the guides, you can buy the coat, and you will still stand at a bus stop in January wondering what you did to deserve this.

I am British, which means I am qualified to complain and not qualified to be proud. My country has a climate that is best described as a long, damp argument. Nigeria has heat, rain, and the harmattan, and it is honest about all three. The comparison is not kind to me, and I am going to make it anyway.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Nigeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan around the wet and dry seasons | Assume it is hot all year with no variation |
| Expect the harmattan to dry out your skin and throat | Forget the moisturiser in the dry season |
| Carry water and avoid the midday sun | Walk long distances at noon in the dry heat |
| Be ready for heavy downpours and flooding in the rainy months | Drive through standing water |

**UK**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a proper winter coat before you arrive | Land in December in a light jacket |
| Dress in layers you can remove indoors | Trust one thick garment to do everything |
| Learn to love a hot drink and a radiator | Fight the darkness at four in the afternoon |
| Carry a small umbrella year round | Believe the morning forecast |

## Nigeria

Nigeria's weather is dramatic, loud and reliable. There is a wet season and a dry season, with the northern dry months brought by the harmattan, the dust-laden wind off the Sahara that turns the air dry, hazy and cool in the mornings. The weather is felt, not just observed. People plan their days around heat, their wardrobes around humidity, and their roads around the rains.

The key point for this comparison is what Nigerians do not experience: cold that you cannot escape. Nigerian coping strategies are designed for heat. You find shade, you drink water, you open windows, you run the fan. Clothing is light, houses are built to breathe, and nobody owns a coat that would survive a British January.

That absence is what makes the first winter such a shock. A writer using the pseudonym Sugar Pendragon, who left Nigeria with her mother and three siblings in 2008 and arrived in London with fourteen suitcases, described it vividly. They landed during the harmattan season at home, which is cool by Nigerian standards, and thought they were prepared. They were not. She wrote: "I found the winter jackets very uncomfortable."

I love that detail, because it is so human. The family did what anyone would do. They took off their jackets too early, wore trainers instead of boots, and paid the price in chilblains after warming numb feet on hot water bottles and radiators. Her parents even bought a Christmas tree because the expected snow did not come. Each decision was reasonable, and each was wrong.

What Nigeria gives you, and what Britain takes away, is daylight and sun. Even in the wet season, the Nigerian day is bright and long. The tropical light is a quiet constant, and you notice its absence in London the way you notice a missing tooth.

## UK

Britain's winter is not the coldest in the world. It is the most insidious. The cold is damp, the sky is low, and the daylight is so short that you commute in the dark in both directions. You do not notice the climate so much as you absorb it, slowly, like a bruise.

Kenneth, a 37-year-old interviewed by Zikoko, moved from Nigeria to Aberdeen in 2020 for a master's degree. His account is not a comic essay, it is a warning. He told the interviewer: "I came in during the winter, and I definitely wasn't dressed for it. It was freezing." He was stuck outside his accommodation for an hour or two on arrival. A few weeks in, he was hospitalised for four days after a cold-related reaction, taken by ambulance from his GP practice to a bigger hospital.

He also learned a lesson about British institutions. He had to get to the GP for half past six, but the practice did not open until seven. "They really respect their opening and closing times here," he said. And he reflected: "I would later learn how to live with the weather here the hard way." That sentence is the one I would pin to the arrivals hall at every airport.

What do locals do about it? They cope the way the British cope with everything: by complaining, layering, drinking tea and carrying on. Houses are heated, with varying degrees of efficiency. Offices are warm. Pubs have fires. There is a whole culture of coping that you can join if you choose, and it is communal in a surprisingly warm way. The weather is the great leveller, and the shared grumble is a form of friendship.

Kenneth also said that Aberdeen has the largest Nigerian population in Scotland, and that his social life was "just work, church, and the occasional friend coming over." Community, in other words, is the real heating system. The weather keeps you indoors, and the people you find there are what make the indoors bearable.

There is one more cultural difference that the weather exposes. Nigerians, in my experience, treat the cold as a problem to be solved together, with advice from cousins, borrowed coats and detailed instructions about thermal vests. The British treat it as a private misfortune to be endured with a stiff upper lip. A newcomer will find the second approach lonelier than the first, which is why the community Kenneth described, work, church and the occasional friend coming over, is not a luxury but a survival tool. If you take only one lesson from the first winter, let it be this: ask for help before you need it, because nobody in Britain will offer it until you do.

## The Verdict

Nigeria wins the weather, and Britain wins the preparation. I cannot, in good conscience, tell anyone that British weather is better. It is not. It is grey, wet, cold and short on light for half the year. If your only criterion is climate, take Nigeria and stop reading.

But the British have built a society around coping with it. The heating, the coats, the opening hours, the fixed routines and the endless tea all exist because the weather demands them. You will get more help dealing with a cold snap in Britain than with a flood in Lagos, because the former is expected and the latter is, in many places, still an emergency.

My practical ruling is simple. Move to the UK in summer, not winter. Give yourself time to buy the right clothes. And find your people early, because the cold is much easier with company.

## Conclusion

Weather is the first thing a new country does to you, and it is rarely gentle. Britain's version is slow and grey, and it has sent more than one Nigerian arrival to a hospital bed before they had learned the bus routes.

Do not be proud about it. Buy the coat, ask for help, and do not take off the jacket too early. The sun will return in a few months. By then you will have learned to complain about the weather like a native, which is the surest sign that you have arrived.
