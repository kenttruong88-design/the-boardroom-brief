# One Country Makes You Wait for a Card, the Other Makes You Wait for a Number

🇰🇼 Kuwait vs 🇵🇱 Poland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Bureaucracy abroad has a particular flavour of misery, and both Kuwait and Poland have perfected their own distinct version of it. Kuwait ties absolutely everything, banking included, to your Civil ID, and until that single card exists, you functionally do not exist either, no matter how much money is sitting in your name back home. Poland ties everything to a PESEL number, a magical eleven digits that unlocks banking, healthcare, and basic adult life, and getting one can turn a simple errand into a week-long pilgrimage between offices that all insist the other office handles it.

I've sat in a Kuwaiti bank branch being told, politely, that nothing could happen until my Civil ID was processed, and I've sat in a Polish urząd being sent to a different building for a stamp that the first building was fully capable of producing itself. Both moments taught me the same lesson: bring snacks, bring patience, and never, ever assume today is the day it gets resolved.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇰🇼 Kuwait

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your Civil ID application moving the moment you land; everything else waits on it | Expect to open a bank account, sign a lease, or get a SIM card without it |
| Use your employer's HR or sponsor's support; they've navigated this process many times before | Assume online banking setup will be quick; in-branch visits are still often required for foreigners |
| Keep physical copies of every document; digital versions are rarely accepted as sufficient alone | Lose your temporary Civil ID receipt; you'll need it for nearly everything in the interim |

### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for your PESEL number as early as possible; almost nothing works administratively without it | Expect one office to handle multiple related processes; be ready to be redirected, often |
| Bring more documents than you think you'll need, and copies of all of them | Assume English will be widely spoken at government offices outside major international companies |
| Book appointments online in advance where possible; walk-ins can mean hours of waiting | Leave banking setup until you urgently need it; account opening can take longer than expected |

## Kuwait: Nothing Exists Until the Civil ID Does

Kuwait's entire administrative and financial system is built around a single document: the Civil ID. Until it's issued, an enormous list of basic adult activities simply isn't available to you, opening a bank account chief among them. Most banks will not process a full account for a foreign resident without a valid Civil ID in hand, which means new arrivals often spend their first several weeks in a strange financial limbo, unable to receive a proper salary payment, sign a lease independently, or get a local SIM card, all while the Civil ID application slowly works its way through processing.

The process itself, once underway, is reasonably systematic — medical testing, biometric registration, employer or sponsor verification — but it takes time, commonly several weeks, and there's genuinely no way to expedite it through sheer persistence or charm. Employers who regularly sponsor foreign workers tend to have this down to a science, guiding new hires through each step with practiced efficiency, and leaning on that institutional experience is far more effective than trying to navigate it solo. Independent newcomers without that support system describe it as one of the more disorienting parts of settling in, a genuine sense of being administratively invisible until a plastic card says otherwise.

Once the Civil ID exists, though, the system moves with surprising smoothness. Banking, in particular, becomes straightforward and even quite service-oriented, with major banks offering solid English-language support and reasonably modern digital banking once the account is actually open. The frustration is entirely front-loaded. Kuwait doesn't make ongoing bureaucracy painful, it makes the very beginning painful, concentrating weeks of friction into a single gatekeeping document, and then, having extracted that patience from you, largely leaves you alone.

## Poland: The PESEL Number Is the Password to Adulthood

Poland runs a strikingly similar system built around a different piece of magic: the PESEL number, an eleven-digit national identification number that underpins banking, healthcare registration, tax matters, and a long list of daily administrative tasks. Getting one as a foreigner requires registering your residence at a local urząd, and this is where things start to genuinely test people, appointment systems that book out weeks in advance, offices that redirect you to other offices for document types they could, in theory, process themselves, and a document checklist that seems to grow slightly every time you return with what you were told last time was everything.

Banking specifically sits in an odd position — some banks will open a basic account for foreigners without a PESEL, but the good rates, full services, and long-term products generally require one, meaning most people end up making the PESEL a priority far earlier than they expected to. The process is not, on its own merits, unreasonable, Poland's bureaucracy is thorough rather than deliberately obstructive, but the sheer number of separate offices involved, combined with limited English support outside major cities, turns a fundamentally simple registration into a genuine time investment, often stretched across multiple visits and multiple buildings.

What softens the experience considerably is that Poland's system, once understood, is genuinely consistent and rule-based rather than arbitrary. Online resources have improved substantially, and larger cities like Warsaw and Kraków have streamlined much of the appointment process digitally, cutting down the walk-in chaos that older expat forum threads describe with real bitterness. Newcomers who research the specific document list for their exact situation in advance, rather than showing up with a generic checklist, tend to get through it in a single extended visit rather than the multi-week saga others report. Preparation, more than patience, is the actual currency here.

## The Verdict

Kuwait front-loads its bureaucratic pain into a single, unavoidable gatekeeping document and then largely gets out of your way. Poland spreads its pain across multiple offices and appointments, rewarding thorough preparation over blind patience. If you want one big hurdle followed by smooth sailing, Kuwait's system, frustrating as the wait is, ultimately delivers that. If you'd rather have more control over the outcome through careful research and preparation, Poland's system, tedious as it is, actually rewards doing your homework. Neither country is going to let you skip the line. Both will, eventually, let you in.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations Kuwait City — paraphrased: An expat described being unable to receive their own salary for nearly six weeks because payroll required a Civil ID number that hadn't been issued yet, and said no one had warned them to expect the delay.*

> *Reddit r/Poland — paraphrased: A commenter said they were sent between three different offices over two weeks trying to register their address, each one insisting the correct office was actually one of the other two.*

> *expat.com Warsaw — paraphrased: A newcomer said booking their urząd appointment online a month in advance, rather than attempting a walk-in, was the single most useful piece of advice they received before moving.*

## Conclusion

Kuwait and Poland have both built entire adult existences around one small piece of paper or plastic, and both will make you wait for it with total indifference to your plans. The trick in either country is the same: start the process the day you arrive, not the day you actually need it, because by the time you need it, you're already weeks behind. Bureaucracy doesn't care about your urgency. It only cares about your paperwork, and whether you brought the right copy of it the first time.
