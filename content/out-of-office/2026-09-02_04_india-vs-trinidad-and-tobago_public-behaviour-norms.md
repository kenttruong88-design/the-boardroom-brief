# One Country Never Queues, the Other Never Stops Talking While Not Queuing

🇮🇳 India vs 🇹🇹 Trinidad and Tobago — public behaviour norms

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

India doesn't queue so much as coalesce — a slow-motion scrum where personal space is treated as an eccentric foreign import nobody's quite sure how to use. Trinidad and Tobago doesn't queue either, but for entirely different reasons: everyone's too busy liming, the noble Caribbean art of hanging around with no particular purpose, to notice there was ever meant to be a line. Put an Englishman in either country and watch him quietly implode.

I've stood in enough queues-that-weren't-queues on both sides of the world to say this with authority: the difference between chaos and community is mostly a matter of tone. India's crowding is competitive, urgent, faintly anxious. Trinidad's is warm, unbothered, and frequently involves someone offering you food. Both will test every boundary you didn't know you had.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇮🇳 India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept that any gap larger than a forearm's length in a queue will get filled within five minutes — plan accordingly | Take crowding personally; it's a rational response to scarcity, not an act of aggression against you specifically |
| Adjust — "adjust kar lo" is a genuine cultural principle, not a suggestion | Expect Western-style personal space in public transport, markets, or lines; it simply isn't the operating norm |
| Recognise hierarchy and familiarity as the real social currency — strangers are navigated differently than acquaintances | Assume rule-breaking is random; it usually maps onto who has status, urgency, or a plausible excuse |

**🇹🇹 Trinidad and Tobago**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a lime if invited — declining is a bigger social error than showing up underdressed | Rush a conversation; "ole talk" — storytelling, teasing, debate — is the actual point, not a delay before it |
| Expect food and drink sharing as standard practice within a lime | Take picong (playful teasing) personally; it's a sign of inclusion, not hostility |
| Treat liming as a legitimate, respected activity — class and status don't gatekeep it | Assume liming has a schedule or an endpoint; it continues for as long as it continues |

## India: Crowding as Coping Mechanism, Not Character Flaw

Ask any newly arrived expat what shocked them most about India and, nine times out of ten, it isn't the food or the heat — it's the queue that isn't one. Research on urban Indian queuing culture found something genuinely counterintuitive: Indians don't just tolerate crowding, they're reassured by it. Leave a gap of more than a forearm's length between yourself and the person ahead of you, and that gap will not survive five minutes — someone will fill it, not out of malice, but because an empty space in a line reads less like courtesy and more like an opportunity being wasted.

Personal space, as a Western concept, simply doesn't carry the same weight here, and there's a structural reason for that. Much of Indian domestic life has historically run on close quarters — multigenerational households, joint family systems, homes where privacy was never the default assumption. That intimacy scales outward into public life: society hasn't drawn the same firm line between "private" and "public" that, say, Japan or Singapore has, so the instinct to guard your six feet of personal bubble in a train station just isn't wired in the same way.

What outsiders often misread as rudeness is closer to a functioning survival logic. A lot of Indian public life runs on hierarchy and familiarity — you are always someone's relative, someone's contact, someone above or below in some invisible ladder — which leaves very little room for the neutral, anonymous stranger Western public-space etiquette assumes exists. Strip that hierarchy away in a crowded space and you get compression: everyone operating in a kind of collective survival mode, where "adjust kar lo" — you have to adjust — isn't a slogan, it's the actual social contract holding the whole crowded, chaotic system together. It works. It's just not comfortable to be inside of, until you stop expecting it to be something else.

## Trinidad and Tobago: The Republic of Doing Nothing, Beautifully

Trinidad and Tobago solved the queue problem differently: it simply decided not to have one, in spirit if not always in practice, and built an entire social institution around unstructured hanging out instead. Liming — the noble, deeply respected Caribbean art of gathering with no larger purpose than gathering — requires nothing more than two people and the standing invitation for others to join whenever they wander by. It might happen outside a shop, on a beach, in someone's yard, or around a food truck at midnight, and crucially, it carries zero social stigma. In plenty of cultures, visibly lounging around with nothing to do reads as laziness. In Trinidad, it's a point of pride, practised with equal enthusiasm by the unemployed and the wealthy alike, class entirely beside the point.

There are no formal rules, but there are very real informal ones: food and drink get shared, conversation runs on "ole talk" — storytelling, debate, and picong, the art of affectionate, merciless teasing — and everyone present is expected to maintain the lime's relaxed, unhurried atmosphere. Researchers studying this have taken it seriously enough to frame liming as a genuine form of social capital-building: a mechanism for passing down knowledge, strengthening networks, and creating the kind of trust that, elsewhere, gets manufactured through much more effortful networking events with name tags and awkward small talk.

What I love about this, unreservedly, is the total absence of guilt. Nobody in a lime is secretly checking whether they should be doing something more productive. The lime is the productive thing — it's how relationships get built, information gets passed, and community gets maintained, and Trinidadians will tell you this plainly, without irony, because it's simply true.

## The Verdict

India's public behaviour runs on necessity — a dense, hierarchical society finding its own workable logic for close quarters. Trinidad's runs on philosophy — a deliberate, unhurried rejection of the idea that hanging around needs justifying. If you need me to pick a winner: give me the lime, every time. There's something genuinely civilised about a culture that has elevated "doing nothing with other people" into a respected art form, rather than something you apologise for. India, for all its chaotic magnificence, could stand to learn that not every gap needs filling immediately.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — "Why do Indians stand so close in queues?" — paraphrased: a respondent explains that leaving visible space in a line isn't read as politeness, it's read as an invitation for someone else to take your spot.*

> *Marching Sheep blog, "The Great Indian Personal Space Mission" — paraphrased: the author argues that Western visitors mistake crowding for aggression when it's actually closer to communal efficiency — everyone packed in, moving as one unit.*

> *TheLocal-style expat blog — paraphrased: a new arrival in Port of Spain describes their first lime as "the most productive three hours I've ever spent doing absolutely nothing," having left with two job leads and a dinner invitation.*

## Conclusion

Two countries, two completely different relationships with the concept of "waiting your turn," and neither one particularly interested in adopting the other's approach. India crowds because scarcity taught it to; Trinidad lingers because abundance of time — real or performed — taught it that hanging around is worth doing properly. If you're the sort of visitor who needs a straight line and a ticket number to feel civilised, brace yourself for both. But give it a few weeks in either country and you'll stop flinching at the crowding, start joining the lime uninvited, and quietly wonder why you ever thought a queue needed to be quiet, orderly, or, frankly, six feet apart.
