# Singapore Will Government-Issue You a Soulmate. Dhaka Will Just Ask You to Bring One.

🇸🇬 Singapore vs 🇧🇩 Bangladesh
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Singapore is the only country I have lived in where the state runs its own dating agency, sells "Love Gift" vouchers, and cross-references your dating app matches against the national marriage registry before letting you message someone. Bangladesh, meanwhile, offers a dating scene so sparse that fifteen years of diplomatic post-reports — written a decade apart, by people who have never met each other — all arrive independently at the same sentence: if you're not already partnered, don't come here expecting to find one.

One country has turned romance into a bureaucratic service with a government ID check. The other has turned it into a cautionary note in a State Department briefing packet. Welcome to the two least organic dating markets I've encountered in fourteen moves across sixty countries.

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Know which app you're even on — Tinder for internationals, Paktor or Tantan for locals, LunchClick if you're ready to hand over your government ID | Assume moving in together is just a relationship milestone — unmarried couples are largely locked out of subsidized housing and singles can't buy alone until 35 |
| Expect roughly 40% of first dates to run on "the man pays" — know the script before you're caught out by it | Treat a romance-scam warning as paranoia — Singapore Police logged S$35.7 million in losses to dating scams in a single year |
| Join something — a hobby group, a running club, anything with recurring faces — since nearly a third of singles here have never been on a single date | Expect legal clarity on same-sex relationships; decriminalized in 2022, still no marriage or civil unions, despite majority public support |

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇧🇩 Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive already partnered if dating matters to you — multiple independent diplomatic reports, a decade apart, all say the same thing | Kiss or hug in public, even with your own partner — it reads as a real faux pas, not just "not done" |

## Singapore: Romance, Administered

No country I've lived in has institutionalized dating quite like Singapore, where the government has run an official matchmaking body since 1984 — originally the unfortunately-acronymed Social Development Unit, now folded into the Social Development Network — complete with $10 "Love Gift" vouchers redeemable at professional matchmaking agencies and seasonal "Dating Fest" programming involving speed-dating, bowling, and, I am not inventing this, pastry workshops. The state is not subtle about why: nearly 30% of adults here are single, and more than a third of singles have never been on a single date, which the government has decided is a demographic problem rather than a personality trait.

The apps split along oddly legible lines — Tinder, Bumble, and OKCupid skew toward the international set, homegrown Paktor and the strong-with-Chinese-Singaporeans Tantan serve the local market, and LunchClick, from an actual professional matchmaker, requires your government ID number so it can check you against the national marriage database and bar you outright if you're already married. It is, structurally, the most surveilled dating ecosystem I've ever opened an app in.

Then there's the money and the furniture. Around 40% of Singaporeans — rising past half for the over-35 crowd — still expect the man to pay on a first date, a Chinese "bride price" of at least S$1,000 plus jewelry remains customary in some families, and just over half of residents say they wouldn't cohabit before marriage. That last one isn't purely cultural squeamishness — it's load-bearing policy. A single Singaporean generally can't buy a resale HDB flat alone until 35, and unmarried couples can't jointly apply for subsidized housing the way married ones can, which means "let's get a place together" as a relationship milestone is, quite literally, gated by your marital status on a government form. Add in legal limbo for same-sex couples — decriminalized in 2022, still no marriage, despite 55% public support for it — and you have a dating culture where the state has an opinion on every stage of your relationship except, mercifully, who you actually fall for.

## Bangladesh: Bring Your Own Plus-One

Dhaka's dating scene is, by comparison, almost aggressively undocumented — which is itself the headline. Public affection is treated as genuinely impolite; one expat guide lists not kissing or hugging in public in the same breath as eating with the correct hand and dressing modestly, which tells you where it ranks on the list of basic cultural literacy. But the real data point is the consistency of the warning across time: diplomatic post-reports on Talesmag from 2009 through 2023 — different authors, different postings, no coordination between them — converge, almost word for word, on "if you're not bringing a partner and want a dating life, don't come." That's not one disgruntled expat; that's a structural feature holding steady across fifteen years.

For LGBTQ+ professionals the picture tightens further. Gay social life exists almost entirely in private homes, with maybe one larger dance party a year citywide, and one respondent said flatly there's "no local dating" for anyone wanting an honest relationship with a Bangladeshi partner, since so few are out beyond closed circles — lesbian social life was described as effectively nonexistent. On the straight side, a Reddit thread from locals themselves is refreshingly candid about the mismatch: traditional families often want an earning wife for financial reasons while still expecting submissiveness after marriage, and career-focused women raise real concerns about being asked to quit paid work entirely once married — a tension that gets handed directly to any foreign partner who shows up asking questions. What social infrastructure does exist is small enough to be organized around named identities — the Dhaka Expat Club, the Dhaka Expat Network, a Facebook group — rather than diffuse nightlife, because there simply isn't diffuse nightlife to diffuse into.

## The Verdict

Singapore wins on infrastructure and loses on soul — you can find a date here with genuine efficiency, assuming you're comfortable handing a government database your relationship history first. Bangladesh loses on infrastructure entirely, but at least it isn't pretending otherwise; nobody there is selling you a $10 voucher and calling it romance. If forced to choose between a state that monitors my love life and a country that simply has none to offer a newcomer, I'd rather be bored in Dhaka than surveilled in Singapore. Bring a partner. Problem solved either way.

## Conclusion

The honest comparison isn't "which country is better for dating" — it's which kind of absence you'd rather navigate. Singapore gives you an oversupply of mechanism and an undersupply of spontaneity; Bangladesh gives you almost no mechanism at all and expects you to have sorted your heart out before you landed. Both, in their own way, tell a relocating professional the same quiet truth: the easy, organic dating life you had back home was never a given anywhere, and in these two cities it was never really on offer to begin with. Pack accordingly, or better yet, pack a partner.
