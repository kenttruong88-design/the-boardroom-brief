# Hong Kong Will Treat You Brilliantly and Quickly If You Can Pay. Pakistan Will Treat You Eventually, and You Will Know Everyone's Cousin

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Healthcare is the great leveller of the expat experience, in the sense that it levels your bank balance and your dignity in equal measure. Hong Kong runs a public system so cheap that a family medicine visit costs HK$150 and a night in an acute public hospital bed costs HK$300, and a private system so expensive that a single night in a Sanatorium suite was once priced at HK$22,500. Pakistan runs a patchwork in which the public hospitals are stretched, the private sector is large, and the real insurance policy is the cousin who knows someone at the hospital.

For a Pakistani professional arriving in Hong Kong, the shock is not that the system is good. It is that it is two systems, one of which is nominally for you and one of which you will actually use. A sourcing note, because I like my honesty undiluted: the pages I could read are all Hong Kong-only. Where I describe Pakistan I am working from general public knowledge, not reporting. Treat my Karachi comparisons accordingly, and my Hong Kong figures with their dates in mind.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Pakistan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Lean on family and friends for a recommendation before you pick a doctor | Assume the public hospital is the quickest route to a specialist |
| Keep your medical records and prescriptions in your own hands | Expect a single, unified record to follow you |
| Ask for an estimate before treatment at private hospitals | Assume private care is a guarantee of low cost; it is mostly paid out of pocket |
| Budget for tests and medication separately | Treat the pharmacy as an afterthought |

**Hong Kong**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether you count as an eligible person for public fees, which generally means a Hong Kong ID holder | Assume a foreigner can walk into public care at resident prices |
| Buy private health insurance, since the private sector is where speed lives | Rely on public specialist clinics for non-urgent problems; waits can run beyond two years |
| Ask for a price before any private procedure | Agree to a 30-minute procedure without confirming the bill in advance |
| Read the public fee schedule, which is published and clear | Mix up the HK$10,000 annual cap with a cap on everything; it covers only specified public fees |

## Pakistan: Public Strain, Private Bills and the Family Phone Tree

Pakistan's healthcare system is best understood as a hierarchy of access. At the bottom are public hospitals, heavily used and often crowded. At the top are private hospitals offering quicker and more comfortable care, mostly paid for out of pocket. Between them sits an enormous informal layer: relatives, family friends, neighbours and the doctor who will see you if the right person calls. I make no claim to precision here, because the sources I was able to read did not cover Pakistan, but this broad picture is widely understood and is why Pakistani arrivals so often look at a Hong Kong clinic waiting room as a kind of bureaucratic miracle.

What Pakistani professionals bring with them is an instinct for networks. If something goes wrong, you ask someone you trust. That instinct is useful in Hong Kong, where expat forums and colleagues will point you to English-speaking private doctors, but it can also mislead. The public system there is not a favour economy; it is a published schedule. You pay the stated fee, you join the stated queue, and no cousin can move you up.

And yet the instinct for personal recommendation is the best antidote to the other hazard of Hong Kong healthcare, which is opacity in the private sector. A Pakistani executive used to negotiating the cost of everything will find, to their surprise, that you cannot always haggle with a private hospital. You can, however, ask for an estimate, and I strongly recommend you do so before anyone puts on gloves.

Pakistani professionals often tell me, with some justice, that the great advantage of their home system is its human flexibility: there is almost always someone you can ring. The great advantage of the Hong Kong system is the reverse, which is that you never need to ring anyone. The rules are printed, the fees are posted and the queue is the queue. Both approaches have their dignity. The trouble comes when you carry the habits of one into the other, and expect a favour where a form is required, or a form where only a favour will do.

## Hong Kong: A Two-Speed System With a Very Long Slow Lane

The Hospital Authority runs the public system. According to a 2018 Localiiz guide, which is the oldest source in my pile, it operated 42 public hospitals, 47 specialist outpatient clinics and 73 general outpatient clinics in seven clusters, against 11 registered private hospitals. The current fees, per the insurer Bowtie, effective from 1 January 2026, are HK$400 for an accident and emergency attendance, HK$300 a day for an acute general inpatient bed, HK$250 per specialist clinic visit plus HK$20 per drug item, and HK$150 per family medicine visit plus HK$5 per drug item. Those are staggeringly low by the standards of almost every other expensive city.

The catch is eligibility and time. Public fees are for eligible persons, generally holders of the Hong Kong ID, children under eleven who are residents, and others approved by the Hospital Authority. An Expat Exchange contributor who moved to Hong Kong wrote that expats have public access but that waits can be long, and that many go private for shorter ones. Localiiz reports that routine specialist referrals can sometimes wait more than two years, while urgent and semi-urgent cases are seen sooner. (One Expat Exchange answer suggests non-residents can use the public system, which conflicts with the eligibility rules Bowtie sets out, so confirm your own status before assuming.)

Then there is the private bill. Localiiz cites a Hong Kong Sanatorium & Hospital private suite at HK$22,500 a night and a maternity package, with basic extras, estimated at upwards of HK$100,000 without complications. Bobbie-Ann Poulton, quoted in a 2013 South China Morning Post report cited by Localiiz, said she was not warned about the price of a 30-minute procedure that came to HK$10,655 and would not have had it done had she known. The lesson is simple and brutal: in Hong Kong, you do not buy health, you buy a place in a queue, and the price of the queue varies.

A final thought on expat medicine in general, which is that the right question is not which country has the better doctors but which country lets you find out what you need to know before you need it. Hong Kong publishes plenty and hides the rest in the private sector. Pakistan hides plenty and compensates with a thick network of people who will tell you the truth over tea. The ideal arrangement for the newcomer is to ask both: read the schedule, then ring the cousin.

## The Verdict

Hong Kong wins on infrastructure, Pakistan on human warmth, and neither wins on transparency. If you are an employee with decent employer insurance, Hong Kong is the better place to be unwell: quick access, English-speaking staff, well-equipped hospitals. If you are dependent on the public system or lack cover, it can be slow, rule-bound and surprisingly cold.

My advice is blunt. Sort your insurance before you land, treat the public system as a safety net rather than a plan, and ask every private provider for a figure in writing. Do that, and Hong Kong is one of the better places in the world to fall ill. Skip it, and you will learn what a HK$10,655 surprise feels like in the first person.

## Conclusion

Every healthcare system is a negotiation between cost, speed and quality, and every country settles on a different two out of three. Hong Kong gives you cost in the public sector and speed in the private. Pakistan gives you warmth, flexibility and a thicket of personal favours that works superbly until it does not.

Move with your paperwork, your cover and your questions. Ask the price, ask the wait, ask whether it is an eligible-person fee, and write down the answers. It is not romantic advice, but I have been in sixty countries and no one has ever died of reading the fee schedule.
