# In One Country the Street Is Watching You, in the Other the Street Is Talking Over You

🇩🇿 Algeria vs 🇲🇽 Mexico

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Public behaviour is where a culture's true rulebook gets revealed, usually the hard way, usually to you, usually in front of an audience. Algeria runs on quiet, watchful propriety — a sense that the street is a shared space with unspoken standards everyone is expected to already know. Mexico runs on volume, warmth, and a personal space bubble roughly the size of a postage stamp, and both will catch a newcomer completely off guard in opposite directions.

I've been stared at politely into silence in Algiers and hugged by a stranger's entire extended family within ten minutes of arriving somewhere in Mexico City. Neither experience was unpleasant. Both required immediate, humbling recalibration.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇩🇿 Algeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress modestly in public, especially outside major cities and coastal tourist areas | Show excessive public affection with a partner; it draws real, sustained attention |
| Greet people, even strangers, with warmth before getting to business | Raise your voice in public disputes; loud confrontation is seen as a serious loss of composure |
| Respect queues and personal space in lines, they're taken more seriously than they look | Photograph people, especially women, without clearly asking first |

### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect and return physical warmth — handshakes, cheek kisses, and hugs are the norm, not the exception | Take punctuality on social plans literally; "ahorita" is a philosophy, not a time |
| Get comfortable with volume; loud, animated conversation in public is completely normal | Assume quiet reserve reads as polite — it more often reads as cold or standoffish |
| Greet everyone in a room individually when arriving somewhere, it matters more than it seems | Stand too far back in a queue or conversation; personal space runs noticeably smaller than you're used to |

## Algeria: Composure Is the Whole Performance

Public life in Algeria runs on a code of quiet propriety that rewards restraint and modesty far more than it rewards charisma. Conversations happen, warmly, but the volume stays controlled, and open displays of anger, frustration, or even excessive enthusiasm in public spaces tend to read as a loss of self-command rather than authenticity. It's a culture where composure is itself a form of social currency, and losing it, even over something as mundane as a delayed bus, costs you standing in a way that's genuinely hard for outsiders to appreciate until they've watched it happen to someone else.

Dress and public affection follow the same logic. Modesty isn't just a religious or traditional expectation, it's a broader social signal about respect for shared space, and it applies with real consistency outside the more cosmopolitan pockets of Algiers and Oran. Couples holding hands is generally fine; anything more affectionate draws stares that communicate disapproval far more effectively than words would. Photographing strangers, particularly women, without asking is a genuine misstep, not a minor faux pas, and it's one of the most common mistakes visiting newcomers make, usually without realising until the damage is done.

Where it gets warmer, and it does get warmer, is in the ritual of greeting. Walking into a shop, an office, even a queue, without a proper greeting first is considered abrupt bordering on rude. Algerians will happily spend a full minute on hellos and how-are-yous before getting to whatever business actually brought you there, and skipping that step to save time reads as coldness, not efficiency. The street watches, quietly, and rewards those who slow down enough to be seen doing things properly.

## Mexico: The Volume Is the Warmth

Mexican public life operates at a completely different register — louder, closer, warmer, and considerably more forgiving of chaos than Algeria's careful composure. Physical affection is everywhere and expected: handshakes that turn into shoulder claps, cheek kisses between women and between women and men, hugs that arrive faster than you're braced for. Personal space, as understood in colder climates, essentially doesn't exist in the same way, and standing at what feels like a respectful distance can genuinely read as distant or unfriendly.

Volume follows the same pattern. Conversations in restaurants, on buses, in markets, run loud and animated, full body language, real laughter, genuine interruption as a form of engagement rather than rudeness. What looks like an argument to an outsider is very often just Tuesday. Newcomers who arrive expecting reserved, quiet public interaction often misread the volume as aggression, when it's usually the opposite — enthusiasm, comfort, familiarity extended generously and immediately to people who haven't technically earned it yet by any cold, Northern European standard.

Time is the other great adjustment. Punctuality is a flexible, almost theoretical concept in social settings — "ahorita," meaning "in a little while," can mean five minutes or two hours, and getting genuinely upset about it marks you as the problem, not the lateness itself. Greeting culture matters enormously here too, arriving anywhere and failing to individually greet each person in a room is a real breach, but the tone is completely different from Algeria's formal restraint — it's warm, physical, immediate, and utterly unbothered by whether you're technically on time for it.

## The Verdict

Algeria asks for restraint, modesty, and patience with a slower, more formal social rhythm, and rewards it with real dignity and respect. Mexico asks for warmth, physical openness, and a total surrender of your relationship with punctuality, and rewards it with a sense of belonging that arrives startlingly fast. If you're someone who finds public displays of anything exhausting, Algeria will feel like relief. If you find quiet, careful spaces lonely, Mexico will feel like being embraced, literally, by an entire country. Pick the version of public life that matches your actual nervous system, not the one that photographs better.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations Algiers — paraphrased: An expat described being gently but firmly corrected by a shopkeeper for skipping the greeting and going straight into a question, and said it changed how they opened every interaction afterward.*

> *Reddit r/mexico — paraphrased: A commenter said they spent their first month assuming loud arguments on the street were serious conflicts, before realising most of them were just enthusiastic conversations about football.*

> *expat.com Mexico City — paraphrased: A newcomer admitted that arriving "on time" to a house party meant being the only guest there for forty-five minutes, and eventually learned to simply add an hour to every invitation without asking.*

## Conclusion

Neither culture is being difficult on purpose. Algeria has simply decided that dignity lives in restraint, and Mexico has decided that warmth lives in volume, and both are right within their own logic. The mistake newcomers make is assuming their home country's version of "normal" public behaviour travels well. It doesn't. Watch first, adjust fast, and stop being surprised when the street has opinions about how you're behaving in it — because in both of these countries, it absolutely does.
