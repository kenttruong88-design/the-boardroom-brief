# India Sells You Speed and Australia Sells You a Queue With a Medicare Card

🇮🇳 India vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In India, if you have money and a fever, a private hospital will see you before you have finished spelling your surname. Test results appear within hours, the specialist is on the phone and the whole experience has the briskness of a well-run airline lounge. If you have neither money nor a fever, the public system will see you eventually, with a patience you did not know you possessed.

In Australia the experience is quieter, slower and more civilised. You book a GP, you wait a few days, you get a calm ten-minute consultation, you receive a referral and a rebate and a sense that someone, somewhere, has a spreadsheet about your health. It is a system built on rules, and the rules are the point.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Choose a reputable private hospital and check its accreditation | Pick a clinic purely on a friend's cousin's recommendation |
| Keep copies of records, prescriptions and insurance papers | Assume one hospital will send your files to another |
| Ask for a cost estimate before a procedure | Wait until the bill to discover the price |
| Use an international or corporate health plan | Count on public hospitals for convenience |

**Australia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Find a regular GP and build a relationship | Go to an emergency department for a minor ache |
| Check what your visa and any reciprocal agreement cover | Assume Medicare applies to you automatically |
| Ask whether a clinic bulk bills before you book | Expect every GP to be free at the point of care |
| Consider private cover for dental, specialists and waiting times | Treat basic cover as a promise of everything |

## India

India is a country with both extraordinary medicine and extraordinary gaps, and the two live side by side. Expat guides such as the Expat Focus overview of India's health service describe a system in which public hospitals serve huge numbers of people on low budgets, while private hospitals and clinics in the large cities offer modern equipment, trained specialists and relatively short waits. Many of the major private hospitals attract international patients, and medical travel is an established industry.

For a corporate expat, the practical picture is simple. You use private care, you carry insurance or have your employer provide it, and you learn which hospitals the company trusts. The experience is service-oriented in a way that surprises Westerners. Appointments are flexible, reports are fast, and the staff are accustomed to explaining things at length to a worried family. The doctor will often see you without a long booking window, and the pharmacy is on the ground floor.

The culture of medicine has a different texture. Family involvement is strong, and relatives often accompany patients to appointments, ask the questions and help make decisions. Pharmacies sell a broad range of medicines over the counter, and self-medication is common, which a careful newcomer will learn to treat with caution. Hygiene standards, antibiotic use and the quality of smaller clinics vary, so the advice from experienced expats is consistent: go to a good hospital, keep your own records, and ask direct questions.

The main drawbacks are cost for the uninsured, uneven quality outside the big cities, and the sheer scale of the demand. Air pollution, food and water also make everyday health a more active concern than in Australia, and a bout of stomach trouble is almost a rite of passage. None of it is fatal, but all of it is better handled with a plan.

## Australia

Australia's system looks calmer from the outside. The public scheme, Medicare, covers residents with a mix of free hospital care and rebates for GP and specialist visits, and the broader system relies on a combination of public funding and private providers. Queensland Health's explanation of how Medicare works describes the basics of what the scheme covers in hospitals and what sits outside it. Expat Focus walks newcomers through the matter of who is eligible, what visa categories change and why private health cover is often a sensible add-on.

For many Indian arrivals, the first shock is that eligibility depends on status. A permanent resident or citizen is generally covered, while many temporary visa holders must rely on private cover, and Migratio's guide for students and migrants from India stresses the importance of getting the right insurance for your visa. A BrightTax article on what nobody tells you about Australian healthcare makes the same point from the expat's side: the system works well if you understand it and badly if you assume it works like your last country's.

The day-to-day experience is quiet and organised. Your GP is the gatekeeper, referring you to specialists when needed, and the quality of primary care is high. Some clinics bulk bill, meaning the doctor charges the government rebate and nothing more, while others charge a gap fee. Pharmacists give advice, prescriptions follow a clear process and the idea of buying strong medicine with no prescription would raise a polite eyebrow.

The weaknesses are access and waiting. Specialist appointments can take weeks or months, dental is mostly outside the public system, and rural areas have fewer doctors. Indian Link has written about Indian migrants being invisible in the Australian healthcare system, a reminder that language, cultural comfort and trust can shape whether people use the services available. The system is reliable, but it expects you to know your way around it.

One last point, offered with the weary authority of someone who has sat in a great many waiting rooms. Healthcare culture is as much about trust as about access. In India, trust is often personal: you follow a doctor you know, ask a relative who is a nurse and compare notes with a neighbour. In Australia, trust is institutional: you rely on the system, the register and the referral. Moving between the two means swapping one kind of trust for another, and the transition is rarely smooth. Expect to feel slightly lost for the first year, to miss the phone number of a doctor who knew your family, and to build a new network of professionals you may never meet socially. That is not a flaw in either country. It is simply the price of moving.

## The Verdict

Australia wins on fairness, and India wins on speed. If you are seriously ill and have no money, Australia is the safer place to be, because a rules-based public system protects the people who need it most. If you are an insured professional with a niche complaint and a deadline, India's private hospitals will usually see you faster and with more attentiveness.

The expat's best advice is to understand your own category. In India, you are a customer, and you choose carefully. In Australia, you are a participant in a scheme, and you learn the rules. People who bring the wrong mindset to either end up frustrated: the Indian who expects walk-in speed in Sydney, or the Australian who expects regulated quality in an unfamiliar clinic.

I give the edge to Australia for the long haul, with the caveat that the wait is real and the paperwork is cruel. If it were my body, my partner and my deadline, I would want the Australian safety net and the Indian response time. Sadly, nobody sells that combination.

## Conclusion

Healthcare is a country's idea of mercy, written in policy. India's idea is quick, private and family-centred, with the quality depending on your wallet. Australia's is slower, regulated and public, with the quality depending on your status. Neither is perfect, and both will save your life if you ask in the right way.

If you are moving from India to Australia, sort out your cover first, find a GP early and be ready to wait. If you are going the other way, choose a good hospital, keep your records and bring a bottle of water you trust. Either way, do not wait for an emergency to learn how the system works.
