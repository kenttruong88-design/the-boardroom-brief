# One Nation Flexes For The Mirror, The Other Flexes For The Mountain

🇮🇹 Italy vs 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is the fundamental difference between these two countries, distilled into one image: an Italian walking into a gym has done his hair. A Swiss person walking into a gym is actually just using it as a waiting room before the real workout, which takes place forty-five minutes up a vertical rock face with a view of three countries. Both nations will tell you, with total sincerity, that they are "quite sporty." Both are lying in interestingly different directions.

Italy's relationship with exercise is performative, social, and faintly erotic, in the way that Italy manages to make the purchase of printer paper feel faintly erotic. Switzerland's relationship with exercise is a civic duty administered with the same brisk, slightly judgmental efficiency as its trains. One country wants you to look magnificent doing it. The other wants you to be quietly, competently fit forever, and will build you sixty-five thousand kilometres of trail to prove the point. Pack a towel. Pack hiking boots. Pack your ego — you'll need somewhere to put it down.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Italy**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your *certificato medico* (a doctor's sports clearance with a resting ECG) before you even think about signing a contract | Expect to just rock up and lift — almost every Italian gym legally requires that certificate first |
| Bring your own towel to lay over the benches, and a padlock for the locker | Wear your outdoor trainers on the gym floor — indoor shoes only, street shoes stay in the changing room |
| Treat the front desk chat as part of the workout — regulars genuinely know each other at the local *palestra* | Assume the air conditioning will save you in August — many Italians believe drafts cause illness and keep it off out of principle |
| Use the free outdoor calisthenics parks in Milan, Rome and Turin if you want zero paperwork | Sign a 24-month contract without checking the cancellation clause — exiting early usually needs a registered letter and a very good excuse |

**Switzerland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat hiking as the default fitness plan — 57% of residents over 15 do it, on a 65,000km trail network that puts most countries' entire tourism boards to shame | Assume a gym membership is a rounding error — Geneva runs upwards of CHF150 a month, and contracts often lock you in for a year with 90 days' notice to leave |
| Check if your university, employer or canton has access to an ASVZ-style subsidised sports centre | Expect friendliness at the squat rack — Swiss gym culture is efficient, not chatty |
| Use a free outdoor *Vitaparcours* exercise trail — there are roughly 500 of them, each with about 15 stations | Be surprised when "going to the gym" means thirty minutes before someone drives you to a mountain for the actual workout |
| Buy a bike — nearly 70% of Swiss households own one and use it for more than Sunday leisure | Confuse a Swiss person's modesty about their fitness level with an actual absence of fitness — they will out-hike you in silence and never mention it again |

## Italy: The Gym As Theatre, With An Admission Requirement

Nowhere else on Earth makes you audition for your own cardio. Before an Italian gym will let you so much as touch a treadmill, you need a *certificato medico per attività sportiva non agonistica* — a formal medical clearance, resting ECG included, from your doctor (around €30–50) or a private sports clinic (€40–60, often same-day). It's valid for a year and then you do it again. This isn't bureaucratic sadism, exactly — it's a country that takes the body seriously enough to put a doctor between you and the leg press. It is, however, the only country I've lived in where getting fit required paperwork before it required effort.

Once you're cleared, the pricing ladder is its own cultural document. Budget chains like McFit and FitActive run €20–40 a month and look exactly like gyms anywhere else. Mid-range local *palestre* — the real social institutions, the ones where the owner remembers your name and your knee injury — sit at €40–70. Premium clubs such as Virgin Active climb to €90–150, and in Milan, a city that has never met a status symbol it didn't want to wear, that figure can hit €300. Gyms love a 12- or 24-month contract, dangled as a discount, and getting out of one early generally requires proof you've left the country, a doctor's note, or a registered letter sent with the quiet formality of a legal summons.

The etiquette is its own small religion: clean indoor shoes only, a towel draped over every bench like a liturgical cloth, flip-flops in the shower, and a near-universal suspicion of air conditioning — plenty of *palestre* run the AC mild or skip it, because Italians have long associated cold drafts (*i colpi d'aria*) with getting ill, gym heat be damned. One expat blogger in Rome described finally finding her rhythm not through the serious weight room but through evening boxing classes, where she "met a whole different group" — which is, frankly, the entire Italian gym pitch in one sentence. Nobody goes to a *palestra* just to work out. They go to be seen working out, chat between sets, and possibly fall in love with the receptionist. The equipment is almost incidental.

Outside the gym, the real national sport is walking, specifically the *passeggiata* — the evening stroll, dressed properly, through the centre of whatever town you happen to be in, which counts as exercise in the same way a glass of wine counts as hydration: technically true, spiritually about something else entirely. CONI's own participation data shows Italians turning out in real numbers for cycling and swimming too, but the passeggiata remains the one form of movement nobody needs convincing to do, because it was never framed as exercise in the first place. It's just what the evening is for.

## Switzerland: Fitness As Infrastructure, Not Identity

The Swiss have made fitness so structurally embedded in daily life that going to an actual gym can feel almost redundant — which is why, even as 2025 gym memberships hit a record 1.45 million people (about one in five adults, per Swiss Active's Key Data Study), the real national workout is happening outdoors, for free, and has been for a century. The country maintains roughly 65,000 kilometres of signed hiking trail. There are about 500 *Vitaparcours* — free outdoor circuit-training stations in forests nationwide, a concept the Swiss quietly invented and never bothered to brag about internationally. Nearly 70% of households own a bicycle, and cycling or mountain biking is a sport for over 40% of residents aged 15 and up. This is not a "fitness culture" in the Instagram sense. It's closer to civic plumbing: it's just there, it works, and nobody discusses it over dinner.

Where the Swiss do pay, they pay a lot. Zurich gym memberships run about CHF70 a month; Geneva's business-district gyms can hit CHF150. One 2012 forum commenter — comparing Swiss gyms unfavourably to American ones — called them "miserable and miserably expensive." Diplomatic household reports from Geneva back this up with specifics: one respondent pegs commercial gyms north of CHF100 monthly with contracts that demand 90 days' notice to cancel; another, writing in 2009, estimated roughly 1,000 CHF a year for a standard membership and 1,500–2,000 CHF for a country club. The workaround, as ever in Switzerland, is institutional: university-linked ASVZ centres and employer-subsidised programmes offer a cheaper door in, though even those get mixed reviews — one forum poster dismissed ASVZ's selection as "not really the best ones I have seen." Either way, nobody's there to chat. You lift, you leave, you go outside where the actual Swiss fitness culture — silent, competent, slightly smug about the view — is waiting.

Winter simply swaps the outdoor equipment rather than cancelling the habit: cross-country skiing, ski touring and the quietly competitive sport of *Schlitteln* (sledging, taken with total seriousness by grown adults on dedicated runs) keep the same population moving once the trails ice over. There's no real off-season in Swiss fitness culture, just a seasonal rotation of which free, publicly maintained piece of terrain you're currently exploiting for cardio.

## The Verdict

Italy wins on joy, charm, and the sheer theatre of a Tuesday-evening *palestra* session that somehow becomes a social event. Switzerland wins on literally every measurable fitness outcome, because when your backup plan for "skipping the gym" is a free, immaculately signed 65,000km trail network, you've already won the argument before it starts. If you want to feel alive, go Italian. If you want to actually live longer, the data — and the mountain air — side unambiguously with the Swiss. I'd rather be fit and bored than charming and winded, which is the most Swiss sentence I will ever write, and I resent it.

## Conclusion

So there you have it: a country that will make you get a cardiogram before a bicep curl, and a country that will quietly out-fit you using nothing but a forest path and institutional restraint. Neither approach is wrong. Both are, in their own ways, deeply, almost comically on-brand. Move to Italy if you want your gym membership to double as a social life. Move to Switzerland if you want your Sunday hike to double as a personality. Just don't try to bring Swiss gym etiquette into an Italian *palestra* — they will notice you not talking to anyone, and they will worry about you.
