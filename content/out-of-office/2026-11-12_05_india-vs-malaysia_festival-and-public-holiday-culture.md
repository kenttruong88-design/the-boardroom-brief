# India Invented the Festival Season. Malaysia Turned It Into a National Operating System

🇮🇳 India vs 🇲🇾 Malaysia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

India has more festivals than most countries have opinions, and every one of them involves enough noise, sweets and relatives to qualify as a logistics event. Malaysia, meanwhile, has taken the principle of celebrating everything and applied civil-service efficiency. Between the Chinese, Malay, Indian, Christian and indigenous calendars, the country operates a rolling programme of public holidays that makes every other nation look underdressed and overworked.

The surprise for an Indian arriving in Kuala Lumpur is not that Deepavali exists. It is that the whole country shows up to it. The surprise for a foreigner living in either place is that a public holiday is never simply a day off. It is an invitation, an obligation and a traffic warning, all in one.

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |

**Malaysia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept open house invitations from any community | Assume a festival belongs to only one group |
| Check the holiday calendar early each year | Schedule a deadline the day after a long weekend |
| Visit Little India during Deepavali | Drive there at peak hour |
| Dress modestly for religious events | Arrive in beachwear |

## India

India's festival culture is both national and fiercely local. Diwali is one thing in Delhi and another in Chennai, and Pongal or Onam may matter to a household that barely notices Holi. The calendar is crowded, regional and layered, and the holidays that matter to your office depend on which state your office is in. For a foreigner, this means the learning curve is vertical.

The constant is the home. The Hyderabad software engineer interviewed at a Kuala Lumpur Deepavali event in 2017 said Deepavali in India is mainly small family gatherings, which is why large public events in Malaysia amazed him. I find that comment fascinating, because to an outsider India looks like a country that celebrates entirely in the street. In reality much of the weight sits indoors: the cleaning, the lamps, the sweets, the visits, the elders. The public noise is the visible tip.

That is the thing that new expats often miss. In India, a festival is a duty to family first and a spectacle second. You may be asked to visit relatives across the city, receive guests all afternoon and eat far beyond reasonable appetite. Turning up with a modest gift and an empty stomach is the correct form. Saying no to a second helping is a negotiation, not a statement.

The downside is infrastructure. Travel before major festivals is brutal, shops close on short notice for local observances, and your workplace will observe holidays according to its own region and community. A foreigner who treats the festival calendar as optional will discover it is not.

The calendar itself is a lesson in organisation. Because Indian festivals follow regional and lunar calendars, dates shift from year to year, and workplaces sometimes announce which holidays apply only days beforehand. A visiting colleague may be astonished to learn that the Diwali break in one office is five days and in another is two. The practical advice from every Indian friend I have is to ask early, clear your leave in advance and never assume that a festival which matters at home matters equally in the next city. India is a country of many calendars, and each of them insists on being the one that counts.

## Malaysia

Malaysia's holiday calendar is its most dependable export. An expat who posted on Threads, as reported by The Sun, said his Kuala Lumpur office had just returned from one public holiday when he learned in a meeting that another was coming the following week. The commenters were unmoved: one local noted that a single day off can become four, and another who had lived in KL for three years said school holidays are what really wreck the traffic.

The Malaysian approach is distinctive because of the open house tradition. In October 2017 a Deepavali open house at Little India in Brickfields drew guests of all races and religions. A Chennai film producer there said he felt proud to see Indian culture embraced by other communities, and a Kedah sales manager said it was her first open house, though she visits Indian friends every year. One visitor's wife was reportedly surprised to see Muslim women in hijab and sari at the same event. Nine years later, The Star's report on Deepavali in Little India described Indian tourists and workers admiring the lamps, floral decorations and festive greetings on restaurant and attraction boards. A Bengaluru bank worker said he was surprised by how much was decorated. A Chennai entrepreneur staying in a homestay asked herself whether she was in India or Malaysia and said she wanted to bring her children next time.

A 63-year-old from Kerala added the necessary footnote: celebrating in her own village is nicer, but it is good to see Deepavali celebrated so openly in a foreign country. That is exactly right. Malaysia cannot replace home. It can, however, make you feel permitted to carry it with you.

The practical bit is the calendar. Thaipusam, Labour Day and Malaysia Day make long weekends in 2025, and Hari Raya, Chinese New Year and Deepavali follow the lunar calendar, so dates move. If you run a team in Malaysia, build the year around them. If you are a visitor, book early.

The Malaysian holiday year, by contrast, is a shared national project. Because every major community's festival is observed publicly, an employee of any background will see colleagues take time off for celebrations that are not their own, and will often be invited to join. That is the real function of the open house. It is not a tourism event, though it looks like one. It is an annual rehearsal of coexistence, held at a table with food, in a country that has had to practise the skill. The traffic is dreadful, the queues are long and the point stands.

## The Verdict

India wins on depth. Malaysia wins on access. Nowhere on earth has more festival meaning per square kilometre than India, and nowhere is more comfortable sharing one community's festival with everyone else than Malaysia.

If I had to give the crown, I would give it to Malaysia for the open house. India's festivals are richer, older and more varied, but they are often walled by community, region or family. Malaysia has built a public holiday system in which a Muslim, a Buddhist, a Christian and a Hindu can all be at the same table on someone else's holy day, eating the same rice, grumbling about the same traffic. That is an achievement of governance as much as culture.

Of course, it is easier to be open when the festival is a long weekend and not a cousin's wedding. India demands more from you and gives more back. If you can survive the sweets, the relatives and the train booking, you will understand something about devotion. If you cannot, Malaysia will forgive you and send you home with a gift box.

## Conclusion

A public holiday is a country's way of telling you what it values. India values the family altar. Malaysia values the open door.

Plan for both. Learn the festival calendar before your first office meeting, keep a stock of small gifts, and eat the sweets without commentary. Do not schedule anything for the day after a long weekend, and do not trust the traffic apps.

When a country offers you a seat at its festival, take it. You will leave full, slightly late, and a little more convinced that the best way to understand a place is to turn up on its holiest day with nothing planned.
