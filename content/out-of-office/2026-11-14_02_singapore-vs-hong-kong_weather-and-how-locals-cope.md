# Singapore Is a Sauna With Good Branding. Hong Kong Is a Sauna With a Typhoon Clause

🇸🇬 Singapore vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

People who move to Asia's two great financial cities tend to ask the wrong weather question. They ask which one is hotter. The answer is that Singapore is hot in a way that never changes, and Hong Kong is hot in a way that comes with a plot, a villain and the occasional evacuation. Choose your pain: a flat line, or a drama.

I have done both, and I will tell you that the calendar matters more than the thermometer. One city lets you forget what month it is. The other makes you buy a second wardrobe, learn what a signal number means, and develop a quiet respect for sellotape.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Singapore**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry a compact umbrella every single day | Trust a blue sky at two in the afternoon |
| Dress in breathable fabrics and expect to change shirts | Wear a suit jacket outdoors and expect to survive |
| Use the covered walkways and air-conditioned links | Walk uncovered between buildings at midday |
| Keep a light layer for the arctic offices | Assume indoors is also tropical |

**Hong Kong**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the typhoon signal system before May | Ignore a warning because it looks sunny |
| Build a proper winter wardrobe for December to February | Assume it never gets cold |
| Plan your outdoor life around autumn | Hike in August |
| Stock food and water when a storm is forecast | Leave grocery shopping until the shelves empty |

## Singapore

Singapore's climate has the consistency of a corporate mission statement. According to the Expat Exchange summary, temperatures run from about 23 to 34 degrees, and the year is split by two monsoons: the Northeast Monsoon from December to March and the Southwest Monsoon from June to September, with heavy rain and thunderstorms in both. In practice, that means there are no seasons in any sense a European would recognise. There is warm and wet, and there is warm and slightly wetter.

The adaptation here is architectural and behavioural. The city has quietly built itself an air-conditioned parallel universe: links between buildings, covered walkways, underground passages and malls that connect to stations. Locals do not battle the climate, they route around it. You learn very quickly which route between your office and your lunch has a roof, and you will defend that route with your life.

The second lesson is the temperature shock. Singapore offices are cooled to something resembling a Scandinavian morgue, so the standard local costume is a cardigan carried everywhere in a country where nobody has needed one outdoors since independence. You will sweat on the street and shiver at your desk, in a cycle that never ends and that no wardrobe fully solves.

What I admire, grudgingly, is that Singapore asks very little of your imagination. You never need to check whether it will be cold. You never need to check whether a storm will cancel the commute. Even the downpours are brisk, well drained and over in an hour. The forecast is so reliable you could set a watch by it, though you would not need to, because the sun comes up at roughly the same time all year.

The cost is monotony, and a certain physical weariness. Humidity is a permanent tax, hair is a lost cause, and shoes left in a cupboard will grow something. If you are a person who needs weather to mark the passing of time, Singapore will quietly flatten you. If you are a person who wants to stop thinking about the weather entirely, it is almost ideal.

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong's weather has seasons, which is both its charm and its problem. According to Expat Arrivals, summer runs from May to September with a peak around 33 degrees, winter from December to February at about 15, and autumn from October to early December is, in the guide's words, arguably the best time of year. A British expat named Alison put it simply: "The outdoor life and good weather is a real improvement." She is not wrong, as long as you have the right month.

Then there are the typhoons. Expat Arrivals notes they can occur from May to November, with heavy rain lasting days and risks of flooding and landslides. A long-term resident on Expat.com, who said he had lived in Hong Kong for seven years, was blunter: "I cannot overestimate the severity of typhoons and everyone would want to be home when they hit." He also described the practical rituals, ferries and transport disrupted, windows taped in a crisscross pattern, and a general sense that the city pauses and waits.

This is the key cultural difference. Singapore treats weather as an inconvenience to be engineered away. Hong Kong treats it as a force to be respected. The warning system is public, graduated and obeyed, and the whole population knows when to go home, when to stay in and when the office can be told to wait. In my experience it is an unusually sane approach to a genuine hazard, and I find the lack of bravado refreshing.

Winter is the other surprise. Hong Kong gets properly cool, with damp, bone-chilling days, and buildings are rarely designed to be heated. The newcomer who arrives in January with only linen shirts learns a hard lesson in a flat with no central heating. Locals respond with layers, hot pot and a general air of mild martyrdom.

Add the humidity, and you have a city that cycles through steam, storm, glorious autumn and damp chill. It is more interesting than Singapore, and in some ways more exhausting. But it feels like weather, and that counts for something.

There is also a social side to coping. In Singapore, the shared experience is the downpour: a crowd under a covered walkway, watching the rain with the patience of people who know it will stop. In Hong Kong, the shared experience is the warning. When a signal goes up, the whole city quietly reorganises, colleagues message each other about trains, and nobody is shy about leaving early. Both cities have a ritual that turns an inconvenience into a communal moment, and in both, the newcomer learns the ritual by watching what everybody else does with their umbrella.

## The Verdict

If you want to stop thinking about the weather, Singapore wins. If you want to feel alive in it, Hong Kong wins. I know which one I find more bearable day to day, and I will admit it: Singapore, because predictability is underrated when you are late for a meeting.

But I also know which city I would rather walk in. Hong Kong's autumn, with its clear skies and cool air, is among the finest weather on earth, and a mountain trail on a crisp December morning beats any air-conditioned link bridge. Singapore never offers that. It offers consistency, which is a different gift.

My ruling is Hong Kong for people who can tolerate drama, and Singapore for people who cannot. If you have small children, a heat intolerance or a weak stomach for storms, pick Singapore and buy a good dehumidifier. If you want seasons and can handle a signal number eight, take Hong Kong.

## Conclusion

Weather is not small talk when you live in the tropics. It decides your commute, your clothes, your mood and your electricity bill. Singapore has made that decision for you, in advance, and delivered it with air conditioning. Hong Kong leaves it open, and then occasionally closes the whole city.

I prefer the second kind of honesty, though I would not wish a signal number eight on anyone who has just unpacked. Whichever you pick, buy the umbrella. Buy the cardigan. And learn to love autumn, wherever you can find it.
