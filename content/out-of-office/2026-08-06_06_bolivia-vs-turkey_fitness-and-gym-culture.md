# Bolivia Makes Walking to the Shop a Cardio Workout; Turkey Makes Recovery an Entire Cultural Institution

🇧🇴 Bolivia vs 🇹🇷 Turkey — Fitness and Gym Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Bolivia doesn't have a fitness culture so much as an involuntary fitness curriculum, administered by altitude, with La Paz sitting at roughly 3,600 metres and El Alto climbing higher still, meaning your body is doing serious cardiovascular work simply existing, let alone attempting a gym session. Turkey, meanwhile, has built an entire cultural infrastructure around the opposite instinct — not exertion but recovery, with the hamam functioning as gym, spa, and social institution rolled into one ancient, steam-filled tradition that modern fitness culture has grown up alongside rather than replaced.

I have climbed three flights of stairs in La Paz and needed an actual sit-down, despite being, by any reasonable measure, a fit adult, and been told cheerfully by a local that this was completely normal and would only take "a few weeks" to adjust to. I have also emerged from a hamam in Istanbul so thoroughly, professionally scrubbed and pummelled that I understood, for the first time, why Turkish gym culture doesn't seem to carry the same anxious, optimising energy as its Western equivalent — recovery here is taken as seriously as exertion, which honestly should be the global standard.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇧🇴 Bolivia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Give yourself real acclimatisation time in La Paz or El Alto before attempting normal workouts | Push through altitude sickness symptoms to "prove" fitness; it's a genuine medical risk |
| Hydrate aggressively and consider coca tea, a local remedy widely used for altitude adjustment | Assume your sea-level fitness baseline transfers; it genuinely doesn't for weeks |
| Explore outdoor sport culture — cycling Death Road, hiking the Cordillera — once acclimatised | Book intense group fitness classes in your first week; locals pace new arrivals for a reason |

**🇹🇷 Turkey**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Experience a proper hamam at least once — it's not a tourist gimmick, it's a genuine institution | Rush the hamam process; it's meant to be slow, social, and unhurried |
| Try neighbourhood gyms for an authentic, unpretentious training culture at low cost | Expect Istanbul's upscale gym scene (in areas like Nişantaşı) to represent the national norm |
| Respect single-sex hamam sessions and etiquette; ask locally if unsure which sessions apply | Treat the hamam purely as spa tourism; it holds real social and family significance |

## Bolivia: Altitude as the Ultimate Personal Trainer

There is no gently working up to fitness in La Paz. The city itself, spilling down a canyon at an elevation that would count as a serious mountaineering achievement almost anywhere else, does the work for you, whether you consented to it or not. Newly arrived visitors, regardless of how fit they consider themselves at sea level, routinely find themselves winded by stairs, dizzy after a brisk walk, and genuinely humbled within the first day. This isn't a lack of fitness. It's oxygen doing what oxygen does at 3,600 metres, and no amount of gym-honed cardiovascular conditioning back home fully prepares you for it.

Locals treat this with a mixture of sympathy and mild amusement that borders on ritual — everyone in La Paz has watched a confident newcomer discover, humiliatingly, that they cannot in fact climb the same stairs they climbed at home without stopping. Coca tea, offered almost reflexively to newcomers struggling with the altitude, carries genuine traditional standing as a remedy, used for generations to ease the symptoms of soroche (altitude sickness) well before it became a curiosity for visitors.

Once acclimatisation happens — genuinely two to three weeks for most people, not the day or two optimists hope for — Bolivia opens up into a serious, legitimate outdoor sport culture. Cycling Bolivia's "Death Road," the old Yungas route down from La Paz, has become a rite of passage for a certain kind of adrenaline-seeking traveller, and the surrounding Cordillera Real offers hiking and climbing that would be genuinely world class if the country's tourism infrastructure marketed it more aggressively. Conventional gyms exist in La Paz and Santa Cruz, increasingly Western in style, but they're almost beside the point in a country where the environment itself is the more demanding, more interesting training ground.

The mistake most newcomers make is pride — pushing through genuine altitude sickness symptoms rather than resting, because admitting a jog left you gasping feels embarrassing. Locals don't read it that way at all. Everyone struggled at first. The only people who earn quiet local judgment are the ones who refuse to acknowledge it and put themselves at real medical risk in the process.

## Turkey: Recovery Is Not Optional, It's Cultural Infrastructure

Turkish fitness culture operates on a philosophy that most Western gym cultures have only recently, awkwardly started to rediscover: that exertion without genuine recovery isn't discipline, it's just self-inflicted wear. The hamam — the Turkish bath, with roots stretching back through Ottoman and earlier Roman and Byzantine bathing traditions — remains a living, actively used institution, not a museum piece performed for tourists, though plenty of tourist-oriented versions exist alongside the neighbourhood ones locals actually frequent weekly.

A proper hamam visit is unhurried by design: steam, a thorough scrub with a kese (an exfoliating mitt that removes a genuinely startling amount of dead skin), often a firm massage, all conducted in a marble-lined space that's been doing this exact job for centuries. Locals treat it as seriously social as it is physical — friends and family go together, conversations happen in the steam room the way they might elsewhere happen over coffee, and the pacing is deliberately, defiantly slow in a way that resists the efficiency-obsessed instincts a lot of foreign visitors bring with them.

Modern gym culture coexists alongside this rather than replacing it, and Istanbul in particular has developed a genuinely bifurcated fitness scene: upscale, international-standard gyms in wealthier districts like Nişantaşı and Etiler, complete with the same optimisation-obsessed energy you'd find in London or Los Angeles, and unpretentious neighbourhood gyms elsewhere in the city, often decades-old, low-cost, and staffed by trainers who learned their craft the hard way rather than through a certification course. Both are legitimate. Assuming the glossy version represents the national norm misses most of how Turks actually train.

Single-sex hamam sessions remain standard practice at most traditional establishments, alternating or separate-facility scheduling by gender, and getting this etiquette wrong — showing up during the wrong session, misunderstanding the changing protocol — is a genuinely common newcomer stumble, easily avoided by simply asking staff or a local friend before your first visit rather than guessing.

## The Verdict

Bolivia forces fitness on you involuntarily, through geography, and rewards patience with genuinely spectacular outdoor sport once your body catches up to the altitude. Turkey builds fitness around a completely different, arguably wiser premise — that recovery deserves the same cultural investment as exertion — and the hamam remains one of the most genuinely restorative traditions I've encountered anywhere. If you want a country that will humble you into better cardiovascular health whether you like it or not, Bolivia wins by sheer elevation. If you want a country that will teach you recovery is not indulgence but infrastructure, Turkey's centuries of bathhouse wisdom will outlast every fad your home gym has ever sold you.

## Conclusion

Bolivia and Turkey both understand something most modern fitness cultures have forgotten in their obsession with tracking every metric: the body needs both real challenge and real rest, and neither can be optimised away. La Paz will hand you the challenge whether you asked for it or not. Istanbul will hand you the rest, unhurried, steam-soaked, and centuries-tested. Show up expecting a normal gym membership to cover either experience and you'll miss the actual point both countries are making — one through altitude, one through architecture.
