# In Senegal, You Pay Cash and Pray. In Poland, You Wait in a Queue and Pray Harder.

🇸🇳 Senegal vs 🇵🇱 Poland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Healthcare abroad is where the fantasy of expat life meets its most sobering reality check, and Senegal and Poland offer two very different flavours of "you'll figure it out eventually." Senegal's system runs largely on private clinics and out-of-pocket payment, meaning care can be fast and personal if you can pay for it, and considerably more precarious if you can't. Poland's public system, the NFZ, is technically comprehensive and often free at the point of use — assuming you can survive the waiting lists, which can stretch from weeks to genuinely alarming months for anything beyond urgent care.

I've paid a Dakar clinic in cash for a same-day consultation with a doctor who spoke three languages fluently and gave me his personal number for follow-up, and I've sat in a Warsaw NFZ waiting room long enough to finish a novel while waiting for a specialist referral that had been booked four months prior. Both systems work. Neither works quickly for the same reasons.

## Do's and Don'ts: Senegal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comprehensive private health insurance before you arrive — public facilities are under-resourced | Assume "clinic" means the same standard everywhere; vet the specific facility, not just the country |
| Keep cash on hand — many private clinics expect payment upfront, before treatment | Delay treatment for cost reasons if it's serious; ask about payment plans, most clinics are flexible if you ask |
| Build a relationship with a trusted local doctor early, before you actually need one | Rely solely on pharmacists for diagnosis, even though they're often extremely knowledgeable and helpful |
| Get recommended clinics from other expats — quality varies enormously between facilities | Assume rural or public hospital care matches Dakar's private clinic standard |

## Do's and Don'ts: Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with the NFZ if you're working and paying into the system — it's genuinely comprehensive | Expect fast specialist appointments through the public system — private is the workaround for a reason |
| Get supplementary private insurance (like Luxmed or Medicover) for speed and English-speaking doctors | Assume public and private are interchangeable; know which one you're actually using in an emergency |
| Learn basic medical Polish vocabulary — English isn't guaranteed even in cities | Skip travel/health insurance as an EU visitor assuming full reciprocity; check your specific coverage first |
| Use private pharmacies for quick over-the-counter advice — pharmacists are highly trained and helpful | Wait passively for an NFZ referral if the wait is unreasonable — private consults can unblock the process |

## Senegal: Fast, Personal, and Entirely Dependent on Your Wallet

Senegal's healthcare landscape is sharply split between well-equipped private clinics, concentrated heavily in Dakar, and a public system that remains under-resourced and stretched thin, particularly outside the capital. For expats with money and decent insurance, this split actually works in your favour: private clinics like Clinique Casahous or Clinique de la Madeleine offer same-day appointments, personal attention from doctors who often trained in France and speak fluent English or French, and a level of individual care that would be unthinkable to expect from an overstretched public hospital anywhere.

The catch, obviously, is cost, and the expectation that you'll pay upfront, in cash or by card, before treatment begins. This isn't a scam or a shakedown — it's simply how the system operates given limited public infrastructure and minimal insurance penetration — but it does mean a genuine emergency without insurance or accessible funds becomes a genuinely frightening situation fast. Comprehensive private health insurance isn't optional for a serious expat stay in Senegal; it's the entire foundation that makes the private clinic system actually work for you rather than against you.

What surprises newcomers is how good the informal medical network can be once you're plugged into it — word-of-mouth recommendations from other expats about which specific doctor to see for which specific issue matter enormously, far more than which clinic's name is on the building. Pharmacists, too, are an underrated resource; many are highly trained and will often diagnose and treat minor ailments directly, sparing you a clinic visit altogether. But step outside Dakar's private ecosystem — into rural areas or under-resourced public hospitals — and the standard of care drops sharply, which is precisely why so much expat medical planning in Senegal revolves around staying within reach of the capital's private network, or arranging medical evacuation coverage for anything serious.

## Poland: Comprehensive, Free, and Agonisingly Slow

Poland's National Health Fund (NFZ) is, on paper, one of the more impressive public healthcare achievements in the region — comprehensive coverage, free at the point of use for anyone registered and contributing, genuinely competent doctors and modern hospitals in major cities. In practice, the defining feature of the NFZ for most people, expat or otherwise, is the wait. A referral to a specialist that should, medically speaking, happen within weeks can instead take months, and it's not unusual for Poles themselves to simply budget for a private consultation to skip the queue entirely rather than wait out the public system for anything non-emergency.

This has created a genuinely two-tier culture around Polish healthcare that every expat eventually has to navigate: the NFZ for anything urgent or for care you're willing to wait for, and private providers — Luxmed, Medicover, and similar subscription-based clinics — for everything you want handled quickly, in English, with an appointment booked for this week rather than this autumn. Most established expats end up paying for private supplementary coverage specifically because the wait times, not the quality, are the actual barrier in the public system.

Language remains an underappreciated obstacle even in a modern EU healthcare system — while younger doctors, especially in Warsaw or Kraków, often speak good English, this isn't universal, and navigating the NFZ system administratively (registration, referrals, prescriptions) can be genuinely difficult without at least basic Polish or a patient, bilingual friend. EU citizens sometimes arrive assuming full reciprocity will smooth everything over, only to discover their specific coverage (EHIC or otherwise) doesn't cover the situation they're actually in. The system works, and works well, for anyone with patience, private backup insurance, and a realistic understanding that "free and comprehensive" and "fast" are, in Poland, two entirely separate promises.

## The Verdict

Senegal wins on speed and personal attention — if you can pay, you'll be seen today, by a doctor who remembers your name. Poland wins on comprehensiveness and genuine equity — the NFZ actually works as intended for the patient willing to wait, which is a rarer achievement than it sounds. But "willing to wait" is doing enormous work in that sentence, and most expats in Poland end up paying for private coverage anyway, which somewhat undercuts the free-healthcare bragging rights. If I need fast, personal care and have the cash, I'm in Dakar. If I'm planning for the long haul and want a safety net that won't bankrupt me, I want Poland's system underneath me — I just won't be using it for anything urgent.

## Conclusion

Senegal and Poland have both built healthcare systems that work — just not in the ways either country's marketing might suggest. Senegal trades universal access for speed and personal care, provided you can pay; Poland trades speed for genuine universal coverage, provided you can wait. Arrive in either without understanding which trade-off you're accepting, and you'll find out the hard way, usually at 2am, usually when it matters most. Get the insurance. Get the local recommendations. And in Poland's case, get comfortable with your phone's calendar app — you'll be living in it.
