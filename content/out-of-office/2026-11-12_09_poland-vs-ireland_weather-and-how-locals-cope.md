# Poland Gets Four Proper Seasons and Complains About Them. Ireland Gets One Long Damp Mood and Calls It Banter

🇵🇱 Poland vs 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Poland has weather. Real weather, with a spine: crisp winters, hot summers, dramatic autumn light, and a genuine spring that arrives like an apology. Ireland has weather in the way a stereotype has a hat. It is soft, grey, changeable and inescapable, and the locals have developed a conversational culture around it that is nearly a second language.

Hundreds of thousands of Poles have moved to Ireland, so the contrast is a daily, lived argument. The Polish winter is cold and clean. The Irish winter is mild and wet, like being slowly folded into a damp towel. Both can ruin your mood. Only one will make you hate your coat.

## Poland

Poland's climate has a simple, honest character. Winters are cold and often snowy, summers are warm and sunny, and the seasons announce themselves clearly. A Polish winter is predictable in the way a bad boss is predictable: you know what is coming, you know what to wear, and you resent it with dignity. The reward is a country that handles cold with confidence, from heated pavements in some cities to a culture of woollen layers, thermal tights and hot tea.

The Expat Arrivals guide puts the cold season at roughly November to March, though it can stretch when eastern winds arrive, and says snow can fall as late as April. Summers, from June to August, average a moderate 20 to 25 degrees, with excessive heat rare and the occasional drought. Spring and autumn, it adds, are the best seasons. Poles cope with weather by planning for it. They dress for it, build their year around it and treat the arrival of the first proper spring day as a public holiday. Summer evenings in a Polish city are among the most underrated social events in Europe, with terraces open late and everyone behaving as though the sun might be repossessed.

This also shapes how Poles view Ireland. Snow, in their mind, is a legitimate climate. Rain is merely an inconvenience. One poster from snow country on an Expat.com forum said he is glad of Irish rain because you never have to shovel it, a remark that I suspect every Pole in Dublin has made at least once.

The point is not that Poland is better. It is that Poland offers contrast. After a long, grey, Polish winter, the explosion of spring means something. You earn it. In Ireland the spring is gentler and the winter shorter, but the sense of earning is lost in the persistent drizzle.

What makes the Polish relationship with weather distinctive is that it is communal and seasonal at the same time. A cold winter is something everyone shares, and the culture has evolved rituals to match: soups, hot drinks, warm kitchens and long evenings with friends. Spring and autumn are celebrated as transitions, and a warm Saturday is treated with something close to reverence. This gives the weather a narrative arc, with a beginning, a middle and a hard-earned end. People who grow up inside that arc find the flat Irish year slightly baffling, as though someone had removed the plot.

## Ireland

Ireland, according to the Expat Arrivals guide, sees summers of roughly 16 to 21 degrees and winters of roughly four to ten, moderated by the Atlantic and the Gulf Stream. Spring tends to be the driest season, except in the east, with precipitation peaking in autumn and winter. Snow is uncommon, though frost occurs. And then comes the line that every Irish tourist board would rather hide: "Rain is pretty much a constant here."

The expats are pragmatic. Agnieszka, a Pole from Bydgoszcz who had moved to Galway a month before her interview, said the weather could be nicer but you do not move to Ireland for the weather. Her chief advice was to buy a good rain jacket. An American in Tennessee noted that Dublin gets about thirty inches of rain a year compared with fifty-five to sixty at home, and welcomed it. Statistics are a comfort; the wet is in the persistence, not the volume.

Another poster in an undated Expat.com thread said the rain is almost always accompanied by wind and that a waxed-cotton jacket helped. Someone else who spent two years in Ireland said they never really enjoyed the weather, only the few sunny days. It is a candid verdict that I find more convincing than the brochure.

Winter is the real test. A sixteen-year-old thread in Dublin asked how to stay positive. The advice from locals and returnees was practical: warm layers and wet-weather gear, get outside while it is light, light a fire with friends, keep your social calendar full and do not skip the parties. The Irish coping mechanism is community. They talk about the weather because it is a shared enemy, and they meet in pubs because the pub is warm.

That is the quiet genius of Ireland. The climate is mild and miserable at the same time, and the culture responds with humour, company and a hot drink. It is unglamorous, and it works.

The Irish habit of discussing the weather deserves a defence. It is not a failure of imagination. It is a social technique, a way of starting a conversation with a stranger on common ground, and it works precisely because the weather is changeable enough to be interesting and universal enough to be safe. The Dublin winter thread's advice to keep socialising and not to skip the parties reads like a municipal policy. In a climate that offers no dramatic seasons, the Irish have made the pub, the kitchen and the chat into the heating system.

## The Verdict

Poland wins on climate quality, Ireland wins on coping. If you like your weather with edges, take Poland. The seasons are bold, the summers are real, and the winter has the decency to be beautiful when it hurts.

Ireland's weather is gentler but more oppressive in its constancy. A grey Tuesday in Cork can feel exactly like the grey Tuesday before it. What Ireland has built in response is a social culture that makes the dreariness bearable: the pub, the chat, the endless cups of tea, the fire in the corner.

Therefore I score it unevenly. For the weather itself, Poland. For how people live with it, Ireland. If you can only pick one, pick the place where you can wear the same coat for the whole year and be comfortable. That is Ireland. If you want a year with a plot, pick Poland.

Perhaps the best advice comes from Agnieszka: do not move for the weather. Move for the people, and then buy the jacket.

## Conclusion

You cannot change the weather, only your relationship with it. Poland teaches preparation. Ireland teaches acceptance.

If you are moving between them, pack for both. Take the thermal layers for a Polish January and the waterproofs for an Irish everything. Plan your social life around the dark months, not despite them.

And remember that the Irish do not talk about the weather because they have nothing else to say. They talk about it because it is the one thing everyone can agree on.
