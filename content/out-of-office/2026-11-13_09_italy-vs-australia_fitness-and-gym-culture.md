# Italy Demands a Medical Certificate Before You Lift a Dumbbell. Australia Assumes You Would Rather Be in the Sea

🇮🇹 Italy vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two ways to stay fit. You can build a system, with paperwork, contracts and a laminated list of rules about towels. Or you can live somewhere that makes the outdoors so appealing that exercise happens by accident. Italy has chosen the first approach for its gyms and the second for everything else. Australia has chosen the second and then wonders why its gyms are mediocre.

I have trained in a lot of countries and joined, abandoned and been billed by gyms in most of them. The pattern is consistent: the country tells you what exercise is for. In Italy it is for staying well, in a way that does not interfere with lunch. In Australia it is for being the kind of person who is seen outdoors.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Italy**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get the sports medical certificate before joining | Turn up at a gym without it |
| Read a 12- or 24-month contract line by line | Assume you can cancel when you feel like it |
| Bring a padlock, a towel and clean indoor shoes | Wear your street shoes on the floor |
| Walk, cycle and use the parks | Treat the gym as the only option |

**Australia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the beach, the pool and the trails | Assume a gym is the centre of fitness life |
| Join a team or club to meet people | Wait to be invited |
| Pick a sport you can do outdoors | Skip the sun protection |
| Treat the gym as a supplement | Expect it to be glamorous |

## Italy

Italy takes bureaucracy into the weights room. The Italy Handbook's guide to joining a gym explains that you need a certificato medico per attività sportiva non agonistica, a medical certificate for non-competitive sport that includes a resting ECG. It is valid for a year, and gyms will block access when it expires. It costs about 30 to 50 euros through a public GP and 40 to 60 at a private clinic. Prices for membership run from 20 to 35 euros a month at budget chains such as McFit, to 40 to 70 at mid-range local gyms, to 90 to 150 or more at premium clubs. CrossFit boxes run 80 to 120 euros, and boutique classes 15 to 25 each. There is also a yearly registration fee of 30 to 80 euros, which covers basic insurance.

Then come the contracts. Gyms push twelve- or twenty-four-month binding agreements, and cancelling normally requires a relocation or serious medical reason and a registered letter, the dreaded raccomandata A/R. Etiquette matters: clean indoor shoes only, a towel over every bench, wipe down the cardio machines, bring your own padlock, wear flip-flops in the showers. Many gyms keep the air conditioning mild because of a widespread belief that cold draughts make you ill. The guide's vocabulary list includes scheda, the workout plan, and spogliatoio, the changing room.

But the gym is not really where Italian fitness lives. Rachel LaBud, writing for aSweatLife after three years in Italy, found Italians less fitness-obsessed than Americans. Older adults stayed active through cycling, yard work and walking, and she walked about ten miles a week herself. Meals were smaller, seasonal and shared. A panel of fourteen Rome expats in Doctors in Italy agreed. One goes to the gym at least four times a week. Another walks or bikes everywhere and goes to the gym only when it rains. A fitness coach recommends training outdoors in Rome's parks, another mentions that some parks have gym equipment and suggests the stairs and hills at the Circus Maximus, and one plays tennis at a circolo and takes group classes through a friend's membership. Others run along the Tiber.

Italy, in short, treats the gym as a formal institution and treats movement as an everyday habit. You need a doctor's note for one and none for the other.

## Australia

Australia's gym culture is the most honest part of this comparison, because most Australians do not seem to care. The most pointed first-person account I could find, a newsletter by a writer who goes by Sheep, bluntly titled "Aussie gyms are a bit shit", argues that gyms in Australia are mediocre because they are not a priority. Australians, he says, prefer surfing, running, volleyball, climbing, hiking, rugby, Aussie rules, tennis, padel, swimming, Hyrox and CrossFit, and treat the gym as a supplement. He contrasts this with the UK, where bad weather and the cost of outdoor sport push people indoors, producing better gyms.

I should be clear that this is one writer's opinion, not a survey. I could not verify specific prices, opening hours or membership rules for Australia from any source I could read, so I will not pretend otherwise. What I can say is that expats on Expat Exchange praise the sun, the blue skies and the outdoor lifestyle, and one Brisbane resident notes that you are never more than an hour from a beach. That tells you where the exercise happens.

The logic is easy to follow. If the sea is warm, the trails are open and the weather rarely forces you indoors, a windowless room full of treadmills is a hard sell. The same Expat Exchange guide advises newcomers to join clubs, and in my experience that is where Australian fitness really lives: in a Saturday morning team, a lane in the local pool and a dawn run that ends with coffee.

The downside is obvious. When it is too hot, too wet or too dark, the gym is where you end up, and it may not be as good as you hoped. Australians appear to have decided that this is a price worth paying.

The business model is worth a moment. Italy Handbook notes that free calisthenics parks exist in Milan, Rome and Turin, that running and cycling clubs are common, and that padel courts can be rented by the hour. In other words, the alternatives to the gym are plentiful, cheap and sociable, which is exactly why the gym itself has to compete by being formal. Australia, by contrast, does not need a formal gym culture because the informal one is so strong. A country that has the ocean at the end of every road has no urgent need for a leg-press machine.

## The Verdict

Italy wins on structure and Australia wins on joy. If you are a person who thrives on rules, receipts and a membership card, Italy will serve you well, though the contract will outlast your enthusiasm. If you are a person who wants to exercise without noticing, Australia is a gift.

I find it telling that Italy requires a doctor to certify you fit for the gym but not for the park. It reveals a country that trusts its streets more than its machines. Australia trusts both, but invests in the ocean.

My pick: Australia for anyone under fifty with a pulse, Italy for anyone who prefers a sensible walk to a spin class. Italy's gyms are fine, Australia's are an afterthought, and the real winner in both cases is the world outside the door. If you must choose, choose the country where you will actually leave the house.

## Conclusion

Fitness is a cultural argument disguised as a lifestyle choice. Italy argues for moderation, paperwork and a long walk after dinner. Australia argues for the sea, the sun and not thinking about it.

Take either and you will end up healthier than you started, as long as you leave the building. The gym is rarely the point.

And if you do join an Italian one, read the contract twice. The certificate lasts a year. The cancellation clause lasts for ever.
