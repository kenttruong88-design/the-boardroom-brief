# One Country's Healthcare Is Nearly Free and Agonisingly Slow. The Other's Is Fast, Excellent, and Will Cost You Dearly.

🇮🇸 Iceland vs 🇸🇬 Singapore — Healthcare and Expat Medical Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Iceland has roughly 380,000 people, a handful of hospitals, and a national healthcare system so heavily subsidised that once you clear an annual cost ceiling, treatment becomes essentially free. The catch, and it is a significant one, is that seeing a specialist for anything non-urgent can involve a wait measured in months, because there simply aren't enough specialists on an island this size to see everyone quickly. Singapore has one of the most efficient healthcare systems on Earth, gleaming private hospitals that operate more like five-star hotels than clinics, and a bill that will arrive with the same brisk efficiency as the treatment itself.

I've waited four months in Iceland for a routine referral that would have taken four days in Singapore — and I've paid a Singapore hospital bill for that same speed that made me physically wince at the currency conversion. Neither system is broken. They've simply made entirely different bets about what healthcare should optimise for: universality and cost, or speed and choice.

## Do's & Don'ts: Iceland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with Sjúkratryggingar Íslands as soon as you have residency | Expect a fast specialist appointment for non-urgent issues |
| Use the free 1700 health helpline before heading to an ER | Skip travel insurance in your first six months before coverage kicks in |
| Bring existing prescriptions and medical records translated | Assume private options exist widely — the system is overwhelmingly public |
| Budget for the annual out-of-pocket cost ceiling, then relax | Panic if a routine referral takes weeks — it's normal, not neglect |

## Do's & Don'ts: Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comprehensive private insurance before you need it | Assume public "subsidised" rates apply to you as a foreigner |
| Use MediSave-linked GPs for cheaper routine visits if eligible | Walk into a private hospital ER without checking your coverage first |
| Compare hospital bill estimates before elective procedures | Assume all hospitals charge similarly — private rates vary hugely |
| Keep detailed receipts for insurance claims and tax purposes | Delay treatment hoping costs will drop — they generally won't |

## Iceland: Slow, Subsidised, and Startlingly Equal

Iceland's healthcare system operates on a principle that would sound almost naive elsewhere: that geography and income shouldn't determine whether you get treated. It's funded overwhelmingly through taxation, and once a patient's out-of-pocket costs in a calendar year cross a defined threshold, further treatment costs drop dramatically or disappear. For a country of this size, that's a genuinely impressive commitment — and it means nobody in Iceland is choosing between rent and a doctor's visit, which is more than can be said for plenty of wealthier nations.

The trade-off is capacity. Iceland simply does not have enough specialists, particularly outside Reykjavík, to match demand for anything beyond urgent or emergency care. Wait times for elective procedures or specialist referrals routinely stretch into months, and Icelanders have developed a very calm, almost Nordic-stoic relationship with this fact — nobody's storming a hospital demanding to be seen faster, because the cultural assumption is that the system is fair even when it's slow. For foreigners used to faster, more transactional healthcare elsewhere, this can be the single hardest adjustment of relocating here: the sinking realisation that money genuinely cannot buy you a faster appointment, because there's no meaningful private alternative to jump the queue.

What Iceland does exceptionally well is emergency and acute care, which is fast, highly competent, and free at the point of use in genuinely urgent situations. The free 1700 health advice line is a small stroke of genius — a phone triage system that keeps people with minor issues out of an already-stretched emergency department, staffed by nurses who will bluntly tell you whether your symptom warrants a hospital trip or a cup of tea and a lie-down.

## Singapore: Excellence, Priced Accordingly

Singapore's healthcare system is often cited as one of the best in the world, and having used it, I understand exactly why — private hospitals here operate with a speed, cleanliness, and hospitality that makes most Western hospitals look under-resourced by comparison. Appointments happen within days, not months. Specialists are plentiful. The infrastructure is immaculate. It genuinely feels less like visiting a hospital and more like checking into a very competent hotel that happens to also perform surgery.

None of this comes cheap, particularly for foreigners. Singaporean citizens and permanent residents benefit from a well-designed system of subsidies, MediSave (a mandatory savings account for medical expenses), and MediShield Life insurance — but expats on employment passes are typically excluded from subsidised public rates and pushed toward private healthcare or employer-provided insurance, where costs can be startling. A routine specialist consultation might run several hundred Singapore dollars; anything involving a hospital stay or surgery can run into tens of thousands, fast, if your insurance coverage has gaps.

The result is a genuinely two-tier medical culture for foreigners: those with strong employer-provided or personal international insurance get access to some of the best, fastest care on the planet, while those who arrive underinsured can find themselves facing bills that would bankrupt them back home. Singapore doesn't hide this reality — it's baked into how the whole system is marketed to expats, with insurance brokers doing brisk business the moment your work visa is approved. Speed and excellence, here, are explicitly things you pay for.

## The Verdict

Iceland wins on fairness — nobody is priced out of care, ever, and the emergency system is genuinely world-class when it matters most. Singapore wins on speed and sheer clinical polish — if you can afford it, or your employer can, you will receive some of the fastest, most comfortable healthcare available anywhere on Earth. If you have a chronic condition requiring frequent specialist attention, get your insurance sorted and head to Singapore. If you're generally healthy and value knowing that the system won't abandon you regardless of your bank balance, Iceland's slow, steady equality is worth the wait — assuming you're not in a hurry.

## Conclusion

Iceland and Singapore represent two entirely coherent, entirely defensible answers to the same impossible question: how should a country decide who gets treated, how fast, and at what cost? Iceland answers with patience and near-universal access. Singapore answers with speed, private polish, and a bill that reflects exactly how good the care was. Neither is a scam. Neither is perfect. But know which system you're stepping into before you need it urgently, because discovering the difference mid-emergency, in either direction, is not a lesson you want to learn the hard way.
