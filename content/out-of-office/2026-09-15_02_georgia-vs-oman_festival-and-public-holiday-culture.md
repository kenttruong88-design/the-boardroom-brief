# Nobody Throws a Party Like a Country That's Been Invaded This Many Times

🇬🇪 Georgia vs 🇴🇲 Oman
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Georgia has been conquered by Persians, Ottomans, Mongols, and Russians, and its response has been to invent roughly nine hundred separate occasions on which it is compulsory to drink wine out of a horn. Oman, meanwhile, ran a maritime empire stretching to Zanzibar, then quietly closed its doors for thirty-odd years, and has since built a holiday calendar around the precise, lunar rhythm of Islam plus a genuinely touching pride in its own sultan.

Both countries call their festivals matters of family, faith, and heritage. Both are lying, slightly. They're also about food, precise social choreography, and the human need for a reason not to be at your desk. I've had enough Christmases in Tbilisi and Eids in Muscat to say which delivers, and I won't take questions from anyone who thinks "exotic" is a compliment.

## Georgia: Do's & Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to receive a toast — standing, listening, drinking, in that order | Clink glasses during a toast to the dead; it's considered bad form |
| Show up to a supra hungry; refusing food is a small social crime | Assume New Year is one day — Georgians run it from December 31 to Orthodox Christmas on January 7 |
| Join the Alilo procession at Christmas if you're in Tbilisi, even as a spectator | Expect much to be open during the ten-day New Year stretch — banks, offices, and patience all close |
| Try the church-specific feast days (Mtskhetoba, Svetitskhovloba) for a look at real local pageantry | Bring up the 2008 war or Abkhazia as small talk at a festival table — read the room |

## Oman: Do's & Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say "Eid Mubarak" to everyone, including your barista, taxi driver, and the man who fixed your aircon | Eat, drink, or smoke visibly in public during Ramadan daylight hours, even if you're not fasting |
| Adjust your entire schedule around Eid — expect government offices shut for several days with little warning of the exact date | Assume the holiday dates are fixed in advance; they follow moon sighting and can shift with days' notice |
| Watch the National Day fireworks and flag displays on November 18 — Omanis take civic pride seriously and warmly | Wear anything skimpy to a public Eid or National Day gathering; dress modestly, full stop |
| Try Khareef season in Salalah if you're anywhere near Dhofar in summer — monsoon fog, waterfalls, and a festival built entirely around weather | Plan a business trip assuming Ramadan is business-as-usual; working hours legally shorten and everything slows |

## Georgia: A Calendar Built on Spite and Wine

Here is the thing about Georgian holidays: there are too many of them, they overlap, and nobody seems bothered. The New Year isn't a night, it's a fortnight-long fugue state stretching from December 31 through Orthodox Christmas on January 7 into Old New Year on January 14, because Georgia hedges between the Julian and Gregorian calendars like a country burned by regime change one too many times. Offices technically reopen in early January. Nobody's heart is in it until after the fourteenth.

Then there's the supra, less a "festival" in the parade-and-fireworks sense and more the operating system underneath every celebration. A supra is a formal feast run by a toastmaster, the tamada, who dictates an escalating sequence of toasts — to peace, ancestors, women, the guests, Georgia itself — each requiring a full glass and genuine eloquence. You do not drink until the tamada toasts. You do not leave food on your plate. The toasting is the event, not the warm-up to it.

Religious festivals lean hard on Georgian Orthodoxy's flair for drama. Svetitskhovloba in October, marking the robe of Christ supposedly buried under Mtskheta's cathedral, draws pilgrims to a town that spends the rest of the year being a pleasant day trip. Alilo, the Christmas Eve procession where children parade through Tbilisi handing out gifts to the poor, is one of the few festivals I've attended that felt unbothered by tourists — it wasn't performed for anyone.

Then, separate from religion, there's Tbilisoba every October: a secular city birthday party with wine stalls, folk dancing, and enough khachapuri to reset your cholesterol. Georgia doesn't do restrained public holidays. It doesn't have the gene for it.

## Oman: Precision, Modesty, and a Sultan Everyone Actually Seems to Like

Oman's holiday calendar runs on the Hijri lunar system, so Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha, and Islamic New Year shift roughly eleven days earlier each Gregorian year and aren't confirmed until moon-sighting committees say so — sometimes with less than 48 hours' notice. If you're used to holidays pinned to a January calendar, this will drive you quietly insane the first year, then become the only scheduling system you trust by year three.

Ramadan is the main event, not a holiday itself but the month that reshapes everything. Government working hours shrink by law. Restaurants close by day and reopen after sunset into something closer to a citywide festival — iftar tents, family gatherings, a nightly reset of energy outsiders consistently underestimate. Eating or drinking visibly in public during fasting hours is not merely rude here, it can be a legal matter, and any expat forum in Muscat will tell you within the first paragraph.

Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha are warm and generous to outsiders in a way that surprises people expecting austerity. Greetings, sweets, and open-house visits are standard, and being a foreigner rarely excludes you from a neighbor's Eid Mubarak. National Day on November 18 is the other pillar — commemorating Oman's founding and the late Sultan Qaboos's birthday — with flag-draped highways, fireworks over Muscat's harbor, and civic affection rare to witness anywhere. Then there's Khareef, the Dhofar monsoon festival each July and August, when Salalah turns fog-cooled greenery into the Gulf's closest thing to a summer resort.

## The Verdict

If you want a festival culture that hands you a script and a moral framework, Oman wins on coherence — everyone knows what Eid means, when Ramadan restraint is expected, how to behave at a National Day gathering. It's dignified, warm once you're inside it, and runs like the sultanate itself: quietly, competently, without fuss.

But if you want a festival culture that feels alive — chaotic, over-long, entirely unconcerned with whether it's convenient for you — Georgia takes it, and it isn't close. A country that dedicates two weeks to New Year and treats dinner as a liturgical event understands something about celebration that efficient nations forget. Oman celebrates beautifully. Georgia celebrates like it's making up for lost time, because, given the history, it is.

## Conclusion

Neither country is performing culture for your benefit, which is why both are worth showing up for properly. Georgia hands you a drinking horn and a toastmaster and expects you to keep up; Oman hands you a plate of dates at sunset and expects you to understand what the silence means. Learn the calendar before you land — one runs on the moon, one runs on spite and stubbornness — or you'll book a flight out during the actual main event and miss the entire point of going. The tourists who call any of this "exotic" can stay home with their all-inclusive itineraries; everyone else gets to enjoy the party.
