# Lagos Will Honk At You. Bamako Will Insult Your Entire Bloodline. Both Mean Well.

🇳🇬 Nigeria vs 🇲🇱 Mali

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular breed of newcomer who arrives in West Africa expecting a single, unified "African" way of queuing, greeting, and generally existing in public. That person is a fool, and I say that with the affection of someone who was once that fool, standing bewildered in a Lagos bank line while a woman physically relocated me by the shoulders to correct my spacing.

Nigeria and Mali sit on the same continent, share a currency corridor's worth of trade, and behave in public like two entirely different species raised by two entirely different grandmothers. One runs on volume, velocity, and the firm belief that personal space is a colonial import nobody asked for. The other runs on ceremony, ritual insult, and a 13th-century social contract that somehow still regulates who's allowed to call whom a bean-eating fool in the middle of a crowded market. I've had both experiences in the same calendar year and I'm still recalibrating.

## Do's and Don'ts: Nigeria 🇳🇬

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget real time for "go-slow" traffic and the queue that isn't really a queue | Take the horn-honking personally; it's a functional language, not road rage |
| Learn the Yoruba or Hausa greeting ritual before you need it for anything important | Expect quiet residential streets; generators and church PA systems are the ambient soundtrack |
| Politely — not aggressively — call out a queue-jumper; everyone will back you up | Assume crowding at an ATM or bus stop means anything is going wrong; it's the default setting |

## Do's and Don'ts: Mali 🇲🇱

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Open every transaction, even a tiny market purchase, with a proper greeting | Rush the bargaining at the Grand Marché; conversation is the point, not an obstacle to it |
| Laugh if a stranger "insults" you using a joking-cousin surname match — it's an honour | Mistake the slower public pace for passivity; it's a civility code, not a lack of urgency |
| Ask a local which "cousin" ethnic group yours jokes with — it's a genuine icebreaker | Interrupt a greeting exchange to get to your actual request; that's the rude part, not the wait |

Nigeria first, because Nigeria does not do waiting its turn, literally or in this article. Lagos runs at a volume and velocity that the Lagos State Environmental Protection Agency has had to legislate against: 65 decibels by day in mixed residential areas, dropping to 55 at night, with officials warning that six hours at 150 decibels causes permanent deafness — numbers that exist precisely because more than 65% of residents are estimated to face harmful daily noise exposure. LASEPA has shut down 30 religious houses, two industries, a television station, and assorted hotels and clubs for violations, and roughly 80% reopened the very next day after paying a fine, which tells you everything about enforcement versus culture. Horn-honking in Lagos traffic isn't aggression; it's communication — a "coming through," a "thank you," a "I see you," deployed roughly every four seconds. Queue-jumping is common enough that Vanguard ran a sincere newspaper column instructing readers to "stay in your line," featuring a writer who jumped a queue abroad using a lifted stanchion rope, only to return home and get stuck obediently queuing at a Lagos bank where the barriers were, infuriatingly, fixed in place. Numbeo's mid-2025 Quality of Life Index ranked Nigeria dead last of 89 countries assessed — worse than Venezuela, worse than Bangladesh — and somehow the culture that emerges from that pressure is not despair but sheer, relentless public-facing hustle. Greetings still matter enormously beneath the chaos: younger Yoruba speakers prostrate or kneel for elders, and a Hausa greeting exchange can run thirty seconds of ritual call-and-response before either party mentions why they're actually talking.

Mali runs at the opposite tempo entirely, and the centrepiece is a genuinely remarkable social technology: cousinage, or sinanguya, a formally UNESCO-recognised joking-relationship system that traces back — by local account — to the 13th-century Kurukan Fuga charter of the Mali Empire. Certain ethnic groups and surnames are paired as "joking cousins": Dogon and Peul, Bambara and Malinké, and most famously the surname pair Coulibaly and Doumbia. In Bamako's markets, a Coulibaly vendor and a Doumbia customer will trade genuine public insults — accusations of excessive bean-stew consumption are a classic opener — as a sanctioned bonding ritual rather than an incident. It is, by design, a conflict-defusion mechanism disguised as banter, and it's considered sacred enough to influence marriage customs between certain paired groups. Beneath that, ordinary public life runs on near-mandatory greeting scripts — a Bambara "I ni ce" and its reply "Nba" open everything, including a two-minute transaction for a single mango — and skipping the greeting to get straight to business reads as a genuine social breach. The Grand Marché's bargaining culture reflects the same unhurried, conversational register: haggling is a relationship, not a race.

Nigeria's noise regime has a specific enforcement culture worth dwelling on, because it's funnier than it sounds on paper: LASEPA's own "No-Horn Day" campaign measurably reduced recorded street noise on the day itself, proving the agency can move the needle when it actually tries — and then every other day of the year, the horn resumes its full duties as Lagos's unofficial second language. The same enforcement sweep that shut down thirty religious houses also closed two industrial sites and a television station for noise violations, which gives you a sense of just how broadly "too loud" gets applied once the agency finally shows up.

Mali's cousinage system runs deeper than market banter, too. The joking-cousin pairings extend beyond the famous Coulibaly-Doumbia match into Dogon-Peul and Bambara-Malinké relationships, and UNESCO's own heritage listing frames the practice as a genuine conflict-resolution mechanism, not mere comic relief — some communities tie it explicitly to blood-type kinship theories and use it to regulate marriage taboos between paired groups. A Bwa man and a Peul man publicly trading jokes about weight and servitude isn't an edge case; it's the system working exactly as eight hundred years of social engineering intended it to.

The contrast, bluntly: Nigeria has built a high-functioning culture around pressure and speed, where public crowding is simply the texture of daily life and the horn is a dialect. Mali has built one around ceremony and sanctioned mockery, where the greeting is the transaction and the insult is the compliment. Neither is "ruder" than the other. Both would find the other's public manners borderline incomprehensible, and I say that having tested both against my own very London instinct to apologise to furniture.

## The Verdict

If you thrive on chaos with a clear internal logic, Lagos will reward you handsomely and deafen you slightly in the process. If you want a public culture where a total stranger insulting your ancestry is a form of welcome, Mali wins, and it isn't close. I'm siding with Mali this round, because any society that formalised "ritual banter as peacekeeping" eight hundred years before Western HR departments invented "radical candour" workshops deserves the trophy outright.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Vanguard Nigeria, reader etiquette column, paraphrased: a Lagos traveller who jumped a queue abroad by lifting the stanchion barrier came home and got stuck queuing properly at a Lagos bank, where the barriers were fixed — "I had sabotaged my image in a foreign land, only to conform by force at home."*

> *Afrique.le360, Bamako market anecdote, paraphrased: a Coulibaly woman and a Doumbia vendor publicly traded insults about who eats more bean stew — entirely good-natured, entirely normal, entirely bewildering to anyone who hasn't been told the rule first.*

> *Expat.com, Ivory Coast regional forum, paraphrased: outsiders are largely forgiven social missteps in West African public life as long as they visibly try to integrate rather than observe from a distance.*

## Conclusion

You will not out-honk Lagos and you should not try. You will not out-insult a Malinké vendor on his home turf and you absolutely should not try that either — you will lose, and everyone around you will enjoy watching it happen. The real skill in both places is the same one Suki has spent fourteen relocations failing to master gracefully: learn the local rhythm fast, stop narrating your own discomfort, and let the greeting — however long, however loud, however personally insulting — do the work it's designed to do.
