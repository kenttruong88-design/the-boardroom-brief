# One Country Treats Hotels as a Quiet Necessity for Business Travellers, the Other Is Building an Entire Tourism Identity Out of Them Overnight

🇰🇼 Kuwait vs 🇸🇦 Saudi Arabia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Kuwait's hotel scene has spent decades quietly serving oil executives, regional business travellers, and the occasional confused tourist who wandered in without a plan, and it shows absolutely no interest in becoming anything more glamorous than that. Saudi Arabia, by contrast, decided a few years ago that it would simply will an entire tourism-grade hospitality sector into existence, and has since been building resorts, giga-projects, and five-star towers at a pace that makes the rest of the Gulf look positively unambitious.

One country isn't trying. The other is trying so hard it's slightly out of breath.

## Do's & Don'ts: Kuwait

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book the established international chains along the Gulf Road for reliable, business-grade comfort | Expect resort culture, beach clubs, or the kind of leisure-focused hospitality Saudi is now chasing |
| Consider serviced apartments for longer stays; they're genuinely well-suited to Kuwait's business-travel rhythm | Assume tourist infrastructure will meet you halfway; Kuwait still isn't really set up for casual visitors |
| Value the discretion and consistency of Kuwaiti hospitality; it's understated but dependable | Expect much innovation or flash; the sector has had little competitive pressure to evolve |
| Use hotel concierge services heavily; they're often the most efficient way to navigate a car-dependent city | Show up without a booking during major regional business events; capacity fills up fast |

## Do's & Don'ts: Saudi Arabia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book giga-project properties (AlUla, the Red Sea developments) well ahead; demand is genuinely outpacing supply | Expect every advertised resort to be fully operational; some marketing runs well ahead of construction |
| Take advantage of the new tourist visa system, which has made independent hotel bookings far more practical | Assume uniform quality; the gap between flagship five-star properties and everyday city hotels is still wide |
| Explore Riyadh and Jeddah's rapidly expanding hotel scenes; both are genuinely transforming year on year | Expect prices to reflect an established market; rapid growth has made pricing volatile and inconsistent |
| Read recent reviews rather than brochures; this market is moving too fast for outdated information to be reliable | Judge the whole country by a single giga-project visit; on-the-ground city hotels tell a very different story |

## Kuwait: A Hospitality Sector That Never Asked to Be Impressive, and Isn't

Kuwait's hotels exist primarily to serve a specific, narrow purpose: housing business travellers, oil-sector executives, and regional visitors efficiently, comfortably, and without any particular ambition beyond that. The Kuwait Central Statistical Bureau's occupancy data reflects a sector built around consistent, predictable business travel rather than leisure tourism, and walking through the international chains lining Gulf Road confirms it instantly — reliable, well-run, entirely unremarkable in the way a good business hotel should be.

What Kuwait does well, it does quietly. Service tends to be genuinely attentive, the major chains maintain consistent standards, and serviced apartments have become a smart, popular option for the many professionals here on medium-term contracts rather than quick visits. The Kuwait Tourism Authority has made some gestures toward developing a broader hospitality identity, but the political and cultural appetite for the kind of leisure-tourism transformation happening next door simply isn't present here in the same way, and Kuwait doesn't seem especially bothered by that.

The honest gap is on the leisure side. There's no real resort culture, minimal beach-club infrastructure, and nothing resembling the giga-project ambition reshaping Saudi Arabia's coastline. If you're coming to Kuwait for a hotel experience built around relaxation and spectacle, you will be disappointed; if you're coming for dependable, professional accommodation that gets out of your way and lets you do business, Kuwait handles that better than almost anywhere in the region, precisely because it isn't distracted by trying to be anything else.

## Saudi Arabia: A Country Building a Five-Star Identity at a Speed That Should Probably Worry Someone

Saudi Arabia's hospitality sector is, right now, one of the most dramatic transformation stories in global tourism, and the Saudi Ministry of Tourism's Vision 2030 figures on giga-project accommodation capacity read less like policy documents and more like science fiction. AlUla's heritage-luxury properties, the Red Sea coastal developments, and Riyadh's rapidly multiplying five-star towers represent an attempt to build, in roughly a decade, the kind of hospitality ecosystem that took other destinations generations to develop organically.

The Saudi Tourism Authority's tourist visa reforms have genuinely opened the country up in a way that would have been unthinkable a decade ago, and independent travellers booking hotels directly, rather than through restrictive business or religious-visa channels, is now a completely normal thing to do. Riyadh and Jeddah's city hotel scenes are transforming visibly year over year, with new international brands arriving at a pace that's hard for even dedicated hospitality journalists to track accurately.

The catch, and it's a real one, is consistency. The flagship giga-projects are extraordinary when they're actually finished and operational, but plenty of marketing material is still describing developments years ahead of their opening dates, and the gap between a glossy AlUla brochure and an average mid-tier city hotel remains genuinely wide. Pricing is volatile, standards vary sharply by property, and anyone booking here should treat recent, specific reviews as far more reliable than the country's frankly relentless promotional output.

## The Verdict

Saudi Arabia wins on ambition, scale, and sheer forward momentum, and if the giga-projects deliver on schedule over the next decade, this won't even be a contest. But Kuwait wins on something quieter and arguably more valuable in the short term: you know exactly what you're getting, every single time, and it's never once let a marketing brochure write a cheque the actual hotel couldn't cash.

Book Saudi Arabia if you want to watch history being built in real time, ideally with a healthy tolerance for inconsistency. Book Kuwait if you just need a reliable room and a hotel that isn't trying to sell you a national transformation narrative along with your key card.

## Conclusion

Kuwait and Saudi Arabia represent two entirely different philosophies of hospitality — one a mature, unglamorous, business-first sector that's stopped pretending to be anything else, the other a country attempting to build an entire tourism identity from scratch at a genuinely startling pace. Book Kuwait for dependability. Book Saudi Arabia for spectacle, provided you're willing to double-check that the spectacle is actually open yet. Neither country is finished becoming what it's going to be, but only one of them seems remotely in a hurry about it.
