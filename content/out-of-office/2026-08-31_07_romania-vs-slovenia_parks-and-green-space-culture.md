# Romania Planted a Forest and Called It a Park. Slovenia Planted a City Inside a Forest. Only One of Them Is Bragging About It Correctly.

🇷🇴 Romania vs 🇸🇮 Slovenia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is a fact that will ruin a dinner party: Romania has more green space per capita in its mountains than almost any country on the continent, and Bucharest still manages to feel like it's rationing shade. Slovenia, a country the size of a regional airport, spent the last decade turning its capital into a municipal brochure for sustainability — and has the gall to be right to do so. I've lived in fourteen cities and watched governments discover "urban greenery" three election cycles too late. Only one of these two finished the thought.

This isn't a contest between pretty trees. It's a contest between a country that inherited extraordinary natural wealth and keeps tripping over its own bureaucracy managing it, and a country that decided green infrastructure was a civic identity rather than a nice-to-have. Pack boots for both. Pack patience for only one.

## Do's & Don'ts: Romania 🇷🇴

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit Cișmigiu at dawn before the joggers and chess hustlers claim every bench | Expect every neighbourhood park to look like the postcard — upkeep varies wildly by district budget |
| Rent a rowboat on Herăstrău Lake and pretend you have somewhere to be | Assume a marked trail in the Carpathians has signage, a refuge, or phone signal past the foothills |
| Ask locals about Parcul IOR before you go — know the recent history | Wander deep into Retezat or the Eastern Carpathians solo without proper gear and a real plan |
| Treat the Botanical Garden as the quiet alternative to the tourist-heavy parks | Be shocked if a "restoration" project runs years over and well past budget |

## Do's & Don'ts: Slovenia 🇸🇮

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Walk or cycle everywhere — Ljubljana's centre is built for it, not just tolerant of it | Expect big-city nightlife energy; the quiet is the point, not a bug |
| Hike the marked national trail network — over 10,000km of alpine paths, properly maintained | Go off-marked-trail in the Alps assuming it's as forgiving as a Sunday stroll in Tivoli |
| Spend a full afternoon in Tivoli Park — it's genuinely five square kilometres of unhurried green | Confuse "green capital" with "low-key" — Ljubljana takes its rules on waste and noise seriously |
| Base yourself outside the centre (Primskovo, for instance) for space without losing access | Underestimate how much of daily social life here happens outdoors, rain or not |

## Romania: Big Parks, Bigger Plot Twists

Bucharest will tell you, correctly, that it has more park space per capita than most European capitals, and on a sunny Sunday in Herăstrău — 188 hectares wrapped around a lake, complete with a Japanese garden, a village museum, and enough rented rowboats to stage a minor regatta — you will believe every word of it. Cișmigiu, the oldest public park in the capital, dating to 1854, is the better argument: shaded paths, a beer garden, chess tables occupied by men who have clearly been playing the same three opponents since the Ceaușescu era, and the kind of unhurried civic life that makes you forgive the city almost everything else.

Then the audit reports arrive, and the forgiveness gets harder. Romania's national auditing office found Bucharest spent roughly 600 million euros between 2007 and 2014 on green-space upkeep — including over 1,000 euros per park bench and 8,200 euros for thirteen exotic-wood rubbish bins — while green space per capita fell from roughly 23 square metres in 2007 to under 10 by 2012. The EU recommended minimum is 26. Land restitution claims, outsourced contracts that ballooned the moment city hall stopped doing the work itself, and a 2021 fire through Parcul IOR have chipped away at a resource that, on paper, should be abundant. This is a country that owns some of the last old-growth forest in Europe and still can't always keep the municipal grass mowed.

The Carpathians redeem a lot of sins. Thirty national parks, genuinely wild stretches in Piatra Craiului and the Bucegi massif, and an access model that's refreshingly honest about its own limits — main roads are fine, side roads are potholed, mountain refuges are sparse, and Retezat in particular has almost no accommodation once you're past the foothills. That's either a thrilling absence of Alpine over-tourism or a logistical headache depending on your temperament. I found it thrilling. I also made sure someone knew where I was.

## Slovenia: The Country That Actually Read Its Own Press Release

Ljubljana won European Green Capital in 2016 and has spent every year since behaving as though it's still being graded. Three-quarters of the city's territory is green space. The centre is pedestrianised to a degree that makes most European capitals look combustion-engine-drunk by comparison. Tivoli Park alone runs five square kilometres from the city centre into forested hills, laced with Jože Plečnik's design touches and busy with everyone from students to pensioners doing the exact same unhurried lap I watched in Bucharest — except here the benches weren't gold-plated and the grass was actually there.

What elevates this past "nice park" territory is that the greenness isn't cosmetic. The city has planted thousands of trees, built five new parks, revitalised the Sava embankments, and earned repeat "Tree City of the World" recognition since 2019 — not for one flagship project but for sustained, unglamorous follow-through, the rarest commodity in urban planning.

Outside the capital, Slovenes barely distinguish between "park" and "country." Over 5,000 kilometres of marked walkways and 10,000 kilometres of alpine trails blanket a nation a third of which is formally protected, and hiking isn't a hobby here so much as a cross-generational reflex — closer to a national personality trait than a leisure activity. The trade-off is scale: Ljubljana is Slovenia's largest city and still doesn't feel like one. If you need big-metropolis noise to feel alive, the quiet will eventually needle you. If you don't, you'll wonder why every other capital insists on being this exhausting.

## The Verdict

Romania has the raw material to out-green Slovenia twice over and keeps finding new ways to mismanage the paperwork. Slovenia has a fraction of the land and somehow turned "we have trees" into a globally cited urban planning case study. This isn't close. Bucharest's parks are genuinely lovely in the moment and genuinely precarious in the budget line; Slovenia's are lovely in the moment and backed by a decade of boring, competent governance that nobody writes travel copy about because competence doesn't photograph well. I'd rather live inside the boring competence. Romania wins on drama and scenery. Slovenia wins on the thing that actually matters once you've unpacked your boxes: whether the green space you fell for will still be there, funded and mowed, in five years.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — Several answers on Bucharest note that the park experience depends heavily on which district you're in, with some green spaces well-kept and others quietly neglected for years before anyone notices.*

> *Expat.com Bucharest forum threads — Long-term residents mention that the showcase parks get the attention and budget, while smaller neighbourhood green spaces are often left to fend for themselves.*

> *Quora — Multiple respondents describing life in Slovenia emphasize how quiet Ljubljana feels compared to other European capitals, warning newcomers expecting capital-city buzz that the pace here is deliberately, permanently slower.*

## Conclusion

Neither country needs your pity or your performative astonishment — green space in Eastern and Central Europe is not a discovery, it's a fact you're late to. Romania will hand you a forest the size of a small nation and then lose the receipt for a park bench. Slovenia will hand you a five-square-kilometre park inside its own capital and a decade of paperwork to prove it'll still be there next year. Go to Romania for the scenery that makes you feel small in the best way. Move to Slovenia if you want your grandchildren's grandchildren to enjoy the same view without a municipal audit explaining why it's gone. Pack boots either way. Pack low expectations only for the Bucharest city council.
