# One Country Ghosts You Politely, the Other One Introduces You to Its Entire Family by Date Two

🇵🇹 Portugal · 🇰🇪 Kenya
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Portugal will date you the way it does everything else: beautifully, quietly, and with absolutely no urgency whatsoever. Kenya will date you the way it does everything else too: with warmth so immediate it's almost alarming, and an audience. I have been "seeing someone" in Lisbon for six lightly-defined weeks and I still don't know if we're official. I have been on exactly one date in Nairobi and met four cousins, a auntie, and a dog named after a former president. Neither country will apologise for its approach. Neither should.

The dating culture gap here isn't about romance so much as pacing — Portugal treats a relationship like a bottle of good vinho verde, meant to be opened slowly and never rushed, while Kenya treats it like an invitation into an entire social ecosystem you didn't know you were applying to join. One of these will leave you wondering if you're dating at all. The other will leave you wondering whether you've accidentally gotten engaged.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Portugal**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Let things develop slowly — modesty and subtlety are the local love language, not big gestures | Don't expect a label on the relationship within the first month; ambiguity is a feature |
| Expect to meet people through friend networks and existing social circles more than apps | Don't treat Tinder like it's socially neutral — there's still a quiet stigma around it outside Lisbon |
| Accept that the expat dating pool is transient — people arrive, date, and leave within months | Don't assume every warm conversation in a smaller town is romantic interest; you're still the new outsider |

**Kenya**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Be punctual and mind your personal hygiene — both are read as basic respect, not extra effort | Don't engage in public displays of affection; it reads as genuinely impolite, not charmingly European |
| Expect to be introduced to family and friends early if things are going well | Don't assume splitting the bill is standard everywhere — Westlands' app-dating crowd does it, older norms don't |
| Learn that directness is fine but bluntness lands as harsh — soften the delivery | Don't assume Kenya's 42-plus ethnic communities share one dating rulebook; ask, don't generalise |

## Portugal: Romance on a Very Long Fuse

Portugal does not have a dating culture in the Anglo, app-swipe, define-the-relationship-by-Thursday sense. It has a social culture that occasionally produces romance as a byproduct, which is an entirely different animal and one that will drive impatient foreigners quietly insane. Most adults meet partners through work, through friends, through the same overlapping social circles they've had since university — not through the algorithmic cattle call the rest of the dating world has resigned itself to.

Dates, when they happen, are lighthearted, unhurried, and — compared to the noisier, more performative dating cultures of Southern Europe's reputation — genuinely tepid. Portuguese men and women value modesty and subtlety; grand romantic gestures are not just uncommon, they're faintly suspicious, the kind of thing that makes people wonder what you're overcompensating for. If you were raised on a diet of romantic comedies, recalibrate immediately. Nobody is running through an airport for you here. They might, eventually, invite you for a coffee that becomes a habit that becomes something you only notice has become serious once you're already introducing each other to friends.

Apps exist — Tinder, Bumble, all the usual suspects — and they're used, particularly by younger people and by the substantial international community clustered around Lisbon and the Algarve. But there's more residual stigma attached to them than you'd expect from a country this modern, a lingering sense that "real" dating still happens through people you already know.

And then there's the expat layer, which operates on entirely different physics. Lisbon's international scene is transient by design: people arrive full of energy, date enthusiastically for a few months, and leave, restarting the whole cycle for whoever's left behind. It's an open, experimental fusion of local reserve and expat urgency that makes for genuinely fascinating chemistry — right up until someone's visa runs out and the whole thing evaporates. Smaller towns offer none of this. There, everyone already knows everyone, the social web predates you by decades, and you will spend a very long time on the outside of it, however charming you are.

## Kenya: Dating as a Group Project

Nairobi's dating scene concentrates almost entirely in Westlands, where the city's young professionals and expats have built something that looks, superficially, like dating culture anywhere else — Hinge and Bumble, first dates at The Alchemist Bar or Mercury Lounge, bills increasingly split rather than assumed. Scratch the surface and it's operating on a completely different social contract than the swipe-and-ghost model most of the dating world has settled into.

What's genuinely different is the speed of integration. Where Portugal keeps you circling the outer edge of someone's life for months, Kenya pulls you into the centre of it fast — meet the friends, meet the family, meet, disconcertingly, the whole extended structure of who this person actually is, often before you've worked out if you even like their taste in music. It's not overwhelming because Kenyans are careless with intimacy; it's because relationships here are understood as inherently social rather than private, something a whole network participates in rather than something two people quietly work out alone.

There are real cultural landmines for the unprepared. Public displays of affection, so casually normalised across much of the West, are genuinely considered impolite here — hand-holding, certainly nothing more, in public. Punctuality and basic hygiene aren't romantic bonus points, they're the baseline expectation, and turning up late or dishevelled reads as active disrespect rather than endearing chaos. Gender dynamics still lean traditional in plenty of circles — men are often expected to initiate — though the city's younger, more progressive crowd increasingly ignores that script entirely, especially among the professionals filling up Westlands on a Friday night.

And critically: Kenya is not one culture. It's home to more than forty distinct ethnic communities, each with genuinely different courtship norms, family expectations, and social rules, all coexisting under one very loose umbrella called "Kenyan dating culture" that barely holds together as a useful phrase. Directness is welcomed; bluntness is not. The distinction matters more than most foreigners bother to learn.

## The Verdict

Portugal will let you take your time and mostly leave you to it, which suits the emotionally cautious and infuriates everyone else. Kenya will fold you into an entire social structure before you've finished your first drink, which suits the emotionally generous and terrifies everyone else. If you want ambiguity with excellent lighting, book Lisbon. If you want to meet somebody's whole family by date two and genuinely mean it, book Nairobi. I'll take the family. At least you know where you stand — which, notably, is the one thing six weeks in Lisbon never quite gave me.

## Conclusion

Neither country is wrong, exactly, though both would find the other's approach faintly bewildering — Portugal would find Kenya's pace presumptuous, Kenya would find Portugal's pace evasive, and I have sympathy for both positions after living through them. What's true everywhere is that dating abroad means dating the culture as much as the person, and the sooner you stop applying your home country's rulebook, the sooner you stop being quietly, avoidably miserable about it. Learn the pace. Learn the etiquette. And for the love of everything, don't try public affection in Nairobi or a defined relationship timeline in Lisbon. Both will end badly, and both will be entirely your own fault.
