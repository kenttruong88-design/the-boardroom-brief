# Ghana Will Give You a Bank Account If You Know a Guy. Gabon Will Give You an ATM That Charges You for the Privilege of Failing.

🇬🇭 Ghana vs 🇬🇦 Gabon
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country has an official version of how its bureaucracy works and an unofficial version of how it actually works, and the gap between the two is usually where you learn the most about a place. In Ghana, the official rule is that you cannot open a bank account without a resident's permit; the actual rule, confirmed by a decade of expat forum posts, is "find someone to introduce you to the branch manager." In Gabon, the official rule is that starting a business takes a brisk ten days; the actual rule is that nobody warned you registering a property afterward would take seventy-two.

Both countries will test your patience. Only one of them will let a friend's cousin solve the problem for you over coffee.

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Find an existing customer to personally introduce you to a branch manager — it works better than any official document | Assume "no bank account" means "no financial life" — mobile money (MoMo) has nearly 18 million active users and runs much of the real economy |
| Get your Ghana Card sorted even if you're a non-resident director — it's now effectively mandatory for signatories on a corporate account | Expect foreign-currency invoicing to be casual — only registered expatriates/non-residents can be invoiced in dollars, and proceeds must land in a specific Foreign Exchange Account |
| Budget for ATM fees around 15 cedis (roughly $3.40) per withdrawal | Trust the cedi to hold steady — it has halved against the dollar over five years, by more than one forum poster's account |

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇬🇦 Gabon

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry cash, always — card acceptance is limited to a handful of large grocery chains even in Libreville | Expect your own postal service — outbound diplomatic mail runs on an actual courier "pouch" because the post office is that threadbare |
| Budget up to $15 per ATM withdrawal, and expect the machine to simply not have cash some days | Assume a fast business registration means a fast anything-else — property registration alone takes 72 days and six procedures |
| Celebrate quietly if your building gets an ATM nearby — one diplomatic report treated "there's an ATM at the Embassy now" as genuine infrastructure news | Expect banking outside Libreville, Port-Gentil or Franceville — the other seven-tenths of the population operates largely outside the formal system |

## Ghana: The Rulebook Is a Suggestion

Ghana's banking bureaucracy has recently gotten stricter on paper and remains, delightfully, as flexible as ever in practice. As of a Bank of Ghana directive effective late 2025, the national Ghana Card is now functionally mandatory for every banking relationship — including, for the first time, foreign company directors and shareholders who may never set foot in the country but now need biometric Ghanaian ID just to stay a signatory on a corporate account. Miss the registration window and new accounts face a 90-day deferral before they're even operational. There are narrow carve-outs — diplomats can use diplomatic credentials, short-stay non-residents under 90 days doing one-off transfers can get by on a passport — but the headline is more paperwork, chasing people who aren't even in the room.

And yet. Spend twenty minutes on the expat.com Ghana forum and you'll find the lived reality cheerfully contradicting the official line. One long-term resident states flatly that "in theory you can't open a bank account in Ghana without a resident's permit" — and then describes how his spouse opened one anyway, simply by walking in with the right introduction. Another poster, on a different thread, opened an account with no residence or work permit at all, purely via a personal referral from an existing customer to a bank manager. The actual rule is not written down anywhere; it's "ask that specific branch, bring an introducer," and outcomes vary bank to bank, sometimes branch to branch within the same bank.

Underneath all of this, mobile money has essentially solved the problem the formal banking system keeps bureaucratizing. MTN's MoMo service alone had roughly 17.7 million active users by late 2025, adding 700,000 more in a year, now representing a quarter of MTN's total service revenue. A newcomer expecting "unbanked" to mean "cash-only chaos" instead finds a fully functioning digital economy running on phone numbers rather than account numbers — which makes the Ghana Card mandate feel almost quaint, a 20th-century solution bolted onto a population that has already moved on.

## Gabon: Everything Works, Slowly, in Cash

Gabon's problem isn't informality — if anything, its banking sector is unusually formalized for the region, with a 30% banking penetration rate roughly three times the African average. It's scale and friction. Just seven commercial banks serve the entire country, regulated through the regional COBAC body headquartered right there in Libreville, with foreign-exchange policy set not by Gabon alone but by the shared CEMAC/BEAC zone covering six Central African states. Banking presence clusters almost entirely in three cities — Libreville, Port-Gentil, Franceville — leaving everywhere else to mobile and microfinance to cover the gap.

The World Bank's last full Doing Business assessment ranked Gabon 169th globally, and the detail behind that number is the real story: starting a business is genuinely fast on paper, ten days and seven procedures, but almost everything downstream collapses that head start. Registering property ranks 171st worldwide and takes 72 days across six procedures; paying taxes ranks 182nd; enforcing a contract, 180th. The bottleneck isn't the step everyone warns you about — it's the one three steps later that nobody mentioned.

Day to day, multiple diplomatic post-reports spanning 2011 to 2020 describe Libreville in nearly identical language: a straightforward cash economy, card acceptance limited to a few large grocery chains, ATMs that "run dry" or charge up to $15 per withdrawal when they work at all. One 2020 report treated the recent arrival of a working ATM at the embassy as genuinely noteworthy infrastructure news — not a joke, an actual quality-of-life upgrade worth documenting. Outbound mail runs on a literal courier "pouch" system because the postal service is that unreliable; one respondent wasn't even sure a functioning post office existed in the capital. And when a Talesmag respondent was asked point-blank whether you could save money living in Gabon, the answer recorded was a single word: "No."

## The Verdict

Ghana's bureaucracy is annoying but navigable — there is always a person, a branch, an introduction that makes the official rule bend. Gabon's bureaucracy isn't even the main obstacle; the obstacle is that the whole infrastructure around banking — the ATMs, the mail, the card readers — simply doesn't scale to meet demand, no matter how compliant you are. I'll take Ghana's "find a guy" workaround over Gabon's "bring cash and lower your expectations" every time. At least in Accra, charm is a legitimate financial instrument.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com forum, Ghana — paraphrased: a poster notes the cedi "has been falling steadily," having halved against the dollar over five years, while banks outside Ghana "charge like wounded bulls" on international transfers.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Libreville (2012) — paraphrased: a diplomatic respondent calls Libreville "a cash society," notes ATMs only work "when they work," and answers a direct question about saving money with a flat "No."*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Libreville (2020) — paraphrased: a respondent describes outbound mail as "pouch-only, extremely limited," and calls the newly working embassy ATM something that "helps significantly."*

## Conclusion

What both countries teach you, in opposite ways, is that a banking system's official rulebook tells you almost nothing about what your actual week will look like. Ghana's rulebook is strict and its practice is loose; Gabon's practice is exactly as strict as its rulebook suggests, just slower everywhere the rulebook doesn't mention. Bring an introduction for Accra. Bring cash, patience, and realistic expectations for Libreville. And in both places, budget for the ATM fee — it is, unfortunately, the one line item that never, ever surprises you by being lower than expected.
