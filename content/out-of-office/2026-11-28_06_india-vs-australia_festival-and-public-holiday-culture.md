# India Throws a Festival for Every Mood, Australia Throws a Barbecue for Every Date

🇮🇳 India vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

India does not have a calendar so much as a rolling carnival with occasional office hours. Diwali, Holi, Eid, Durga Puja, Pongal, Onam, Ganesh Chaturthi and a hundred regional celebrations come around with such regularity that a foreigner can lose track of whether it is a working Tuesday or a festival of colour. Australia, by contrast, has a very tidy list of public holidays and a very firm belief that each of them should involve meat.

Moving between the two is a lesson in what a nation chooses to celebrate and how loudly it does so. One country celebrates what it believes. The other celebrates that it has a day off.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇮🇳 India ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept an invitation to a festival meal, even from near strangers | Refuse sweets with a flat "no" |
| Wear modest, colourful clothes for temple visits and family events | Show up in beachwear at a religious celebration |
| Check the regional calendar, since holidays differ by state | Assume a festival is observed the same way everywhere |
| Bring a small gift such as sweets or dried fruit when visiting | Arrive empty-handed at a family home |

| 🇦🇺 Australia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Observe a minute of respect on Anzac Day mornings | Treat the dawn service as a photo opportunity |
| Plan ahead for long weekends, when travel prices climb | Book a flight on the Friday afternoon of a holiday |
| Bring a plate or a drink to a community barbecue | Show up with nothing and expect to be fed |
| Check local rules, since some holidays are state-specific | Assume Melbourne Cup Day is a national day off |

## 🇮🇳 India

A festival in India is not a holiday you observe. It is a force that happens to you. The streets fill with decorations, the markets sell sweets by the kilo, relatives travel across the country, and the whole neighbourhood seems to agree that nothing will be achieved before it is over.

Diwali is the great one. The festival of lights lasts several days, with oil lamps, fireworks, rangoli patterns on doorsteps, new clothes and an unreasonable quantity of sweets. Families clean their homes, exchange gifts and visit each other, and shops treat the season as the commercial climax of the year. It is hard to be indifferent to a celebration in which a whole country lights candles and then tries to outdo the neighbours.

Holi, the festival of colour, is the other famous one, and the one visitors tend to find most difficult. The pigment, the water balloons and the sheer enthusiasm are glorious and not to be undertaken in good clothes. Beyond the headline events, there is a regional layer: Pongal in the south, Durga Puja in Bengal, Onam in Kerala, Baisakhi in Punjab, Eid and Christmas observed with equal gusto in their own communities. India's public holiday list is long, and it still understates how many days are, in practice, festivals.

For an expat, the joy is how inclusive it feels. Neighbours will hand you sweets, invite you to their celebrations and explain what everything means without being asked. The challenge is logistical. Offices close, shops shut, travel is chaotic, and the date moves because the calendar follows the moon.

The wise newcomer learns to plan around the festival season, and to keep a spare stomach for the sweets.

Food is the thread that runs through every Indian festival, and the rules are as precise as any legal code. Diwali means mithai, Holi means gujiya, Eid means biryani and sheer khurma, Pongal means the sweet rice that boils over on purpose. A guest who comes hungry will leave overwhelmed, and a host who offers a third helping is not asking but informing you.

## 🇦🇺 Australia

Australia's public holiday calendar is shorter and more predictable. Australia Day, Anzac Day, Easter, Christmas, Boxing Day and the King's Birthday, with additional days that vary by state, make up the bulk of it. The result is a year with the occasional long weekend and a strong sense of how to spend one: outside, with friends, in the sun, near a grill.

Anzac Day is the most solemn. Dawn services and marches remember those who served, and the day carries a weight that surprises many visitors who expected only a barbecue. It is a reminder that Australian public culture, for all its casual surface, holds some things very seriously.

Australia Day is more complicated. For many people it is a day of beach parties and flags, and for many others, especially in Indigenous communities, it is a day of mourning and protest. A newcomer is wise to listen before speaking. Elsewhere in the year, Melbourne Cup Day stops a city for a horse race, Christmas arrives in summer with a seafood lunch, and the Easter long weekend is mainly an excuse to leave town.

The country's multicultural character has changed the picture. Diwali, Lunar New Year, Eid and other festivals are widely celebrated in cities, with public events, lights on civic buildings and local council support. There is an ongoing public debate about whether more of these should be recognised as official holidays, and Indian community groups have campaigned for Diwali in particular.

For an expat, the lesson is that public life is orderly, and that your own festivals will be welcome but not automatically a day off. You may need to take annual leave to celebrate Diwali, then explain to a colleague that the lights are not for the neighbours' benefit.

Australian celebrations are no less food-led, only simpler. Anzac biscuits, meat pies, lamingtons, pavlova and the sausage sizzle outside the hardware store have all attained quasi-sacred status, and few events are considered successful without one. The Indian-Australian community has added its own layer, so that a Diwali fair in a suburban park may sell samosas next to snags in bread, and nobody sees a contradiction.

## The Verdict

India wins on abundance and Australia wins on organisation, and the gap between them is the gap between a feast and a timetable. If you want a calendar that surprises you every month with colour, food and noise, India has no rival. If you want a predictable list of days off, clear rules and a sense that a holiday means a holiday, Australia delivers.

Personally, I would take the Indian version, because a festival you did not plan is more memorable than a long weekend you did. But I would not want to administer it, and I would not want to be the one trying to book a train the week before Diwali.

The sting is that both countries think they invented hospitality. India offers it in the form of a plate pressed into your hands. Australia offers it in the form of a cold drink and the phrase "help yourself". Both are sincere, and only one of them will make you feel you owe an apology for leaving early.

If you move between them, bring an open mind and a flexible diary. You will be invited to more than you can attend, and you will learn that saying yes to the second helping is the correct answer in both countries.

One practical point for the expat calendar: because many Indian festivals follow the lunar calendar, their dates shift each year, and Australian employers rarely know them. Giving your manager a few weeks' notice, and a small box of sweets, is usually enough to make leave a formality.

## Conclusion

A country's holidays are its autobiography. India writes its own in sweets, colour and fire, a story with too many chapters and no apology. Australia writes its in remembrance, sport and the beach, shorter and a good deal more orderly.

My advice is to take part, with respect, in whatever is going on. Learn the story behind the day, accept the plate and ask what you are eating. Nobody will mind that you do not know the rules, provided you try.

And when you are in Sydney during Diwali, and the neighbour asks why you are lighting a lamp on a Tuesday evening, tell them. They will almost certainly bring a plate.
