# Two Countries Perfected the Art of Making You Beg for a Piece of Paper

🇫🇷🇧🇪 By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office

France and Belgium share a border, a language in half the country, and an near-religious devotion to the proposition that nothing important should ever be easy. I have opened bank accounts on four continents. Only these two made me feel, at various points, like I was auditioning for a role I hadn't been told I was up for.

The French will tell you their bureaucracy is a reflection of the Republic's grand commitment to order and equality. The Belgians will tell you nothing at all, because the relevant civil servant is at lunch, and lunch in Belgium is a constitutionally protected two hours. Both systems produce the same result: you, standing in a corridor, clutching a folder of documents that was correct yesterday and is now, inexplicably, wrong.

## Do's & Don'ts: France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring three copies of every document, always, forever | Assume one utility bill will ever be "recent enough" |
| Get an attestation d'hébergement if you don't have a lease yet | Expect a bank to open an account without a French address first |
| Learn the phrase "j'ai rendez-vous" and say it with total confidence | Walk in without an appointment and expect anything but a leaflet |
| Use a courtier if your visa status is remotely unusual | Trust that the rules haven't changed since your last visit |

## Do's & Don'ts: Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register at your commune within eight working days of arrival | Skip the commune registration — everything else depends on it |
| Bring a Belgian phone number to your bank appointment | Expect the appointment to run on your schedule, or any schedule |
| Keep every stamped document; you'll need it again, differently | Assume Flanders and Wallonia process anything identically |
| Budget two to three months for a full residency-to-banking pipeline | Believe "next week" means anything close to next week |

## France: The Chicken, the Egg, and the Attestation

France's central trick is circular logic dressed up as due diligence. To open a bank account, you need a fixed address. To sign a lease, many landlords want proof of a French bank account, or at minimum a French guarantor with one. To get the guarantor situation sorted, you often need — you'll never guess — paperwork that assumes you already have a bank account. This is not a bug. French institutions have simply decided that everyone arrives with a network of family, notaries, and inherited property, and if you don't, that's a personality flaw you can address by suffering.

The workaround, which every long-term expat learns within the first excruciating month, is to open a "compte non-résident" first, or to lean on an attestation d'hébergement from whoever's sofa you're currently occupying. Then you queue. The French bank appointment is a genre of theatre: you arrive on time, the advisor arrives twelve minutes later with the specific weariness of someone who has explained the Livret A savings account four times already that morning, and you leave having handed over your passport, your rental contract, your birth certificate (occasionally with an official translation, occasionally not, and nobody will tell you which in advance), and a utility bill that must be dated within three months — not two, not four, three, and God help you if it's electronic and unstamped.

What redeems it, slightly, is that once you're in, you're properly in. The French system, for all its front-loaded cruelty, produces genuinely functional universal services on the other side — healthcare access, tenant protections, a Livret A account that will not charge you a single fee for the rest of your natural life. It's a nation that makes you climb an enormous, poorly-lit staircase to reach a room that, once you're inside it, is actually rather nice. The stairs, though. The stairs are non-negotiable, and there are more of them than the brochure implies.

## Belgium: Nineteen Communes, Zero Consensus

Belgium does not have a bureaucracy so much as it has several bureaucracies wearing a trench coat and calling itself one country. Brussels alone is carved into nineteen communes, each with its own registration office, its own appointment system, its own interpretation of federal rules that are, on paper, identical across all of them. Move from Ixelles to Schaerbeek and you will discover that the rules you learned no longer apply, because apparently nobody told Schaerbeek.

The opening move for any Belgian resident is commune registration — a home visit from a police officer to confirm you actually live where you say you live, followed by an appointment to collect your residency card, followed by the part nobody warns you about: none of your other paperwork, including your bank account, moves forward until this is stamped, sealed, and logged in the national registry, which can take anywhere from two weeks to what feels like a geological era depending entirely on which of the nineteen fiefdoms you've landed in.

Once registered, Belgian banking is almost suspiciously pleasant by comparison — efficient digital onboarding, decent English-language support in Brussels, and banks that have clearly given up pretending you'll ever master French, Dutch, and the correct commune procedure simultaneously. The maddening part is the sequencing: everything is downstream of that one police visit, and the police visit is downstream of a scheduling system that appears to run on goodwill and the phases of the moon. Belgium isn't cruel. It's just disorganised at a scale that starts to resemble cruelty if you're the one waiting.

The Verdict

If I'm choosing which bureaucracy gets to ruin my week, I'm picking Belgium, and it isn't close. French bureaucracy is a deliberate, load-bearing act of institutional suspicion — it assumes the worst about you and makes you prove otherwise, repeatedly, in triplicate. Belgian bureaucracy is chaos, but it's honest chaos: nobody designed the nineteen-commune farce to punish you specifically, it's just what happens when a small country divides itself with more enthusiasm than logistics. France makes you feel judged. Belgium makes you feel forgotten. I'll take being forgotten. At least in Belgium you can console yourself with a beer selection that makes the wait almost, almost, worth it. France gives you wine you can't afford yet and a bank advisor who still hasn't called you back about the Livret A.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/expats — paraphrased: opened a French account only after a friend let them use their address for the attestation, then discovered the bank wanted a second utility bill in their own name within a month anyway.*

> *Internations Brussels — paraphrased: the commune appointment came through in ten days, but the residency card itself took another six weeks, during which their bank account sat "provisionally" open and unusable for transfers.*

> *expat.com — paraphrased: moved communes mid-process in Brussels and had to restart registration entirely, because the new commune didn't trust the old commune's paperwork.*

## Conclusion

Neither country will apologise, and neither should have to — this is simply how they've always done it, and both nations regard the resulting inconvenience as a small, acceptable price for order (France) or as somebody else's problem (Belgium). What you can control is your own preparation: over-document for France until it borders on parody, and for Belgium, treat the commune visit as the single hinge on which your entire administrative life swings, and guard that appointment like it owes you money. Neither will thank you for the effort. Bureaucracies never do. But you'll get your account, your card, and eventually your dignity back — bruised, slightly damp from the queueing, but back.
