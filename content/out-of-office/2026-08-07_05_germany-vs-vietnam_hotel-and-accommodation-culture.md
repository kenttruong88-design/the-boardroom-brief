# The Form and the Family: Two Nations' Wildly Different Ways of Handing You a Key

🇩🇪 Germany vs 🇻🇳 Vietnam — Hotel and Accommodation Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Checking into a German hotel feels like being processed. Checking into a Vietnamese guesthouse feels like being adopted. Both experiences get you a bed and a lockable door. Only one of them ends with someone's grandmother bringing you tea you didn't order and refusing payment for it.

Germany's accommodation culture is built on the Meldeschein, a registration form so thorough it makes airport immigration feel improvisational, and on a broader national instinct that hospitality is a transaction best conducted with maximum documentation and minimum small talk. Vietnam's is built on family-run guesthouses where the front desk is also the living room, the owner's children do homework behind the counter, and the "check-in process" often consists of someone examining your passport for exactly four seconds before deciding you seem fine.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry your passport or ID to every check-in; the Meldeschein is a legal requirement, not a formality | Expect to skip registration paperwork even for a one-night stay |
| Arrive within stated check-in windows; German hotels take posted hours seriously | Show up hours early expecting flexibility — rooms are rarely ready ahead of schedule |
| Expect spotless, efficient rooms with minimal frills | Expect effusive friendliness; professional courtesy is the norm, not warmth |
| Settle your bill precisely; itemised charges are exact and expected to be checked | Assume tipping housekeeping is mandatory — it's appreciated but not obligatory |

### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate rates directly, especially for longer stays at family-run guesthouses | Assume listed prices are fixed; there is almost always room to talk |
| Accept offered tea, fruit, or advice from the owners — it's genuine hospitality, not a sales pitch | Rush past the front-desk conversation; it's often how you learn the best local food nearby |
| Keep a photocopy of your passport handy; some guesthouses hold the original briefly for registration | Panic if your passport is taken for an hour — it's standard local registration practice |
| Expect variability in quality even within the same star rating | Book purely on star rating; read recent reviews, standards swing wildly between properties |

## Germany: Hospitality by the Book

German hotel check-in is less a welcome than an audit. The Meldeschein — the mandatory guest registration form required by law at nearly every hotel, pension and guesthouse — asks for full name, nationality, passport number, and duration of stay, and staff will not hand over a key until it's completed correctly. This isn't unique to budget properties; five-star hotels enforce it with the same bureaucratic rigour as a youth hostel. It is, in classic German fashion, entirely explicable, entirely necessary by law, and entirely joyless in execution.

What Germany delivers in exchange is reliability verging on the metronomic. Rooms are clean to a standard you could set a watch by. Check-in and check-out times are respected on both sides — arrive at 3 p.m. and your room will be ready; leave by 11 a.m. and nobody will hunt you down. Staff are professional, competent, and almost entirely uninterested in becoming your friend, which is its own kind of comfort if you simply want a bed and a shower without emotional labour attached.

Warmth is not the product. Precision is. If your radiator breaks, it will be fixed promptly and without excuses. If you want someone to ask about your day, you will need to find that elsewhere.

## Vietnam: Hospitality as Relationship

Vietnamese guesthouse culture, especially outside the largest international chains, operates on an entirely different logic: the property is often a family home first and a business second, and you are, for the duration of your stay, treated somewhere between guest and long-lost relative. Check-in can be almost comically informal — a quick passport glance, a room key, and within minutes you'll likely be handed a cup of tea and a recommendation for where to get the best banh mi within walking distance, unprompted and free of charge.

Rates are rarely fixed in stone, particularly for longer stays; a polite, friendly negotiation is expected and often welcomed rather than resented. The owner's actual life happens around you — children doing homework at the front desk, meals being prepared in a kitchen you can see and sometimes smell, a grandmother watching a soap opera in the corner. It can feel intrusive if you're used to hotel anonymity, or it can feel like the single best part of travelling in Vietnam, depending entirely on your appetite for closeness with strangers who quickly stop being strangers.

Quality swings widely. A guesthouse rated the same as its neighbour can be immaculate or merely adequate, and star ratings mean considerably less here than recent reviews and word of mouth.

## The Verdict

Germany gives you certainty: what you book is precisely what you get, on time, to standard, no surprises, no warmth either. Vietnam gives you unpredictability wrapped in genuine kindness: you might get a slightly tired room and the best conversation of your trip, free tea included. If you want your accommodation to function like a well-oiled machine, book Germany. If you want it to feel like you've briefly joined someone's family, book Vietnam, and don't be surprised if you're invited back for Tet.

## Conclusion

Germany will never lose your booking, overcharge you, or surprise you with anything other than a perfectly itemised bill. Vietnam will never let you feel like a transaction, even when you technically are one. Choose Germany if you want your accommodation to behave like a well-run institution. Choose Vietnam if you want it to behave like somebody's home that happens to have a spare room. I know which one remembers my name.
