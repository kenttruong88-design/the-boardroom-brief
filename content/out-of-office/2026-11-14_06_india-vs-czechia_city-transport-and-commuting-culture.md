# Mumbai Commutes Like It's an Endurance Sport. Prague Commutes Like It's a Spreadsheet

🇮🇳 India vs 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two ways to get to work in a major city. You can fight for a place in a system that was never designed for the crowds it carries, or you can sit down in a clean tram that arrives when the app says it will. I have done both, and I want to be clear that the second one feels like a scam. It is too easy. Somebody must be paying for this.

India and Czechia are about as far apart on the commuting spectrum as two countries can get. One is a heroic, improvised, deeply human transport culture that works in spite of itself. The other is a Central European machine that works because it was told to. The interesting question is not which is better. It is what each one asks of you.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Travel off-peak if you value your ribs | Board a packed train carrying a large bag |
| Use ride-hailing apps and local trains for different needs | Assume one mode will do everything |
| Learn the rhythm of your route before judging it | Compare it to a European timetable |
| Keep your belongings close and your wallet closer | Fall asleep with a phone in your hand |

**Czechia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Validate your ticket at the start of the journey | Board and hope nobody checks |
| Buy tickets through the PID Lítačka app, which is cheaper | Queue for a paper ticket |
| Know the metro stops at midnight and night trams take over | Plan a late metro ride home |
| Keep your ticket until you leave the station | Throw it away on arrival |

## India

I should be clear that I did not find a source for this half of the comparison, so what follows is opinion built on the general shape of Indian urban transport, which is well known to anyone who has used it. The defining feature is scale. Indian cities move enormous numbers of people with systems that were built for a fraction of them, and the result is a culture of improvisation, tolerance and physical negotiation that Europeans find astonishing.

The local train is the headline example. In a city like Mumbai, commuters learn to board a moving carriage, to shift without a word, to stand in a space that does not appear to exist. It is not chaos. It is collective, practised and surprisingly courteous in its own way, with strangers making room and passing along bags. There is a social contract written entirely in elbows and eye contact.

Alongside it you have auto-rickshaws, buses, shared taxis and an expanding set of metro systems in cities like Delhi, which are clean, air-conditioned and fast, and which sit in odd contrast to the surface mayhem. Ride-hailing apps have transformed the daily negotiation with drivers. The honest summary is that India offers many ways to get there, none of them uniform, and the traveller's skill lies in picking the right one for the hour.

What I admire is the flexibility. If one option fails, there are five more. Somebody will always take you somewhere. What I do not admire is the cost to the body and the time: a long commute in the heat, in a crowd, with no guarantee of a seat, is a daily tax that takes years off you.

If you arrive from a quieter country, expect to spend your first month recalibrating your sense of what is possible. It is possible to board a bus at a traffic light, to cross a six-lane road by walking steadily, and to be home in time for dinner. It is also possible to lose two hours to a monsoon flood. Both are normal.

## Czechia

Prague, by contrast, is an exercise in civic competence. According to Prague Behind the Scenes, the network includes three metro lines, A, B and C, plus trams and buses, with tickets priced by time: roughly 39 crowns for 30 minutes, 50 for 90 minutes, 150 for 24 hours and 350 for 72 hours. Another guide on Expat.com gives somewhat lower numbers, around 30, 40, 120 and 330, and a yearly pass of about 3,650 crowns. The sources disagree, so check the official site before you budget.

The logic is trust, backed by inspection. There are no turnstiles. Tickets must be validated, and inspectors check at random. The first source gives the fine as 2,000 crowns, or 1,500 if paid within 14 days, while the second says it starts at about 1,000. Seniors over 65, prams and dogs travel free, and the PID Lítačka app sells tickets more cheaply than the machines.

The local guide Dana, who runs Prague Behind the Scenes, is the sort of person who makes this system sound like a pleasure. She wrote: "I don't have a car. I only use public transport!" She also said, "there wasn't a single moment I would feel unsafe or uncomfortable," and recommended that a visitor wanting to try the system "would buy the 24-hour ticket for 150 CZK." It is a voice of ordinary confidence, and ordinary confidence is exactly what Prague's network produces.

The metro runs from about five in the morning until midnight, and night trams and buses take over afterwards. That is the one real limitation: if you are used to a city that never sleeps, you will find yourself waiting for a night tram in the cold. But the day service is dense, clean and punctual, and it reaches almost everywhere.

The difference from India is the absence of drama. Nobody fights for a place. Nobody hangs from a door. You tap, sit, read and arrive. For someone raised on improvised transport, the first week is eerie. By the second, you are annoyed when a tram is two minutes late.

It is worth adding what the two systems do to your sense of time. In Prague, a timetable is a promise, and you stop leaving buffer minutes after about a fortnight. In an Indian city, you learn to treat any journey as an estimate with a generous margin, and the habit of building in slack is a form of wisdom, not pessimism. Move between the two and you will carry the wrong clock in your head for weeks: either you arrive early and bored, or late and apologetic. Neither is a catastrophe, but it is the kind of small mismatch that makes a relocation feel like a permanent jet lag.

## The Verdict

Czechia wins on comfort, India wins on resilience. If I am being honest about which commute I would choose five days a week for the rest of my working life, it is Prague, and I do not need to think about it. A seat, a schedule and a ticket that costs less than a sandwich is a better deal than any amount of heroic crowd management.

But I will not pretend the Indian system is a failure. It is a miracle of coordination under impossible pressure, and the human cost is the price of carrying so many people on so little track. It deserves respect, not pity.

For an Indian professional moving to Prague, the shock will be how easy everything is, and how quiet. For a Czech professional moving to India, the shock will be how everything is possible and nothing is predictable. Both will adapt. Only one will miss the other.

## Conclusion

Transport is a mirror. In Prague it reflects a society that trusts its rules and expects you to keep them. In India it reflects a society that has learned to work around the absence of rules, and to be generous while it does so.

Take the tram when you can, take the local train when you must, and keep your ticket in your hand in one and your bag in front of you in the other. Wherever you arrive, you will have learned something about the people who got you there.
