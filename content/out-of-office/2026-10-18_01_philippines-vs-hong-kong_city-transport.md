# The MTR Will Judge You For Being Ninety Seconds Late. EDSA Won't Even Notice You're There.

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two cities, two philosophies of time. In Hong Kong, the train arrives when it says it will, to the second, and if it doesn't, the city files a formal complaint with itself. In Manila, "arrival" is a devout hope rather than a scheduling commitment, and the jeepney leaves when the driver feels it's full enough — a number that exceeds the vehicle's design capacity by roughly double.

I've relocated fourteen times and sat in traffic on four continents, and there is no commute more spiritually instructive than one that makes you late for everything. Manila teaches humility. Hong Kong teaches you to stand perfectly still on the correct side of the escalator and never ask a stranger a question they can answer by pointing. Both lessons are valuable. Neither is in the relocation brochure.

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Download Grab before you land — it is the de facto taxi meter nobody admits is also the rationing system | Hail a regular taxi at the airport and expect the meter to mean anything |
| Learn the EDSA Busway and MRT/LRT lines; they're the only predictable spine in the whole city | Schedule two meetings less than ninety minutes apart anywhere on opposite sides of Metro Manila |
| Treat jeepneys and UV Express vans as a cultural education, not just transit | Expect anyone — driver, conductor, fellow passenger — to apologize for the squeeze |
| Build "EDSA time" into every plan: leave earlier than feels rational, then leave earlier than that | Assume rain, a flag ceremony, or a presidential motorcade won't personally ruin your afternoon |

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get an Octopus card on day one; tapping in and out defines you as a person who belongs here | Fumble for cash on a bus or tram — drivers don't give change and the queue will remember you |
| Stand right, walk left on every escalator, always, no exceptions, forever | Dawdle through an MTR interchange during rush hour unless you enjoy being physically relocated |
| Use the MTR for anything cross-harbour; it is faster than reason | Buy a car. Parking costs more than some people's rent and the roads are just as jammed as Manila's at peak hours |
| Carry your destination written in Chinese for taxis, since English fluency isn't guaranteed | Expect a taxi driver to engage in small talk, eye contact, or acknowledgment of your existence |

## 🇵🇭 Philippines: A Masterclass in Collective Patience

Metro Manila's traffic is not an inconvenience; it's a load-bearing feature of national identity. The city oscillates between "worst traffic in the world" and merely "top forty," which the local press reports as an improvement with a straight face. The 2025 TomTom Traffic Index put Metro Manila at 40th worst globally — up from 15th the year before — yet in the same breath noted motorists lost *more* time than in 2024: 143 hours in peak congestion, up by over an hour, with city-centre speeds now topping half an hour for just ten kilometres. The ranking improved and the suffering increased. That's the most Filipino sentence I've written all year, and I mean it affectionately.

The jeepney remains the great leveller — a shared ride on a WWII army-jeep chassis that still runs the informal transit network more reliably than most formal ones. The government has spent the better part of a decade trying to "modernize" the fleet; the LTFRB has reported, at various checkpoints, only a few thousand of the roughly 170,000 jeepneys nationwide actually converted to the new models. Riders are, politely, unbothered. The EDSA Busway is the rarer success story — a dedicated-lane system BusinessWorld reported was on track to carry over 70 million riders in a year, proof Manila can move people when it commits.

What nobody tells incoming expats: owning a car doesn't solve anything, it just relocates your suffering to a better air-conditioned seat with worse decision fatigue. The real skill isn't the mode of transport. It's accepting that "ETA" is a conversation opener, not a promise, and building a life where nothing urgent happens between 4pm and 8pm.

## 🇭🇰 Hong Kong: Punctuality As A Personality Trait

Hong Kong's MTR does not merely run trains; it runs a cult of precision so complete that locals have, by multiple accounts, developed the pathology of complaining about two-minute delays as though they were infrastructural collapse. The system's investor disclosures year after year report passenger-journey punctuality in the high 99 percent range — a number so close to perfection the remaining fraction probably has its own apology hotline. The MTR is air-conditioned, mercilessly clean, and arrives so reliably that the city has organized its entire sense of urgency around trains that are, structurally, never late.

This precision comes at a price: your personal space, which does not exist between roughly 8:15 and 9:30am. Riders packed into an Island Line carriage during rush hour aren't being rude when they lean their full weight against you — they're exercising an equal right to the twelve square centimetres of floor beneath their feet. You will be shoved getting off. You're expected to shove back. Waiting for a less-crowded train is a rookie move; the next one is exactly as full, and now you've lost two minutes you'll never get back.

Octopus card tapped, escalator etiquette observed, you'll notice the city's one real blind spot: the roads. Central and Causeway Bay at peak hours turn into gridlock that makes a taxi ride feel like a personal failure, which is exactly why nobody who understands this city drives when they could take the train. Hong Kong solved public transport so thoroughly it forgot to solve the roads, and doesn't seem to mind.

## The Verdict

If you want a commute that functions, Hong Kong wins, obviously, embarrassingly, by a landslide. The MTR is one of the most reliable transit systems on the planet, and I've ridden transit on every continent that has any. But "functions" and "teaches you something" are different categories, and Manila wins the second by a wider margin still. Hong Kong will make you punctual. Manila will make you patient, humble, and weirdly fond of a jeepney driver who once got you home through a typhoon on pure vibes and horn usage. One city optimized the machine. The other optimized the humans riding it. Choose your character development accordingly — but if someone tells you Manila's traffic is "part of the charm," they've never lost three hours of their one finite life to EDSA on a Friday.

## Conclusion

Here's what fourteen moves have taught me: a city's transport system is a confession. Hong Kong confesses that it values order above almost everything, including your right to breathe unshared air at 8:45am. Manila confesses that it values community, improvisation, and the quiet dignity of accepting that some things — traffic, jeepney capacity, the exact definition of "soon" — are simply beyond any one person's control. Neither confession is wrong. Both will test you in ways your old commute never did. Just don't call either of them "exotic" in front of me, or I will personally escort you onto a rush-hour MTR car and watch you learn about the concept of "personal space" the hard way.
