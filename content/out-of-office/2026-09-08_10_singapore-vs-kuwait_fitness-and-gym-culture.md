# One Country Runs Outside at 6 A.M., The Other Walks Laps Around a Mall

🇸🇬 Singapore × 🇰🇼 Kuwait — *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Singapore has engineered an entire city around the assumption that its citizens want to exercise outdoors, constantly, in tropical humidity that would flatten a lesser population — park connectors, void deck gyms, and a 6 a.m. running culture so devoted it makes actual insomniacs feel lazy. Kuwait, defeated by summer heat that regularly exceeds 45°C, has quietly relocated its entire fitness culture indoors, where malls double as walking tracks and gyms operate on air-conditioned schedules that would make an outdoor Singaporean jogger weep with relief.

I've been overtaken on a Singapore park connector at dawn by a septuagenarian moving with the brisk purpose of someone late for an important meeting, and I've walked briskly through a Kuwaiti mall at 10 p.m. alongside dozens of others doing exactly the same thing, laps logged not in kilometres but in store names passed. Both are legitimate fitness cultures. Neither would recognise the other as normal.

## Do's & Don'ts: 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get up early and use the park connector network — it's genuinely one of the best urban running infrastructures anywhere | Underestimate the humidity even at dawn; hydrate properly even for a "short" morning run |
| Try a void deck or HDB estate fitness corner — free outdoor equipment is everywhere and well used | Assume gym culture means only private studios; plenty of excellent, cheap public facilities exist across the island |
| Join a running or cycling group (there are many, and they're welcoming) if you want built-in accountability | Skip sun protection just because you're exercising early — the tropical sun is intense even before 8 a.m. |

## Do's & Don'ts: 🇰🇼 Kuwait

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use mall walking during peak summer heat — it's a genuinely mainstream, socially normal form of exercise, not a compromise | Plan any serious outdoor exercise between roughly May and September during daylight hours; the heat is dangerous, not just unpleasant |
| Join a gym with a strong women's-only or family-friendly schedule if that matters to you — most major gyms offer one | Assume fitness culture is minimal just because outdoor exercise looks sparse; the indoor gym and home-training scene is substantial |
| Exercise outdoors in the cooler winter months (November–March) when conditions genuinely allow for it | Ignore air quality and heat advisories; even acclimatised locals adjust their routines seasonally, not just visitors |

## Singapore: a city engineered for outdoor exertion

Singapore's fitness culture is, in many ways, a direct product of deliberate urban planning as much as personal enthusiasm. The Park Connector Network stitches together over 300 kilometres of green corridors linking parks, reservoirs, and neighbourhoods, purpose-built to make running, cycling, and walking not just possible but genuinely pleasant across an otherwise dense, high-rise city. Combine that with free outdoor fitness corners built into nearly every HDB housing estate and void deck, and Singapore has essentially removed every conceivable excuse for skipping exercise, a fact that its notoriously disciplined fitness scene takes full advantage of.

The defining feature, though, is timing. Serious exercisers here move their schedules dramatically earlier than almost anywhere else, with 6 a.m. group runs and open-air gym sessions genuinely common, not a niche extreme-early-riser phenomenon. This isn't really optional dedication so much as a rational response to Singapore's relentless tropical humidity, which makes anything beyond a gentle stroll genuinely unpleasant by mid-morning and borderline unbearable by early afternoon. Locals have simply built their fitness culture around beating the heat rather than fighting it, and newcomers who insist on a leisurely 9 a.m. jog quickly understand why nobody else is out there with them.

What often surprises visitors is how egalitarian and communal Singapore's fitness scene is compared to a more privatised, studio-driven Western model. Free public infrastructure means fitness isn't gated behind expensive memberships — grandparents doing tai chi, teenagers on outdoor pull-up bars, and serious marathon trainees all share the same park connector at dawn, forming an unusually broad cross-section of society all pursuing the same 6 a.m. window before the humidity claims the day.

## Kuwait: the mall as the new outdoors

Kuwaiti fitness culture has adapted, with real ingenuity, to a climate that makes Singapore's humidity look almost gentle by comparison — summer temperatures that regularly exceed 45°C and can push past 50°C make outdoor exercise during daylight hours not merely unpleasant but genuinely dangerous for a solid five to six months of the year. Rather than simply abandoning physical activity for half the calendar, Kuwaiti society has relocated it almost entirely indoors, and mall walking has emerged as a completely mainstream, socially unremarkable form of daily exercise rather than the slightly odd compromise it might seem to an outsider.

Kuwait's large, air-conditioned shopping malls function, especially in summer evenings, as genuine fitness venues in their own right — groups and individuals doing measured laps, tracking distance on phones, moving briskly among the shoppers with the same seriousness a Singaporean might bring to a park connector run. It's a pragmatic, community-normalised solution to a genuinely hostile outdoor environment, and dismissing it as a lesser substitute for "real" exercise misunderstands both the climate constraint and how seriously it's actually practised.

The gym scene, separately, is substantial and increasingly sophisticated, with many facilities offering dedicated women's-only hours or family-oriented scheduling that reflects broader social norms around gender-separated spaces. Winter, by contrast, flips the entire equation — from roughly November through March, conditions genuinely allow for outdoor activity, and Kuwaitis take enthusiastic advantage of the shorter window, with parks and waterfront paths considerably busier than the punishing summer months would ever suggest to a first-time visitor forming their impression only from a July trip.

## The Verdict

Singapore wins on sheer infrastructure and inclusivity — a genuinely world-class, free, outdoor fitness network available to anyone at any income level, engineered around a climate challenge it has mostly out-planned rather than avoided. Kuwait wins on pragmatic adaptability, having built a legitimate, socially normalised fitness culture around a climate constraint that's considerably more extreme and, for roughly half the year, simply non-negotiable.

If I want the better daily fitness routine, Singapore's dawn park connector culture is genuinely hard to beat — free, communal, gorgeous scenery, and a level of civic investment in outdoor exercise infrastructure most cities can only envy. But I have real respect for Kuwait's mall-walking culture, which proves that "no outdoor exercise for five months a year" doesn't have to mean "no exercise culture" — just a different, air-conditioned one.

## Conclusion

Singapore built a city that makes outdoor fitness the path of least resistance, and its population responded exactly as planned. Kuwait, facing a climate that makes the same approach genuinely dangerous for half the year, built an equally sincere fitness culture indoors instead, mall laps and all. Neither city is wrong about how to move a human body through extreme conditions — they've just answered the same problem, heat, with completely opposite architecture. Set your alarm for dawn in Singapore. Save your fitness ambitions for the mall, or November, in Kuwait.
