# Paraguay Celebrates Quietly and Devoutly. Malta Celebrates With Fireworks Loud Enough to Register on a Seismograph, Every Single Weekend, All Summer.

🇵🇾 Paraguay + 🇲🇹 Malta
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Paraguay's festival calendar is deeply devotional, rooted in Guaraní-Catholic syncretism, and carries an understated reverence that rewards patience over spectacle. Malta's festival calendar is, by contrast, an explosive, village-by-village, weekend-by-weekend arms race of fireworks, brass bands and religious statues carried through the streets to a soundtrack that would frighten most NATO air defences. Both countries take their festivals with total seriousness. Only one of them has occasionally caused actual property damage in the enthusiasm of celebrating.

I've sat through the hushed candlelit processions of Caacupé and been physically rattled awake by a Maltese festa's midnight fireworks finale, and I can say with total confidence that these two islands of devotion — one landlocked, one genuinely an island — have arrived at wildly different volumes for expressing exactly the same underlying faith.

## Do's & Don'ts: Paraguay 🇵🇾

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend the Caacupé pilgrimage in December if you get the chance — it's genuinely moving | Treat religious processions as photo opportunities only; quiet respect is expected |
| Try festival food specific to the occasion — chipa during religious holidays is essential | Skip local guaraní cultural elements assuming festivals are purely Catholic; they're syncretic |
| Dress modestly at religious festivals, even outdoor ones | Expect festival crowds to be loud or rowdy; Paraguayan celebration tends toward the reverent |
| Join family-based holiday gatherings if invited — public and private celebration blend closely | Assume Independence Day (May 14-15) celebrations are subdued; that one gets considerably livelier |

## Do's & Don'ts: Malta 🇲🇹

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the village festa calendar before booking accommodation near a village in summer | Book a quiet weekend stay in a festa village without checking the schedule first — you will not sleep |
| Join the street celebration — locals genuinely welcome outsiders into festa crowds | Wear earplugs and expect nobody to notice; leaning into the noise is part of the fun |
| Try festival sweets like nougat (qubbajt) sold from street stalls | Assume one village's festa is representative; each has fierce, specific local pride |
| Watch a statue procession at least once — the devotion behind the spectacle is genuine | Underestimate the fireworks; Malta's per-capita firework consumption is reportedly among the world's highest |

## Paraguay: Devotion in a Lower Register

Paraguayan festival culture carries a distinctive quiet intensity, shaped by centuries of Guaraní and Catholic traditions blending into something neither purely indigenous nor purely colonial, but genuinely its own. The pilgrimage to Caacupé each December, honouring the Virgin of Miracles, draws hundreds of thousands of pilgrims, many walking for days, into a candlelit basilica town for a celebration that's overwhelming in scale but remarkably restrained in volume — devotion expressed through endurance and quiet rather than noise.

Food plays a central, almost liturgical role. Chipa, the cheese-and-cassava bread associated with Holy Week and other religious occasions, isn't just festival snacking — its preparation and sharing carries genuine ritual weight within families, passed down through generations of recipes fiercely, affectionately defended as "the correct" version. Public holidays tend to fold seamlessly into private, family-centred gatherings, blurring the line between civic celebration and domestic ritual in a way that makes outsider participation feel less like attending an event and more like being quietly absorbed into someone's family tradition.

The exception to this restraint is Independence Day in May, when Paraguayan celebration does turn genuinely festive and public — parades, folk dancing, traditional harp music filling plazas across the country. But even here, the tone stays warmer than wild, reverent even at its loudest, a country celebrating its identity with pride rather than pyrotechnics.

## Malta: Devotion, Amplified Considerably

Malta takes an almost diametrically opposed approach to expressing the same underlying Catholic devotion: through spectacle, volume and an intensity of local pride that borders on competitive sport. Village festa season, running roughly June through September, sees nearly every town and village host its own multi-day festival honouring its patron saint, complete with elaborately decorated churches, brass band marches, street parties, and fireworks displays of a scale genuinely disproportionate to the size of the village hosting them.

The fireworks alone deserve their own paragraph. Maltese festa fireworks aren't a brief evening display — they're a competitive art form, with rival firework factories from neighbouring villages sometimes staging displays that rattle windows for kilometres and run well past midnight. Malta's per-capita firework consumption during festa season is genuinely staggering, a fact locals mention with more pride than apology. Renting accommodation near a festa village without checking the calendar first is a rookie mistake that guarantees at least one entirely sleepless weekend, whether you wanted to participate or not.

What's easy to miss beneath the noise is the genuine devotion underneath it. The statue of the patron saint, carried through the streets on the shoulders of local men who've often waited years for the honour, remains the emotional centre of the entire event — the fireworks and brass bands are the expression, not the substance, of faith that runs just as deep as Paraguay's, just considerably louder about announcing itself.

## The Verdict

If you want festival culture that moves you quietly, Paraguay's Caacupé pilgrimage is one of the most genuinely affecting religious spectacles I've witnessed anywhere, precisely because of its restraint. If you want festival culture that moves your furniture, Malta's festa season is unmatched — joyful, communal, deafening chaos that somehow never loses its sincerity beneath the fireworks. Paraguay wins on reverence. Malta wins on sheer, glorious excess. Bring earplugs to one and a very good pair of walking shoes to the other.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a relocated teacher wrote that no amount of warning prepared them for their first Maltese village festa; the fireworks felt closer to a small-scale bombardment than a celebration, in the most joyful way possible.*

> *expat.com, Asunción thread — a longtime resident said walking even a portion of the Caacupé pilgrimage route changed how they understood Paraguayan identity, describing it as more moving than any cathedral visit elsewhere.*

> *InterNations Malta — a member noted that renting an Airbnb in a festa village without checking the calendar first led to a genuinely sleepless weekend, and that this is apparently a rite of passage every newcomer eventually experiences.*

## Conclusion

Paraguay and Malta both treat festivals as the truest expression of national identity — just tuned to opposite volumes. Paraguay's quiet devotion asks you to slow down, walk further, and feel something in the silence between candles. Malta's fireworks-and-brass-band spectacle asks you to surrender to the noise and dance in the smoke of a rival village's competing display. Neither is more sincere than the other, whatever the decibel difference suggests. Just don't plan a peaceful weekend in a Maltese festa village in July, and don't expect Caacupé to end with fireworks. Both countries know exactly what they're doing. Trust them.
