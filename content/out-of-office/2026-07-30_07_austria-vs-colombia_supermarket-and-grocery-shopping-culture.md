# One Country Closes the Fridge on Sunday, the Other Never Really Closes At All

🇦🇹 Austria vs 🇨🇴 Colombia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Austria enforces Sunday closing laws with a rigidity that borders on theological — supermarkets, shuttered, full stop, no exceptions worth mentioning, so plan your week or eat crackers. Colombia, by contrast, offers a grocery ecosystem that barely acknowledges the concept of "closed": sprawling plazas de mercado at dawn, corner tiendas open until absurd hours, supermarkets running full schedules seven days a week, and a haggling culture at the markets that treats the sticker price as a opening bid rather than a final answer.

I've stood outside a locked Billa in Vienna on a Sunday evening, mentally reviewing every food item in my apartment with the focus of a woman planning a siege. I've also spent forty minutes haggling over tomatoes at a Medellín plaza de mercado, lost by a considerable margin, and walked away feeling like I'd won something anyway. Neither country's system is broken. One is just designed to make you plan; the other is designed to make you negotiate.

## Do's & Don'ts: Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Do your full weekly shop by Saturday afternoon — supermarkets close Saturday evening and stay shut through Sunday | Assume any exceptions exist for regular grocery shopping; train stations and petrol stations are the rare loopholes, not a backup plan |
| Bring your own bags — most stores charge for them and expect you to have planned ahead | Expect small talk at the till; Austrian checkout culture is efficient, brisk, and not the place for chat |
| Learn the bottle deposit (Pfand) system — you'll get money back for returning bottles at machines | Block the checkout belt organising your bags slowly; there's an unspoken pace expectation and people will sigh audibly |

## Do's & Don'ts: Colombia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit the local plaza de mercado for fresh produce — better quality and prices than supermarkets, if you know how to buy | Pay the first price quoted at a market stall without at least a token negotiation — it's expected, not rude |
| Use neighbourhood tiendas for small, frequent top-ups — that's genuinely how most locals shop day-to-day | Assume supermarket hours mirror Austria's — most run long hours daily, including Sundays |
| Bring small bills and coins to markets — vendors often can't break large notes | Rush the interaction at a market stall; a bit of friendly back-and-forth is part of the actual transaction |

## Austria: Grocery Shopping as a Disciplined Weekly Ritual

Austria's Sunday closing laws (Ladenschlussgesetz, if you want to sound like you've done your homework) are not a quirky historical leftover quietly ignored in practice — they are actively enforced, broadly supported, and treated as a genuine labour-rights achievement rather than an inconvenience to be worked around. Supermarkets close Saturday afternoon or early evening and simply do not reopen until Monday. There is no late-night bodega culture to fall back on, no 24-hour convenience chain waiting in the wings. The handful of exceptions — small shops at major train stations, some petrol station convenience counters — exist for genuine emergencies, not as a loophole for the poorly organised.

What this produces, culturally, is a population that has internalised weekly meal planning as a basic life skill rather than an aspiration from a productivity app. Austrians do their big shop on Friday or Saturday with a thoroughness that would impress a military logistics officer, then treat Sunday as genuinely, structurally free of errands — which, not coincidentally, is rather the point of the law in the first place. It's less about restricting consumers and more about guaranteeing retail workers an actual day off, a rationale that becomes more sympathetic the longer you live inside the system.

The trap for newcomers is treating the first missed Saturday shop as a minor inconvenience rather than the genuine planning failure it is. Miscalculate once, and Sunday becomes a long, hungry lesson in Austrian civic priorities.

## Colombia: Grocery Shopping as a Living, Negotiated Marketplace

Colombia's grocery culture runs on an entirely different premise: abundance and access, nearly around the clock, layered across formats that coexist rather than compete. The plaza de mercado — the traditional covered market found in nearly every city and town — remains the beating heart of fresh food shopping for locals, offering produce, meat, and flowers at prices and quality that put supermarket produce aisles to shame, provided you're willing to engage with the culture properly: greeting the vendor, asking questions, and yes, negotiating.

Haggling at the plaza isn't an awkward imposition on the vendor — it's the expected choreography of the transaction. Paying the first quoted price without any pushback is almost read as a missed social cue, a failure to participate in a ritual that both sides generally enjoy. Alongside the plazas, the neighbourhood tienda — small, family-run corner shops — handles the day-to-day top-ups: bread, milk, a forgotten ingredient, open at hours that would make an Austrian shopkeeper faint. Formal supermarkets, meanwhile, run long daily hours including Sundays, filling the gap for anything the plaza and tienda don't cover.

The learning curve for newcomers is less about hours — Colombia is forgivingly available almost whenever you need it — and more about etiquette: carrying small bills, engaging rather than rushing the haggle, and understanding that the plaza experience is social infrastructure as much as it is commerce.

## The Verdict

Austria wins on structural fairness — a system engineered around giving workers a guaranteed day off, dressed up as an inconvenience for shoppers who didn't plan ahead. Colombia wins on sheer aliveness — a grocery culture that treats shopping as a genuine social and sensory experience rather than a chore to be minimised. I respect Austria's principles. I enjoy Colombia's plazas considerably more, tomatoes and haggling losses included.

## Conclusion

Austria's supermarkets close because the country decided workers deserve rest more than shoppers deserve convenience, and once you understand that, the Sunday lockout stops feeling punitive and starts feeling principled. Colombia's markets stay open, loud, and negotiable because commerce there is treated as a social act, not a transaction to be automated away. Plan ahead in Vienna. Show up ready to chat in Medellín. Confuse the two countries' rhythms and you'll either go hungry on a Sunday or offend a vendor by paying full price without a fight.
