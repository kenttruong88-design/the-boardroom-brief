# One Country Gets Fit By Joining a Club, the Other Gets Fit By Joining a Retreat

🇮🇪 Ireland vs 🇮🇩 Indonesia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ireland's approach to fitness runs almost entirely through community sport — GAA clubs specifically, where hurling and Gaelic football aren't hobbies so much as parish-level religious observance, and where "getting fit" is really a side effect of belonging to something bigger than yourself. Indonesia, or more specifically the wellness economy that's swallowed Bali whole, has built an entire industry around fitness as aspiration and lifestyle branding — juice bars adjacent to yoga shalas adjacent to gyms with better Wi-Fi than most co-working spaces, all photographed constantly, all deeply, sincerely committed to looking effortless.

I've been dragged, more or less against my will, to a GAA training session in rural Clare, where a coach who'd known everyone present since birth screamed encouragement with a tenderness that only made sense once I understood the club was basically an extended family. I've also done a sunrise yoga class in Canggu surrounded by influencers holding poses specifically for camera angles, in a studio so aesthetically considered it could double as a furniture showroom. Both cultures move the body. Only one of them means it without an audience.

## Do's & Don'ts: Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join your local GAA club if you're staying any length of time — it's the fastest route to genuine community | Expect gym culture to dominate the way it does elsewhere; team and club sport still leads in most towns |
| Show up to training consistently — commitment matters more than raw athletic ability at grassroots level | Underestimate the physicality of hurling or Gaelic football; both are considerably rougher than they look |
| Embrace the post-match pub tradition — it's as much a part of the sport as the match itself | Treat it as purely recreational; local club rivalries run generations deep and are taken seriously |

## Do's & Don'ts: Indonesia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Try a proper Bali gym or studio (Canggu, Ubud) if you want serious training alongside the wellness branding | Assume the whole island is a photo backdrop; genuine, hardworking fitness communities exist beneath the aesthetic |
| Book popular classes in advance — the best studios fill up fast, especially in peak tourist season | Expect consistency in facility quality outside the main wellness hubs; standards vary a lot by region |
| Respect local modesty norms at gyms outside the international wellness bubble, especially beyond Bali | Confuse Bali's wellness scene with Indonesian fitness culture broadly — it's a specific, tourist-driven pocket, not the national norm |

## Ireland: Fitness as Community Membership, Not a Personal Project

To understand Irish fitness culture, you have to understand that the Gaelic Athletic Association isn't really a sports league in the way that phrase suggests elsewhere. It's a parish-based institution, older than the state itself in spirit, where nearly every town and rural townland maintains its own club, its own pitch, and a fierce, generational loyalty that has almost nothing to do with fitness as a personal goal and everything to do with belonging to a place. People don't join GAA clubs to get in shape. They get in shape because they joined the club, and the club was simply what you did.

This distinction matters enormously for anyone moving to Ireland hoping to build a social life through sport. Commercial gyms exist, certainly, concentrated in cities, growing steadily — but they don't carry the same cultural weight. The GAA club is where actual community happens: training sessions that double as social gatherings, underage coaching staffed by parents and grandparents, and a post-match pub tradition so integral that separating the sport from the socialising afterward would be like separating a wedding from the reception. Hurling in particular is a startlingly physical sport — a hardened ball travelling at genuinely dangerous speeds, wielded with a wooden stick, played with an enthusiasm that borders on the reckless — and underestimating it as "just a hobby sport" is a fast way to both an injury and a reputation.

For a foreigner, showing up and simply trying, consistently, matters more than arriving with any particular skill. Irish club culture rewards persistence and communal spirit over athletic pedigree, which makes it one of the more genuinely welcoming entry points into local life anywhere in Europe — provided you can handle the pace of both the training and the pints afterward.

## Indonesia: Fitness as Aspirational Lifestyle Branding

Bali's fitness scene, concentrated in Canggu and Ubud especially, has become a genuine global industry unto itself — an ecosystem of yoga shalas, boutique gyms, cold-plunge studios, and juice bars that collectively sell not just fitness but an entire aesthetic of intentional, photogenic wellbeing. It is, at its most cynical, a backdrop for content creation. It is also, underneath that, home to genuinely excellent instructors, serious practitioners, and facilities that would be impressive by any global city's standards, not just a beach town's.

The tension is real, though: much of what dominates the visible scene is built for an international, transient, Instagram-fluent audience rather than for Indonesian locals, whose relationship to fitness — particularly outside Bali — looks entirely different, more modest, more community and mosque-and-family-embedded, and considerably less branded. Treating Canggu's wellness bubble as representative of "Indonesian fitness culture" broadly is a category error tourists make constantly, and one that locals find quietly amusing at best.

For newcomers who actually want serious training rather than a scenic backdrop, the advice holds regardless of the branding: the good studios and gyms exist, they're just easier to find once you look past the juice bar signage and toward the instructors' actual credentials and the class intensity, rather than the studio's interior design budget.

## The Verdict

Ireland wins on sincerity — nobody's doing hurling training for the aesthetic, and the community you gain is real, generational, and unphotographed. Indonesia, or Bali specifically, wins on sheer accessibility of serious wellness infrastructure for a transient population, provided you can see past the branding to the substance underneath. I'd rather be adopted by a GAA club than photographed doing a handstand at sunrise, but I understand why plenty of people would choose differently.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/ireland — paraphrased: a newcomer said joining their local GAA club got them more genuine friends in three months than five years of city gym memberships back home ever had.*

> *Internations Dublin — paraphrased: a member described being handed a hurl on their first training session with zero prior explanation and said the bruises taught them more about the sport than any conversation could.*

> *Reddit r/bali — paraphrased: a long-term Canggu resident said it took real effort to find the "real" gym community underneath the influencer layer, but once they did, it was the most dedicated training group they'd trained with anywhere.*

## Conclusion

Ireland's fitness culture is a side effect of belonging; Indonesia's, at least in its most visible Bali form, is a lifestyle sold deliberately as an image, with genuine substance available to anyone willing to look past the marketing. Join the GAA club in Ireland and you'll gain a second family, bruises included. Join a Bali studio and you'll gain excellent instruction, provided you can tell the difference between a serious teacher and a well-lit backdrop. Both will get you fit. Only one will get you invited to Christmas.
