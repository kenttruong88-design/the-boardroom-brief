🇮🇳 India vs 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

India exported more than 1.8 million foreign medical-tourism patients between 2023 and 2025, built an entire industry on cut-rate, world-class private surgery — and still ranks 45th globally on Numbeo's own Health Care Index, well behind Czechia's 13th. That's not a contradiction. That's the entire Indian healthcare system in one sentence: breathtaking at the top, genuinely alarming at the bottom, and almost nothing reliable in between unless you know exactly where to look.

Czechia, meanwhile, has built the opposite problem — a system so bureaucratically airtight that one expat forum post reduces the entire experience to a single administrative tip: check which doctors actually accept your insurer before you get sick, because plenty of good doctors simply don't. Hofstede's Uncertainty Avoidance score puts a number on it — Czechia scores 74, India scores 40 — and if you've ever tried to get anything done in either country's hospital system, that forty-point gap will make immediate, visceral sense.

## Do's & Don'ts: India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Consider paying out of pocket — multiple expats on forums genuinely decided against insurance, calling costs "reasonable compared to US" | Walk into just any public hospital expecting CGHS-level reliability; one forum veteran warns bluntly, "never even think about stepping into any other public hospital here" |
| Name-check AIIMS if you want the credible public-sector benchmark everyone else measures against | Assume universal coverage extends to you; Ayushman Bharat covers over 600 million Indian citizens but is essentially invisible to foreign corporate hires on employer visas |
| Get a second opinion as standard practice, especially after one forum poster flagged a reported scheme of doctors receiving pharma gifts for prescriptions | Expect consistent quality hospital to hospital; multiple sources independently describe care as "varying a lot" depending on where you land |
| Lean into India's genuinely strong private international-hospital tier if you can afford it — "modern, clean, and friendly," per one expat who switched after a bad public-hospital experience | Underestimate the public/private gap; it's the single defining fact of the entire system, not a minor caveat |

## Do's & Don'ts: Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy commercial insurance immediately if you're not on a local Czech contract — public insurance is not automatic for most expats | Assume any policy will do; the legal minimum commercial coverage has been €400,000 per claim since September 2023 |
| Check your insurer's accepted-doctor list before you need it, not after | Expect every clinic to take every insurer; "doctor-shopping friction" is a specifically documented, recurring expat complaint |
| Consider Nemocnice Na Homolce in Prague, specifically known for its dedicated foreigners' section | Assume VZP is your only option; foreigners have been able to choose their own compliant commercial insurer only since 2023, when VZP's monopoly on that market ended |
| Trust the system's cost efficiency — one expat's family paid roughly $26 a night for a private maternity room with a balcony over the Vltava | Treat a 2006-era EU health-system ranking as current; a two-decade-old comparative study still gets cited more than its age really justifies |

## India: a system defined entirely by which door you walk through

The single most useful fact about Indian healthcare for an incoming expat is this: the public and private tiers are not variations on a theme, they are effectively two different healthcare systems sharing a border. CGHS, the Central Government Health Scheme, gets repeated, specific praise from forum veterans as the one reliable public option; everything else in the public tier draws warnings ranging from cautious to outright alarmed. One Expat.com forum contributor puts it in the starkest possible terms: "never even think about stepping into any other public hospital here," while separately flagging a reported pattern of doctors receiving gifts from pharmaceutical companies tied to prescriptions, and recommending a second opinion as standard, not exceptional, practice. Another forum veteran, with firsthand experience on both sides of the divide, describes a government hospital as run-down, with staff rude to a foreign patient and minor surgery performed in what they call a dirty, outdated operating room with poor privacy — followed, later, by treatment for a high fever at a private international hospital that was, in their words, "modern, clean, and friendly" and worth the extra cost. Their conclusion is unambiguous: "I'll never use a government hospital again in India."

And yet, improbably, several forum contributors on ExpatExchange report simply skipping insurance altogether and paying out of pocket, describing costs as "reasonable compared to US" even without coverage — a genuinely counterintuitive selling point that inverts the usual expat risk calculus entirely. AIIMS, the All India Institute of Medical Sciences, repeatedly comes up by name as the credible benchmark institution within the public system, the proof that the infrastructure for excellence exists, just not evenly distributed. The numbers make the scale of that unevenness concrete: India spends roughly $346 per capita on healthcare against Czechia's $4,674, fields 7.2 doctors per 10,000 people against Czechia's 43.5, and posts a life expectancy of 72.2 years against Czechia's 80.0. And yet, in the same breath, India pulled in over 1.8 million foreign medical-tourism visa holders across 2023 to 2025 alone — mostly regional patients from Bangladesh and Iraq seeking cut-rate private surgery — a genuine, government-documented export industry running in parallel to a domestic Ayushman Bharat scheme that covers more than 600 million Indian citizens with roughly $6,000 of annual family coverage, none of which reaches a foreign corporate hire on an employer visa, who experiences almost none of that universal-coverage headline and nearly all of the private, international-hospital-chain reality instead.

## Czechia: a system so orderly it needs its own paperwork to navigate

Czechia's defining feature, structurally, is the opposite problem to India's: not unevenness, but rigidity. Public insurance is automatic only for permanent residents, people employed directly by a Czech company, and under-18s — which rules out a sizeable share of corporate expats working for foreign entities on local contracts, who must instead buy commercial insurance meeting a legal minimum of €400,000 per claim, a threshold that's been in force since September 2023. Until that same year, the state insurer VZP held an effective monopoly on compliant commercial policies for foreigners; expats can now shop around, but the practical friction hasn't disappeared, it's just relocated. A recurring, specific complaint across forum threads is that foreigners "often struggle to find doctors who accept their [commercial] insurer" — not a question of trusting the system, as in India, but a narrower, more bureaucratic problem of simply finding a doctor on the approved list.

What the system does deliver, when the paperwork clears, is genuinely excellent and often startlingly cheap by Western standards. Nemocnice Na Homolce in Prague, a hospital with a dedicated foreigners' section, gets named directly by one expat who underwent four major surgeries there and found the care inexpensive even without insurance, concluding flatly: "CZ is still the place to be sick or have surgery!" Another expat's specific, dated anecdote sticks: a private maternity room with a balcony over the Vltava River cost roughly $26 a night, nearly two decades ago, delivering what he calls simply, "I love the health care system here." Numbeo's own current Health Care Index backs this up with real numbers — Czechia scores 76.0, ranking around 13th globally, against India's 65.5 at roughly 45th — and broader World Bank data shows the gap holding across nearly every metric: infant mortality of 2.1 per 1,000 births against India's 23.3, an adult doctor density six times higher. One older, frequently recycled statistic deserves a caveat here: a 2006 Health Consumer Powerhouse study ranking Czechia 22nd among EU states is nearly twenty years stale and shouldn't be treated as a current verdict, however often it still gets cited. Hofstede's Uncertainty Avoidance score of 74 — the highest kind of rule-bound, risk-averse cultural posture — maps almost perfectly onto what expats actually describe: a system that is excellent once you've correctly filed the paperwork, and genuinely obstructive if you haven't.

## The Verdict

India will give you some of the best private medical care in the world at a fraction of Western prices, provided you know enough to avoid the public hospitals entirely — a system that rewards research and punishes assumption. Czechia will give you consistently excellent, genuinely affordable care, provided your insurance paperwork is in order before you need it — a system that rewards preparation and punishes improvisation. If I had to bet my own appendix on one system, I'm taking Czechia: a €400,000 minimum coverage requirement is annoying, but it's a known, fixed obstacle. India's public-private gap is a much larger, much less predictable one, and "never even think about" a government hospital is not advice I want to be relying on while already in pain.

## Conclusion

The real lesson here isn't which country has "better" healthcare in the abstract — it's that both systems punish the same mistake in different ways: arriving without doing your homework first. India will let an uninformed assumption land you in a hospital you'll regret for years. Czechia will let an unfiled form leave you stranded without a doctor who'll actually see you. Do the research before you need either system, not after. Nobody's appendix waits for paperwork to clear.
