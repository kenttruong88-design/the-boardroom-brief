# One Country Picnics on Traffic Islands. The Other Built a Colonial Garden Nobody Can Agree On.

🇮🇷 Iran · 🇩🇿 Algeria
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Iranians will picnic anywhere green enough to unroll a blanket, including, memorably, the grassy median of a six-lane Tehran highway, with the same unbothered commitment they'd bring to an actual park. Algeria's green space culture centres more on grand, formally designed gardens — chief among them Algiers' Jardin d'Essai, a colonial-era botanical garden whose legacy remains, politely, complicated. Both countries treat green space as essential rather than decorative. They just disagree wildly on where it's acceptable to find some.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Iran**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a Friday family picnic if invited — it's a genuine institution | Be surprised by improvised picnic spots; grassy traffic medians count |
| Bring tea and snacks to share if visiting a park with locals | Assume parks are quiet; Friday afternoons are loud, social, and packed |
| Visit a traditional Persian garden (bagh) for the historical context | Rush through; Persian garden design rewards slow, deliberate wandering |

**Algeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit Jardin d'Essai in Algiers for the scale and botanical variety | Expect it to feel entirely separate from its French colonial history |
| Explore smaller neighbourhood squares for a more local, daily feel | Assume all cities have Algiers' density of large formal gardens |
| Go on a weekend morning before the heat and crowds both build | Litter or damage plantings; municipal upkeep varies and locals notice |

## Iran: The Picnic Is Non-Negotiable, and Neither Is the Location

Iranian green space culture operates on a principle that took me an embarrassingly long time to fully absorb: the picnic itself matters more than where it happens. Friday, the weekly day off, sends entire extended families out with rugs, samovars, and an amount of food wildly disproportionate to the group size, and they will set up wherever there's grass — a proper park, certainly, but just as often a highway median, a roundabout, a narrow strip beside a parking structure. I have genuinely watched a family of six set up a full spread, complete with a small gas burner for tea, on a traffic island in central Tehran, with the calm confidence of people who had done this many times before and saw nothing unusual about it.

This isn't desperation for green space — Tehran has real, substantial parks, and cities across Iran maintain the traditional Persian garden, or bagh, a design tradition dating back millennia and built around a rigorous formal geometry: symmetrical water channels, shade trees, walled enclosures creating a deliberate, cultivated paradise (the English word "paradise" itself descends from the old Persian word for these walled gardens). Visiting a well-preserved bagh, several of which carry UNESCO recognition, gives you the formal, historical version of Iranian green space philosophy: control, symmetry, water as the organizing element throughout.

But the Friday picnic tradition reveals something different and, frankly, more charming — a cultural insistence that outdoor social time is a right rather than a luxury contingent on ideal circumstances. Families don't wait for a perfect park; they claim whatever green patch is available and make it work, turning marginal, overlooked urban space into genuine social infrastructure through sheer collective habit. Parks that do exist get used with real intensity on Fridays — packed, loud, full of children, badminton games, and the specific chaos of dozens of simultaneous family gatherings sharing adjacent patches of grass with practiced, easy tolerance for the noise.

## Algeria: Grand Gardens, Complicated Inheritance

Algiers' green space culture centres, quite literally, on one extraordinary site: the Jardin d'Essai du Hamma, a sprawling botanical garden established in the 1830s under French colonial rule, now one of the largest and most architecturally distinctive botanical gardens in North Africa. Walking through its famous palm-lined avenue — instantly recognisable, having appeared in numerous films over the decades — is genuinely one of the more striking green-space experiences on this continent, dense with tropical and Mediterranean plant collections arranged across formally landscaped terraces.

What's impossible to ignore, and what Algeria itself has never pretended to ignore, is the garden's colonial origin and what that means for how Algerians relate to it today. It was built as a showcase of French colonial botanical ambition, and its legacy sits, understandably, in a more complicated place in the national memory than a simple beloved civic amenity. Algerians visit it, enjoy it, and maintain it as genuinely important public infrastructure — but conversations about it, when they happen with locals, often carry an undercurrent about who it was originally built for and what that history means now.

Beyond Jardin d'Essai, Algeria's green space culture is considerably more modest and localized — smaller neighbourhood squares and plazas serve most of the daily, informal social function that grand parks might elsewhere, and their upkeep varies noticeably by municipality and by neighbourhood investment. Algiers itself, hemmed in by dense hillside housing and challenging topography, has less flat, easily developable park land than the scale of the city might suggest, which makes existing green spaces disproportionately valuable and, on weekends, genuinely crowded.

Outside the capital, smaller cities and towns often lean on public squares tied to mosques or municipal buildings rather than dedicated park infrastructure, and green space culture there feels less like "the park" as a distinct destination and more like the ordinary texture of the town centre. It's a different model from Iran's — less about claiming any available grass, more about a smaller number of genuinely significant, well-defined sites carrying an outsized cultural and historical weight.

## The Verdict

Iran wins on sheer democratic spirit — a culture that has decided green space is a right worth improvising when the official supply falls short, traffic median be damned. Algeria wins on architectural and botanical grandeur, with Jardin d'Essai standing as one of the genuinely great gardens of the region, even carrying its complicated colonial history honestly rather than pretending it away. If I want a spontaneous, chaotic, deeply social afternoon, I'm finding an Iranian family's picnic and hoping they wave me over. If I want to actually sit with history while surrounded by extraordinary botany, I'm under Jardin d'Essai's palms, thinking about who built them and why.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations Tehran — paraphrased: a newcomer describing their disbelief at first seeing a family fully picnicking on a highway median, before realizing it was completely normal and happened everywhere.*

## Conclusion

Iran turns any patch of grass into legitimate social infrastructure through sheer collective willpower, traffic hazards notwithstanding. Algeria concentrates its green space ambition into fewer, grander sites, each carrying more historical weight than a simple neighbourhood park usually has to bear. Neither approach is wrong. Just don't picnic on an Algiers traffic median expecting the same easy acceptance you'd get in Tehran. Different country, considerably less forgiving drivers.
