# One Country Will Let You Open A Bank Account Before Lunch. The Other Wants To See Your Soul, In Triplicate, Within Three Working Days.

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇦🇹 Austria

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Central Europe has a reputation, among people who have never had to actually live there, for being efficient. Trains run, trams are clean, everyone seems to own exactly one very good coat. What that reputation conveniently omits is the paperwork — the specific, bureaucratic choreography required before you are permitted to do anything as basic as depositing your own salary into your own bank account.

Czechia and Austria, separated by a border you can drive across without noticing, have built two entirely different temples to administrative order, and I have been required to worship at both. One moves with surprising, almost suspicious speed. The other will register your address, your religion, and quite possibly your intentions, and will do so entirely in German, on a form last redesigned sometime during a different century.

## Do's & Don'ts: Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your passport, proof of address, and patience for a mid-length queue to open a current account | Assume every bank branch has English-speaking staff; some do, many don't, ask first |
| Register your long-term stay (trvalý pobyt / přechodný pobyt) promptly if you're staying beyond 90 days | Leave registration to the last minute; appointment slots at busy Prague offices fill up fast |
| Get comfortable with online banking apps; most Czech banks have genuinely good ones | Expect a cheque book, a branch on every corner, or much sympathy for either complaint |
| Keep your "rodné číslo" (birth number) equivalent documentation organised; it gets requested constantly | Be surprised when a landlord, employer, or bank all separately ask for the same three documents |

## Do's & Don'ts: Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register your address (Meldezettel) within three working days of arrival, no exceptions | List a religion on the form you don't actually practise; it can trigger church tax |
| Bring your passport and a landlord's signature or stamp confirming your address, in person, the first time | Expect to open a bank account, join a gym, or do much of anything official before that registration is done |
| Book an appointment at your district's Meldeservice rather than risk a long walk-in queue | Assume the form or the office hours will be in English; bring a translation or a patient friend |
| Treat the Meldebestätigung like a passport-adjacent document; you'll need it constantly | Miss the deadline and assume nobody will notice. Missing it is a legal violation with a real fine |

## Czechia: Bureaucracy With Unexpectedly Good Posture

Czech bureaucracy has a reputation, largely inherited from its Habsburg and Soviet-era past, for being slow, suspicious, and designed by committee. The modern reality, at least on the banking side, is a pleasant surprise: opening a current account at a Czech bank is, by Central European standards, almost breezy. Bring a passport and proof of address, sit through one reasonably short queue, and you can walk out with an account and a functioning banking app that, unlike several of its Western European counterparts, actually does what you need without charging you a fee to look at your own balance.

Where the old bureaucratic instincts reassert themselves is residence registration — trvalý pobyt for permanent stays, přechodný pobyt for temporary ones — a process that rewards early planning and punishes procrastination with appointment slots booked weeks out at the busiest Prague offices. The documentation trail repeats itself constantly: the same handful of papers get requested by your landlord, your employer, your bank, and the foreign police, each wanting their own certified copy, each apparently unable to simply ask one of the others.

What foreign residents tend to find, once they're through the initial registration gauntlet, is a system that then leaves them largely alone. There is no equivalent of a doorstep registration officer checking in on you. Online banking, tax filing, and most day-to-day admin can be handled from a laptop, which is more than can be said for several of Czechia's neighbours. The front door is heavier than you'd expect. Once you're through it, the house is surprisingly comfortable.

## Austria: Bureaucracy as a Love Language, Apparently

Austria, by contrast, asks you to prove your love for its administrative process early and often, starting with the Meldezettel — the residence registration form that must be filed within three working days of arrival, in person, with original documents, at your district's Meldeservice. Vienna alone runs twenty of these offices because one would clearly not be dramatic enough. Miss the three-day window and you are, technically, in violation of Austrian law, a fact the country is entirely serious about even for a jet-lagged newcomer who was simply trying to find a working SIM card first.

The form itself is a small masterpiece of unintended comedy: a line asking your religious affiliation, which — if you tick Catholic or Protestant and actually mean it — triggers church tax, a fact buried deep in expat forums rather than announced on the form itself. Another line wants your nationality, which gets complicated fast if you hold two. None of this is explained with any particular warmth; you are expected to have researched it, or to have a friend who has.

Clear the Meldezettel hurdle, though, and genuinely delightful efficiency kicks in. The confirmation document — the Meldebestätigung — becomes the golden key that opens a bank account, a gym membership, a mobile contract, almost anything requiring proof you exist at a specific address. Austrian institutions, once they have that one piece of paper, move with impressive, almost automated speed. The entire system is simply front-loaded: all the pain happens in the first seventy-two hours, and almost none of it happens again.

One more layer worth flagging before you relax too fully into either system: tax season. Czechia's annual tax filing is handled mostly online, through a portal most residents find workable once they've done it once, with relatively few surprises if your employer withholds correctly throughout the year. Austria, having already extracted your religious affiliation and your signed landlord confirmation at the door, keeps the bureaucratic theatre going into tax season too — Austrians joke, not entirely fondly, that the country's tax code has a provision for almost everything, and finding the one that applies to you is a genuine specialist skill, not a DIY afternoon. Several expat forums recommend a local tax advisor for the first year in Austria specifically for this reason, a step most Czech-based expats report skipping without consequence. It's a small, telling difference: Czechia front-loads its paperwork pain at entry and eases off; Austria spreads its paperwork pain more evenly across the whole calendar year, just when you'd started to think you were finally settled.

## The Verdict

Czechia makes the easy things easy and the residence-permit things genuinely effortful. Austria makes the first seventy-two hours a small bureaucratic boot camp and then, having extracted its pound of flesh (and your religious affiliation, if you weren't careful), leaves you largely in peace. If you want quick banking and are prepared to be patient about your long-term paperwork, Prague suits you. If you want one short, sharp, slightly humiliating ordeal that unlocks everything else, Vienna is your venue.

I will say this for Austria: at least it's honest about the deadline. Three working days, a fine if you miss it, no ambiguity. Czechia's system is gentler on arrival and more opaque about what happens later. Pick the bureaucracy whose flavour of unpleasantness you'd rather get over with quickly.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *The Local Austria, reader comments — a recurring correction from newcomers who learned the hard way: the three-day registration deadline counts business days only, so a Friday arrival buys you more breathing room than the rule first suggests, but don't bank on it.*

> *Expat.com — a Prague-based poster's advice to new arrivals: pick a bank with a genuinely bilingual branch near your flat before you need it, because discovering mid-queue that nobody speaks English turns a twenty-minute errand into an afternoon.*

> *Reddit r/CzechRepublic — a long-term resident's observation that the paperwork itself isn't the hard part, it's the fact that every office wants its own certified copy of the same three documents, as if filing cabinets still can't talk to each other.*

## Conclusion

Bureaucracy, properly understood, is just a country's values written down in a particularly unglamorous font. Austria's says: prove yourself immediately, thoroughly, and in German, and we'll reward you with smooth sailing after. Czechia's says: the first transaction is easy, trust is earned gradually, and we reserve our love of paperwork for the things that really matter, like your residence permit. Both systems work. Neither will apologise for how they made you feel on day one. Bring documents, bring patience, and bring, ideally, a local friend who has already done this and lived to complain about it.
