# Austria Closes The Whole Country For A Holiday, The Dominican Republic Opens The Whole Street

🇦🇹 Austria 🇩🇴 Dominican Republic
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Austria treats a public holiday as an opportunity for complete, total, nationwide shutdown — shops closed, streets quiet, a kind of sanctioned civic stillness descending with impressive consistency across a calendar stuffed full of Catholic feast days nobody outside Central Europe has heard of. The Dominican Republic treats a public holiday, and especially Carnival, as an invitation to fill the street with as many people, as much colour, and as much percussion as physically possible, often for days at a stretch, with the entire country's social energy rerouted toward the celebration rather than away from it.

I've been caught out by both — once standing outside a shuttered Viennese pharmacy on Epiphany, utterly baffled that January 6th was apparently a sacred non-negotiable day off, and once swept bodily into a Dominican carnival parade in La Vega, handed a devil mask I hadn't asked for, and informed I was now, functionally, part of the procession.

## Do's & Don'ts: Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Memorise the Catholic feast-day calendar — it governs far more closures than you'd expect | Assume a holiday only affects government offices; retail and services shut too, broadly |
| Visit a Christmas market at least once in December; it's genuinely central to national identity | Expect shops open the day before or after a major holiday either — bridge closures are common |
| Plan grocery runs the day before any public holiday without exception | Schedule anything requiring an open bank, post office, or pharmacy on a feast day |

## Do's & Don'ts: Dominican Republic

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the regional carnival calendar — dates and traditions vary significantly by town | Plan to drive through a carnival town on parade day; roads close for hours |
| Join in — costumes, dancing, and participation are welcomed, not just observed | Wear anything you're precious about near a "vejigante" performer with a inflated cow-bladder whip |
| Expect Independence Day (February 27) energy to blend straight into Carnival season | Assume carnival is a single national event; each city stages its own distinct version |

## Austria: The Sanctity of the Shut Door

Austria's calendar is dense with public holidays rooted overwhelmingly in the Catholic liturgical year — Epiphany, Ascension Day, Corpus Christi, Assumption, All Saints', Immaculate Conception, and more besides the familiar Christmas and Easter anchors — and the country observes every single one with a thoroughness that catches newcomers off guard roughly a dozen times a year. Shops close. Banks close. Pharmacies run reduced hours or shut entirely. Public transport may run on a Sunday schedule even on a Tuesday. There is no "soft" holiday in Austria; the closure is comprehensive and, crucially, assumed — nobody posts a reminder, because nobody imagines you'd need one.

This creates a particular trap for expats from countries with sparser or more secular holiday calendars: you learn, the hard way, that a "bridge day" — the single working day between a Thursday holiday and the weekend — often gets unofficially absorbed into the break anyway, with many businesses simply choosing not to open, turning what should be one lost day into four. Plan a grocery run around this rhythm or suffer the indignity of standing outside a locked Spar on a Thursday evening, holding an empty tote bag and reconsidering your relationship with the concept of planning ahead.

What redeems the rigidity, utterly, is December. Austria's Christmas markets — Vienna's in particular, sprawling across multiple squares with mulled wine, roasted chestnuts, handcrafted ornaments, and a warmth that cuts straight through the cold — represent the one stretch of the year where the holiday calendar's density becomes pure civic joy rather than mere inconvenience. The same culture that shuts its doors with such severe discipline the rest of the year throws them wide open here, and the contrast makes the markets feel genuinely earned rather than merely seasonal.

## Dominican Republic: The Street Is The Celebration

Dominican public holiday culture runs on an entirely different register: expansion rather than contraction. Independence Day on February 27th flows almost seamlessly into Carnival season, and by the time Carnival proper arrives, entire towns have rerouted their public life around the celebration rather than retreating from it. The famous regional carnivals — La Vega's being the most elaborate, with its devil-horned "vejigantes" cracking inflated cow-bladder whips through delighted, screaming crowds — aren't quiet civic observances, they're loud, costumed, deliberately chaotic performances that spill across entire main streets for hours at a stretch, with road closures, vendor stalls, and sound systems that make a Viennese "Ruhetag" sound like a different planet entirely.

Crucially, these aren't uniform national events staged identically everywhere — each city and town has developed its own distinct carnival character over generations: La Vega's devils, Santiago's lechones (masked pig-faced characters), Cabral's ghostly "cachúas." A newcomer who assumes they've "done" Dominican Carnival after attending one city's parade has, in fact, only sampled a single regional dialect of a much richer tradition.

The practical upshot for anyone living or working here is that holiday season isn't a stretch of quiet closures to plan around, it's a stretch of heightened activity to plan into — road closures, shifted work schedules, and a social expectation that you'll participate rather than simply observe from a polite distance. Turning up to a carnival parade in neutral, forgettable clothing, intending to watch from the sidelines, tends not to last; someone will hand you a mask, pull you into the rhythm, and the distinction between spectator and participant dissolves within minutes, whether you'd planned for it or not.

## The Verdict

Austria's holiday calendar is about order, observance, and a kind of collective, almost sacred stillness — rest as civic duty, enforced with the same precision the country applies to its trains. The Dominican Republic's holiday calendar is about expansion, noise, and collective participation — celebration as civic duty, enforced not by closed doors but by the simple social gravity of a street party nobody is allowed to watch passively. Austria will teach you to plan three days ahead for a single feast day. The Dominican Republic will teach you to simply surrender once the music starts, because resistance is both futile and, frankly, no fun at all. Given the choice between a locked pharmacy and a devil mask thrust into my hands, I know exactly which inconvenience I'd rather have.

## Conclusion

Public holidays, in the end, say something true about what each country believes a shared pause is for. Austria believes it's for retreat — quiet, orderly, devoutly observed, doors locked without apology. The Dominican Republic believes it's for release — loud, costumed, collectively performed in the open street, with every passerby a potential participant. Arrive in Vienna expecting a parade and you'll find a locked bakery instead. Arrive in La Vega expecting a quiet civic observance and you'll be handed a devil mask before you've finished your first sentence of protest. Both are right, in their own completely incompatible ways, and both are worth experiencing at least once, preferably with snacks already purchased in advance.
