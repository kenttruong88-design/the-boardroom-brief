# One Country Lets You Choose Your Own Specialist, the Other Makes You Beg a Gatekeeper First

🇨🇱 Chile vs 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Santiago, I called a dermatologist directly, got an appointment within days, and paid a bill that, while real, was manageable enough that I didn't spend the visit calculating its cost in my head. In Amsterdam, I spent two years believing I simply couldn't get antibiotics for anything short of visible organ failure, because the Dutch huisarts, the mandatory general practitioner gatekeeper standing between you and literally any specialist care, operates on a philosophy that most ailments resolve themselves and most requests for intervention are, gently but firmly, declined.

Chile's healthcare system, split between public Fonasa and private Isapre insurance, rewards those who can pay for private care with fast, direct specialist access. The Netherlands' system, universal and mandatory, rewards patience and a genuine trust that the huisarts' famous reluctance to prescribe anything is usually, infuriatingly, correct.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇨🇱 Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get private Isapre insurance if you can afford it | Rely solely on Fonasa if you want short wait times |
| Book specialists directly — no referral usually needed | Assume public and private hospitals offer comparable wait times |
| Keep cash or a card on hand for smaller clinic visits | Expect prices to be identical across clinics — they vary widely |
| Ask your clinic about co-pay percentages upfront | Assume all private clinics accept international insurance |

### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a local huisarts within your first weeks | Expect antibiotics or specialist referrals on the first visit |
| Trust the "wait and see" approach — it's evidence-based, not neglect | Try to see a specialist directly without a referral |
| Get mandatory basic insurance immediately upon residency | Assume paracetamol-first advice means your concern isn't taken seriously |
| Ask direct questions — Dutch doctors respect bluntness | Show up expecting American-style diagnostic urgency |

## Chile: Pay for Speed, and You'll Get It

Chilean healthcare runs on a genuinely two-tiered system that shapes the entire experience depending on which side of it you're on. Fonasa, the public option, is accessible and affordable but comes with the wait times and resource constraints typical of any large public system. Isapre, the private insurance alternative that most expats with stable income opt into, buys something close to healthcare on demand — same-week specialist appointments, modern private clinics, a level of direct access that would be unthinkable in most universal systems.

What surprised me most was how normalised specialist self-referral is. Want a dermatologist, an orthopaedist, a cardiologist? Call and book directly, no general practitioner gatekeeping required, no referral letter, no justification needed beyond your own judgment that you'd like to see one. Coming from systems where a GP stands firmly between you and any specialist, this felt almost suspiciously easy the first few times, before I simply accepted it as one of the system's genuine advantages.

The tradeoff, inevitably, is cost variability and the real gap between Fonasa and Isapre experiences. Private clinic pricing isn't always transparent upfront, and co-pay percentages differ meaningfully between plans and providers, meaning a bill that seemed manageable in estimate occasionally arrived rather less manageable in reality. Expats without private insurance navigating the public Fonasa system alone should expect a considerably different, slower experience than the one most foreign residents describe.

## Netherlands: The Huisarts as Benevolent Immovable Object

Dutch healthcare culture is defined, more than anything else, by the huisarts — the general practitioner who serves as an absolute, non-negotiable gatekeeper to virtually all further care. No specialist visit happens without a huisarts referral first, and the huisarts, trained within a system that genuinely prizes evidence-based restraint over intervention, will very often recommend paracetamol, rest, and a "wait and see" approach that feels, to newcomers raised on more interventionist healthcare cultures, like being dismissed.

It isn't dismissal, though it took me embarrassingly long, and several increasingly frustrated appointments, to actually believe that. Dutch general practice is built on genuinely strong evidence that most common ailments resolve on their own, that antibiotic overuse carries real long-term costs, and that referring every complaint to a specialist wastes resources without improving outcomes. Once I stopped fighting the system and started trusting it, most of my complaints did, genuinely, resolve exactly as predicted, which was almost more annoying than if they hadn't.

Registration matters enormously and should happen immediately upon arrival — showing up without an assigned huisarts when you actually need one produces real complications, since most practices won't take same-day registrations for non-emergencies. Mandatory basic insurance is required by law from day one of residency, non-negotiable and enforced, covering huisarts visits and hospital care once you're actually referred. Directness works well here too: Dutch doctors respond better to plainly stated concerns than performed distress, and asking specifically why a referral isn't being given usually produces a genuinely reasonable, evidence-based answer rather than a brush-off.

## The Verdict

Chile rewards those who can pay for direct, fast specialist access with a system that largely trusts patients' own judgment about what care they need. The Netherlands rewards patience and trust in a gatekeeper trained to resist unnecessary intervention, even when that resistance feels, in the moment, deeply frustrating.

I got faster answers in Chile and, ultimately, more correct ones in the Netherlands. Suki's verdict: the Netherlands wins on genuine evidence-based rigour, even though it took me two years and an unreasonable amount of huisarts-directed frustration to admit it — but Chile wins for anyone who simply wants to see a specialist this week without first convincing a gatekeeper they deserve to.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/chile — a foreigner described switching from Fonasa to Isapre after a six-week public wait, calling it "the best money I've spent since moving here."*

> *Reddit r/Netherlands — someone recounted being told to take paracetamol for what turned out to be a genuine infection, then admitted "the huisarts was right, infuriatingly, again."*

> *expat.com Amsterdam — a longtime resident warned new arrivals to register with a huisarts "the week you land, not the week you actually get sick."*

## Conclusion

Chile and the Netherlands have built healthcare systems around opposite philosophies of access — one rewarding direct action and the ability to pay, the other rewarding trust in restraint and a system designed to resist overtreatment. Move to Santiago and get private insurance if your budget allows it. Move to Amsterdam and register with a huisarts before you need one, then brace yourself for a lot of paracetamol advice that will, against all instinct, usually turn out to be correct.
