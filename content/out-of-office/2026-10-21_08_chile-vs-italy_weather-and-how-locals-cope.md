🇨🇱 Chile 🇮🇹 Italy
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Chile is a country that experiences roughly every climate on Earth within a single north-south drive — Atacama desert aridity so extreme NASA tests Mars rovers there, Mediterranean warmth around Santiago, and Patagonian sleet further south — and somehow the national coping strategy for all of it is the same: ignore it and have another glass of wine. Italy has a far narrower climate range but responds to its one real weather event, August heat, with a solution so drastic it borders on civilisational: the entire country simply leaves.

I've sweated through a Roman August with the shutters closed against the sun like everyone else, and I've shivered through a Santiago winter where the cold isn't severe by the thermometer but somehow gets into your bones because nobody believes in proper indoor heating. Both countries have decided that adapting your building design and your calendar is easier than admitting the weather occasionally wins.

## Do's & Don'ts: Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in proper warm layers for a Santiago winter — indoor heating is often minimal, even indoors | Assume South America means year-round warmth; central and southern Chile get genuinely cold, especially inside |
| Check air-quality alerts in Santiago in winter; the valley traps smog badly on cold, windless days | Exercise outdoors in Santiago during a declared "pre-emergencia ambiental" smog alert; locals take these seriously |
| Pack serious sun protection for the north; Atacama UV exposure is extreme even in mild-feeling temperatures | Underestimate desert sun because it doesn't feel hot; the Atacama's dryness disguises real UV intensity |
| Expect huge regional climate variation; "Chilean weather" isn't one thing at all | Pack for only one climate; a trip from Arica to Punta Arenas spans desert to near-Antarctic in one country |

## Do's & Don'ts: Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan around ferragosto (mid-August) — much of the country, including many businesses, simply closes | Schedule important business or admin tasks for mid-to-late August; you will likely find the office empty |
| Adopt the "riposo" instinct — avoid the harshest early-afternoon sun, as locals structure days around it | Fight the midday heat by pushing through outdoor activity; locals schedule around it, not through it |
| Use shutters and tiled floors as the locals do — older buildings are designed to stay cool without AC | Assume a lack of air conditioning means a lack of climate strategy; the architecture is the strategy |
| Expect regional extremes — the south and islands run hotter and drier than the north for much of the year | Treat "Italian weather" as uniform; Milan's climate and Palermo's climate are genuinely different countries' worth of difference |

## Chile: one country, five climates, zero central heating

Chile's geography is almost comically extreme for its width — a narrow ribbon of land stretching over 4,000 kilometres, encompassing the driest non-polar desert on Earth in the north (the Atacama, where some weather stations have never recorded rainfall) down to sub-Antarctic conditions in Patagonia. Most expats land somewhere in the Santiago-to-Valparaíso corridor, which has a pleasant Mediterranean-adjacent climate on paper — and then discover, every single winter, that "pleasant on paper" doesn't account for the fact that Chilean buildings, broadly, were not designed with serious indoor heating in mind. Central heating is far less standard than in equivalent-latitude countries elsewhere, and newcomers from genuinely cold climates are frequently baffled to find themselves colder indoors in Santiago than they ever were outdoors back home.

Santiago's winter also brings a specific, locally dreaded phenomenon: the city sits in a valley ringed by the Andes, which traps cold air and pollution in a thermal inversion layer, producing serious smog days with officially declared "preemergencia" or "emergencia ambiental" alerts that restrict vehicle use and advise against outdoor exercise. Locals check air-quality apps the way other cultures check rain forecasts, and treat a bad smog day with the same practical seriousness as a storm warning.

Further north, the Atacama's dry heat is deceptive — because humidity is almost nonexistent, temperatures that sound moderate don't "feel" as punishing as they would elsewhere, which leads newcomers to badly underestimate UV exposure and sun risk in a desert that is, by several measures, among the most intensely sun-exposed places on the planet.

## Italy: when the heat wins, the whole country surrenders

Italy's relationship with hot weather, especially from late June through August, is defined by a near-total national institution: ferragosto, a mid-August holiday period during which an enormous share of businesses, government offices, and even some hospitals' non-urgent services simply close, as much of the population decamps to the coast or the mountains. This isn't a loosely observed custom — in many towns, you will find the pharmacy, the bakery, and the local government office all shuttered with a handwritten "chiuso per ferie" sign, and the assumption that essential services will remain staffed as normal is a newcomer's mistake made exactly once.

Italian architecture, particularly in the south, has quietly solved for heat for centuries without relying on air conditioning the way many hotter countries do: thick stone walls, shuttered windows that stay closed through the hottest midday hours, tiled rather than carpeted floors, and a daily rhythm — the riposo, a long lunch-and-rest period roughly matching the worst of the afternoon heat — that structures the entire working day around avoiding the sun rather than fighting it with machinery. Newer construction has increasingly added air conditioning, but the older cultural instinct to simply reschedule around the heat, rather than engineer it away, remains strong, especially outside the biggest cities.

Regional variation matters enormously here too: Milan's climate behaves more like continental Europe, with real winters and humid summers, while Sicily and the south run hot and dry for much of the year — meaning "Italian weather" is really several distinct climate strategies operating under one flag, much like Chile's own north-south spread.

Natural hazard preparedness, rather than everyday weather, is where both countries show their deepest cultural conditioning. Chile sits on one of the most seismically active stretches of the planet, and earthquake readiness isn't a niche concern but a baked-in cultural reflex — building codes are strict and seriously enforced after the lessons of past major quakes, school drills are routine from childhood, and most Chileans can recite exactly what to do in a serious tremor with the same unthinking fluency that other cultures reserve for stop-light etiquette. Italy's equivalent anxiety is more localised and water-based: Venice's acqua alta flooding has become its own seasonal ritual, with raised walkways deployed through the city and a culture of checking tide forecasts the way other places check for rain, while seismic risk in Italy is real but concentrated in specific regions (the central Apennines, Campania) rather than treated as a near-universal national expectation the way it is across the whole of Chile. Both countries, in other words, have built an entire layer of civic habit around a hazard that has nothing to do with daily temperature at all — and newcomers from more geologically placid countries tend to find this instinctive calm about genuinely serious natural risk the most quietly impressive thing about either place.

## The Verdict

Chile wins on sheer geographic drama — nowhere else can you drive from the driest desert on Earth to near-Antarctic tundra without leaving one country — but loses badly on indoor comfort, because nobody, anywhere in Chile, seems willing to simply install proper heating. Italy wins on cultural confidence — deciding, as a nation, to just close for most of August rather than grind through peak heat, is either magnificent or deeply impractical depending on whether you're the one trying to get a passport renewed that week. If you want weather that constantly surprises you, Chile delivers endlessly. If you want a culture that's made peace with its weather by simply declining to work through the worst of it, Italy has that down to an art form.

## Conclusion

Here's what both countries agree on, without ever having discussed it: fighting the weather head-on is a losing strategy, so don't bother. Chile copes by accepting enormous geographic extremity as background noise and simply under-investing in indoor heating out of what can only be described as collective denial. Italy copes by declaring a truce with August and walking away from the whole month. Neither approach would work transplanted into the other country, and neither culture has the slightest interest in adopting the other's method. They've each made their peace, on their own terms, and if you show up expecting either one to fight the climate the way your home country does, you'll just be the one still at your desk in ferragosto while everyone else is at the beach.
