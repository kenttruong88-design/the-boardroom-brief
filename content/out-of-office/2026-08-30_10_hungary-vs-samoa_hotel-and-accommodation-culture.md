# One Country Puts a Thermal Spa in Every Hotel, the Other Puts You Ten Feet From the Ocean With No Walls at All

🇭🇺 Hungary vs 🇼🇸 Samoa — Hotel and Accommodation Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Hungary has built an entire hospitality identity around its geothermal geology — grand, historic bathhouse hotels where a soak in mineral-rich thermal water is treated as a core amenity, not a spa upsell, in a tradition stretching back to Ottoman-era bathhouses that never really went out of fashion. Samoa has built the opposite kind of accommodation entirely: the beach fale, an open-sided, thatched-roof hut a few steps from the sand, with a woven mat for a bed, a mosquito net for a wall, and the ocean doing most of the interior decorating.

I have soaked in Budapest's Széchenyi Baths at 8am in genuinely freezing winter air, steam rising off the yellow neo-baroque building, feeling like I'd wandered into a very warm, very wet 19th-century painting. I have also slept in a Samoan beach fale with no actual walls, woken up to the sound of waves roughly eight metres from where my head had been, and realised I hadn't missed air conditioning even once.

## Do's & Don'ts: Hungary

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Choose a hotel with direct thermal bath access — it's a genuinely different experience from a regular spa | Rush the bath experience — Hungarians treat a proper soak as an unhurried, multi-hour ritual |
| Try a historic bathhouse hotel in Budapest, like those near Széchenyi or Gellért, for full atmosphere | Assume thermal bathing is only a summer activity — winter bathing, steam rising into cold air, is iconic |
| Book ahead during peak spa season — the best historic bath hotels fill up quickly | Wear regular swimwear rules loosely — some baths have specific dress or cap requirements |
| Ask staff about the mineral properties of different pools — each often targets different ailments | Expect fast turnover service — Hungarian hospitality tends toward a slower, more formal pace |

## Do's & Don'ts: Samoa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Embrace the open-air fale experience — it's the authentic, deliberately minimal way to stay near the coast | Expect Western hotel-standard privacy or soundproofing — fales are intentionally open |
| Respect village protocols (fa'a Samoa) around noise, dress, and Sunday observance near village-run fales | Wander into village land uninvited — many beach fales sit on customary land with local etiquette attached |
| Bring insect repellent and a light sarong — practical essentials for open-air sleeping | Assume all accommodation is beach fale style — Samoa also has conventional resort hotels |
| Ask your host about local customs before your stay — most beach fale operations are family-run | Underestimate how early village life starts — mornings begin at dawn, quiet hours matter |

## Hungary: Bathing as a National Hospitality Tradition

Hungarian hotel culture is inseparable from the country's genuinely unusual geothermal geology — Hungary sits atop one of the richest concentrations of thermal springs in Europe, and Budapest alone has more thermal baths than almost any other capital city on Earth, a legacy that stretches back through Ottoman-era bathhouses to the grand neo-baroque and Art Nouveau bath palaces built during the Austro-Hungarian era. This history has shaped hotel culture directly: many of the city's most iconic hotels are built around or directly connected to historic thermal baths, treating a soak in mineral-rich water not as a luxury spa add-on but as a genuine core amenity, woven into the pace and rhythm of the stay itself.

The ritual carries real cultural weight beyond simple relaxation — Hungarians have historically ascribed specific therapeutic properties to different mineral springs, and the tradition of visiting a bath regularly, sometimes multiple times a week, for genuine health reasons rather than pure leisure, persists among older generations especially. Winter bathing is, if anything, the more iconic version of the experience — steam rising visibly off outdoor thermal pools into freezing air, bathers playing chess on floating boards at Széchenyi in scenes that have become genuinely internationally recognisable images of the city.

Hospitality pacing in Hungarian hotels tends to reflect this unhurried bathing culture more broadly — service is generally formal, thorough, and not built for rushed turnover, an approach that occasionally frustrates visitors used to fast, transactional hotel interactions but rewards those willing to slow down and treat a stay, much like a bath, as something not to be rushed through.

## Samoa: Open-Air Simplicity as a Deliberate Choice

Samoan accommodation culture, at least along its most iconic coastal stretches, runs on a philosophy almost entirely opposite to Hungary's ornate bathhouse tradition: the beach fale, a simple, open-sided structure with a thatched or tin roof, woven mats for bedding, and mosquito netting standing in for actual walls, positioned as close to the waterline as practically possible. This isn't budget austerity so much as a deliberate design choice rooted in traditional Samoan architecture, adapted specifically for tourism — the openness maximises airflow in a tropical climate, keeps the ocean audible and visible at all times, and reflects a broader Samoan cultural comfort with communal, unenclosed living spaces that predates any tourist demand for it.

Most beach fale operations are small, family-run businesses situated on customary village land, which means a stay here comes bundled with real cultural etiquette rather than the anonymous transaction of a chain hotel — respecting fa'a Samoa (the Samoan way), including appropriate dress near villages, quiet observance of Sunday as a day of rest and church attendance in many communities, and general deference to the family hosting you, forms an implicit part of the accommodation experience whether or not a guest is told this explicitly in advance.

The trade-off for this intimacy and simplicity is a genuine absence of the privacy and soundproofing a Western hotel guest might take for granted — conversations, weather, and the ocean itself are all fully audible, and adjusting to sleeping essentially outdoors, mosquito net notwithstanding, takes most first-time visitors a night or two. Samoa does also offer more conventional resort-style hotels for travellers who want the option, but the beach fale remains the accommodation experience most closely associated with the country, precisely because it offers something no air-conditioned resort room can replicate.

## The Verdict

Hungary offers accommodation built around centuries of geothermal tradition, formal, mineral-rich, and unhurried, treating a hotel stay as inseparable from a genuine wellness ritual. Samoa offers the radical opposite: accommodation stripped down to almost nothing beyond a roof and a mat, built around proximity to the ocean and genuine cultural intimacy with the family hosting you. Hungary wins for anyone who wants their hotel stay to feel like a 19th-century wellness pilgrimage. Samoa wins for anyone who's ever wanted to fall asleep to actual waves instead of a white noise machine pretending to be them.

## Conclusion

Hungary teaches you that hospitality can be built around centuries of geology and tradition, slow, mineral-rich, and worth the unhurried pace it demands of you. Samoa teaches you the opposite lesson just as convincingly — that sometimes the best accommodation is the one that gets entirely out of the way and lets the ocean do the talking. Go to Hungary and soak until your fingers prune. Go to Samoa and just listen; the walls were never the point.
