# Addis Ababa Has Never Heard of a Heatwave and Tunis Has Never Recovered From One

🇪🇹 Ethiopia 🇹🇳 Tunisia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Everyone arrives in Africa packing for the Africa of their imagination — linen, sunscreen, a hat bought specifically for the occasion — and one of these two countries will make that hat look deeply foolish. Addis Ababa sits at 2,300-odd metres above sea level and behaves accordingly: cardigans at dinner, a jacket for the walk home, nights dipping toward 3°C in January while you stand there in your safari shirt wondering which continent you've landed on. Ethiopia's highlands never got the memo about being tropical, and nobody there seems remotely sorry about it.

Tunisia, meanwhile, is exactly as hot as you feared and then, with a flourish, hotter. Its summers don't simply climb into the thirties — they erupt past 40°C and stay there, while the sirocco, known locally as the chehili, rolls in off the Sahara and hurls desert dust into the mix for good measure. One country asks you to layer up. The other asks you to surrender.

## Do's & Don'ts: Ethiopia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pack real layers — a fleece or jacket earns its keep every evening | Don't assume "Africa" means you can leave the jumpers at home |
| Carry a compact umbrella from June to September for the kiremt rains | Don't schedule anything time-sensitive during a July downpour — roads flood fast |
| Ask your landlord about heating before signing anything | Don't expect central heating even in brand-new apartments — most simply lack it |
| Use sunscreen on cool, overcast days — altitude intensifies UV | Don't mistake mild temperatures for mild sun — you'll burn at 18°C too |

## Do's & Don'ts: Tunisia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Close shutters and blinds on sun-facing rooms from morning | Don't ventilate during the day — you'll just invite the oven air in |
| Schedule errands before 11am or after 4pm in summer | Don't go outside midday in July or August without good reason |
| Keep bottled water and electrolytes on hand during sirocco spells | Don't assume the coast is cooler — sirocco heat and dust reach Tunis too |
| Open windows wide once evening air actually cools | Don't expect air conditioning everywhere — use is often restricted off-season |

## Ethiopia: The Country That Broke the African Weather Stereotype

Here is the thing nobody tells you before landing in Addis Ababa: you are about to be cold, and not in the charming, occasional way. The city sits at roughly 2,300 metres, high enough that the thin air refuses to trap heat the way it does at sea level, producing what guidebooks call "the city of eternal spring" — daytime highs around 22–24°C, nighttime lows regularly in single digits, and a January low that can scrape down to 3°C. It is mild by any reasonable definition. It is also a bait-and-switch of spectacular proportions for anyone who packed for savannah heat.

Locals cope by layering intelligently and building days around it: markets wind down before the evening chill, and the gabi — a thick white cotton shawl — goes on the moment the sun dips, which happens with Swiss-train punctuality year-round. What catches newcomers out isn't the cold itself but the missing infrastructure: central heating is essentially nonexistent, even in gleaming new apartments, so expats fall back on space heaters, extra blankets, and an earlier bedtime.

Then there's kiremt, the rainy season from June through September, when up to 80% of annual rainfall arrives in a sustained daily assault, turning dirt roads to soup and clogging drains choked with eight months of dry-season dust. Addis residents call this stretch "winter" and the dry months "summer" — a telling inversion of everything a visitor assumes about the calendar. The altitude sun, despite the cool air, is unforgiving; sunburn at 18°C surprises almost everyone exactly once.

## Tunisia: Where the Sahara Sends Its Regards

Tunisia's weather problem has a name that changes depending on who you ask — sirocco to the rest of the Mediterranean, ghibli or chehili if you're Tunisian — but the effect is identical: a hot, dry wind barrels up from the Sahara, shoves temperatures 10–15°C above whatever was already uncomfortable, and drags a curtain of dust along with it. Wind speeds can hit 100 km/h, arriving most often in spring and summer, exactly when Tunisia least needs an extra ten degrees. Add summer highs already between 30 and 38°C, with interior peaks cresting 45°C, and the climate doesn't ease you in — it detonates around you.

Tunisians have spent generations building a culture engineered around not melting. Older medina architecture favours thick stone walls, small high windows, and courtyards that trap cool air and exhale the hot — passive cooling that predates the compressor by centuries. Shutters slam shut the moment morning light hits a wall, windows stay sealed until evening temperatures drop into the low-to-mid twenties, and only then does cross-ventilation become worth attempting. Errands cluster into early morning and late afternoon; the hours between are a write-off.

The catch for expats is that air conditioning is neither universal nor unconditional — its use is often informally restricted outside peak summer — meaning the passive-cooling tricks aren't a quaint cultural footnote, they're load-bearing. Hydration, loose natural fabrics, and accepting that the sirocco will occasionally cancel your plans outright are survival skills, not suggestions.

## The Verdict

If you want weather that behaves like a reasonable adult, Ethiopia wins without breaking a sweat — quite literally. Addis Ababa's highland mildness is one of the most underrated facts in relocation writing, a genuine city of eternal spring nobody markets correctly because "bring a jacket to Africa" doesn't sell package holidays. The only tax you pay is psychological: recalibrating your mental model of this continent's weather, and buying a space heater nobody warned you you'd need.

Tunisia doesn't lose so much as demand something different — resilience, a strict relationship with your own shutters, and zero illusions about what "Mediterranean" means in August. It's not a gentler climate doing you a favour; it's one you must out-strategise daily. Both countries solved their weather problems centuries before thermostats existed: Ethiopia with altitude and a shawl, Tunisia with stone, geometry, and stubbornness. Only one lets you skip the fight altogether.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Tales from a Small Planet — "Real Post Report" contributors on Addis Ababa consistently flag that the city gets genuinely cold at night, and new arrivals keep showing up dressed for a continent that doesn't exist at this altitude.*

> *Quora, on expat life in Tunisia — several answers name the sirocco as the real shock: the air turns bone-dry and thick with dust in a way no amount of pre-arrival reading quite prepares you for.*

> *Tripadvisor's Tunisia Forum — a recurring thread theme is confusion over air conditioning timing, with visitors surprised that AC isn't switched on everywhere until well into summer, regardless of how hot May already feels.*

## Conclusion

Both countries have been quietly correct about their own weather for a very long time; it's outsiders who keep getting it wrong. Ethiopia never asked to be filed under "hot continent" — its highlands have produced jumper-weather evenings since long before anyone drew the map. Tunisia never promised a gentle Mediterranean afternoon in August; the sirocco has been showing up on schedule for millennia, dust included. Want predictable mildness with the occasional monsoon inconvenience? Go highland. Want drama, architecture that outsmarts the sun, and a wind with its own menacing nickname? Go coastal-meets-Sahara. Just don't pack the same suitcase for both. That hat is not going to save you in either country, for entirely opposite reasons.
