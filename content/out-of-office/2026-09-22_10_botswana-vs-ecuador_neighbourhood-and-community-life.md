# One Country Settles Neighbourhood Disputes at a Village Meeting Where Literally Anyone Can Speak. The Other Just Paints a Mural and Calls It Resolved

🇧🇼 Botswana vs Ecuador 🇪🇨

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Botswana's kgotla is one of the more genuinely impressive pieces of functioning direct democracy I've encountered anywhere — a village assembly where chiefs preside but anyone, regardless of age or education, can speak, challenge, or even respectfully object to the chief's own actions. Ecuador's barrios, meanwhile, express community identity through something louder and more visual: murals, street art, artisan traditions passed down through specific blocks for generations, community expressed as aesthetic as much as governance. One country debates its way to consensus. The other paints its way to belonging.

I've sat quietly at the edge of a kgotla gathering in rural Botswana, deeply aware I had no standing to speak, and I've wandered San Roque in Quito reading protest murals that function as a kind of neighbourhood diary. Both taught me more about "community" than any tourist board pamphlet ever has.

## Do's & Don'ts: Botswana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend a kgotla gathering respectfully as an observer if invited — it's a genuine, functioning civic institution, not a performance | Interrupt or speak out of turn if you're a guest — the format relies on people speaking in sequence, respected by all present |
| Understand that botho — mutual respect and shared humanity — underpins the entire system, not just its formal rules | Assume the kgotla is purely ceremonial or historical — it remains an active decision-making body in many communities today |
| Appreciate that even challenges to a chief's own actions can be raised, as long as they're done with proper respect | Mistake the kgotla's openness for informality — the consensus-driven process is structured and taken seriously by everyone involved |

## Do's & Don'ts: Ecuador

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Walk through barrios like San Marcos and San Roque slowly — the murals, workshops, and street life are the actual point | Treat historic barrios as static tourist sets — they're lived-in neighbourhoods with real, ongoing daily rhythms |
| Visit La Ronda in the evening when its restaurants, bars, and craft shops come alive | Assume every colonial-era street looks the same — each barrio (La Tola, San Marcos, San Roque) carries a genuinely distinct identity and history |
| Look closely at street murals — many carry explicit social and political commentary specific to that neighbourhood's history | Photograph residents' homes or private spaces without asking, even in famously photogenic barrios |

## Botswana: Democracy You Can Actually Watch Happen

The kgotla is, without exaggeration, one of the more remarkable pieces of living civic infrastructure I've come across in this job. It's the traditional village meeting place — practically and symbolically the most important spot in any community — where social, judicial, and political matters get discussed and resolved, openly, in front of everyone. What sets it apart from most other "traditional" governance structures still limping along elsewhere is that it hasn't been hollowed out into ceremony. It functions. People genuinely use it to settle real disputes, plan real communal decisions, and hold real leaders accountable.

The rule that makes this genuinely radical, even by contemporary democratic standards, is that everyone present has the right to speak, regardless of age, gender, or education — a principle that was operating in rural Botswana long before most Western democracies extended anything resembling universal voice to their own citizens. Even actions taken by the chief himself can be challenged at a kgotla, provided the challenge is delivered with appropriate respect — a system that manages to be simultaneously hierarchical (chiefs preside, elders facilitate) and radically egalitarian (anyone can speak) without those two things collapsing into contradiction. This balance rests on the concept of botho — a foundational value of mutual respect and shared humanity that underlies not just kgotla proceedings specifically, but Botswana's broader social contract.

Historically, kgotla meetings decided everything from planting schedules to grazing land management to punishment for community offences, always weighing decisions against both environmental and social impact — an early, genuinely sophisticated form of participatory resource management. Villages themselves were traditionally built around these considerations too, often sited defensively in hilly areas or near reliable water sources, with homesteads of circular reed-and-mud huts clustered in a way that reinforced, physically, the same communal logic the kgotla enforced socially. Contemporary Botswana still treats the kgotla as functionally indispensable — no viable community governance structure exists without it, even as the country has modernised around it in almost every other respect.

## Ecuador: Community Life Written on the Walls

Quito's historic barrios operate on a completely different register of community expression — less about formal deliberative process, more about identity accumulated, quite literally, on the walls and streets themselves over centuries. Take La Tola, home to roughly 5,000 residents and rife with its own local legends, where excellent food, ever-rotating art exhibitions, and generations of artisan tradition have built a neighbourhood identity that residents actively maintain rather than simply inherit. Or San Marcos, where residents genuinely outnumber visitors and daily life plays out visibly among centuries-old buildings, home to generations of artists, musicians, and writers who've given the area's streets a distinct, ongoing creative pulse rather than a preserved-in-amber historical vibe.

La Ronda offers a slightly different flavour of the same phenomenon — a small street within the historic centre with roots stretching back to Incan times, transformed under Spanish colonial rule and now a lively corridor of restaurants, bars, craft shops, and art galleries that comes properly alive in the evenings. What's notable is that this liveliness isn't a manufactured tourist experience bolted onto a museum piece; it's a genuine continuation of the street's centuries-long role as a social gathering point, just with contemporary business models layered over an old civic function.

San Roque takes the visual-identity angle furthest. Founded at the end of the 18th century specifically as housing for the city's artisan class, it remains, to this day, defined by colour and public art — walls decorated not with decorative murals for their own sake, but with explicit messages of resistance and social struggle, turning the neighbourhood's physical fabric into an ongoing, publicly legible record of its own political consciousness. This is community life as visual archive: you can read a barrio's history, values, and current concerns directly off its walls, in a way that requires no formal meeting, no elected spokesperson, no kgotla-style gathering to communicate. The message is simply already there, painted at eye level, for anyone willing to actually look.

## The Verdict

Botswana wins on genuine democratic substance — a functioning, egalitarian civic institution that has survived colonialism, modernisation, and urbanisation while still doing real governance work is a rare and remarkable thing, and treating it as folklore rather than an active system undersells it badly. Ecuador wins on sheer expressive richness — barrios that communicate identity, resistance, and belonging through art and lived daily rhythm rather than formal process offer a different but equally valid model of what community actually means. If you want to watch democracy function in something close to its purest, most participatory form, Botswana's kgotla will humble you. If you want community identity you can literally read off a wall while eating exceptional street food, Quito's barrios have been writing their own story for centuries, mural by mural.

## Conclusion

Botswana and Ecuador both demonstrate that "community" is something actively built and maintained, not a passive byproduct of people simply living near each other. One does it through a centuries-old, still-functioning assembly where literally anyone can have their say. The other does it through generations of accumulated street art, artisan tradition, and neighbourhood-specific identity you can trace on foot. Show genuine respect to both systems — sit quietly at a kgotla if you're lucky enough to observe one, and look properly, not just photograph reflexively, at Quito's murals. Community, done properly, was never going to explain itself to you in English on a plaque.
