# One Country Celebrates With Horseback Wrestling. The Other Celebrates by Shutting Down an Entire Capital for Wine.

🇰🇬🇲🇩 By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office

Kyrgyzstan marks its biggest festivals by putting men on horses and having them wrestle over a goat carcass, which is, genuinely, one of the more honest expressions of national character I've encountered anywhere. Moldova marks its biggest holiday by closing central Chisinau to traffic and turning the entire city into a single, sprawling wine tasting. Both countries understand that a proper celebration requires total commitment. They simply differ on whether that commitment involves livestock.

I've watched buzkashi from a hillside in the Tian Shan mountains with a bowl of fermented mare's milk I hadn't asked for, and I've been handed more free wine in one October afternoon in Chisinau than most countries offer in a calendar year. Neither experience left me entirely sober, in one sense or another.

## Do's & Don'ts: Kyrgyzstan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Time a visit around Nowruz (March) or the World Nomad Games | Expect fixed, predictable scheduling — dates shift with lunar and local factors |
| Accept kymyz (fermented mare's milk) if offered during celebrations | Refuse hospitality outright; it's genuinely considered impolite |
| Book yurt stays early during festival season — availability vanishes fast | Assume city hotels near festival sites will have late-notice rooms |
| Dress for dramatic temperature swings at high-altitude festival sites | Underestimate mountain weather even in what looks like a summer month |

## Do's & Don'ts: Moldova

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan around National Wine Day (first weekend of October) deliberately | Try to drive through central Chisinau that weekend — roads close entirely |
| Book Chisinau accommodation months ahead of Wine Day | Assume last-minute rooms will be available at any reasonable price |
| Visit Cricova or Mileștii Mici wine cellars as part of the holiday | Skip the cellars thinking the city tasting alone covers it — it doesn't |
| Pace yourself; tastings are generous and numerous | Treat it as a casual afternoon — it's an all-day, structured commitment |

## Kyrgyzstan: Festivals Built on Nomadic Memory

Kyrgyzstan's festival calendar draws directly from a nomadic heritage that the country wears with evident, unforced pride. Nowruz, the Persian and Central Asian new year celebration in late March, marks the arrival of spring with communal meals, traditional games, and a warmth of hospitality that borders on overwhelming for anyone unused to having food and drink pressed on them by total strangers within minutes of arriving anywhere. Refusing it, incidentally, is a genuine faux pas — you drink the kymyz, you eat the plov, and you thank your hosts with real enthusiasm, whatever your actual opinion of fermented mare's milk turns out to be.

The country's signature event, though, is the World Nomad Games — a biennial spectacle held near Issyk-Kul that gathers competitors from across Central Asia and beyond for events like kok-boru (a fierce, full-contact game played on horseback with a goat carcass in place of a ball), traditional wrestling, and eagle hunting demonstrations that feel like stepping directly into a National Geographic feature rather than a tourist attraction. It's genuinely spectacular, genuinely under-visited by Western tourists relative to its scale, and genuinely logistically demanding — yurt camps fill up fast, mountain weather turns without warning, and the whole event operates on a scale and remoteness that rewards flexibility over rigid planning.

What makes Kyrgyz festival culture distinct is how unmediated it feels. There's little sense of performance for an outside audience; these are celebrations Kyrgyz communities would hold with or without visitors watching, which gives the whole experience an authenticity that's increasingly rare and, frankly, occasionally a little intimidating for a foreigner trying to figure out the correct etiquette for horseback goat-wrestling spectatorship in real time.

## Moldova: A National Holiday That Runs on Wine, Literally

Moldova's National Wine Day, held the first weekend of October, is less a festival than a genuine act of civic infrastructure: central Chisinau closes to traffic entirely, hundreds of wineries set up stalls along the main boulevards, and the city transforms into an open-air tasting event on a scale that reflects Moldova's genuine, deeply held identity as one of the world's oldest and most underrated wine-producing nations. This isn't tourist theatre — it's a national point of pride, backed by a wine industry that dates back thousands of years and includes Mileștii Mici, home to the world's largest wine cellar by volume, an actual underground city of tunnels stretching for kilometres beneath the countryside.

The day itself is structured, almost ceremonially so: opening remarks, folk performances, and then hours of methodical tasting as visitors move from stall to stall, sampling wines that range from serious, internationally competitive vintages to homemade family producers' offerings that will never leave the country but are, in their own way, just as compelling. The generosity is real and, for the unprepared, genuinely dangerous — pacing yourself isn't optional advice, it's survival strategy for anyone hoping to still be upright by early evening.

Beyond Wine Day, Moldova's public holiday calendar leans heavily on Orthodox Christian observances and a set of national days still finding their footing following independence, but nothing matches Wine Day's scale or symbolic weight. It's a holiday that tells you almost everything you need to know about Moldovan self-image: a small, often-overlooked country insisting, loudly and with excellent evidence, that it deserves recognition for one of the things it does genuinely, exceptionally well.

## The Verdict

If I want spectacle rooted in something ancient and largely untouched by tourism marketing, Kyrgyzstan's Nowruz and the World Nomad Games win outright — there's nothing else quite like watching kok-boru unfold against a backdrop of the Tian Shan mountains. But if I want a holiday that's genuinely, structurally fun to participate in rather than simply observe, Moldova's Wine Day takes it. Buzkashi is spectacular to witness. National Wine Day is spectacular to be inside of, glass in hand, city closed around you specifically so you can enjoy it properly. I know which one leaves me with a better story. I also know which one leaves me with a better headache the next morning.

## Conclusion

Both countries have built holidays that function as a kind of national self-portrait, and both portraits are more honest than any tourism campaign could manage. Kyrgyzstan shows you an unbroken nomadic identity, tested against mountains and horses and games that have nothing to prove to anyone. Moldova shows you a small country that has decided, correctly, that being extraordinary at one specific thing is worth building an entire day around. Book early for both, pace yourself for one of them very specifically, and don't, under any circumstances, refuse what you're offered. In either country, that offer is the whole point.
