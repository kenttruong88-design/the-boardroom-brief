# One Country Will Hug You Before It Knows Your Name. The Other Will Judge You Silently for Breathing Too Loudly on the Subway.

🇲🇽 🇰🇷 Mexico vs South Korea
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Mexico and South Korea have built two of the most sophisticated public-behaviour systems on Earth, and they point in almost exactly opposite directions. Mexican public life runs hot — loud, tactile, generous with eye contact and physical affection, a culture that treats strangers as provisional friends until proven otherwise. South Korean public life runs on a code of restraint so precise it borders on choreography, where the highest compliment you can pay a fellow commuter is to make them completely forget you exist.

I have been hugged by a Mexico City taco vendor I'd known for ninety seconds, and I have been silently, comprehensively judged by an entire Seoul subway car for the crime of my phone making an audible notification sound. Both reactions were, within their own logic, completely appropriate. Learning which country you're in before you calibrate your volume is not optional; it's survival.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet with warmth — a handshake minimum, often a cheek kiss among acquaintances | Rush a greeting or skip small talk before business |
| Expect and return physical warmth — a hand on the arm, a hug, is normal | Be alarmed by loud, animated conversation in public — it's enthusiasm, not conflict |
| Make eye contact and smile at strangers; it's read as basic decency | Assume silence in a group means something is wrong — it rarely does |
| Arrive with some flexibility on timing for social gatherings | Take offence at directness about your appearance, weight, or age — it's not meant as insult |

### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep your voice low on public transport, always | Talk on the phone loudly on the subway — it's a minor social crime |
| Bow slightly and use two hands when giving or receiving items from elders | Make prolonged eye contact with strangers — it can read as confrontational |
| Queue precisely and wait your turn without exception | Blow your nose in public — step away or turn aside, it's considered impolite |
| Offer your seat to elders on public transport without being asked | Show public displays of affection beyond hand-holding — it draws real disapproval |

## Mexico: Warmth as Default Setting

Public behaviour in Mexico operates on the assumption that strangers are simply friends you haven't been introduced to yet, and the entire texture of daily life reflects it. Greetings are physical and unhurried — a handshake at minimum, often a cheek kiss between women or between men and women who've met even once before, and skipping this step to get straight to business is read as borderline rude, a refusal of the basic human warmth the interaction is supposed to include.

Volume, too, works differently. A Mexican family dinner, a market stall, a bus queue — all of it happens at a decibel level that would read as an argument in Seoul but is, in context, simply enthusiasm, the normal register of people who are engaged with each other and unafraid to show it. Directness about physical appearance — "you've put on weight," "you look tired" — lands as observation rather than insult, delivered with genuine affection rather than judgment, though it takes real recalibration for anyone raised on Anglo-American politesse.

What surprises newcomers most is the sheer tactility of daily interaction — a hand on the forearm during conversation, a hug from someone you've met twice, physical closeness in queues and on transport that would trigger a personal-space violation in half the world. It comes from a genuine cultural conviction that emotional distance is the real rudeness, and once you stop flinching at it, Mexican public life feels like being embraced by an entire society, which, more or less, it is. The trade-off is a lower tolerance for the kind of icy, task-focused efficiency prized elsewhere — small talk is not a delay before the real interaction, it is the real interaction, and trying to skip it will get you nowhere, socially or practically.

## South Korea: Restraint as Respect

South Korean public behaviour runs on an entirely different logic: that shared space is a kind of collective resource, and the considerate citizen minimises their footprint in it. The subway is the clearest expression of this — priority seats for elders are respected with real consistency, phones are set to silent as a baseline assumption rather than a courtesy, and a loud phone call in a train car draws the kind of quiet, collective disapproval that needs no words to land.

Hierarchy shapes nearly every public interaction, expressed through small physical grammar — a slight bow, two hands offering or receiving an item, a slightly lowered head when passing in front of someone older. These aren't performed for show; they're deeply internalised markers of respect that Koreans extend to each other automatically and that foreigners are expected to at least attempt, however imperfectly. Queuing, similarly, is treated as close to sacred — cutting a line, even accidentally, produces a level of quiet, controlled disapproval that somehow feels worse than being shouted at.

Eye contact, too, works in reverse of the Mexican default: prolonged eye contact with a stranger can read as a challenge or an intrusion rather than a friendly gesture, and the safer default in public is a kind of practised, comfortable invisibility. Public affection beyond hand-holding draws real, if unspoken, disapproval, particularly from older generations, and blowing your nose in public is treated with a mild horror disproportionate to the act itself. What nobody explains upfront is how much genuine warmth exists underneath this restraint — expressed instead through acts of consideration, an offered seat, a held door, a stranger who quietly points out you've dropped something rather than through any Mexican-style physical or verbal warmth. It's a culture that shows care by taking up less space, not more.

## The Verdict

Mexico wins if your idea of a functioning society is one where strangers hug you within a week and directness about your love handles is a form of intimacy. South Korea wins if your idea of a functioning society is one where nobody's phone rings on the subway and an entire train car can coordinate perfect silence without a single announcement. I have never felt more instantly welcomed than in Mexico City, and I have never felt more genuinely, collectively respected than on a silent Seoul subway where a stranger gave up their seat for an elderly woman without being asked, without eye contact, without a word. Both are forms of care. They just wear completely different clothes.

## Conclusion

Mexico and South Korea have each built a coherent, functioning theory of how strangers should treat each other in shared space — they've just landed on opposite answers. One says warmth first, volume welcome, physical closeness as connection. The other says restraint first, silence as consideration, hierarchy as the grammar of respect. Arrive in either expecting the other's rules and you'll either come across as cold and standoffish in Mexico City, or loud and invasive in Seoul. Learn the local register fast — your elbows, your volume, and your willingness to hug strangers all need recalibrating at the border.
