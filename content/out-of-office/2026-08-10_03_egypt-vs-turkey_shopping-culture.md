# Everyone Will Try to Sell You a Carpet, Only One Country Means It Kindly

🇪🇬 Egypt vs 🇹🇷 Turkey — Shopping Culture
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nothing calibrates your tolerance for commerce-as-theatre quite like being called "my friend" by a stranger holding a rug the size of a small car. Egypt and Turkey have both built entire retail economies on the premise that shopping is a performance with a plot, a cast, and an expected runtime, and expats who arrive assuming a price tag means what it says are in for a rude, occasionally hilarious awakening.

I've been talked into buying things I didn't want in both countries, by vendors who I would, frankly, follow into battle. But the choreography is different enough that treating Cairo's Khan el-Khalili and Istanbul's Grand Bazaar as interchangeable is the fastest way to overpay in one and underestimate the other.

## Do's & Don'ts: Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Start your counter-offer at roughly a third of the asking price | Pay the first number quoted — it is, structurally, a fiction |
| Accept the offered tea and treat the haggle as a social visit | Rush the exchange — impatience reads as weakness, and you'll pay for it |
| Bring cash in small denominations for fixed-price grocery stalls | Assume every stall is negotiable — some genuinely aren't, ask first |
| Walk away mid-haggle if the price stalls — it usually improves | Get openly frustrated; irritation is the one move that ends the game badly |

## Do's & Don'ts: Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat bazaar haggling as friendly banter, not a fight | Haggle in a fixed-price mall — you will just look confused |
| Compare a few stalls selling the same item before committing | Buy the first "genuine" leather jacket you're shown |
| Learn a few Turkish numbers — it visibly shifts the price | Assume tourist-zone prices reflect what locals actually pay |
| Enjoy the apple tea, it's part of the ritual, not a trap | Feel obligated to buy just because you accepted tea |

## Egypt: The Price Is a Conversation Opener, Not an Answer

Khan el-Khalili operates on a pricing model best described as aspirational fiction: the first number you're quoted bears a loose, almost sentimental relationship to what the vendor actually expects to receive, and everyone in the transaction knows this, which is precisely what makes it work. Tourists who pay full asking price aren't admired for their generosity, they're quietly regarded as marks, and the vendor who successfully overcharges you will absolutely tell the story to the next stall over.

What catches newcomers off guard is how personal the whole exchange is designed to feel. You'll be offered tea, asked where you're from, told a story about a cousin who lives in whatever city you name, and the haggling itself unfolds less like a negotiation and more like an extended, warm, mildly theatrical friendship that happens to end in a financial transaction. Rushing this is read as an insult to the form. I once tried to speed-run a haggle for a brass lamp and the vendor looked genuinely wounded, as though I'd shown up to a dinner party and eaten standing at the door.

The real skill isn't aggression, it's patience and a willingness to walk away — literally, physically leave the stall — which nine times out of ten produces a dramatically improved offer shouted after you before you've made it ten steps. Fixed-price supermarkets and modern malls, increasingly common in Cairo and Alexandria, operate on entirely different, thoroughly Western rules, and conflating the two is how you end up either haggling awkwardly over a jar of tahini in a Carrefour, or paying triple for a papyrus painting because you assumed the souvenir shop price was final. It rarely is, until it explicitly says it is.

## Turkey: Structured Chaos With Genuine Off-Ramps

Istanbul's Grand Bazaar runs a version of the same game with noticeably more structure — some 4,000 shops across sixty-odd streets, many family-run for generations, with reputations to protect that occasionally push back against pure opportunism. Haggling here is expected and enjoyed, treated openly as a form of social sport rather than a slightly uncomfortable necessity, and vendors will often name their actual acceptable floor faster than their Egyptian counterparts, because repeat tourist traffic and reputation matter more in a bazaar this internationally famous.

What nobody explains to first-time visitors is the tiered geography of Turkish shopping. The touristic core of the Grand Bazaar and the Spice Bazaar runs on inflated opening prices calibrated for foreigners; wander a few streets further into local neighbourhood markets or Kadıköy on the Asian side, and prices drop to something far closer to what residents actually pay, with far less theatrical resistance to a fair first offer. Expats who only ever shop in the tourist core end up convinced Turkey is expensive; expats who venture two tram stops away discover it very much is not.

Turkish mall culture, meanwhile, is enormous, genuinely modern, and entirely non-negotiable — Istanbul alone has well over a hundred shopping malls, and attempting to haggle over a pair of jeans in one will get you a politely baffled look rather than a counteroffer. The two retail worlds, bazaar and mall, coexist without much overlap in etiquette, and knowing which one you're standing in matters more than any bargaining technique you bring with you.

## The Verdict

Egypt rewards emotional stamina — the slower, warmer, more personal haggle, where the tea and the small talk are doing real transactional work. Turkey rewards research — knowing which street, which neighbourhood, which shop has already priced fairly so you're not haggling against a number that was never realistic to begin with. If forced to choose, I give this one to Turkey, narrowly, because the off-ramp into fairly priced local markets means you're never entirely at the mercy of tourist-zone theatre the way you sometimes are in central Cairo. But both will sell you a carpet you didn't plan on buying, and both will make you feel, briefly, like the most interesting customer they've had all week. You weren't. That's the job.

## Conclusion

Both countries have turned shopping into performance art, and both will quietly judge you for treating it like a Western supermarket transaction — silent, fast, price-as-stated. Learn to enjoy the theatre instead of resenting it: accept the tea, ask the questions, walk away when the number stalls, and stop assuming the first price is anything more than an opening line in a much longer, much funnier conversation. You'll pay less, and you'll leave with a considerably better story than "I bought a lamp."
