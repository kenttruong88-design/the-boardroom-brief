# In Pakistan the Family Votes First; in Hong Kong the Algorithm Does

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Romance in Pakistan is a group project. There is the person, there is the person's mother, and there is a rolling committee of aunts who have views on height, profession, complexion and whether the family's cooking will be understood. It is a system that has produced a great many lasting marriages and an equally great number of private, perfectly reasonable sighs. Dating exists, of course, and it is increasingly an app-assisted affair among the young and urban, but it exists in the shadow of a rather large and loving audience.

Hong Kong does romance the way it does everything else: quickly, densely and on a schedule. You meet someone through friends, an app or a rooftop in Central, you have three dates in a week because everyone is busy, and then someone mentions meeting the parents and you realise you have been auditioned. I have lived in cities where dating is a sport and cities where it is a liturgy. Pakistan and Hong Kong, to my slight alarm, are both a little of each, in opposite proportions.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇰 Pakistan ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat family as part of the courtship | Assume a relationship is a private matter |
| Be discreet in public about affection | Make a show of romance in front of elders |
| Accept that introductions often come through relatives or friends | Dismiss a family suggestion before you have met the person |
| Be clear early about intentions | Date casually without thinking about the reputational cost |

| 🇭🇰 Hong Kong ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the apps and the clubs and societies | Expect to meet people only by chance |
| Take "miss you" and "love you" seriously in Cantonese | Toss the words around as filler |
| Meet friends of friends, which is how half of Hong Kong pairs up | Ignore the social network you already have |
| Treat an invitation to meet the parents as a big signal | Turn up to it unprepared |

## 🇵🇰 Pakistan

I should declare the limits of what I have to hand. I could not reach a reliable Pakistan-specific source on dating for this piece, so what follows is general, cautious and drawn from long observation rather than a fetched page. Take it as a sketch, and ask a Pakistani colleague before you rely on any of it.

The first thing to understand is that marriage, not dating, is the organising idea. Many relationships, particularly in more traditional families, begin with an introduction through relatives or friends, and the person is evaluated as a prospective member of a household rather than as a private companion. The phrase "rishta" covers the whole business of proposals and matchmaking, and the rishta aunty is a figure of such power that she should have a diplomatic passport. Education, profession, family reputation and, to be blunt, the opinions of the elders carry real weight. It is not the whole picture, since love marriages are common and growing, and urban, educated, social-media-fluent Pakistanis meet in colleges, offices and online like anyone else. But the family is never very far from the table.

Public behaviour follows. Open affection is discouraged, couples are careful about reputation, and the tone of a courtship is often shaped by who will find out. For women the stakes are generally higher than for men, and the social scene is more often organised around mixed groups, family events and weddings than around the bar-hopping pattern an expat from London or Singapore might expect. Weddings, in fact, are the great social engine. They are where cousins, friends and prospective partners circulate under supervision, and they run for days.

For a foreigner or a returning Pakistani, the practical advice is to be clear, respectful and slow. Say what you want. Do not assume a casual dinner is a casual dinner to the other party's relatives. And do not mistake the formality for coldness, because behind the choreography you will find a society that takes commitment very seriously and has a gift for turning a stranger into a relative in about a fortnight.

## 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

Hong Kong has more sources and fewer chaperones. Expat Arrivals describes the geography of a social life: the Tsim Sha Tsui waterfront and Clock Tower for meetups in Kowloon, Lan Kwai Fong in Central for bars and clubs with plenty of expats, Wan Chai for pubs and live music, and SoHo for locals who have studied or worked abroad. Clubs and societies are the sensible route into a social circle, and the apps are Tinder, Bumble, Coffee Meets Bagel and Tantan. Casual dating is common. It is also, the guide notes, the case that traditional families may read commitment into a handful of dates sooner than you expect, and that words like "miss you" or "love you" are taken more seriously in Cantonese than the English equivalents travel.

The numbers add some colour. A Lunch Actually survey of more than 2,800 singles aged 21 to 51 across five Asian markets, reported by the Bangkok Post, found that around 60 percent of Hong Kong singles were open to dating foreigners, a figure that had stayed steady for six years, against roughly eight in ten in Thailand. Half of the Hong Kong respondents said they met partners through friends, and 31 percent used commercial dating services. The company's explanation was that Hong Kong has plenty of expats and therefore plenty of chances. I would add the caveat that Lunch Actually sells dating services, so the survey is a pitch as well as a poll.

It also found Hong Kong men were unusually relaxed about dating higher earners and better-educated women, with 87 percent open to a woman who earns more and 93 percent to one with more education, while 62 percent of Hong Kong women would date a man earning less. In a city where rents are a hostile act, perhaps everyone has made peace with the arithmetic.

The sting in the tail is the pace. Hong Kong is a city of long hours and short evenings, and dating is compressed accordingly. You will meet someone, be wildly keen for ten days, and find that the diary has defeated you both. If a person meets your friends and then your parents, take it seriously: the city's casual surface sits on top of some rather traditional foundations.

## The Verdict

Hong Kong wins for access and Pakistan wins for seriousness, and which you prefer depends on whether you are looking for a person or a partner. If you are a single expat arriving with a laptop and a weekend to fill, Hong Kong will hand you a social life by Friday, with clubs, apps and a rooftop for every mood. The infrastructure of meeting people is superb.

Pakistan makes you work for it, and what it gives back is depth. The family attention is exhausting, and also the reason a relationship there tends to arrive with a ready-made support system. I would not want to date in Karachi as a visiting stranger with no cousins. I would very much like to be married there, with all of them. For pure convenience I give the point to Hong Kong. For consequence, to Pakistan.

## Conclusion

Hong Kong will find you a date by Thursday and a diary clash by Friday. Pakistan will find you a family by Thursday and a wedding date by the weekend. Neither is wrong, and neither is a place for the faint of heart. Bring patience, a decent wardrobe, and the discipline to say what you mean in whichever language is listening, because in both cities somebody is paying close attention.
