# 🇬🇭 Ghana vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom: One Trains at Dawn in the Street, the Other Pays £80 a Month to Avoid It

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Accra at 5:30am is full of people running, stretching, and doing bodyweight circuits on patches of grass, kerbs, and the occasional unfinished construction site, for free, because the culture of fitness here never needed a membership card to begin with. London at 5:30am is full of nobody, except the small, grim, slightly self-satisfied subset who've paid a premium gym £80 a month specifically so that guilt, not community, gets them out of bed.

I have trained in both cities. One of them made me feel like I'd joined something. The other made me feel like I'd purchased something, reluctantly, on a direct debit I keep meaning to cancel.

## Do's & Don'ts: Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join an outdoor morning group if you see one — they're almost always open to newcomers and ferociously motivating | Expect air-conditioned, mirror-walled gym infrastructure everywhere; many neighbourhoods simply don't have it, and don't need it |
| Train early, before the heat builds — by 9am outdoor exercise becomes a genuinely different proposition | Underestimate the informal competitiveness; nobody announced a race, but you're absolutely in one |
| Respect that fitness here is often communal and intergenerational — you'll train next to a sixty-year-old who will humble you completely | Assume a lack of visible branded gym culture means a lack of fitness culture — it means the opposite, just unbundled from retail |

## Do's & Don'ts: United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate your gym contract terms before signing — cancellation clauses are notoriously unfriendly and worth reading twice | Sign a 12-month contract in the January rush without checking if a flexible monthly option exists; it almost always does, just not advertised |
| Try a park run (free, timed, every Saturday, nationwide) — it's one of the UK's genuinely brilliant fitness exports | Expect the same energy in your gym come February; the January crowd famously, reliably evaporates |
| Book classes in advance for popular studios — spontaneity gets you a waitlist, not a spot | Assume "all-weather" means anyone's actually running outside in February without serious convincing |

### Ghana: fitness as a shared, unbundled daily ritual

What's genuinely striking about fitness culture in Accra is how little it depends on commercial infrastructure. Yes, branded gyms exist, increasingly, catering to a growing middle class and the expat professional crowd, and they're perfectly decent. But the dominant fitness culture — the one you actually see, every single morning, across almost every neighbourhood — happens outdoors, for free, organised informally by groups of neighbours, church communities, or just people who showed up at the same patch of grass often enough that it became a standing appointment. Running groups form around a shared junction corner. Bodyweight circuits happen on verandas, half-built structures, or wherever flat ground presents itself. Nobody's tracking macros on an app; everybody's showing up, which turns out to matter more.

Corporate transferees, especially from gym-membership cultures, consistently underestimate how competitive and how social this informal structure actually is. Join one of these morning groups and you'll find yourself, within a week, unofficially enrolled in standards you didn't sign up for — pace expectations, rep counts, a sixty-year-old grandmother outpacing you on a hill rep and saying nothing about it, which somehow stings more than if she'd gloated. The heat dictates everything: the 5:30-7am window is sacred precisely because the alternative, training at midday, is genuinely unwise, and anyone who's tried it exactly once will confirm this without needing to be asked twice.

### United Kingdom: fitness as a retail product with a January spike

The UK's fitness culture runs almost entirely through formal, paid infrastructure — gym chains, boutique studios, class subscriptions — and the entire sector has, as a business model, essentially priced in the fact that most members will stop showing up by February. "New Year, New Me" isn't a joke here so much as an actual, trackable annual revenue curve that every gym operator plans around: a surge of memberships in the first week of January, a steady bleed through the month, and a famously predictable cliff by the second week of February, after which the gym floor empties out to the same reliable core who were there in November anyway.

What the UK does genuinely brilliantly — and this deserves real credit, not just my usual scorn — is Park Run: free, timed, volunteer-run 5k events happening every single Saturday morning in parks across the entire country, open to absolutely anyone regardless of ability, with a culture of genuine, unpretentious encouragement that stands in stark contrast to the slightly transactional energy of the paid gym sector. It's the closest the UK gets to Ghana's communal, unbundled fitness ethos, and it's not an accident that it's also free. Beyond that, the default mode is contractual: sign a membership, commit to a term, negotiate a cancellation clause you'll need eventually, and accept that motivation here is largely manufactured through sunk cost rather than community — you keep going because you're already paying for it, not because your neighbour will notice if you don't.

### What's actually on the plate afterward matters more than either admits

Fitness culture doesn't exist separately from food culture, and the contrast here is just as sharp as the training styles themselves. In Ghana, the post-workout meal is rarely a protein shake and a Tupperware of pre-weighed chicken breast — it's whatever the household or the street vendor nearby is already serving, jollof rice, waakye, kenkey with fish, eaten communally and without the calorie-counting anxiety that defines so much of Western fitness culture. The emphasis is on strength and function, not aesthetic optimisation, and nobody's tracking macros on an app because nobody's being sold the idea that they should. The UK's fitness culture, particularly post-2015, has become deeply entangled with meal-prep Instagram aesthetics and macro-tracking apps, turning the gym visit into just one component of a broader, often expensive lifestyle project involving protein powder subscriptions and Tupperware. It's a genuinely different relationship with the same basic human activity — one treats training as a natural extension of daily communal life, the other has increasingly turned it into a quantified, optimised, slightly joyless personal project. Transferees moving from Accra to London often describe a specific, disorienting moment where they realise their UK colleagues are genuinely anxious about a missed gym session in a way nobody in their Ghanaian running group ever was, because the London version has quietly become tangled up with self-worth and productivity culture, while the Accra version stayed, stubbornly and happily, just exercise.

## The Verdict

Ghana wins, decisively, on culture — fitness here is woven into daily communal life in a way that costs nothing and asks only that you show up, and the social accountability that generates beats any membership contract I've ever read. The UK wins on infrastructure variety and, genuinely, on Park Run, which I will defend as one of the best free civic ideas any country has produced. But paying £80 a month to walk past a receptionist into a room of strangers, alone, on a treadmill, is not a fitness culture — it's a transaction wearing gym clothes.

## Conclusion

Fitness, it turns out, reveals a lot about how each culture organises motivation — Ghana builds it into community and timing, the UK builds it into contracts and sunk cost, and both, somehow, get people moving. If you want to actually enjoy the process, find Accra's 5:30am crowd. If you want a free, well-run, nationwide institution the Brits got unambiguously right, show up to a Park Run. Just don't sign anything in January you haven't read twice.
