# Italy Gives You a Card and a Waiting List. Spain Gives You a Queue and a Private Plan Quote

🇮🇹 Italy vs 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Italy and Spain share the Mediterranean, a love of lunch and a public health system that works beautifully until you need it urgently. Both have tax-funded national services, both have regional variation that makes the word "national" generous, and both politely suggest that foreigners buy private insurance as well. The two systems are so similar that comparing them feels like comparing two quarrelsome cousins.

And yet the differences matter to anyone relocating. One system ties you to a card issued after residency is certified, with an annual enrolment that expires on a fixed date. The other ties you to Social Security contributions and a waiting game for those not employed locally. A source note: all four pages I read are guides or tip compilations, not forum threads, and one is a sales preview, so treat the numbers as indicative.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Italy**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register at your local health authority for a tessera sanitaria | Assume the card arrives the day you do |
| Choose a family doctor after registering | Skip the GP and go straight to a specialist |
| Keep private insurance until public enrolment works | Cancel cover because the public system is "free" |
| Note the regional difference between north and south | Assume the same quality everywhere |

**Spain**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether you are employed and contributing, which gives automatic enrolment | Assume residents are covered on arrival |
| Compare private plans, which run about 50 to 200 euros a month | Buy the first quote |
| Dial 112 and ask for an English-speaking operator in an emergency | Panic about insurance before treatment |
| Budget for dental and glasses, which are not covered | Expect the public system to pay for them |

## Italy

Italy's Servizio Sanitario Nazionale dates from the 1970s, and one compilation of expat tips describes it as focused on treating the sick rather than preventing sickness. The guide sets out the mechanics: non-EU expats need private insurance, or must register with the SSN once their residence status is final and they hold a carta d'identità. You register at the local health authority for a tessera sanitaria, bringing your residence permit, tax number, ID and proof of employment, and the card is renewed yearly. After registering, you choose a family doctor.

The financial detail is a classic. One expat recalled the enrolment fee on an income-based sliding scale that tops out at 2,700 euros a year for a single person, paid in full at once and expiring on 31 December whatever month you join. Getting the card requires the commune to certify residency, which one expat said could take about three months of continuous residence. A friend reportedly waited seven months for a colonoscopy on the public system, while a Rome mother described the Gemelli public hospital as free, with private delivery rooms.

Regional governments run the system, so standards differ, with the north and centre described as stronger than the south. Prescribed medicines are free or subsidised, while over-the-counter medicines are paid in full. Emergency numbers are 118, or the EU-wide 112 if English is unavailable. The guide calls private care excellent but costly, and insurance "a must", and adds that ambulance response is fast in cities and slower in rural areas.

The sting is the timing. A system that requires a card, a certified address and an annual payment is designed for people who have settled, not people who have arrived. In your first months, you are on your own.

For families, the Italian system has one more feature worth knowing: you choose a family doctor and, if relevant, a paediatrician after registering, which gives children a named point of contact. Family members need a certificate of family status for registration, and the card is renewed yearly. The practical effect is that a relocating household has to assemble a small stack of documents, and the stack is assembled more easily with the help of an employer's relocation team or a local contact. Do not underestimate the value of someone who can attend the first appointment at the health authority and translate not only the language but the expectations. It can turn a three-visit saga into a one-visit errand.

## Spain

Spain's Sistema Nacional de Salud is funded by taxes and Social Security contributions, and automatic enrolment applies to residents and expats who are employed and contributing. A sales preview of a gated report says admission can take several months for people not employed locally, that prescription medicine is covered only about forty percent, and that dental care outside emergencies and glasses are not covered. Private plans cost about 50 to 200 euros a month, depending on age, coverage and provider.

A second guide lists the eligibility rules: EU citizens use the EHIC, working expats register with Social Security, non-EU residents must hold private insurance with no co-payments and a minimum cover it gives as 30,000 euros, EU pensioners need an S1 form, and students need cover for their whole study period. It gives public specialist waits as two to six months, though without citing a source, and names insurers including Sanitas, ASSSA, Adeslas, DKV and Cigna Global.

The emergency system is reassuring. You dial 112 and ask for an English-speaking operator, state your location and have your passport and insurance details ready. Public emergency departments treat everyone regardless of insurance, while private hospitals may ask for a payment guarantee. Public primary care runs through the Centros de Salud, and the guide recommends Doctoralia and Top Doctors Spain for finding a physician.

The same guide says private care offers same-day appointments and English-speaking staff, and notes that regional quality varies. The sting is the same as in Italy: the public system is a safety net, not a service, and the private plan is the thing you actually use. It is just that in Spain, the price of that plan is posted clearly.

Spain's emergency system is the part to memorise before you need it. Dial 112, ask for an English-speaking operator, say where you are and have your passport and insurance details ready. Public emergency departments treat everyone regardless of insurance, whereas private hospitals may ask for a payment guarantee, so it helps to know which kind of hospital the ambulance is heading for. Centros de Salud handle public primary care, and the guide names Doctoralia and Top Doctors Spain as ways to find a physician. None of this is difficult, but it is much easier to learn at a kitchen table than from a hospital corridor with a feverish child.

## The Verdict

Spain edges it for the foreign arrival, because the private market is transparent, English-friendly and priced in monthly instalments, whereas Italy's annual lump sum and residency-certification delay can feel like a tax on newcomers. Both countries offer excellent doctors and indifferent paperwork, and both reward people who plan.

For a relocating executive, the practical lesson is identical: treat the public system as a backstop, buy private cover on day one and register for the public scheme in parallel. In Italy, expect to pay a large annual sum and wait for your card. In Spain, expect a contribution-based scheme and, if you are not employed locally, a wait of several months.

If I had to pick the place to be ill, I would choose Spain, but only because the quote arrives faster. The cultural truth is that in both countries the hospital is excellent and the bureaucracy is the illness. Bring documents, bring patience and bring a friend who can read the forms.

## Conclusion

Healthcare abroad is a test of how well you read small print before you are unwell. Italy asks for a card, a certified address and an annual sum. Spain asks for contributions, patience and a private plan.

Buy cover first, register second and keep your documents in one folder. The doctors in both countries are better than the paperwork deserves.
