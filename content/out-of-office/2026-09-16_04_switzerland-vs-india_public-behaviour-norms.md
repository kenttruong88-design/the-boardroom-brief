# One Country Will Silently Judge You for Jaywalking on an Empty Street, the Other Has Never Heard of a Queue

🇨🇭 Switzerland vs India 🇮🇳
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Zurich, I once crossed an entirely empty road against a red pedestrian light at 11 p.m., saw no cars for half a kilometre in either direction, and still felt the full disapproving weight of a woman on a balcony who witnessed the entire crime. In Mumbai, I have stood in what I believed was a queue for a train ticket window, only to discover forty minutes later that the concept of "queue" was, at best, a loose spiritual suggestion rather than a binding social contract.

Switzerland operates on the assumption that rules exist to be followed precisely, silently, and by everyone, all the time, with public shaming as the enforcement mechanism of last resort. India operates on a far more fluid, negotiated, high-context system of personal space and public order that looks like chaos to the untrained eye and is, in fact, its own deeply functional logic. Both will make you feel, at various points, like the worst-behaved person in the room.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇨🇭 Switzerland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wait for the pedestrian light, even at 2 a.m. with zero traffic | Make noise after 10 p.m. or on a Sunday — this is not a suggestion, it's practically law |
| Keep your voice down on public transport | Jaywalk, ever, even mid-block on a dead street. You will be seen |
| Separate your recycling with genuine precision | Assume "quiet hours" are flexible for a birthday party |

**🇮🇳 India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept that personal space in queues is smaller and more fluid | Expect a single-file line at a ticket counter or bus stop |
| Use a firm, friendly assertiveness to hold your place | Take mild jostling or close proximity as rudeness — it rarely is |
| Honk lightly to signal your presence, not your anger | Assume silence means agreement — verbal negotiation is normal and expected |

## Switzerland: Order as a Moral Framework

Swiss public behaviour isn't just etiquette, it's closer to a civic religion, and the jaywalking taboo is the purest distillation of it. Cross against a red light on an empty street and you won't be arrested, you'll simply be witnessed — by a retiree at a window, a mother with a pram, a teenager who somehow radiates more disapproval than either — and that witnessing carries a genuine social weight that keeps compliance astonishingly high without any visible enforcement at all. It's rule-following as a form of mutual respect: I follow the rule because you're also following it, and neither of us wants to be the crack in the system.

Noise culture takes this further. Quiet hours (Ruhezeit) after 10 p.m. and on Sundays are treated with a seriousness that borders on sacred — flush a toilet too loudly in some older apartment buildings after 10 and you risk a genuinely awkward conversation with a neighbour the next morning. Public transport is hushed almost to the point of monastic; a phone call taken at normal volume on a train draws the kind of stares usually reserved for actual crimes. Recycling, similarly, isn't a chore, it's an exam you're expected to have already passed — PET bottles here, glass sorted by colour there, and heaven help you if you mix your organic waste with the wrong bag on the wrong day.

The reward for all this precision is a genuinely astonishing baseline of public order — trains that run silently and on time, streets that feel safe at any hour, a low-grade hum of collective good behaviour that, once you adapt to it, becomes oddly soothing. The cost is a permanent, low-level anxiety that you are, at any given moment, one careless act away from disappointing an entire nation.

## India: Negotiated Space, Fluid Order

What looks like chaos in an Indian queue, market, or traffic junction is, on closer inspection, an intricate and highly functional system of negotiated proximity — space is claimed assertively, held through presence and confidence rather than a marked line on the floor, and constantly renegotiated through small verbal and physical cues that outsiders initially read as rudeness or disorder. It isn't. It's just a different operating system for shared public space, one built on high-context communication rather than rigid, universally posted rules.

Personal space itself is calibrated differently — closer proximity in queues, on public transport, in markets, is simply normal, not an act of aggression or a breach of etiquette, and Western-trained instincts to bristle at someone standing "too close" have to be actively unlearned. Honking, similarly, isn't necessarily anger, it's communication — a light tap to say "I'm here," a longer blast to say "I'm coming through," a whole vocabulary of horn use that reads as constant noise to a newcomer and as a functioning traffic language to everyone else.

The genuinely disorienting part for someone raised on British-style queue etiquette is realising that holding your place requires active, ongoing assertion, not passive waiting — step back mentally for even a moment and the gap will be filled, not out of malice, but because the space was never "yours" in the fixed sense you assumed. Once you adjust your posture, both literal and social, from passive to actively present, the entire system stops feeling chaotic and starts feeling, frankly, more honest than the polite fiction of a queue that nobody's actually enforcing anyway.

## The Verdict

Switzerland offers order as a guarantee, purchased at the price of constant low-level self-monitoring. India offers order as an ongoing negotiation, purchased at the price of constant low-level assertiveness. I have never felt safer crossing a street than in Zurich at midnight, and I have never felt more alive navigating a queue than in a Mumbai train station at rush hour. One system trusts rules; the other trusts people to work it out. Given a choice, I'd rather live somewhere the social contract is actively renegotiated daily than somewhere it's simply, silently obeyed. But I wouldn't try jaywalking in Zurich to prove the point.

## Conclusion

Switzerland will make you a quieter, more orderly, more anxious version of yourself, permanently attuned to the judgment of unseen balconies. India will make you a more assertive, more adaptable, more comfortable-with-ambiguity version of yourself, permanently unbothered by close quarters and shifting rules. Both are improvements, in their own way, on whatever passive-aggressive queuing culture you arrived with. I now cross empty streets on red lights nowhere in the world without a twinge of Swiss guilt, and I have never queued passively again in my life. You're welcome, and also, I'm sorry.
