# Canada Built a Country Around the Car; Hong Kong Built a City Around Not Needing One

🇨🇦 Canada vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Canada, commuting is a private act of endurance conducted inside a heated steel box. You scrape ice off the windscreen, you swear at a tailgater on a highway wide enough to land a Boeing, and you arrive at work having listened to an entire podcast about sourdough. The journey is long, lonely and entirely yours. Transit exists, in the way that a fire extinguisher exists: reassuring, rarely used, and a little bit embarrassing if you are seen holding it.

Hong Kong looked at all that space and decided it was a character flaw. Here, commuting is a public sport played in close quarters at speed, and the rules are known by everyone except you. Trains arrive every two minutes, minibuses barrel up hills like they owe someone money, and a tiny contactless card does the work of a wallet. I have commuted in both. Canada makes you feel in control and bored. Hong Kong makes you feel like a pinball, and weirdly, more alive.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇨🇦 Canada ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget real time for winter, and warm the car | Assume the roads are clear because the app says so |
| Learn your city's transit card (Presto, Compass and friends) | Pay cash and hold up a bus |
| Say thank you to the bus driver as you get off | Block the aisle with a hockey bag |
| Leave a gap on the highway, snow or not | Tailgate in a whiteout |

| 🇭🇰 Hong Kong ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get an Octopus card on day one | Fumble for cash and expect change on a bus |
| Keep pace with the MTR crowd, especially at interchanges | Stop dead at the top of an escalator |
| Tell the minibus driver your stop in advance | Sit silently and hope they guess |
| Have your destination written in Chinese for taxis | Rely on your accent alone |

## 🇨🇦 Canada

Canadian commuting is defined by distance and weather. Outside the cores of Toronto, Montreal and Vancouver, most people drive, because the places they need to reach were laid out by developers who assumed everybody owned a Ford. Even where transit is good, it asks for a certain faith. You check the app, you watch the little bus icon crawl across a map, and you stand at a stop at minus twenty wondering which of your life choices led you here. The bus arrives, the doors sigh open, and everyone inside is quietly, politely, immovably in their own bubble.

There is a lot to like. Etiquette is gentle. People queue, thank the driver on the way out, and give up seats without being asked twice. Cities have their own contactless cards, Presto in Ontario and Compass in Metro Vancouver, and they all work in the same slightly irritating way: you tap, it beeps, and a balance tells you how little you have left. A commute of forty minutes each way is considered normal and a commute of ninety is considered a story. Cycling exists, heroically, for about five months of the year.

What the system gives you is predictability and personal space. What it takes is time and a certain amount of your mental health. Canadians complain about commuting with the stoic good humour they apply to everything, which is to say they do it constantly and as if it were someone else's fault. The solution, in every city I have lived in, is the same: a second coffee, a longer playlist, and a firm refusal to ask whether any of this makes sense.

For the expat arriving from a denser place, the first Canadian winter is the real initiation. Public transport delays are not a failure of the system but a seasonal weather event, to be accepted like the tide. You will buy a proper coat. You will own an ice scraper. You will learn that your street is cleared on a schedule you cannot predict and that the car, for all its sins, is the only thing keeping you from hating the place. That is the deal. Canada gives you space and asks for patience in return.

## 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

Hong Kong's transport is so dense that it feels less like a network and more like a nervous system. According to Expat Arrivals, the MTR has around ten lines and roughly a hundred stations, air conditioning and mobile reception underground, and the Octopus card works on trains, buses, trams, ferries and minibuses, and in plenty of small shops besides. Contactless bank cards and phone wallets are now accepted across most of the network, but the guide still calls Octopus the handiest option, and I agree. It is the single best piece of public design I have ever owned.

Then there is everything else. Trams, which locals call Ding Ding, rattle along the north shore of Hong Kong Island from Kennedy Town to Shau Kei Wan for a flat fare, which Expat Exchange puts at HK$2.60. The Star Ferry has been crossing the harbour since 1888. Taxis come in three colours: red for most of the territory, green for the New Territories and blue for Lantau. Many drivers speak limited English, so have your address written in Chinese. Bus drivers give no change, so tap or bring exact coins. And when a Typhoon Signal No. 8 goes up, most public transport, ferries included, shuts down, which is Hong Kong's way of telling you to go home and think about your life.

The minibus is where the city shows you who you are. Green minibuses run fixed routes and fares; red minibuses set their own and will stop on request. A China Daily columnist wrote that they avoided minibuses for two years after arriving in 2013, dreading the moment you must tell the driver where you are getting off, and that learning Cantonese eventually changed the experience. The column described a ritual: say your destination when you board, call your stop in advance, and wait for the driver to raise a hand to confirm. It is a small act of trust, and it is how you stop being a tourist.

The MTR, by contrast, asks nothing of you except speed. Expat Arrivals notes that Hongkongers are perpetually in a hurry, especially at interchanges, so keep pace with the crowd. Signage is in Chinese and English, stations have multiple exits, and you need not speak to anyone for the whole journey. It is efficient to the point of being rude, in the way a surgeon is rude, and I have never once been late because of it.

## The Verdict

Hong Kong wins, and it is not close. A commute should be something you do on the way to your life, not a small separate life of its own, and Hong Kong has understood this for forty years. You can live there without owning a car, without checking a timetable, and without losing an hour of every day to traffic. That is not a lifestyle perk. It is a civil right dressed up as a card.

Canada deserves credit for scale, safety and manners. It is a lovely place to drive, if you enjoy driving, and the coffee is better. But for a corporate expat, the maths is simple. Hong Kong returns your time. Canada rents it back to you at a premium, with a seasonal surcharge. If you must choose a city to commute in, pick the one where a stranger will help you find the right exit and then run past you to catch the next train.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Edouard, a French expat quoted by Expat Arrivals — calls Hong Kong public transport very convenient, with a wide, well organised network of MTR, bus, tram, minibus and ferry that reaches almost anywhere quickly and cheaply.*

> *China Daily Hong Kong "Monday Vibes" column, a first-person newcomer — avoided minibuses for the first couple of years after arriving in 2013, dreading the stop announcement and feeling like an outsider, until improved Cantonese and a patient driver changed things.*

> *Expat Arrivals guide — keep pace with the crowd on the MTR, because Hongkongers are perpetually in a hurry, particularly at interchanges.*

## Conclusion

Canada will teach you to wait. Hong Kong will teach you to move. One is a meditation, and the other is a sprint, and both make you a better person in ways you will not thank either country for. Get the card, learn the stop announcement, buy the warm coat, and accept that you will, at some point, tell a minibus driver your destination in the wrong tone of voice. They will take you there anyway. That is the whole point of a city.
