🇮🇷 **Iran** vs 🇦🇺 **Australia**
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Iran and Australia have built two of the most elaborate public-behaviour systems on earth, and they run on almost opposite fuel. Iranian public life runs on *taarof* — a dense, beautiful, maddening choreography of ritual politeness, refusal, and re-offering that governs everything from accepting tea to paying a taxi fare. Australian public life runs on a studied, almost aggressive casualness, where the entire social contract is "don't make this weird," enforced through queueing, understatement, and an apology reflex so strong people say sorry when you step on their foot.

Drop an Iranian newcomer into an Australian queue and they'll find it eerily silent and under-negotiated. Drop an Australian into a Tehran dinner where they're being offered food for the fourth time after politely declining three times and they'll start to panic that they've accidentally agreed to something enormous. Both systems work. Neither translates without a manual, which is annoying, because nobody hands you one at the airport.

## Do's & Don'ts: Iran

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to refuse an offer two or three times before genuinely accepting — it's ritual, not rudeness | Take a first "no thank you" at face value; offer again, it's expected |
| Dress modestly in public regardless of your own background or beliefs | Assume private-home warmth and public-space caution follow the same rules |
| Greet elders and authority figures with visible deference | Rush straight to business before the social pleasantries are properly done |
| Let an older or more senior person go first through a door, in a queue, at a counter | Point your feet at someone or show the sole of your shoe — it reads as genuinely disrespectful |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Queue without being told to — it is simply assumed, everywhere, for everything | Push in, even slightly, even with a "good" reason — it will be noticed and silently judged |
| Keep a solid arm's length of personal space, even in friendly conversation | Stand too close while talking; Australians will physically step back, repeatedly, until you get it |
| Use understatement — "not bad" can mean genuinely excellent | Oversell yourself or your achievements out loud; it reads as try-hard, even when true |
| Say sorry reflexively, even when something isn't remotely your fault | Expect a direct, Western-style apology to carry the emotional weight it might elsewhere — it's often just verbal filler |

## Iran: Politeness as Architecture

*Taarof* is the word for it, but the word barely covers what it actually governs. It's the reason a shopkeeper might initially wave away payment, the reason a host will pile your plate high after you've said you're full, the reason someone might insist you take the better seat, the window seat, the last piece of cake — and mean it, while also expecting you to refuse at least once before accepting. This isn't insincerity. It's a shared script that lets everyone perform generosity and humility simultaneously, with both parties understanding the choreography and nobody actually confused by it, except the newcomer standing there wondering if they just offended someone by saying "no thanks" a single time.

Public behaviour in Iran extends this ritual politeness into visible hierarchy: deference to elders, formal greetings, careful attention to who enters a room first or who's served first at a table. None of this is rigid or cold — Iranian hospitality is justifiably legendary — but it operates inside clear, inherited rules about status and respect that are rarely spoken aloud because everyone already knows them. Public modesty norms, particularly around dress, add another visible layer that outsiders sometimes read as purely religious regulation, when for many residents it's simply the baseline of how public space is navigated, regardless of personal belief. The common mistake newcomers make is either over-literalising the refusal ritual (actually taking the first "no" and walking away, which can read as stingy) or under-reading the hierarchy cues (skipping the small, specific gestures of respect that signal you understand the room).

Queuing itself works differently too, in ways that trip up visitors from queue-obsessed cultures. Iranian queues can look, to an outsider, like barely organised scrums — but there's usually an informal hierarchy at work, where age, visible authority, or simply being first acknowledged by the person being served takes precedence over strict arrival order. This isn't chaos so much as a different organising principle, one that rewards social awareness over mechanical fairness. Visitors who insist on rigid turn-taking sometimes find themselves waiting far longer than necessary, having missed the actual, unspoken system everyone else is fluently reading.

## Australia: The Tyranny of Not Making It Weird

Australian public behaviour looks like the absence of rules, which is exactly the trap. There are rules — they're just enforced through silence, distance, and mild social consequence rather than explicit correction. The queue is sacred and self-organising; nobody manages it, and nobody needs to, because cutting it is treated with the kind of quiet, lasting disapproval usually reserved for actual crimes. Personal space is non-negotiable and will be defended, politely but firmly, by someone simply taking a step back mid-conversation until you've both recalibrated to the correct distance, no words required.

The real cultural tell is the apology reflex and the understatement habit working in tandem. An Australian will say sorry for something that is unambiguously not their fault — you stepped on them, they apologise — not out of guilt, but because "sorry" functions as social lubricant, a way of smoothing a moment rather than assigning blame. Simultaneously, genuine enthusiasm gets flattened into dry understatement: "yeah, it was alright" can describe the best meal of someone's year. Newcomers who take either literally end up either confused (why is he apologising, he did nothing wrong) or misled (he said it was just okay, but he's clearly obsessed with this restaurant). The underlying logic is consistent, though: don't be too much, in either complaint or praise, and let behaviour — showing up, queueing properly, not being a nuisance — do the talking that words are deliberately not doing.

Mateship adds another layer that confuses newcomers expecting pure reserve. Australians will rib each other mercilessly, trade insults that would read as genuinely hostile elsewhere, and treat this teasing as a clear sign of affection rather than its opposite — the colder and more formally polite someone remains, the less likely they actually like you. Working out when you've been accepted into this teasing register, rather than simply being tolerated at a polite distance, is one of the trickier social calibrations for anyone used to politeness scaling up, rather than down, with genuine warmth.

## The Verdict

Both systems are, in their own way, forms of extreme social discipline disguised as something else — Iran's as warmth and hierarchy, Australia's as casualness and understatement. Iran will make you work harder in the moment, decoding layers of ritual refusal and respect in real time. Australia will make you work harder over time, slowly learning to read enthusiasm and sincerity out of sentences explicitly designed to hide both.

If forced to choose which one I'd rather navigate hungover, it's Australia — queueing requires no vocabulary. But if I want to actually feel cared for by a stranger, Iran wins every time, refusal rituals and all.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Tehran forum — one newcomer described accidentally insulting a host by accepting food on the first offer, having been warned about the opposite mistake and overcorrecting completely.*

> *Reddit r/AskAnAustralian — a popular thread agreed that the single clearest sign of a "blow-in" was standing too close while chatting; several posters said they can spot a newcomer from the forced step back alone.*

> *Internations Sydney — a member originally from Tehran said it took nearly a year to stop reflexively offering to pay for things three times, because Australians just accepted the first offer and moved on.*

## Conclusion

Here's the thing both cultures would agree on, if either admitted to reading the other's rulebook: public behaviour is never really about the specific gesture. It's about what the gesture signals — respect, restraint, generosity, distance — and both Iran and Australia have simply chosen wildly different signals to encode the same basic human need to not seem rude in public. Learn the local signal, not the local vocabulary, and you'll do fine in either. Ignore it, and you'll spend your first six months either over-apologising or wondering why nobody will just take the cake.
