# Lima Will Make You Run Uphill Beside the Ocean. Bogotá Will Just Close the Streets and Let You Take Over the City.

🇵🇪 Peru 🇨🇴 Colombia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Lima's fitness culture is a negotiation with geography — a city built on cliffs above the Pacific, where the reward for a punishing uphill run along the Costa Verde is a view so good you almost forget your lungs are on fire. Bogotá's fitness culture is a negotiation with infrastructure — a city that simply shuts down 120 kilometres of its own streets every single Sunday and turns the entire urban grid into one enormous, car-free, city-wide gym. Both are extraordinary. Only one of them requires an act of municipal will so large it's genuinely hard to believe other cities haven't copied it wholesale by now.

I've run both — gasping up Lima's clifftop paths with the grey Pacific mist rolling in, and gliding through Bogotá's Ciclovía on a rented bike surrounded by what felt like half the city's population doing the exact same thing. Peru gives you drama and altitude. Colombia gives you scale and civic generosity. Neither will let you skip leg day gracefully, but only one of them makes the whole city complicit in your fitness routine.

## Peru: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Run or cycle the Costa Verde boardwalk early morning — cooler, safer, stunning | Attempt serious altitude training in Cusco or the Sacred Valley without acclimatising first |
| Try a paragliding launch point at Miraflores if you want adrenaline over cardio | Run alone after dark in unfamiliar neighbourhoods — stick to well-populated, well-lit routes |
| Join a local futsal or beach volleyball game — Peruvians are welcoming to newcomers | Underestimate Lima traffic when jogging near main roads — pavements can vanish without warning |

## Colombia: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join Bogotá's Sunday Ciclovía — free, city-wide, genuinely one of the best urban fitness traditions anywhere | Try to drive through Ciclovía routes on a Sunday morning; the streets are closed, plan around it |
| Rent a bike near Parque de la 93 or the Carrera Séptima for the full route experience | Skip sun protection at altitude — Bogotá sits at 2,600 metres and the sun is deceptively strong |
| Try outdoor aerobics classes that pop up along the Ciclovía route — free and open to all | Assume every neighbourhood is equally safe for an early solo run — ask locals which routes are trusted |

## Peru: Fitness Shaped by Cliffs, Altitude, and a Punishing Kind of Beauty

Lima's geography does most of the design work for its fitness culture. The city sits on dramatic clifftops overlooking the Pacific, and the Costa Verde — the coastal boardwalk and park system threading beneath and along those cliffs — has become the city's de facto outdoor gym, filled every morning with runners, cyclists, and paragliders taking advantage of the consistent onshore winds off Miraflores. The appeal is visceral rather than institutional: there's no equivalent of a citywide programme shutting down streets, just the simple, repeated fact that Lima's coastline is spectacular enough that running along it never really gets old, punishing hills notwithstanding.

Beyond Lima, Peru's fitness culture takes on an entirely different character once altitude enters the picture — Cusco and the Sacred Valley sit at elevations above 3,000 metres, and anyone attempting serious exercise there without acclimatising discovers quickly and unpleasantly why locals pace themselves the way they do. This altitude reality shapes a broader Peruvian relationship with physical exertion that's less about optimised training and more about respect for terrain — hiking the Inca Trail, working fields at altitude, or simply walking Cusco's steep colonial streets all demand a fitness that's functional and earned rather than gym-cultivated. Urban commercial gym culture exists in Lima's wealthier districts and is growing, particularly among younger professionals, but it remains secondary to the city's real fitness identity: an outdoor, geography-driven culture where the landscape itself sets the difficulty level, and the payoff is a view over the Pacific that no treadmill will ever replicate.

## Colombia: The City That Turns Itself Into a Gym Every Single Sunday

Bogotá's Ciclovía is, without exaggeration, one of the most genuinely impressive pieces of public fitness infrastructure in the world — every Sunday and on public holidays, the city closes roughly 120 kilometres of its main roads to motor traffic from 7am to 2pm, opening the entire network to cyclists, runners, skaters, and walkers. It's been running in some form since the 1970s, making it one of the oldest and largest programmes of its kind globally, and it draws well over a million participants on a good week — an extraordinary civic commitment that other cities study and attempt to replicate, rarely with the same scale or cultural buy-in.

What makes Ciclovía remarkable isn't just the road closures but the ecosystem that's grown up around them: free outdoor aerobics classes appear at intervals along the route, bike rental stalls do brisk business, street food vendors cater to the crowds, and the whole event functions as much as a social occasion as an athletic one — families, elderly couples on slow bikes, serious cyclists in full kit, and teenagers on skateboards all sharing the same closed streets without much friction. Beyond the Sunday ritual, Bogotá's altitude — sitting at 2,600 metres — means even casual exercise carries a noticeable cardiovascular tax that visiting flatlanders discover within the first ten minutes of any run, a humbling equalizer that makes the city's committed cycling culture, where locals regularly climb significant altitude on weekend rides outside the city, genuinely impressive rather than merely aspirational.

## The Verdict

Peru wins on drama — nothing beats a clifftop Pacific sunrise run for sheer visual reward, and the altitude challenge of the Andes adds a genuine physical edge that most fitness cultures never ask of you. But Colombia wins decisively on civic generosity — turning over 120 kilometres of a capital city to its own citizens' fitness, every single week, for half a century, is the kind of public commitment that should embarrass every other government that's never even attempted it. Lima gives you a stunning backdrop. Bogotá gives you the whole city, gift-wrapped, every Sunday morning.

## Conclusion

Both cities have figured out, in wildly different ways, that fitness works best when the environment does some of the persuading for you. Peru persuades you with a cliff and an ocean. Colombia persuades you by simply removing the cars and letting a million people decide what to do with the space. I'd take Ciclovía as a policy idea every other capital city should be forced to copy, but I'd take a Lima sunrise run as the better story to tell afterward. Bring sunscreen either way — altitude and equatorial sun show no mercy to anyone, however fit you think you are.
