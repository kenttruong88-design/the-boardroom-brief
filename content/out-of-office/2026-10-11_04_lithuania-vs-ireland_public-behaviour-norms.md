# Lithuania Will Let You Suffer in Silence; Ireland Will Not Let You Suffer Alone, Whether You Asked or Not

🇱🇹 Lithuania vs 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is a useful rule of thumb for Northern Europe: the closer a country sits to the Baltic Sea, the less it wants to know how your day was, and the closer a country sits to the Atlantic with a pint in hand, the more it will insist on knowing, in detail, whether you want to tell it or not. Lithuania and Ireland sit at opposite ends of a very specific spectrum: reserve versus relentless warmth, silence versus small talk as a competitive sport.

I have stood in queues in both countries. In Vilnius, the queue was a silent, orderly, almost monastic affair — nobody spoke, nobody made eye contact, and the whole thing moved with the quiet efficiency of people who have collectively decided conversation is a waste of a perfectly good queue. In Dublin, the queue became, within four minutes, a semi-formal support group, complete with unsolicited opinions on the weather, my accent, and apparently my life choices.

## Do's & Don'ts: Lithuania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Respect personal space — a comfortable gap in a queue or on a bus is not rudeness, it's courtesy | Make loud small talk with strangers on public transport; you will get polite confusion, not warmth |
| Wait to be formally introduced before switching to first-name informality | Assume reserve means unfriendliness — Lithuanians warm up considerably once you're actually known |
| Follow queue order precisely, even when it looks informal; the social contract is strict | Expect a smile from a shop assistant as a baseline service standard; efficiency, not warmth, is the metric |

## Do's & Don'ts: Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept that small talk is mandatory, not optional, and that the weather is always a valid topic | Rush a conversation at the till, the pub, or the bus stop; it is considered a small social event |
| Learn "the craic" as a concept, not just a word — it governs an enormous amount of social life | Take self-deprecating humour literally; it's often a warm-up act for genuine affection |
| Buy a round at the pub and understand you now owe, and are owed, future rounds forever | Assume a vague "we should meet up sometime" is a concrete plan; it frequently, charmingly, is not |

## Lithuania: Reserve as Respect, Not Coldness

Lithuanian public behaviour makes far more sense once you understand that silence here isn't withholding warmth — it's a default setting of respect for other people's space, time, and mental energy. Queues move with an almost eerie efficiency: people stand at a measured, comfortable distance, don't chat unnecessarily, and simply wait their turn without performing patience for anyone's benefit. It can read, to a newly arrived extrovert, as borderline hostile. It isn't. It's just a culture that doesn't believe strangers owe each other conversation by default.

This extends everywhere: on buses, in shops, in government offices. Service staff aren't rude when they're brisk and unsmiling — they're being efficient, which is the actual value being optimised for, rather than performed friendliness. Warmth exists, genuinely and deeply, but it's switched on deliberately, for people who've earned it through actual relationship — not extended automatically to strangers as a social lubricant. Ask a Lithuanian how they're doing as small talk, and you may get an actual, considered answer, which can be its own kind of culture shock if you were only asking rhetorically.

The flip side is a real appreciation for order. Queue-jumping, loud phone calls in quiet public spaces, and general performative behaviour in public are met with quiet, withering disapproval rather than confrontation — Lithuanians will simply judge you silently and move on, which is, in its own way, more devastating than being shouted at.

## Ireland: Where Small Talk Is Load-Bearing Social Infrastructure

Ireland runs on precisely the opposite operating system. Small talk here isn't filler before the real conversation — it is frequently the entire point, a genuine social ritual that binds strangers together for the ninety seconds you're both standing at a bus stop, and everyone commits to it fully. Ask how someone's day is going in Dublin and prepare for an actual, detailed, often very funny answer, delivered whether you were braced for one or not.

The pub occupies a genuinely load-bearing structural role in Irish social life that outsiders consistently underestimate. Buying a round isn't a transaction, it's entry into an unspoken, binding social contract: you buy this round, someone else buys the next, and the whole system runs on trust and memory in a way that would collapse instantly anywhere more transactional. "The craic" — a concept of fun, banter, and collective good humour — governs enormous amounts of social behaviour, and newcomers who take Irish self-deprecating humour at face value often miss that it's frequently a warm invitation into the group, not an actual statement of low self-worth.

What catches people out most is the gap between Irish social warmth and Irish social follow-through. "We should absolutely get a pint sometime" is often genuine in spirit and entirely non-binding in practice — a lovely, warm sentiment that may or may not ever become an actual calendar event, and newcomers who chase it down literally sometimes find the Irish person mildly baffled that anyone took the pleasantry as a concrete plan.

Transport offers its own small case study in the difference. On a Vilnius trolleybus, silence is the social contract, and anyone narrating their day loudly into a phone will be met with pointed, collective disapproval delivered entirely through posture. On a Dublin bus, by contrast, it's entirely normal for a complete stranger to comment on your shopping bags, ask where you got your coat, or simply announce that it's "lashing out there" to the carriage at large, and receive several enthusiastic replies. Neither behaviour is rude by its own country's rules. Swap the passengers and you'd have chaos within four stops.

## The Verdict

Lithuania wins for anyone who finds unsolicited chat from strangers mildly violent — here, your personal space is sacred, your silence is respected, and nobody will ask about your weekend unless they genuinely want to know. Ireland wins for anyone who finds silence lonely rather than peaceful — here, you are never truly alone in public, for better and occasionally for worse.

I know which one is more restful. I also know which one I'd rather be stuck at an airport with for six hours. These are, infuriatingly, different answers.

## What Nobody Warned You About

## Conclusion

Both countries are, in their own ways, extremely sincere — they've just built sincerity on opposite foundations. Lithuania's sincerity lives in what's withheld: respect expressed through space, warmth rationed carefully and meant completely when given. Ireland's sincerity lives in what's offered freely: warmth distributed generously to strangers, at the minor cost of occasionally meaning slightly less in the moment than it sounds. Move between them and you will, at some point, stand in a Dublin queue craving Vilnius's silence, and stand in a Vilnius queue craving Dublin's noise. That's not confusion. That's just what it feels like to actually notice culture instead of tolerating it.
