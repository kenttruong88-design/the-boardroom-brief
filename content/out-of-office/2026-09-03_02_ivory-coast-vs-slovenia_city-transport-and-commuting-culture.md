# One City Commutes by Improvisation, the Other by Immaculately Organised Bicycle

🇨🇮 Ivory Coast 🇸🇮 Slovenia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two schools of thought on getting from home to work in a city, and Abidjan and Ljubljana represent them with almost comic precision. One school says: negotiate everything, trust no fixed schedule, and treat the shared minibus as a live improv performance with a fare attached. The other school says: build 180 kilometres of cycle track, hand every household a heavily subsidised bicycle, and quietly congratulate yourself for being the most liveable capital nobody outside Central Europe can point to on a map.

I've spent time commuting in both, and the whiplash is real. In Abidjan you learn the rhythm of the gbaka, the yellow minibuses that run set routes with zero fixed departure times and drivers who consider "full" a purely aspirational concept. In Ljubljana you learn that the entire old town is functionally car-free, that the bike-share system is so smoothly integrated it feels faintly utopian, and that pedestrians have more legal and cultural rights than in almost any other European capital. Neither city is objectively correct. Both will absolutely judge you for not knowing the unwritten rules.

## Do's & Don'ts: Ivory Coast

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the hand signals for hailing a woro-woro shared taxi, they matter more than words | Expect a printed timetable for anything, gbaka schedules exist only in the driver's head |
| Negotiate the fare before you get in, every time, even on routes you've done before | Sit in the front seat of a gbaka unless you enjoy being the unofficial fare collector |
| Keep small bills on you constantly, nobody has change for a large note, ever | Assume traffic will move on any logic you recognise, Abidjan gridlock has its own physics |
| Build in an extra hour for any commute during rainy season, the roads flood fast | Get frustrated at the driver waiting to fill every last seat, that's the whole business model |

## Do's & Don'ts: Slovenia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a BicikeLJ bike-share card immediately, it will genuinely change your daily life | Try to drive into the old town centre, it's pedestrianised and your car isn't welcome |
| Learn to cycle in the rain without complaint, locals do it year-round without fuss | Block a cycle lane while parked "just for a minute," you will be honked at, correctly |
| Use the excellent LPP bus network for anything outside cycling range, it's reliable to the minute | Jaywalk carelessly, Ljubljana pedestrians follow the signals with near-Germanic discipline |

## Ivory Coast: Chaos That Somehow Runs on Time Anyway

Abidjan's transport system looks, to an outsider, like pure anarchy. There's no unified app, no central timetable, no single authority you can point to and say "that's who's in charge." What there is instead is an extraordinarily dense, self-organising web of gbakas, woro-woro shared taxis, and the newer, marginally more regulated Bus Rapide Transitway that everyone agrees is nicer and nobody quite trusts to actually show up.

The gbaka experience deserves its own museum exhibit. These are privately owned minibuses running semi-fixed routes, and the entire business model depends on cramming in as many passengers as physically possible before departing, meaning "the bus is leaving" is a statement with considerable elasticity. You will wait. You will negotiate a seat. You will hand your fare to whichever stranger is closest to the door because the apprenti, the fare collector hanging half out of the vehicle, cannot reach you directly. It is, against all reasonable expectation, an efficient system, just not one that resembles any transport network taught in an urban planning textbook.

What genuinely impresses me is the local fluency with all of this. Abidjanais navigate a system with no official map using pure oral tradition, passed-down knowledge of which junction the woro-woro for your neighbourhood actually departs from, which drivers overcharge, which routes flood first in a downpour. It's a masterclass in decentralised logistics run entirely on trust, cash, and reputation, and once you're plugged into it, it moves people around a metropolis of over six million with a resourcefulness that Ljubljana's tidy bike lanes have never once had to demonstrate.

## Slovenia: The Smugness of a City That Actually Planned Ahead

Ljubljana, by contrast, is what happens when a small capital city decides, deliberately and with considerable EU funding, to be insufferably well organised about getting around. The mayor pedestrianised the entire historic centre back in 2007, kicked private cars out of the core, and built one of the most genuinely pleasant cycling networks in Europe, not flashy, just relentlessly functional.

The BicikeLJ bike-share scheme is the backbone of daily life here in a way that took me a surprising amount of time to fully appreciate. It's cheap, the stations are everywhere, and the first hour is free, which means locals treat bikes less like a novelty and more like an extension of their own legs. You see it in the posture: nobody in Ljubljana looks like they're "cycling for exercise," they look like they're simply getting to the shop, and it happens to involve two wheels.

Where it gets a little smug is the weather stoicism. Ljubljana isn't blessed with year-round sunshine, it gets proper rain and genuinely cold winters, and locals cycle through all of it with a straight face and a decent rain jacket, treating anyone who takes the bus instead as making a slightly soft choice. The LPP bus network is there as backup, clean, on time, and comprehensively signposted, but using it too often marks you as someone who hasn't fully committed to the Ljubljana way of life. It's a small city playing at Copenhagen-levels of cycling virtue, and mostly, infuriatingly, pulling it off.

## The Verdict

Abidjan wins on raw human ingenuity, moving millions of people daily through a system with essentially no central planning, held together by drivers, fare collectors, and passengers who've all silently agreed on the rules without a single meeting ever being held. Ljubljana wins on sheer liveability, a city that made a decision and then actually followed through on it for two decades running. If you want to be tested and occasionally delighted by unpredictability, Abidjan will oblige you daily. If you want a commute so smooth it borders on smug, get yourself a BicikeLJ card and never look back. I know which one gave me better stories. I also know which one got me to work on time.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — "What is it really like commuting in Abidjan?" paraphrased: a longtime resident explained that the real skill isn't finding a gbaka, it's knowing which driver actually leaves when he says he will, and that takes months to learn.*

> *expat.com Abidjan forum, paraphrased: a newcomer admitted they overpaid every woro-woro fare for their first two months simply because they didn't know the going rate, and that locals eventually just started negotiating for them out of pity.*

> *Quora — "Why does everyone in Ljubljana cycle everywhere?" paraphrased: a resident said it stopped feeling like a lifestyle choice within a year, it's just what your legs do now, and owning a car in the centre started to feel almost embarrassing.*

## Conclusion

Two cities, two completely different definitions of a good commute, and both completely convinced they've got it figured out. Abidjan's system shouldn't work on paper and works brilliantly in practice. Ljubljana's system works exactly as designed and never lets you forget it. Move to one and you'll learn to read chaos like a language. Move to the other and you'll learn that smugness, it turns out, pairs beautifully with a well-maintained cycle lane.
