# Prague Will Sell You the View. Vienna Will Make You Earn the Lease.

🇨🇿 🇦🇹 Czech Republic vs Austria
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is the thing nobody tells you before you book either country: one of them wants your money and will charge you for the privilege of being charged, and the other wants your paperwork, your patience, and ideally three generations of your family history before it lets you near a lightswitch. Both will eventually let you sleep somewhere. The journey there is where the character reveal happens.

Prague has become so good at hosting people it has started hosting too many of them — 7.4 million visitors in 2023 alone, a quarter more than the year before, crammed into a city of 1.3 million residents who would, at this point, like their Old Town back, please. Austria, meanwhile, has spent the same period perfecting the art of making you fill out a form in triplicate to rent a flat that smells faintly of 1987. One country is drowning in demand. The other is drowning you in bureaucracy. Pack accordingly.

**Do's & Don'ts: Czech Republic**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book your Prague stay six weeks out — landlords and hoteliers both move fast now | Expect a "quiet old town" anywhere near Charles Bridge after 8pm |
| Bring a full tenant profile — references, proof of income, the works — if you're renting long-term | Lowball a Prague landlord; the next 49 applicants won't |
| Try a family-run pension outside the centre for real local warmth | Assume "guesthouse" means "cheap" — overtourism pricing has arrived everywhere |
| Read the noise ordinances before 10pm — the city is actively policing stag parties now | Wander onto Dlouhá Street expecting vehicle access at night — it's banned |

**Do's & Don'ts: Austria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Save three to four months' rent upfront — deposit plus advance plus possible *Ablöse* | Expect to negotiate; Austrian landlords negotiate the way Austrian trains run late — rarely |
| Learn the word *Ablöse* before you learn the word for "hello" | Assume government correspondence arrives by email — it's still coming by post |
| Trust a rural *Gasthaus* with precision timing — breakfast ends exactly when they say it ends | Show up expecting small talk from your host; efficient does not mean effusive |
| Check if brokerage fees apply — rules have shifted and nobody will volunteer the update | Try renting without a *Meldezettel* (registration form) — the bureaucracy eats its own first |

## Prague: A City That Sold Its Spare Room to the World

Let's be clear about what happened to Prague's accommodation scene: it worked too well. Twenty years ago this was a city of pensions run by women named Jana who'd rent you a room above a bakery and feed you dumplings whether you asked for them or not. That version of Prague still technically exists, mostly in Vinohrady and Žižkov, if you know where to look and don't mind a landlady who assesses your life choices in Czech while handing you a key the size of a dessert spoon.

The version everyone actually encounters now is a city that has quietly become one of the most tourist-saturated destinations on the continent — roughly six tourists for every resident, a ratio that puts it in the same conversation as Venice and Dubrovnik, cities whose entire personalities have been hollowed out and replaced with gift shops selling the same personality back to you at markup. Prague's city hall knows this. Hence the midnight vehicle ban on Dlouhá Street, hence the "Enjoy Respect Prague" billboards politely begging stag parties to stop screaming at 2am, hence the proposed crackdown on pub crawls and novelty costumes. None of it is subtle. All of it is necessary.

For the people actually trying to live there rather than photograph it, the accommodation story is a different kind of brutal. Bezrealitky.cz data shows the average rental listing nationally pulls about 30 enquiries; in Prague that climbs to 50. Flats get signed within three days of first contact. Landlords aren't chasing the highest bidder anymore — they're chasing the applicant with the cleanest paperwork, the steadiest income proof, and the best odds of not causing trouble. Supply a full profile and your odds improve roughly fivefold. This is not a rental market. This is a job application with a shower included.

The upshot: Prague will happily, efficiently, almost aggressively accommodate you for a weekend. Ask it to accommodate you for a year and it suddenly develops standards.

## Austria: Hospitality by Appointment Only

Austria's hospitality tradition runs deep and genuinely sincere — the Alpine *Gasthaus*, the family-run guesthouse with the geranium-stuffed balcony, the innkeeper who has served the same breakfast at the same hour since the Habsburgs were relevant. It's real, it's lovely, and it operates on a clock so precise you could set your watch to the moment they stop serving coffee. Arrive one minute after and you will be met not with hostility, exactly, but with the specific Austrian disappointment of someone watching you fail a test you didn't know you were taking.

That same precision, charming in a mountain inn, turns into something considerably less charming the moment you try to actually live there. Vienna has topped Mercer's Quality of Living ranking eleven times running — genuinely, deservedly, the city works — but "quality of living" apparently does not include "ease of acquiring housing." Tenants face a security deposit of two to three months' rent, often paid alongside rent in advance, and frequently an additional *Ablöse* — a legacy payment to the outgoing tenant for furniture or fixtures you may never have asked for and definitely never chose. By the time you've covered deposit, advance rent, Ablöse, and furnishing a flat that arrived as four walls and a radiator, you've spent something approaching a year's rent before you've slept there once.

Then there's the bureaucracy, which Austria treats less as an obstacle and more as a national art form. Government correspondence still often arrives by post. Civil servants are often, by policy, not permitted to speak English with you even when they can. The country, by its own expats' repeated description, is "founded on bureaucracy" — not a quirk, a founding principle. Vienna and the Alpine regions are saturated enough that even locals struggle. And once you're in, don't expect your landlord to warm up: efficient does not translate to friendly, and Vienna's reputation for genteel grumpiness is earned honestly, one unsmiling interaction at a time.

## The Verdict

If you want a place to sleep for three nights, Prague wins, loudly and immediately — it has had a decade of practice turning strangers into customers at scale, even if the Old Town now resembles a theme park with better beer. If you want a place to actually live, Austria wins on paper — better-built flats, a functioning rental law, a capital that keeps winning "best city" awards it clearly takes seriously — but it extracts a toll in patience, paperwork, and upfront cash that makes Prague's rental gauntlet look almost casual by comparison.

Neither country is trying to make this easy for you, for entirely opposite reasons. Prague is too busy serving the other 7.4 million people who showed up this year to notice you specifically. Austria has noticed you specifically, and has decided you need to prove yourself first. Pick your poison: being ignored, or being audited.

## Conclusion

Here's the uncomfortable truth both countries would rather you didn't notice: the hospitality industries built their reputations on hosting strangers brilliantly, and both quietly fall apart the moment a stranger wants to stay. Prague's warmth was always transactional at scale — charming when there were fewer of you, a logistics exercise now that there are millions. Austria's precision was always conditional — glorious when you're a guest, glacial the second you ask to become a resident. Visit either one and you'll be treated like royalty. Try to move in, and you'll discover the crown comes with an invoice, a deposit, or both. Choose your discomfort. Nobody's handing out refunds.
