# Two Nations Built Entire Personalities Around Not Complaining About the Cold (They Complain Constantly)

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country insists its weather builds character. Czechia and Canada have simply taken this further than anyone else and turned the weather into a personality trait, a tourism strategy, and in Canada's case, a minor driver of automotive legislation. One nation copes with months of flat grey sky by drinking hot wine in a square and pretending it's romantic. The other copes with temperatures that can kill you in minutes by legislating the tires on your car. Both will tell you, completely straight-faced, that you get used to it.

You do not get used to it. You simply develop new coping mechanisms, most of which involve alcohol, extremely specific outerwear, or a willingness to discuss the forecast with the same reverence other cultures reserve for religion. I have relocated fourteen times. I have shivered in both of these countries in socially unacceptable amounts of fleece. What follows is the truth nobody puts in the relocation brochure, because the relocation brochure is, as ever, lying to you with a nice photo of a Christmas market taken on the one sunny day in November.

## Do's & Don'ts — Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy proper boots before the first frost, not after you've fallen on the tram steps | Assume "mild" winter means anything above -5°C; it doesn't, and the valley fog makes it feel worse |
| Learn to love a Thursday-afternoon beer in a warm pub; it is load-bearing for Czech mental health | Schedule anything outdoors between November and March without a backup indoor plan |
| Stock up on ginger-lemon-honey-rum tea and an Alpa Francovka rub, both taken seriously here | Expect your office to agree on a temperature; a third of Czechs are perpetually cold at their desks and furious about it |
| Go mushroom foraging in September before the grey arrives — it is the last joy of the year | Mock the "zmijovka" zigzag winter hat; it is both ancient and extremely warm |

## Do's & Don'ts — Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy your winter coat and boots IN Canada, not before you arrive — they're built for the actual job | Step outside without checking the wind chill; a "mild minus 5" can be a brutal minus 20 with wind |
| Learn to layer properly: base, insulation, shell, and a face covering you won't remove indoors fast enough | Touch a metal gas pump handle with bare skin, ever, in January |
| Check your province's winter tire rules before November; Quebec makes them mandatory by law | Assume the excitement of the first snowfall lasts past week two — it does not |
| Make an actual winter plan — projects, light therapy, people to see — before the dark 4pm sets in | Underestimate minus 30-plus; newcomers have been caught out badly enough to need frostbite treatment |

## Czechia: Grey Is a Lifestyle, Not a Weather Pattern

Prague does not really do extreme weather. It does persistent, institutional greyness, punctuated by the occasional Arctic snap that drops the thermometer to around -9°C, with mornings dipping past -12°C, cold enough that the Czech Hydrometeorological Institute blames the city's valley setting and bare, snowless ground for trapping the chill rather than reflecting it away. That's not dramatic by Canadian standards. It is, however, relentless, and relentless is somehow worse than dramatic, because nobody gets to have a main character moment about it. You just wake up, it's grey, you go to work, it's grey, you go home, it's grey, and six months later someone asks if you've acclimatised and you realise you've simply stopped looking at the sky.

What the Czechs have instead of drama is ritual. Christmas markets are not quaint tourist theatre, although they are sold to tourists as exactly that; they are structural psychological support, a scheduled, caffeinated, boozy excuse to stand outside in the cold with other humans and pretend it's fine, fuelled by svařené víno and trdelník. Office life has its own cold war: a Czech workplace survey found a third of employees routinely complain about office temperature, over 40% can't control it themselves, and more than a third of workplaces miss the supposed 21–23°C comfort standard entirely — meaning the fight over the thermostat is a genuine, year-round national pastime, not an exaggeration for this article. Home remedies are taken with total sincerity: hot tea with ginger, lemon, honey and rum; fermented cabbage; pine-needle syrup; and Alpa Francovka, a herbal rub that every Czech grandmother treats as closer to scripture than skincare. There are entire taxonomies of folk sayings for how cold it is — "cold as a butcher's cellar," "chilly as a dog's nose" — because apparently plain English-style complaining wasn't expressive enough.

And then there's the exit strategy nobody admits to loudly: mushroom foraging in the brief golden window of September, before the grey descends, is treated with the seriousness of a harvest festival, because it is, functionally, the last emotionally stable month of the year. Saunas, mountain cabins, and a well-timed trip to Thailand round out the coping toolkit. It isn't dramatic. It's a quiet, communal shrug performed with mulled wine in hand.

## Canada: The Cold That Requires Government Advisories

Canada does not do quiet shrugs, because Canadian winter can genuinely kill you, and the official guidance reflects that with a bluntness Czechia never bothers with. The Canadian Red Cross runs entire newcomer workshops on frostbite and hypothermia, because tissue freezing and life-threatening cold exposure are not metaphors here, they're seasonal realities with warning signs — shiny, rosy skin; confusion; numbness. Government of Canada guidance, relayed through immigration outlets, tells new arrivals to check the wind chill specifically, not just the temperature, because the number on the thermometer is cheerfully, dangerously misleading once the wind gets involved. Newcomer Fulera Dikki, profiled by New Canadian Media after moving from northern Nigeria to Regina, recalled a day she underestimated: "I almost had frostbite. It was minus 37." She adjusted fast. Most people do, or they don't last the winter.

The culture that's grown up around this is intensely, almost comically practical. You're told not to buy your coat before arriving but after, because Canadian retailers sell gear actually engineered for Canadian winter, not aspirational European fashion-wear. Layering isn't a style choice, it's taught like a safety procedure: base layer, insulation, shell, repeat. Quebec has made winter tires a legal requirement, not a suggestion, because enough people found out the hard way that all-seasons are a myth sold to the optimistic. One newcomer blogger, writing as Ottawa Is Not Boring, described the specific agony of pumping gas bare-handed in the cold as "incredibly painful, even more if it was a windy day" — a sentence that tells you everything about a country where touching metal outdoors is an occupational hazard.

Then there's the dark. Days end around 4pm in deep winter, and the resulting low mood is common enough that light therapy and structured "winter plans" — projects, movie nights, deliberately scheduled human contact — are standard, semi-official advice, not a personal quirk. Canadians have simply decided that seasonal depression gets a flowchart.

## The Verdict

Czechia wins on charm, Canada wins on survival, and nobody wins on actual sunlight between November and March. If you want your winter suffering wrapped in mulled wine and historical architecture, performed communally and taken with a shrug, Czechia is your decor. If you want your winter suffering to come with an official government pamphlet on frostbite symptoms and a legally mandated tire requirement, Canada has built you an entire bureaucracy for survival, which is, in its own grim way, more honest.

I'll give the win to Canada, purely on the basis that a country willing to tell you plainly "you could lose a finger, here's how to avoid it" respects you more than one that hands you a seasonal beverage and calls it coping. Czechia is prettier about its despair. Canada is more useful. Neither will let you forget, even for a week, that you chose to live somewhere the sky actively resents you.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *AAU Lennon Wall, Prague — an exchange student admits, flatly, that she "misses the sun already" and knows, with total certainty, that it's only going to get worse before it gets better.*

> *Tom Fish Is Away (Substack) — a visitor layers so many clothes against the Prague cold that he describes moving as "swimming through treacle," then retreats indoors from a boat tour because the seats are "indistinguishable from blocks of ice."*

> *New Canadian Media, via newcomer Fulera Dikki in Regina — "I almost had frostbite. It was minus 37," followed by the only lesson that matters: cover yourself properly, every single time, before you go outside.*

## Conclusion

Here is what both brochures leave out: the weather in Czechia and Canada isn't a backdrop, it's a co-worker, a flatmate, and occasionally a direct threat to your extremities, and you will organise your entire personality around managing the relationship. You'll learn new vocabulary — wind chill, pohoda, zmijovka — purely to describe how unwell the sky is making you feel. You'll develop opinions about tea blends and tire compounds you never asked to have. And eventually you'll catch yourself, mid-conversation, cheerfully recommending a specific brand of coat to a total stranger, and realise with quiet horror that you've become one of them. That's not acclimatising. That's surrender, dressed, appropriately, in several warm layers.
