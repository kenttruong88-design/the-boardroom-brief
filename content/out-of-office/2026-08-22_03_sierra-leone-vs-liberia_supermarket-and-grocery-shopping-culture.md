# The Supermarket Is a Rumour Here, and the Market Is the Truth

🇸🇱 Sierra Leone and 🇱🇷 Liberia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

If you arrive in Freetown or Monrovia expecting the gleaming aisle logic of a European Tesco, you will be disappointed in roughly the first ninety seconds and delighted for every day after, once you understand that the supermarket here is a decorative afterthought bolted onto a much older, much smarter system: the open market, where the actual food economy of both countries lives, breathes, and negotiates.

Both nations spent years being described by outsiders purely through the lens of civil war and epidemic, a lazy shorthand that tells you nothing useful about how someone in Freetown actually buys their onions on a Tuesday. So let's talk about onions, and rice, and the fine art of not getting overcharged as the obvious foreigner you very much are.

## Do's & Don'ts: Sierra Leone

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go to Big Market or Government Wharf in Freetown for produce, not the mini-marts | Expect fixed pricing on anything sold from a table rather than a shelf |
| Buy rice in the exact local variety your neighbours recommend — brand loyalty is fierce | Assume all rice is interchangeable; you will be corrected, firmly |
| Bring small notes — leones in large denominations frustrate small vendors | Hand over a large bill expecting exact change on a small purchase |
| Greet the vendor before naming what you want | Launch straight into your order like you're at a self-checkout |

## Do's & Don'ts: Liberia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shop at Waterside Market in Monrovia for the real prices and real selection | Rely on the handful of supermarkets for anything beyond imported snacks |
| Negotiate gently but expect it — the first price is a conversation starter | Pay the first quoted price and assume you've been fair to yourself |
| Learn the difference between "country rice" and imported rice before you argue about price | Insist imported goods are automatically better; locals will tell you otherwise |
| Bring a reusable bag — plastic bags are informally taxed by vendors' patience | Expect free bagging the way you might elsewhere |

## Sierra Leone: Big Market Logic

Freetown does have supermarkets — Choithram's and a handful of others cater to the diplomatic and NGO crowd with imported pasta and eye-wateringly priced cereal — but treating them as your primary grocery strategy is a rookie mistake that marks you instantly as someone who hasn't been here long. The real shopping happens at Big Market, at Government Wharf, and in the sprawling informal stalls that line nearly every major road, where the produce is fresher, the prices are lower, and the entire transaction is conducted as a small piece of theatre neither party is allowed to skip.

Rice is not a commodity in Sierra Leone, it's closer to a matter of regional identity. Ask ten different households which variety is "real" rice and you'll get ten confident, slightly competitive answers, usually followed by an offer to cook you some to prove the point. Fish comes straight off the boats at wharves like Susan's Bay, still moving in some cases, and buying it requires you to actually engage with the vendor rather than point at a chilled tray under plastic wrap. Haggling is expected but has a rhythm — you don't slash the first price in half and expect to be taken seriously; you counter reasonably, accept a small concession, and both parties walk away feeling like they won something, which is really the entire point of the exercise.

What foreigners get wrong most often is treating the market as a transaction rather than a relationship. Return to the same onion seller three times and by the fourth visit you have a standing arrangement, occasional credit, and a vendor who'll set aside the good tomatoes before the rest of the market sees them. Skip that relationship-building and you'll pay the "stranger tax" indefinitely, which, frankly, is a fair enough system given how much of the developed world walks in assuming they're owed the local price on day one.

## Liberia: Waterside and the Rice Question

Liberia's Waterside Market in Monrovia is the country's grocery-shopping heart, a sprawling, loud, gloriously chaotic institution that makes the handful of American-style supermarkets serving the expat crowd look like sad, overpriced pantries by comparison. Liberia's history with American settlement gives its retail culture an odd double identity — you'll find imported goods with English-language branding sitting a few stalls away from cassava leaf and country rice sold exactly the way it has been for generations, and the two economies coexist without much overlap in who actually shops where.

The rice question in Liberia carries real weight — "country rice," grown locally, versus imported rice, and asking a Liberian which one is superior is a conversation with genuine emotional stakes, not a matter of simple preference. Get this wrong by assuming imported automatically means better and you'll be corrected, sometimes at length, sometimes with a small lecture on self-sufficiency that you probably deserved.

Negotiation in Monrovia's markets follows a friendlier, slower rhythm than some West African markets — the opening price is understood by everyone as an opening position, not an insult, and the back-and-forth is almost affectionate once you get the hang of it. What surprises newcomers most is how social the whole exercise is: vendors know regulars by name, ask after your family, and will hold back the best produce for people who show up consistently rather than the highest bidder on any given day. Liberia's market culture rewards loyalty over efficiency every single time, and trying to optimise your grocery run like a spreadsheet exercise will get you nowhere except overcharged and mildly resented.

## The Verdict

Sierra Leone wins on sheer market theatre — Big Market's chaos has a rhythm that, once you learn it, becomes genuinely enjoyable rather than merely tolerable. Liberia wins on the quieter, more personal loyalty economy at Waterside, where showing up consistently gets you treated like an actual member of the community rather than a wallet with legs.

Both countries have thoroughly out-organised the supermarket concept, which exists mostly to reassure homesick expats that cereal still exists somewhere. If you actually want to eat well and understand either place, skip the imported aisle and go build a relationship with an onion seller. It'll serve you better than any loyalty card ever has.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a Freetown-based NGO worker paraphrased: after eight months at the same market stall, the vendor started setting aside the good tomatoes before the rest of the market even arrived, and that's when I knew I'd actually moved in rather than just visited.*

> *expat.com forum — a Monrovia resident noted that arguing about country rice versus imported rice with a Liberian taxi driver turned into a forty-minute conversation that ended with an invitation to Sunday dinner.*

> *Internations Freetown forum — a long-term expat paraphrased: bringing exact change to Big Market isn't optional politeness, it's the difference between a smooth transaction and standing around for ten minutes while someone hunts for coins.*

## Conclusion

Neither Sierra Leone nor Liberia needs a supermarket chain to prove it has a functioning food economy — it has one, and it's louder, fresher, and considerably more socially intelligent than anything with fluorescent lighting and a loyalty app. Go to the market. Learn a vendor's name. Argue gently about rice. You'll eat better, spend less once the stranger tax lifts, and walk away with something no supermarket has ever offered anyone: an actual relationship with the person who fed you.
