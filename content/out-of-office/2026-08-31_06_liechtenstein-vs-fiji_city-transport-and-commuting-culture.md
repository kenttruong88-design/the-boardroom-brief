# A Country With No Train Station Is Beating an Archipelago With Forty Ferries

🇱🇮 **Liechtenstein** · 🇫🇯 **Fiji**

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Liechtenstein has no airport, no train station it actually uses, and a population smaller than a decent shopping mall's catchment area, and yet it runs one of the most quietly smug commuting operations I have seen anywhere. Fiji has three hundred-plus islands, a road network that treats "sealed" as aspirational outside the main towns, and a commuting culture built on minibuses, shared carriers, and a collective agreement that the timetable is a suggestion. Both countries move people without pretending to be a metropolis, which I find more admirable than anything a tourism board has written about either.

What makes this pairing delicious is that Liechtenstein's entire transport identity is built around *not having infrastructure* — it borrows its trains, outsources its airport to Switzerland and Austria, and still runs a bus network so efficient that cross-border commuters find it boring, the highest compliment a transport system can receive. Fiji's identity is the opposite: visible, tactile, occasionally chaotic — carriers, buses with personalities, ferries that answer to the weather rather than a schedule — and it also works, just on a completely different emotional register.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇱🇮 Liechtenstein

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a Lake Constance Ticket if commuting from Switzerland or Austria — unlimited bus, train and ferry across all four countries | Expect to fly or train directly in — no airport, no real domestic station, every trip starts on a bus |
| Learn the LIEmobil bus map early — 25 routes in a country you could walk in an afternoon, frequent enough to beat driving | Assume commuters only flow outward — 10,000+ Swiss workers commute *into* Liechtenstein daily, reversing the usual pattern |
| Accept Schaan-Vaduz as the closest thing to a "main station," even on a line Liechtenstein doesn't control | Treat the Swiss border as a template for Austria's — Switzerland has zero checks, Austria still spot-checks |
| Budget extra time around the Feldkirch–Buchs rail corridor's modernisation works | Underestimate the slack — commutes timed to the minute mean a five-minute delay cascades fast |

#### 🇫🇯 Fiji

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Flag a carrier (minivan) from the roadside — same route and fare as the bus, but faster | Rent the cheapest car in Nadi and expect Suva to be gentle — 8am and 5pm mean real gridlock |
| Build slack into inter-island ferry plans to the Mamanuca or Yasawa groups — services are weather-dependent | Expect air conditioning or personal space on a standard local bus — only express Nadi–Suva routes come close |
| Keep small change and low expectations of punctuality — the fare is nearly free, patience is the real cost | Assume every inland village is bus-reachable — that's what carriers exist for |
| Drive defensively on rural roads — potholes, speed bumps and wandering livestock are standard | Forget Fiji drives on the left — it trips up more visitors than any pothole |

## Liechtenstein: The Microstate That Outsources Everything and Still Wins

Here is a fact that should embarrass every mid-sized European city with a half-built tram line: Liechtenstein, a country you can drive the length of in twenty-five minutes, has no airport and essentially no domestic train service, and its commuting culture is still considered enviably efficient by the people who live there. The nearest functional rail connection, Schaan-Vaduz, sits on an Austrian Federal Railways line between Feldkirch and Buchs — Liechtenstein didn't build it and doesn't fully control it, it simply benefits from sitting in the way of a perfectly good Austro-Swiss corridor. Lucky or well negotiated, it doesn't matter which: you get where you're going.

The real backbone is LIEmobil, the government-owned bus operator running roughly 25 routes — including night buses and ski buses — across a country most people could jog through. The buses are bright green, frequent, and treated as boring by daily users, which in transport terms is the highest compliment available. Nobody writes an angry email about a bus system that simply works.

What makes this story interesting, not just tidy, is the direction of traffic. You'd expect Liechtensteiners streaming into wealthier Switzerland for work, and they do — but the flow also runs backwards, with over ten thousand Swiss workers crossing daily into Liechtenstein, something that almost never happens elsewhere in Europe. Switzerland counted roughly 400,000 cross-border commuters at the end of 2025, and Liechtenstein both sends and receives that traffic at meaningful volume.

The December 2025 expansion of the Lake Constance Ticket to cover Liechtenstein — one regional pass for unlimited bus, train and ferry travel across Switzerland, Austria, Germany and Liechtenstein — is the kind of quietly excellent bureaucratic tidiness only a country this small and well-organised could pull off without years of cross-border committee warfare. Meanwhile the Feldkirch–Buchs corridor is mid-upgrade, a genuinely enormous project for a genuinely tiny country, proof of how seriously Liechtenstein takes "we don't have our own railway" as a permanent arrangement worth investing in anyway.

## Fiji: Carriers, Chaos, and a System That Runs on Trust

Fiji's commuting culture could not be a more different animal, and I mean that as admiration. There is no elegant cross-border ticketing scheme here — there is a bus, a carrier, your own two feet, or a boat, and the boat may or may not show up depending on what the ocean is doing. Inter-island ferries to the Mamanuca and Yasawa groups are essential infrastructure for a large share of the population, and weather-sensitive enough that "I'll be there Tuesday" is a statement of hope, not fact.

On the main island, the carrier system is the unsung genius of Fijian mobility: ten-seater minivans running the same roads as proper buses, for the same rock-bottom fare, but faster, because they leave when full rather than on a schedule — either the most efficient system imaginable or a daily test of your tolerance for uncertainty. Local buses, meanwhile, have a reputation — noisy, crowded, unapologetically slow — that every expat guide repeats because it's true, and the buses are somehow beloved anyway. Ride a standing-room-only Fijian bus once and you understand something about the country no resort transfer will teach you.

Suva itself has become a genuine congestion story, with 8am and 5pm producing gridlock that surprises visitors expecting a sleepy Pacific capital. Add aggressive local driving, livestock on rural roads, potholes that could double as swimming pools, and left-hand driving that undoes most North American visitors within ten minutes, and you have a transport culture demanding far more improvisation than Liechtenstein's — rewarded with a texture a frictionless Swiss-adjacent bus network simply cannot offer.

## The Verdict

If you want your commute to disappear from your mental load entirely — to become something you never think about, the way you don't think about breathing — move to Liechtenstein. It has engineered scarcity into an advantage through sheer organisational discipline, and the result is so reliable it becomes invisible, the entire point of good infrastructure.

If you want your commute to actually mean something — other humans, unpredictable weather, a driver who knows your stop without being told, and the occasional boat that simply doesn't come — move to Fiji, and calibrate your expectations accordingly.

I'm supposed to pick a winner, so: Liechtenstein wins on competence, Fiji wins on character, and anyone who tells you those are the same thing has never stood in the rain waiting for a carrier that left ten minutes early because it filled up.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a recurring theme across threads on cross-border commuting into Switzerland and Liechtenstein is that the G-permit system, requiring frontier workers to return home at least weekly, catches new commuters off guard; people budget for the commute but not for the administrative rhythm it imposes on their week.*

> *expat.com Fiji — the most repeated advice for newcomers riding local buses is simply to be patient, because the timetable posted at the stop is aspirational at best and the bus genuinely will not arrive when it says it will.*

> *Expat Arrivals and Suva-based expat blogs both independently flag the same surprise: the sheer aggression of Suva's peak-hour traffic and taxi drivers, since most newcomers arrive expecting a sleepy Pacific pace rather than 8am gridlock and horns leaning at the slightest hesitation.*

## Conclusion

Liechtenstein and Fiji should not belong in the same sentence, let alone the same comparison, and that is exactly why this one works. One is a landlocked European accounting exercise that happens to also be a country, moving its people on borrowed rails and a bus fleet so reliable it has become culturally irrelevant to discuss. The other is a scattered Pacific nation where getting to work can depend on the tide, the rain, or whether the carrier driver likes your face enough to wait an extra ninety seconds.

Neither is "better" in any sense that matters to an actual commuter standing at an actual stop. Liechtenstein will spoil you into forgetting transport used to be difficult anywhere. Fiji will remind you, daily, cheerfully, that it still is — and that there is a particular dignity in a system that runs on trust rather than timetables. Choose your inconvenience. Just choose it with your eyes open.
