# The Philippines Starts Christmas in September. Macao Starts It Whenever a Filipino Needs It

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇲🇴 Macao

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The Philippines does not so much celebrate festivals as live inside them. The Christmas season famously begins in the "ber" months, which is to say September, and runs well into January, with a lantern, a carol or a parade arriving at every corner of every month in between. Macao, a tiny enclave where Portuguese, Chinese and Catholic traditions overlap like three transparencies on a light box, has a more compact approach: a calendar written into law, a list of holidays with dates, and a pleasing refusal to waste any of them.

The interesting meeting point is the Filipino community in Macao, which carries its own calendar into a city that already has several. It is a story about how a festival survives displacement, and about what the relocating executive should expect to do with their days off. A source note: the pages I fetched cover Macao's holidays and the Filipino community's Simbang Gabi there, but not the Philippines' own festival calendar. Where I describe the Philippines, I am working from general public knowledge.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Philippines**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept every fiesta invitation and eat at every table | Show up without food or a gift for a family gathering |
| Join in the long Christmas season, which starts months early | Be surprised by carols in September |
| Observe Holy Week with respect; it is quiet and widely observed | Plan a loud outing on Good Friday |
| Learn the local patron-saint fiesta in whichever town you live | Assume one national calendar covers everything |

**Macao**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the official holiday list; it blends Chinese, Western, Catholic and Macanese festivals | Assume Philippine holidays are observed locally; none appear on the Macao list |
| Plan around Chinese New Year, with the eve and first three days of the first lunar month as public holidays | Book a trip home on the first day of the lunar new year |
| Note Good Friday, Holy Saturday and the Feast of the Immaculate Conception on 8 December | Forget that Macao SAR Establishment Day falls on 20 December |
| Join the Simbang Gabi community if you are Filipino and Catholic | Expect work to stop for it; it happens before dawn |

## Philippines: A Year Built Out of Fiestas

I will state the obvious and then stop before it becomes a lecture: the Philippines is a country of festival density. Nearly every town has a patron saint and a fiesta to go with it, Holy Week is observed with seriousness, and Christmas is not a day but a season. The famous dawn masses of Simbang Gabi, nine days of pre-Christmas worship, are part of that season. The dates, in which the "ber" months start the countdown, are common knowledge rather than anything I can pin to one of my sources.

The social function of all this is as important as the religious one. A fiesta is when a neighbourhood puts the kettle on for an entire district. Cousins return, food appears from no obvious kitchen, and the host's duty is to feed anyone who passes. An outsider can expect to be adopted for a day. The social cost of refusing is higher than any bill you will meet, because a decline is read as distance.

That is the baseline Filipino migrants carry. When they move abroad, as millions do, the calendar goes with them. The festival is not a place; it is a set of habits: the early mass, the food, the call to relatives, the exchange of gifts. That portability is the secret to why Filipino communities abroad are so conspicuous on the days that matter. They bring the party with them, folded in a suitcase.

Think about what it means for a festival to travel. In the Philippines, Simbang Gabi is woven into the whole social fabric: neighbours go together, the streets are busy at dawn, and the food is waiting. In Macao, it is held in churches in a city that is not, by and large, observing it. The tradition survives because the community supplies the missing infrastructure, which is to say the company. A festival is a social technology, and the Filipino community in Macao has proved it can be installed on almost any city's hardware.

## Macao: A Holiday Calendar Written by Committee

Macao's public holidays are listed under Executive Order No. 60/2000, according to Wikipedia, and they are a mixture of traditional Chinese, Western, Catholic and Macanese local observances. Government rest days are Saturdays and Sundays. Chinese New Year's Eve and the first three days of the first lunar month are holidays, along with Ching Ming (April 5, or April 4 in leap years), Tuen Ng or Dragon Boat on the fifth day of the fifth lunar month, and Buddha's Birthday on the eighth day of the fourth. Catholic observances include Good Friday and Holy Saturday. December delivers the Feast of the Immaculate Conception on the 8th, Macao SAR Establishment Day on the 20th, Christmas Eve, Christmas Day and New Year's Eve.

That is the official structure, and it contains no Philippine national holidays, which is an observation from the table rather than something Wikipedia states outright. So how does a Filipino celebrate in Macao? The answer is in the churches. The Macau Daily Times, reporting in December 2016, described Simbang Gabi as a nine-day tradition of dawn masses before Christmas, led by Filipino priests, with Filipinos gathering at St Lawrence's Church at 5:30 in the morning and a second series of novena masses in the evening at St Joseph Seminary Chapel. The paper's account, which I attribute to the article and not to a primary source, says Spanish friars introduced the tradition to Filipino farmers in the eighteenth century.

The Diocese of Macau confirmed the tradition was alive in December 2025, reporting that Simbang Gabi began the day before its post of 16 December, with Filipino devotees filling churches across the territory. The coverage is nine years apart and says the same thing: the festival has not weakened with distance. If anything, the shortage of other Philippine holidays on the official calendar makes the church dates carry more weight. Macao supplies the public holiday. The community supplies the meaning.

For the executive reading this with a posting in mind, the practical point is that a public holiday calendar and a lived festival calendar are different documents. Read the first to know when the office is shut. Ask around for the second to know when the neighbourhood is awake. In both places the second will turn out to be more fun and more demanding, and you should treat any invitation as a small honour and a larger obligation. Bring a dish, arrive early, and stay longer than feels polite. That is the local custom, and it is one of the better ones.

## The Verdict

The Philippines wins on festival generosity; Macao wins on tidiness. A Filipino executive moving to Macao gets the calendar of a very organised city and the comfort of a community that carries its own. A non-Filipino moving the other way gets a year of surprises and meals in the best possible sense. If I must pick a winner, I pick the Philippines for the sheer amount of fun, but I would pay money to see Macao's civil servants try to schedule it.

What impresses me is the way the two meet. A tradition that began in Philippine villages now wakes up a Chinese city at five-thirty in the morning, and nobody involved seems to find that strange. It is a reminder that a festival is less about place than about people who refuse to let it lapse.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Anna Gail Avanzado, Simbang Gabi attendee, via the Macau Daily Times (2016) — says the nine days anticipate Christmas and honour the Virgin Mary, and mentions the folk belief that a wish is granted after completing all nine masses.*

> *Ayrelyn Benas, devoted Catholic, via the Macau Daily Times (2016) — says the tradition is about remembering God when people so often focus on work, and describes Noche Buena in Macau as cooking Filipino noodles and sweets, exchanging gifts and calling family in the Philippines.*

> *Fr Artemio Balana, pastoral care for Filipino migrant missionaries, via the Macau Daily Times (2016) — says the tradition keeps the filial bond alive, that churches are full during it, and that Filipinos feel the Christmas spirit even in Macao.*

## Conclusion

Public holidays tell you what a government wants you to remember. Festivals tell you what a people refuse to forget. Macao supplies the first, in an elegant legal list. The Philippines supplies the second, in a joyful excess that no list could contain.

If you are heading to either, mark the official dates and then ask a local what they actually do. Wake early for the mass, stay late for the food, and bring a gift. The calendar is a suggestion. The community is the event.
