# Courtship by Committee, Two Different Committees

🇧🇴 Bolivia vs 🇹🇴 Tonga — Dating and Social Scene

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nobody warns you, before you move abroad for love or the possibility of it, that dating culture is rarely about the two people involved. It's about their families, their church, their neighbourhood, and in some cases their entire kainga deciding whether you're worth the trouble. Bolivia filters romance through family approval and a fairly traditional, chaperone-adjacent courtship rhythm depending on where in the country you land. Tonga filters it through the church and the extended family with even less room for a quiet, unsupervised first date. Both countries will politely, thoroughly, and repeatedly remind you that you are not dating one person. You're auditioning for an entire social structure.

I have sat through a family Sunday lunch in La Paz that functioned, unmistakably, as a vetting interview I hadn't been told I was attending, and I've had a Tongan friend explain, with total seriousness, that "getting to know someone" traditionally happens at church functions under the direct supervision of at least one relative. Romance, it turns out, is rarely a private matter anywhere worth visiting.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Bolivia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to meet the family early — it's a sign of seriousness, not overreach | Assume urban La Paz dating norms match rural or Indigenous community customs |
| Be visibly respectful toward parents and elders from the first meeting | Push for a fast, private relationship pace — group outings often come first |
| Learn regional differences — the highlands and the lowlands date differently | Assume machismo-adjacent gender norms don't still shape expectations, especially outside cities |
| Show genuine interest in family and community, not just the person | Treat a family dinner invitation as casual — it usually isn't |

**Tonga**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand church and community events as the primary meeting ground | Expect to date privately or informally before families are involved |
| Show deference to the family from the outset — approval matters enormously | Assume physical affection in public is acceptable — modesty is expected |
| Respect that a civil and a church ceremony are both required for marriage | Rush the pace — courtship is traditionally deliberate and closely observed |
| Engage sincerely with the "kainga" — the extended family is not optional | Treat religious customs as background colour rather than genuinely load-bearing |

## Bolivia: The Family Dinner Is the First Date

Bolivia's dating culture varies significantly by altitude and geography — La Paz's cosmopolitan dating scene, increasingly shaped by apps and a younger, more independent generation, sits at some distance from the more traditional courtship rhythms still common in smaller towns and Indigenous Aymara and Quechua communities, where family involvement and community reputation carry considerably more weight. What holds fairly consistently across both, though, is the speed at which family enters the picture. Where some cultures treat "meeting the parents" as a milestone reached after months, Bolivian relationships often fold family in far earlier, treating it less as a hurdle and more as a natural, expected part of getting serious.

Machismo-adjacent gender expectations, while gradually loosening in urban centres, still shape a fair amount of dating etiquette, particularly outside the largest cities — traditional gestures of pursuit and provision remain culturally legible in a way that catches some expats, particularly those arriving with more egalitarian dating scripts, genuinely off guard. This isn't universal, and plenty of younger, urban Bolivians date in ways that would feel entirely familiar to a European or North American visitor, but assuming the entire country operates that way is a fast route to a confusing miscommunication.

What genuinely surprises newcomers is how central the family dinner becomes as a relationship checkpoint. It's not a formality tacked onto a serious relationship late in the game — it often functions as an early, semi-explicit evaluation, where warmth toward parents and grandparents reads as a genuine signal of intent. Show up distracted or dismissive at that dinner, and word travels through the family network faster than you'd expect, regardless of how the actual relationship is going. Bolivians take the collective, communal nature of a serious relationship seriously, and expecting otherwise is the single fastest way to accidentally offend an entire extended family without meaning to.

## Tonga: Courtship Under Supervision

Tonga takes the family-and-community dynamic considerably further, embedding it directly in how young people are even permitted to meet. Traditional Tongan social structure historically separated boys and girls from a fairly young age, meaning interaction between potential romantic partners happens overwhelmingly through supervised settings — church youth groups, village events, school functions — rather than the unstructured, independent socialising common elsewhere. Dating in the more private, casual Western sense simply isn't the default script here; courtship, where it happens at all before marriage, traditionally occurs in the girl's home, under her family's watchful presence, which functions less as a chaperone requirement and more as the entire community's way of formally recognising the relationship exists.

Christianity threads through nearly every layer of this, and it's not a light presence — modesty in dress and behaviour, restraint in public affection, and church attendance as a genuine social and spiritual anchor all shape what's considered appropriate courtship conduct. Reaching an understanding between families matters as much as, sometimes more than, the connection between the couple themselves, and Tongan weddings reflect this directly: a civil ceremony must precede the religious one, and by law, all marriages require confirmation by a registered church official, collapsing any notion that romance here is purely a private, two-person affair.

For a foreigner used to app-based, low-commitment dating culture, Tonga's rhythm can initially read as impossibly slow, even restrictive. But spend real time in a village or church community, and the framing shifts: this isn't restriction for its own sake, it's a system built around the idea that a relationship strong enough to survive the kainga's full attention and the church's genuine involvement is one likelier to actually last. There's a certain hard-headed pragmatism buried in all that supervision.

## The Verdict

Bolivia, if only because it leaves you slightly more room to breathe. Both countries insist that dating is a family and community affair rather than a private one, and I have made peace with that as simply how most of the world actually does this, whatever Hollywood's told you. But Bolivia's version at least permits a recognisable dating arc — meeting, dinner, gradual family integration — where Tonga's traditional structure removes the private, exploratory phase almost entirely. If you're the sort who needs a few unsupervised conversations before you can honestly know what you feel, Bolivia gives you slightly more room to find out before the whole extended family weighs in.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a foreign teacher in La Paz paraphrased: she said the family dinner arrived far sooner than she expected, and it was made clear, without anyone quite saying it, that this was a test.*

> *Expat.com forum, Bolivia thread — paraphrased: one longtime resident warned that dating norms shift sharply once you leave the bigger cities, and to "recalibrate your entire approach" outside La Paz or Santa Cruz.*

> *Expat Focus community, Pacific Islands thread — paraphrased: a missionary-adjacent worker in Tonga described being genuinely moved by how seriously the whole community treated a couple's courtship, "like everyone had a stake in it going well."*

## Conclusion

Neither country will let you date quietly, and honestly, after enough time in both, I stopped wanting to. There's something almost restful about a culture that treats your relationship as everyone's business rather than pretending it's purely private while secretly gossiping about it anyway. Bolivia gives you a family dinner to survive. Tonga gives you an entire church community's blessing to earn. Show up prepared for either, and skip the fantasy that you were ever just dating one person.
