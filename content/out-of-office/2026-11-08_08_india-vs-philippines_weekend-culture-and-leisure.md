# India Has Started Leaving The Mall; The Philippines Never Left, And Isn't Planning To

🇮🇳 🇵🇭 *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every culture eventually decides what a weekend is for, and the honest answer, in both of these countries, used to be identical: the mall. Air conditioning, food court, cinema, a few hours of pretending the heat outside doesn't exist. India, per its own restless, striving urban press, is now visibly outgrowing that answer — standup comedy nights, weekend farm trips, "experiential" outings replacing the food-court default. The Philippines, by contrast, has fully, unapologetically, structurally committed. The mall isn't a weekend activity here. It is the weekend.

I have watched an entire Manila family spend six consecutive hours in SM Mall of Asia without once appearing to run out of things to do. I have also watched a Bengaluru friend group actively boycott their childhood mall in favour of a stand-up night above a bar, narrating the decision like it was a small act of personal liberation.

## India: The Mall Is Slowly Losing Its Monopoly

For roughly two decades, the mall functioned in urban India exactly as it still does in the Philippines: the default answer to "what should we do this weekend," air-conditioned, safe, reliably entertaining for every generation present at once. DNA India's own reporting on "Mumbai's mall habit" captured this model at its peak — multi-generational families treating the food court and multiplex as genuine, unhurried quality time, not a compromise.

What's shifted, and shifted fast enough that India's own urban press now writes about it as a trend with a name, is the younger, metro-city cohort actively seeking alternatives. The Week's recent feature tracked this explicitly: standup comedy nights, live music venues, and weekend farm getaways eating into territory the mall used to own outright, driven by a demographic that associates the mall with their parents' idea of a good time rather than their own. Newsbytes' own data on weekend spending — urban Indians spending roughly 60% more on weekends than weekdays — confirms the appetite for leisure spending hasn't shrunk at all; it's just diversifying into escape rooms, gaming lounges, and immersive dining concepts competing directly with the traditional cinema-and-food-court combo.

This creates a genuinely interesting generational split for anyone relocating here: your in-laws' idea of a perfect Sunday and your new 28-year-old colleagues' idea of one can be, within the same city, almost unrecognisable from each other — one still happily anchored at the mall, the other actively, vocally rejecting it as a weekend default.

The farm-stay trend specifically deserves a closer look, because it's not just a leisure fad, it's a mild class signal too. Weekend farm getaways near Bengaluru, Pune and the National Capital Region have grown into a genuine micro-industry of boutique "farm resorts" marketed explicitly as an antidote to mall fatigue and screen time, priced squarely at the same urban professional class that used to default to the food court. It's leisure-as-contrast: having grown up with the mall as the only accessible air-conditioned option, this generation now treats proximity to actual soil and open sky as the premium experience, a near-total inversion of what their parents' generation aspired to.

## Philippines: The Mall Isn't Declining, It's Being Defended

The Philippines has taken the opposite trajectory entirely, and does so with a kind of cheerful, unembarrassed cultural confidence that Filipino commentators themselves have named and claimed: "malling." Gulf News's own feature on the phenomenon, and a steady stream of local opinion columns — Sunstar's "7 ways malls make sense" chief among them — don't treat mall culture as something to outgrow, they actively defend it as smart, climate-appropriate, safe, and genuinely social infrastructure in a country where good public parks and reliable air conditioning at home are far rarer than they should be.

The scale alone tells you this isn't casual. SM Mall of Asia is routinely, only half-jokingly, compared in size to Vatican City, and it functions as a complete self-contained ecosystem: cinema, karaoke, gym, market stalls, food courts spanning every regional cuisine, and — genuinely, in some locations — chapel services, because why would you need to leave for anything, including church. Multi-generational families treat an entire Saturday there as completely unremarkable, arriving mid-morning and leaving well into the evening, grazing through activities rather than running a single errand.

What distinguishes this from India's version isn't the behaviour itself, which looks nearly identical on the surface — it's the trajectory. Where urban India's cultural conversation is actively asking "is there something better than the mall," the Philippine conversation, at least so far, is confidently answering "no, and here's why," with academic researchers and newspaper columnists alike treating "malling" as a legitimate, worthy subject rather than a phase to be outgrown.

SM Prime's own framing of "malling as a way of life," examined seriously in academic retail literature out of De La Salle University, points to something deeper than convenience: the mall functions here as the one reliably safe, climate-controlled, genuinely free-to-loiter-in public space in cities where good government-funded parks, reliable air conditioning at home, and affordable third spaces more broadly remain real gaps. Framed that way, "malling" isn't really a consumerist habit at all — it's a rational adaptation to what the built environment actually offers, which is precisely why it hasn't faced the same generational backlash visible in India's wealthier, more infrastructure-diverse metro cores.

## The Verdict

India wins on sheer variety right now — if you want a weekend that could plausibly include a farm, a comedy club, and an escape room rather than a fourth consecutive mall visit, urban India's shifting leisure economy will deliver it. The Philippines wins on commitment and confidence — nobody here is embarrassed about spending Saturday until 9pm in a building the size of a small country, and the sheer completeness of the mall-as-ecosystem model means you genuinely never have to leave, or want to. One culture is actively evolving past its own answer. The other has perfected it and sees no reason to apologise.

Neither verdict should read as a judgment on which country has the "better" weekend, because both are solving the same basic equation — heat, safety, and affordability — with the resources actually available to them. India's wealthier metro cores now have enough alternative infrastructure (farm resorts, standalone entertainment venues, a genuine live-events scene) to make the mall optional. The Philippines, for now, largely doesn't, and has responded not with resentment but with genuine cultural ownership of the one good option it does have. That confidence, more than the square footage, is the real story here.

## The Parting Shot

India is quietly, generationally, breaking up with the mall. The Philippines married it decades ago and is still, unapologetically, bringing the whole extended family along for the honeymoon. Pick your weekend accordingly — just don't expect either country's grandparents to understand why you'd rather be anywhere else.
