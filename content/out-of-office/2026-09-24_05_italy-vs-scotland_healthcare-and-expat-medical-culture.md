# In Italy, Your Pharmacist Is Basically a Doctor. In Scotland, Your Doctor Is Basically Unreachable.

🇮🇹 Italy 🇬🇧 Scotland
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two ways a country can handle the fact that you, a foreigner, have woken up with a mystery rash. In Italy, you walk into a farmacia, describe the rash to a pharmacist in green-crossed white coats who has effectively completed a pharmacy degree and several decades of informal diagnostic experience, and walk out eleven minutes later with a cream, a professional opinion, and possibly some unsolicited commentary on your diet. In Scotland, you attempt to register with a GP, discover the practice isn't accepting new patients, try NHS 24, wait on hold long enough to finish reading a novel, and eventually get a callback appointment for a week Thursday, by which point the rash has either resolved itself or evolved into something with its own personality.

I've had genuine medical emergencies in both countries — a wasp sting gone feral in Puglia, a truly alarming ear infection in Edinburgh — and I can report that these systems reflect two entirely different philosophies about who gets to be a healthcare gatekeeper. Italy has quietly decentralised frontline medicine to an army of extremely competent pharmacists. Scotland has centralised everything into a single, chronically underfunded, deeply beloved institution that everyone complains about and nobody, genuinely nobody, wants to actually privatise.

## Italy: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for your tessera sanitaria (health card) immediately upon residency | Assume every region offers the same quality — the north-south gap is real and well documented |
| Trust your farmacista for minor ailments — genuinely well trained and fast | Expect appointment booking to be digital and seamless everywhere; many regions still run on phone calls and paper |
| Keep private supplemental insurance if you want to skip specialist waitlists | Show up at pronto soccorso (ER) for anything non-urgent — triage will make you wait, correctly |

## Scotland: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a local GP practice the moment you have a fixed address | Expect same-day GP appointments outside genuine urgency — book ahead or use NHS 24 |
| Use the NHS app and NHS 24 (111) for triage before heading anywhere in person | Assume "free at the point of use" means fast — free and fast are different promises |
| Carry your CHI number (Community Health Index) to every appointment | Skip registering "because I'm only here temporarily" — you'll regret it the day you need it |

## Italy: A Universal System Held Together by Excellent Pharmacists and Deep Regional Inequality

Italy's Servizio Sanitario Nazionale is, on paper, a genuinely strong universal system — funded through taxation, guaranteeing coverage to residents, and delivering, particularly in the north, hospital care that rivals anywhere in Europe. What makes it feel distinct on the ground, though, is the farmacia, an institution that punches so far above its weight it's practically doing the job of urgent care. Italian pharmacists undergo rigorous training and are legally empowered to advise on and treat a huge range of minor conditions without a doctor's involvement — which means for the vast majority of everyday health complaints, a green cross on a street corner is faster, cheaper, and often more useful than trying to get a GP appointment. This isn't a workaround; it's structurally how the system is designed to triage low-acuity care, and it works beautifully for tourists and residents alike.

The uncomfortable truth Italians will tell you themselves, often with a resigned shrug, is that the quality of the actual hospital and specialist system varies dramatically by region — Lombardy and Emilia-Romagna run healthcare systems that compete with the best in Europe, while parts of the south struggle with underfunding, longer waits, and a documented pattern of "health tourism" where southern patients travel north for serious procedures. This north-south gap is one of the most persistent and openly acknowledged fractures in Italian public life, and it means your experience of "Italian healthcare" depends enormously on your postcode. Private supplemental insurance has become common among both Italians and expats specifically to route around the specialist waitlists that affect the public system, particularly outside the wealthier north — a two-tier reality most people navigate quietly rather than complain about publicly.

## Scotland: A System Everyone Loves and Nobody Can Actually Get Into on Time

The NHS in Scotland operates as a genuinely beloved national institution — free at the point of use, funded through general taxation, and treated by most Scots as a near-sacred piece of the social contract, the kind of thing people will defend passionately in a pub argument even while simultaneously describing their own three-week wait for a GP appointment. This isn't hypocrisy; it's a coherent position once you understand it: Scots believe fiercely in the principle of universal, free healthcare while being entirely clear-eyed and often furious about the system's chronic underfunding, staffing shortages, and the resulting waits that have become a permanent feature of public conversation, especially post-pandemic.

For an expat, the practical reality is a system that requires patience and a bit of savvy to navigate well. GP registration is the crucial first move — practices in busy areas can be full, appointment booking can require calling right when phone lines open, and NHS 24 (the 111 service) has become the de facto triage layer for anything after hours or before you're properly registered. Prescriptions in Scotland are free for everyone, a genuinely notable policy difference from England, and once you're in the system — properly registered, with your CHI number, plugged into a GP practice — the care itself is thorough, free, and delivered by staff who are, almost without exception, kind and overworked in equal measure. The frustration isn't the quality of care once you receive it; it's the friction of getting there, a structural problem that's been a live political issue in Scotland for years with no quick fix on the horizon.

## The Verdict

Italy wins on immediate accessibility for minor ailments — nowhere else have I gotten competent medical advice faster than at an Italian farmacia, and that alone makes daily life easier. But Scotland wins on equity of the underlying promise — however long the wait, the care itself doesn't depend on your postcode's wealth the way Italian specialist care quietly does, and the NHS's universalism is real, not aspirational. If you're a healthy expat handling minor complaints, Italy will spoil you. If you're dealing with something serious and want to know the system won't bankrupt you or route you to a worse hospital because of where you live, Scotland's flawed, beloved NHS is still, somehow, fighting the good fight.

## Conclusion

Both systems are, in their own imperfect ways, quietly heroic — Italy for turning pharmacists into a genuine first line of defence, Scotland for refusing to let a national healthcare crisis erode the principle that care shouldn't depend on your bank balance. Neither is perfect. Both will test your patience in different registers — Italy's regional lottery, Scotland's queue. Register early, learn the workaround, and for heaven's sake, in Italy, just go to the pharmacy first.
