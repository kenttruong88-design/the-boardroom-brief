# Japan Turns Every Season Into a Festival. Finland Just Wants Everyone to Go Away for Midsummer.

🇯🇵 Japan · 🇫🇮 Finland
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Japan's calendar is stacked, densely and deliberately, with matsuri — seasonal festivals involving lanterns, portable shrines hauled through streets by chanting crowds, fireworks that seem engineered to physically move your internal organs, and food stalls selling things on sticks that you will crave for the rest of your life. Finland has exactly one festival that matters, Juhannus, Midsummer, and its primary ritual is not communal celebration but mass, coordinated disappearance — the entire population evacuates to lakeside cottages and the cities become genuinely, eerily empty for several days straight.

I have been swept along in a Kyoto matsuri crowd so dense I lost a shoe and simply kept moving, carried by sheer human momentum. I have also stood in central Helsinki on Midsummer weekend, alone on a street that would normally be packed, feeling like the last person left after some quiet, orderly evacuation I hadn't been told about.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇯🇵 Japan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive early to matsuri for the best food stalls before they sell out | Wear anything you're precious about — crowds are dense and things get spilled |
| Try wearing a yukata if invited — it's appreciated, not appropriative, when done respectfully | Expect to move quickly anywhere during Golden Week — book everything well in advance |
| Learn a few key festival foods (takoyaki, yakisoba, kakigori) before you go | Miss the fireworks (hanabi) — arguably the emotional centrepiece of summer matsuri |

**🇫🇮 Finland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept a Juhannus cottage invitation if you get one — it's the real Finnish experience | Plan anything requiring shops, restaurants, or services to be open during Midsummer weekend |
| Try the sauna-then-lake-swim ritual — it's genuinely central to the celebration | Expect a big public party — Juhannus is intentionally private and family-oriented |
| Bring your own supplies if invited somewhere remote — many cottages are deliberately off-grid | Underestimate how empty Helsinki gets — even taxis can be hard to find |

## Japan: A Culture Built Around Communal Spectacle

Japan doesn't so much have festivals as it has an entire cultural infrastructure organised around them. The Japan National Tourism Organization's matsuri calendar reads like a year-round itinerary of communal ritual — spring cherry blossom festivals, summer fireworks displays and street matsuri, autumn harvest celebrations, winter illuminations — each tied intricately to season, shrine, and centuries of local tradition. These aren't tourist performances; they're genuinely, deeply local events that happen to also delight visitors, run by neighbourhood associations and shrine committees who take enormous pride in getting every detail right.

The sensory experience is the whole point. Portable shrines (mikoshi) hoisted through crowded streets by chanting locals, taiko drumming that you feel in your chest before you hear it, food stalls selling takoyaki and yakisoba and shaved ice in a dizzying, delicious blur, and fireworks displays (hanabi) so elaborately choreographed they function as the genuine emotional climax of the summer season, drawing crowds who've planned their entire evening, their outfit, their picnic spot around a forty-minute sky show.

Golden Week — the cluster of national holidays the Cabinet Office schedules in late April and early May — deserves its own warning label. It's less a holiday than a national logistics event, with trains, flights, and hotels booked to capacity months in advance as the entire country moves simultaneously. What makes Japanese festival culture so remarkable, though, is how it manages to be both intensely communal and completely orderly — dense crowds that somehow queue politely, celebrate joyfully, and clean up after themselves without anyone being asked. It's chaos with exceptional manners.

## Finland: A Culture Built Around Sanctioned Absence

Finland's approach to public holidays, particularly Juhannus, could not be more different if it tried. Visit Finland's own guidance to the Midsummer tradition essentially amounts to: everyone leaves. Cities that are normally bustling — even by Finland's already reserved standards — empty out almost entirely as the population decamps to lakeside summer cottages (mökki), a tradition the Finnish Ministry of Education and Culture treats as genuinely central to national cultural heritage, not just a quirky preference.

The ritual itself is deliberately private and family-centred rather than public and communal: bonfires by the lake, sauna followed immediately by a plunge into cold water, quiet gatherings with close friends and family that explicitly exclude the wider public spectacle Japan builds its festivals around. There are no crowds to fight, no fireworks to jostle for a view of — because the entire point of Juhannus is escaping crowds altogether, retreating into nature and near-total silence.

This creates a genuinely surreal experience for anyone left in the city over Midsummer weekend. Restaurants close. Shops shutter. Even taxis become scarce, because the drivers, too, have gone to a cottage somewhere. I've heard multiple expats describe walking through central Helsinki during Juhannus and feeling like they'd wandered into the aftermath of some polite, orderly apocalypse. It's not unfriendly — it's simply that Finnish celebration doesn't require, or even want, an audience. The whole culture leans toward the private, the natural, and the deliberately unspectacular, treating a good holiday as one where nobody's watching and nothing needs to be performed.

## The Verdict

Japan wins on sheer spectacle, sensory density, and the communal joy of celebrating alongside thousands of strangers who somehow all know the unwritten rules. Finland wins if your idea of a perfect holiday is total, sanctioned withdrawal from public life into something quieter and more personal. I love the fireworks. I love the lake more. It's close, but Finland's Midsummer silence has stayed with me longer than any matsuri crowd ever did.

## Conclusion

Japan and Finland represent two entirely opposite theories of what a holiday is for: one believes celebration should be loud, shared, and staged with maximum sensory impact; the other believes it should be quiet, private, and shared only with the people who matter most, ideally beside a lake with no phone signal. I wouldn't trade either experience, but if forced to choose where I'd actually want to spend my own holiday, I'm booking the cottage, the sauna, and the blissful, glorious absence of a crowd.
