# Cyprus Sells You Convenience at a Markup. Colombia Makes You Haggle for Dinner, and It's Better For It.

🇨🇾 Cyprus vs 🇨🇴 Colombia — Supermarket and Grocery Shopping Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Cyprus has fully, cheerfully surrendered to the supermarket chain — clean aisles, imported goods stacked next to local ones, prices that quietly punish you for wanting a taste of home in the form of a €9 jar of peanut butter. Colombia, meanwhile, still runs a genuinely thriving parallel economy of plazas de mercado — sprawling, loud, fragrant covered markets where you haggle, sample, and build actual relationships with the woman who's sold you avocados every Saturday for the last eight months.

One country has decided convenience is worth the premium. The other has decided a relationship with your food source is worth the extra ten minutes of negotiation. I know which one makes better ceviche.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇨🇾 Cyprus**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shop local Cypriot brands and produce for genuinely reasonable prices | Expect imported "home comfort" products (specific cereals, sauces) to be cheap — they rarely are |
| Visit weekly village and town markets for fresher, often cheaper produce than the supermarket | Assume every supermarket chain stocks the same range; regional and chain differences are real |
| Compare prices across the two or three dominant chains — margins do shift between them | Do a big weekly shop expecting US-style bulk discounting; Cypriot retail isn't built around that model |
| Ask locals which bakery or butcher beats the supermarket equivalent — usually, one clearly does | Assume North Cyprus and the Republic share identical retail chains or pricing; they largely don't |

**🇨🇴 Colombia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to haggle gently at plazas de mercado — it's expected, not rude, within reasonable limits | Show up to a plaza de mercado expecting fixed, posted prices on everything, especially produce |
| Build a relationship with a regular vendor — loyalty is rewarded with better prices and better cuts | Assume supermarket chains (Éxito, Carulla) are always more expensive; pricing varies sharply by item |
| Use PriceSmart or wholesale options for genuine bulk savings if you're stocking a full kitchen | Skip washing/treating market produce carefully — hygiene standards vary more than in chain supermarkets |
| Visit the plaza early for the best selection — the good stuff moves fast | Assume every city's plaza de mercado operates identically; Bogotá, Medellín, and smaller towns differ |

## Cyprus: Efficient, Familiar, and Quietly Expensive for It

Cyprus's grocery landscape will feel almost aggressively familiar to most Western expats within the first week — a small handful of dominant supermarket chains, clean and well-organized, stocking a genuinely impressive range of both local Cypriot staples (halloumi, olives, local wine, all excellent and reasonably priced) and imported goods aimed squarely at the island's substantial expat and tourist population. This familiarity is, for many newcomers, a genuine relief after the adjustment period of an unfamiliar country — you know exactly how to navigate a Cypriot supermarket on day one, because it operates almost identically to the one you left behind.

The cost of that familiarity is real, though, and it lands specifically on anything imported. A jar of a specific brand of peanut butter, a particular breakfast cereal, anything that reads as a taste of home rather than a local staple, carries a markup that catches newcomers off guard consistently — island logistics and import costs get passed straight through, and expats quickly learn which specific comfort items are worth the premium and which aren't. What tends to get overlooked, meanwhile, is that Cyprus's weekly village and town markets — smaller, more traditional, running on local produce and lower overhead — routinely beat the supermarket on both price and freshness for anything grown on the island itself, and expats who bother seeking them out consistently report better fruit, better vegetables, and a noticeably lower grocery bill for the effort.

Worth flagging too: North Cyprus and the Republic of Cyprus operate genuinely separate retail ecosystems, with different dominant chains and different pricing structures, and expats moving between the two sides shouldn't assume continuity — it's a different shopping landscape entirely, not just a different currency.

## Colombia: The Plaza Still Wins, If You Show Up For It

Colombia offers a genuinely different bargain. Modern supermarket chains — Éxito, Carulla, and others — exist in force and serve plenty of daily needs perfectly well, air-conditioned and orderly in the way Cyprus's chains are. But running alongside them, and in many neighborhoods still thriving rather than fading, is the plaza de mercado: sprawling covered markets, thick with the smell of fresh produce, exotic fruit stacked in pyramids, meat and fish counters run by vendors who've often worked the same stall for decades.

What makes the plaza genuinely different from a supermarket experience — and, frankly, better — isn't just the prices, though gentle haggling on produce is expected and can meaningfully reduce your bill if you engage with it properly rather than awkwardly avoiding it. It's the relationship. Expats who commit to shopping the same plaza regularly, greeting the same vendors, learning names, report a shift that never happens in a supermarket line: better cuts set aside for them, tips on what's freshest that particular morning, genuine warmth that compounds over months into something closer to friendship than commerce. This is Colombian social fabric operating through grocery shopping, not incidental to it.

The tradeoff is real too: plazas require more time, more Spanish, more comfort navigating an environment without posted prices or standardized hygiene certification, and produce handling standards vary vendor to vendor in a way a regulated supermarket simply doesn't allow. Expats stocking a full kitchen for a family often blend both worlds sensibly — wholesale options like PriceSmart for genuine bulk staples, the plaza for produce, meat, and the relationship that comes with it, supermarket chains filling in whatever's left.

## The Verdict

Cyprus optimizes for familiarity and convenience, charges accordingly for anything imported, and rewards the expats who bother seeking out its local markets instead of defaulting entirely to the supermarket chain. Colombia keeps a genuinely vibrant, relational alternative to the supermarket alive and thriving, and the expats who lean into it — haggling, showing up regularly, learning names — get rewarded with something no supermarket loyalty program has ever offered me: an actual human relationship built on produce.

If I'm ranking which grocery culture I'd rather live inside long-term, Colombia wins, decisively, on texture and human connection alone. Cyprus is easier. Colombia is better. I'll take ten extra minutes of gentle haggling over aisle six efficiency every single time.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com Cyprus forum — paraphrased: a new resident was genuinely shocked to find a specific imported breakfast cereal cost nearly triple what they'd paid at home, and eventually just gave it up as a lost cause rather than keep paying the premium.*

> *Quora, thread on Colombian plaza de mercado pricing — paraphrased: a longtime Bogotá resident said produce at their local plaza consistently ran 30 to 40 percent cheaper than the nearest Éxito, provided you were willing to haggle politely rather than accept the first price offered.*

> *EasyExpat Bogotá shopping guide comments — paraphrased: a reader noted that building a relationship with one plaza vendor over several months eventually got them free extra produce tossed into the bag "for the regulars," something no supermarket had ever once done for them.*

## Conclusion

Cyprus and Colombia both feed you well, but they ask for entirely different currencies in exchange — Cyprus wants your money for convenience, Colombia wants your time and a bit of your personality for a genuinely better product and, eventually, something like community. I've done both. I've never once missed the supermarket peanut butter markup. I still think about the woman at my old Bogotá plaza who used to save me the good mangoes.
