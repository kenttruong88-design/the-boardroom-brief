# One Country Turns Carnival Into a 40-Day Endurance Sport, the Other Just Wants You Off the Beach by the Feast Day

🇺🇾 Uruguay × 🇭🇷 Croatia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Most countries have the good sense to get Carnival over with in a week. Uruguay looked at that convention, decided it was for cowards, and stretched its Carnaval into the longest in the world — a sprawling, unhinged, genuinely exhausting forty-odd days of murga rehearsals, tablado stages, and drumming that starts in January and refuses to release you until March. Croatia, meanwhile, treats its public holidays with the brisk efficiency of a country that has a saint for every occasion and intends to observe each one properly, thank you, before getting back to the coast.

I have stood in a Montevideo neighbourhood at midnight in February listening to a candombe drum troupe that showed no signs of stopping, ever, and I have also been quietly informed by a Croatian shopkeeper that no, the bakery will not be open on Assumption Day, and no, she will not be discussing it further. Both countries take their calendars extremely seriously. They simply disagree on whether a celebration should be a sprint or a marathon.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇺🇾 Uruguay**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pace yourself for a Carnaval season that runs six weeks, not six days | Assume it's over after the first big parade; it has barely begun |
| Learn at least one murga chant; neighbours will judge your enthusiasm | Complain about drumming after 11pm during Carnaval season |
| Follow a favourite comparsa through the neighbourhood tablado circuit | Expect quiet residential streets in February; they don't exist |
| Budget real money for a tablado seat if you want to sit down | Assume standing at the barricade all night is optional |

**🇭🇷 Croatia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the saint's day calendar before planning any errand-heavy day | Assume shops follow the same hours as the rest of Europe |
| Enjoy the regional festivals properly; each town takes its own seriously | Treat a local patron saint's day as a minor, skippable event |
| Expect August closures around Assumption Day, especially on the coast | Plan a big move or paperwork day in mid-August |
| Ask locals about their town's specific saint; it matters to them | Generalise "Croatian holidays" as one uniform national experience |

## Uruguay: Carnival as a Feat of National Stamina

Uruguay does not do a modest Carnival. It does the longest Carnival on the planet, and it does it with a seriousness that borders on civic duty. Where other countries wrap the whole affair up in a week of parades before Lent, Uruguay's Carnaval unfurls over roughly forty days, built around murga troupes — theatrical, satirical, wickedly political singing groups — who compete in the Concurso Oficial de Agrupaciones Carnavalescas, a festival-within-a-festival that Montevideo residents follow with the same intensity other nations reserve for league football.

The neighbourhood tablado stages are where the real texture lives: small, local, plastic-chaired venues where a comparsa or murga performs for a crowd that already knows every lyric and isn't shy about singing along. Candombe, the Afro-Uruguayan drumming tradition with UNESCO recognition, adds its own layer entirely, with llamadas processions sending drum troupes through specific historic neighbourhoods on a schedule locals have memorised since childhood. Visitors who expect one big parade and then normal life resuming are, gently, wrong — this is a sustained cultural event that reorganises the entire late-summer social calendar, and treating it as a one-night spectacle is the fastest way to miss what actually matters about it.

What's most striking is the total lack of exhaustion in how Uruguayans discuss it. Ask a Montevideo resident in week five of Carnaval how they're holding up and you won't get complaints about fatigue, you'll get an update on which murga is favoured to win this year and why the judging panel got it wrong last time. This is endurance treated as a point of pride, not an ordeal — a national holiday season built less like a party and more like a season ticket.

## Croatia: A Calendar That Means Business

Croatia's relationship with public holidays runs on a different logic entirely: fewer performances, more genuine closure. Between national holidays and an extensive roster of local patron saint's days — each town, city, and even parish honouring its own — the country operates on a calendar where "closed today" is a perfectly acceptable, non-negotiable answer, and asking twice doesn't change it.

Assumption Day in mid-August lands squarely in peak coastal tourist season and yet Croatia does not soften its observance for visitor convenience — bakeries, small shops, and municipal offices in coastal towns close as they would on any other major holiday, tourists or no tourists. This isn't performative religiosity so much as a deeply ingrained sense that some days belong to the calendar and not to commerce, a position that occasionally baffles visitors expecting the coast's tourism economy to override tradition. It rarely does.

The regional festival culture compounds this further. A town's patron saint's day isn't a quaint local footnote, it's often the single biggest event of that town's year, complete with processions, brass bands, and a level of civic pride that a passing visitor could easily underestimate if they didn't ask. Dubrovnik takes the Feast of St. Blaise with a seriousness that dwarfs how most cities treat their own patron saint days, and smaller towns follow the same template at their own scale. Croatia's holiday calendar isn't about stamina, in other words — it's about precision, specificity, and the quiet insistence that some things simply close, regardless of who's inconvenienced.

## The Verdict

Uruguay stages Carnival as an act of collective endurance, an entire population committing to forty-plus days of drumming, satire, and neighbourhood pride without once appearing tired of it. Croatia treats its holidays as fixed, specific, and non-negotiable — closures that happen on schedule whether or not it suits the tourists currently occupying the coastline. One is a marathon of shared spectacle; the other is a series of firm, well-defended full stops.

If I had to choose which approach better respects the idea of a holiday actually meaning something, it's Croatia's refusal to bend a saint's day around a tourist's itinerary — but I will confess that nothing in my life has matched the specific joy of standing at a Montevideo tablado at 1am, utterly unable to leave, completely unwilling to.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora, "Why does Uruguay's Carnaval last so much longer than Brazil's?" — paraphrased: a respondent explained that Uruguay treats Carnaval less as a spectacle and more as a sustained competitive season, which is exactly why it can't be compressed into a week.*

> *expat.com Montevideo — paraphrased: a resident described the specific sleep-deprivation of living two blocks from a tablado stage for six straight weekends and eventually just giving in and going every time instead of fighting it.*

> *Internations Zagreb — paraphrased: an expat recounted planning a moving day around what they assumed was a minor local holiday, only to find every shop, locksmith, and removal company in the city closed without exception.*

## Conclusion

Uruguay and Croatia have built two entirely different philosophies out of the same basic material — a calendar full of days meant to be honoured rather than worked through. Uruguay says: commit fully, for weeks, and let the whole city reorganise itself around the noise. Croatia says: mark the day precisely, close firmly, and don't expect an exception just because it's inconvenient. Try to bring Uruguay's stamina to Croatia's calendar and you'll find yourself alone at a shuttered bakery wondering what you did wrong. Try to bring Croatia's brisk closure to Uruguay's Carnaval and you'll miss the whole point of the six weeks that follow the first parade.
