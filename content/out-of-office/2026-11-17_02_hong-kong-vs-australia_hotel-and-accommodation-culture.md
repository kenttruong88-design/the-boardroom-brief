# Hong Kong Sells You the Room by the Square Foot and Australia Sells You the Room by the Verandah

🇭🇰 Hong Kong vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Hong Kong a hotel room is a precision instrument. Every centimetre is accounted for, the suitcase has a designated corner, and you will learn to open the wardrobe before you open the bed. It is not a complaint; it is a worldview. The city has the most expensive dirt on earth and has decided, with admirable consistency, that nothing is to be wasted on a metre of sentiment.

In Australia a hotel is a small act of generosity at the edge of a very large country. There is a pool, a car park, a bar that is unaccountably proud of its schnitzel, and a view of something improbable like a river, a harbour or a car dealership. Space is not a luxury. It is a default setting, and the result is a hospitality culture that treats guests like adults with lungs.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Hong Kong**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Consider a serviced apartment for any stay beyond a fortnight | Book a standard room for a month and expect to unpack |
| Check exactly which district and which MTR exit you are near | Trust a listing that says "central" without looking at the map |
| Pack light and use the lobby for meetings | Spread your life across a room you cannot cross in four steps |
| Ask early about laundry, kitchenette and desk space | Assume every hotel room has somewhere to work |

**Australia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book ahead for school holidays and major events | Turn up in a regional town on a long weekend without a bed |
| Ask about parking before you book | Assume the city centre is cheap to park in |
| Tip modestly or not at all, as local custom allows | Perform the full American routine at reception |
| Greet staff like a person, not a service | Treat the front desk as invisible |

## Hong Kong

Start with the size, because everyone does. A guide such as the one on Expat Living Hong Kong lays out the standard menu of accommodation for newcomers: hotels, serviced apartments and the rental market proper. The serviced apartment is the sensible heart of it. As the South China Morning Post has noted in coverage of the sector, these places grew up serving executives and relocating families who needed something between a hotel and a lease, with daily cleaning, a kitchenette and a desk, in a city where the standard hotel room is a fairly cramped proposition.

That is the practical story. The cultural story is more interesting. Hong Kong hospitality is built around efficiency and ritual. Check-in is fast. Luggage appears before you do. Staff are courteous with a precision that never quite becomes warmth, and the concierge will find you a table, a tailor, a doctor and a taxi without ever asking you how your day has been. It is service as a craft, not service as a conversation.

The luxury end is spectacular. A harbour view from a high floor, a spa, breakfast in a dining room that thinks of itself as a theatre: Hong Kong does vertical grandeur better than almost anyone. The middle of the market is where the character shows. Compact rooms, thin walls, a lift that works like a rumour, and a corridor carpet that has seen several decades of conference delegates. Business guests in particular learn that the lobby is the real office, which is why every good hotel in the city has a lobby bar that doubles as a meeting room.

Length of stay changes everything. Beyond a few weeks, a hotel stops making sense: laundry, cooking and the simple human need to put a bag down somewhere all push people toward serviced apartments or short leases. The agents who place relocating staff understand this perfectly, which is why the serviced apartment has become a standard item in a corporate relocation package. It is the least romantic accommodation in the world, and it works.

## Australia

Australian accommodation is the antidote. The hotel exists in a different relationship to space: rooms are bigger, the foyer has a plant that is allowed to grow, and the car park is large enough to hold a family of five and a surfboard. A hotel in a major city is recognisably cosmopolitan. A hotel in a country town is a pub with rooms, and the pub is the point.

That pub is a defining piece of Australian hospitality. The old country hotels, with their wrought-iron verandahs and public bar downstairs, still do a version of the job that predates the motel: a bed upstairs, a counter meal below and a landlord who will tell you precisely what is wrong with the neighbouring town. The modern motel, long-suffering and underrated, serves the road. It gives you a room, a kettle, a car park outside your door and an extremely reasonable lack of fuss.

Service culture is cheerful and informal, and a newcomer from Hong Kong may find it disarmingly relaxed. Staff will chat. Staff will recommend a coffee place. Staff will, on a bad day, be a little slow, and the response will be a shrug and a joke rather than a bow. There is little of the ceremonial courtesy that Hong Kong does so well, and there is no tipping expectation to smooth it over, since wages are meant to cover the job.

The practical traps are about distance and timing. Australia is enormous, so a booking for a regional town can mean a hundred kilometres of nothing before you arrive, and school holidays, race days and festivals can empty the whole region of beds. Expats on short stays get used to checking the calendar before they commit. Long-stayers find that Australia's equivalent of the serviced apartment is plentiful in the capital cities, comfortable, and notably larger than anything in Hong Kong for the same budget in spirit if not in dollars.

There is a subtler point about how each country treats the guest, and it is worth stating plainly. Hong Kong assumes you are in a hurry and builds the entire experience around saving you time: the fast check-in, the pressed shirt, the lift that arrives before you have finished pressing the button. Australia assumes you are on holiday even when you are not, and builds the experience around letting you slow down. A visitor who confuses the two will be disappointed twice. The Hong Kong traveller who expects leisurely charm will find efficiency instead, and the Australian traveller who expects efficiency will find a cheerful shrug. Neither country is wrong. They are simply selling different things under the same word, and the sooner you work out which product you are buying, the happier your stay will be, whatever the thread count.

## The Verdict

Australia wins the room and Hong Kong wins the service. If I am paying for a night I would like to be able to walk around the bed, and Australia lets me. If I am paying for a week and I want my shirt pressed, my meeting room found and my taxi waiting before I have stepped outside, Hong Kong is the only city that does it with that cold, exquisite competence.

For anyone staying longer than a month, the answer is neither. Take the serviced apartment in Hong Kong, accept the compression, and treat the city as the living room. Take the roomy apartment in Sydney or Melbourne and treat the hotel as a pit stop. The right accommodation depends less on the star rating and more on whether you plan to live in it, and that is a lesson both countries teach in completely opposite accents.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *SCMP, serviced-apartment coverage — the sector grew up serving executives and relocating families who needed something between a hotel and a lease, which tells you how many people have discovered that a standard room is a poor place to live.*

> *Expat.com, housing-differences feature — a writer who has moved between Hong Kong and northern Europe notes that the size of a home is the first thing that changes the way you live, from how often you cook to how many people you invite.*

> *Expat Living Hong Kong, accommodation guide — newcomers are advised to think about district and commute before they think about square footage, since where you sleep decides how far you travel.*

## Conclusion

A hotel is a country with a key card. Hong Kong gives you a compressed, efficient, deeply professional version of the world, where every metre earns its keep. Australia gives you a spacious, informal, slightly sunburnt one, where the staff will gladly tell you what to do on a Sunday.

If you are moving from Hong Kong to Australia, enjoy the extra room, learn to chat at reception and stop apologising for taking up space. If you are going the other way, bring a good packing cube, a sense of proportion and a willingness to treat the lobby as your office. Either way, the room is just the beginning of the story.
