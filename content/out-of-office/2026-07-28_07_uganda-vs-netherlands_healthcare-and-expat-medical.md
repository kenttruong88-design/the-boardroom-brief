# One Country Will See You Immediately, the Other Wants You to Try Paracetamol First

🇺🇬 Uganda vs 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Healthcare abroad splits into two categories: places where money buys you speed, and places where speed simply isn't offered at any price because the entire system has collectively decided you're probably fine. Uganda, for anyone with private insurance or cash, offers startlingly fast, personal, private care. The Netherlands, for anyone at all, offers a famously stubborn gatekeeping system built around the national suspicion that most ailments will resolve themselves with rest, water, and paracetamol.

I've had a same-day private consultation in Kampala that felt like being genuinely cared for as a human being, and I've had a Dutch GP tell me, not unkindly, to come back if the symptoms I'd had for two weeks somehow got worse. Both experiences taught me something. Only one of them left me actually reassured.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇺🇬 Uganda

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comprehensive private health insurance before arrival; the private-public gap is significant | Rely on public facilities for anything beyond the most basic care if you have any alternative |
| Research and vet private hospitals in advance — quality varies enormously between providers | Assume rural facilities can handle serious emergencies; medevac coverage is genuinely worth having |
| Keep a stock of any regular prescription medication; availability can be inconsistent outside Kampala | Skip vaccinations and malaria prophylaxis recommendations; this isn't optional caution, it's necessary |

### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a local huisarts (GP) immediately — you legally need one and can't easily walk into a hospital directly | Expect a specialist referral or scan on a first visit; the default response is genuinely "wait and see" |
| Get mandatory basic health insurance within four months of arrival, it's a legal requirement, not optional | Show up at the emergency room for non-emergencies; you'll be firmly redirected to your huisarts |
| Trust the system even when it feels frustratingly conservative; outcomes are genuinely excellent long-term | Assume the level of caution means poor care; it reflects a different philosophy, not lower quality |

## Uganda: Private Care Moves Fast, Public Care Moves When It Can

Healthcare in Uganda exists in two very different tiers, and the gap between them shapes almost every expat's experience of the system. Public hospitals are underfunded and overstretched, dealing with genuinely enormous patient loads on limited resources, and while the staff are frequently skilled and dedicated, the facilities themselves often can't match what a well-insured expat would expect from home. Private hospitals and clinics, concentrated heavily in Kampala, are an entirely different experience — same-day appointments, attentive doctors who actually spend time with you, and a level of personal, unhurried care that many Western systems have simply stopped offering.

The catch, and it's a significant one, is cost and access. Quality private care in Uganda is genuinely excellent but concentrated in urban centres, meaning anyone living or travelling outside Kampala needs a real plan for emergencies, ideally including medical evacuation coverage as part of their insurance. Malaria remains a serious, ongoing concern rather than a travel-brochure footnote, and prophylaxis, vaccinations, and a working relationship with a trusted private clinic aren't optional extras, they're basic infrastructure for staying healthy long-term.

What surprises a lot of newcomers, particularly those coming from systems where doctors are rushed and distant, is the warmth and directness of private Ugandan healthcare. Doctors tend to explain things thoroughly, follow up personally, and treat a consultation as a genuine conversation rather than a fifteen-minute box to check. It's a system built on a very different foundation than European public healthcare, one where money buys not just faster access but a noticeably more human experience, and expats who invest properly in good insurance often describe Ugandan private healthcare as some of the most attentive care they've ever received anywhere.

## Netherlands: Excellent Outcomes, Delivered With Maximum Patience

Dutch healthcare is, by almost every international measure, excellent — high life expectancy, strong outcomes, a well-funded and well-organised universal system built on mandatory private insurance. And yet it has developed a reputation among expats, entirely earned, for a level of conservative caution that borders on the legendary. The Dutch huisarts, your mandatory gatekeeper GP, operates on a philosophy of "wait and see" that can feel almost aggressively unhurried to anyone used to scans, referrals, and prescriptions on the first visit.

Paracetamol has become something of a national punchline among the expat community, not because Dutch doctors are careless, but because the entire system is built around avoiding unnecessary intervention, trusting the body to resolve minor issues on its own, and reserving specialist care, imaging, and stronger medication for situations that genuinely warrant it. It's a philosophy rooted in solid evidence about overtreatment and antibiotic resistance, and Dutch health outcomes back it up convincingly. It just doesn't feel reassuring in the moment, especially to someone who's spent two weeks fighting a stubborn infection and been told, calmly, to give it more time.

The structural side takes real adjustment too. You cannot simply walk into a hospital or see a specialist directly, everything routes through your registered huisarts first, and skipping that step by showing up at an emergency room for a non-urgent issue gets you politely, firmly redirected. Registration with a GP and mandatory basic insurance within four months of arrival are both legal requirements, not suggestions, and expats who delay either one often find themselves scrambling during their first real health scare. The system rewards patience and trust; it does not reward urgency, no matter how urgent it feels to you personally.

## The Verdict

Uganda's private healthcare offers speed, warmth, and genuine personal attention, at a real cost and with real geographic limits. The Netherlands offers a slower, more conservative, but ultimately excellent system that asks you to trust a process that will, at every turn, feel like it's taking your concerns less seriously than you'd like. If you want to feel cared for in the moment, Uganda's private clinics win outright. If you want the best long-term statistical outcome and can tolerate being told to wait it out, the Dutch system, frustrating as it is, genuinely delivers. Bring good insurance to Uganda. Bring patience to the Netherlands.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations Kampala — paraphrased: An expat said their private clinic doctor personally called to follow up two days after a routine appointment, something they said had never happened to them anywhere else they'd lived.*

> *Reddit r/Netherlands — paraphrased: A commenter joked that the Dutch healthcare system has one universal treatment plan — paracetamol and fluids — and that getting a referral for anything short of visible bone fracture feels like winning a small lottery.*

> *expat.com Amsterdam — paraphrased: A newcomer described panicking after being told to "wait two weeks and see" for a persistent symptom, only for a Dutch friend to explain that this genuinely is the standard, evidence-based first response here.*

## Conclusion

Neither system is wrong, exactly, they're just built on completely different assumptions about what good care actually looks like. Uganda's private tier assumes speed and personal attention are worth paying for. The Netherlands assumes patience and restraint produce better outcomes than urgency ever does. Figure out which philosophy you can actually live with before you need either system in a real emergency, because that is absolutely not the moment to discover you picked the wrong expectations to bring with you.
