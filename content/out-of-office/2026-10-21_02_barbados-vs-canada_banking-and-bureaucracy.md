🇧🇧 Barbados 🇨🇦 Canada
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country has a bureaucratic ritual it performs instead of simply trusting you, and banking is where you meet it fastest. In Barbados, that ritual is foreign-exchange control — a polite, unhurried, utterly non-negotiable insistence that every Barbadian dollar leaving the island be accounted for, in person, by someone with the authority to look mildly suspicious of your travel plans. In Canada, the ritual is the credit-history paradox: you cannot get credit until you've had credit, which you cannot get until someone takes a leap of faith on you, which no one will do, because you have no credit.

I have opened accounts in both systems and emerged from each slightly more philosophical about money as an abstract concept than I went in. Barbados makes you perform patience as theatre; Canada makes you perform creditworthiness as a personality trait. Neither is efficient. Both are, in their own way, deeply sincere about the bureaucracy being for your own good.

## Do's & Don'ts: Barbados

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring every original document, twice — passport, proof of address, employment letter, reference letter | Assume a photocopy or a PDF will do; originals are inspected like relics |
| Expect the Central Bank's exchange-control rules to govern any significant transfer off-island | Try to move large sums without pre-clearance; it will simply be refused, politely, at the counter |
| Build a relationship with one branch and one teller — continuity genuinely speeds things up | Bank-hop for convenience; unfamiliar staff restart the scrutiny from zero |
| Treat the "just stepping away for five minutes" from a teller as a true five-to-forty-five minute window | Schedule anything tightly around a bank visit; it will eat your whole morning, cheerfully |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book an appointment with a newcomer-banking advisor before you land, if the bank offers one | Walk in expecting same-day full service; newcomer streams exist for a reason — use them |
| Apply for a secured credit card immediately to start building a credit score from zero | Expect a "normal" credit card on arrival; your spotless overseas credit history means nothing here |
| Keep every piece of mail from the bank — paper statements are still a quiet trust signal | Go fully paperless in year one; some systems still want a visible paper trail for verification |
| Ask explicitly about wire transfer fees and timelines before sending money home | Assume a transfer is instant; interbank transfers can vanish into a multi-day black hole with no explanation |

## Barbados: trust, but verify, then verify again

Barbadian banking etiquette is built around the Central Bank's foreign-exchange control regime, a holdover from a small-island economy's entirely rational fear of capital flight. Moving money off the island above a modest threshold requires documentation, a clearly stated purpose, and often a conversation with someone who has the discretion to say "not today." This isn't corruption or inefficiency for its own sake — it's a currency peg (the Barbados dollar has been pegged to the US dollar since 1975) being actively defended, transaction by transaction, by tellers who have essentially been deputised as junior monetary-policy enforcers.

What catches newcomers off guard isn't the rule itself but the pace at which it's enforced. A visit to a Barbadian bank branch is a social occasion as much as a transaction: you will be known by name within a few visits, greeted properly, asked about your family, and then — only then — helped. Rushing this sequence, treating the greeting as filler before the "real" business, marks you instantly as someone who doesn't understand that the relationship is the collateral. Expats who show up irritated and transactional find the bureaucracy mysteriously slower; those who show up and chat find forms pre-filled before they've finished their coffee.

The flip side is a system that, once you're known, is remarkably forgiving and personal — a teller who remembers you can quietly expedite things that would take a stranger weeks. It's banking as relationship management, with the Central Bank's capital controls as the immovable backdrop everyone has simply learned to work around with patience and a decent attitude.

## Canada: the algorithm pretending to be a person

Canadian banking presents itself as modern, digital, and frictionless, right up until you are a newcomer with no Canadian credit history, at which point it reveals itself as a closed loop that privileges people who already have what you're there to get. You will be offered a chequing account almost instantly — Canada is genuinely good at this part, especially through dedicated newcomer programs at the big five banks — but credit is a different animal entirely. A spotless twenty-year credit history from anywhere else on earth counts for precisely nothing, because Canadian lenders only read Canadian bureaus, and you don't exist in them yet.

The fix — a secured credit card, where you deposit your own money as the credit limit, then slowly earn the right to borrow your own money back under scrutiny — is sensible once explained, and deeply strange the first time you encounter it unexplained. Nobody warns you that the "starter" product is this literal: you're not borrowing the bank's money, you're borrowing your own money back from them while they watch to see if you behave.

Where Canadian banking genuinely surprises Caribbean newcomers is the sheer facelessness of it. There is no teller who will remember you, no relationship to cultivate, no "just mention my name" shortcut — only a call centre, a case number, and a transfer system (especially international wires) that can take days to clear with status updates that range from vague to nonexistent. The system is fair in the sense that it treats everyone with the same mild indifference. Whether that's better or worse than Barbados's warm-but-watchful approach depends entirely on whether you'd rather be known or left alone.

Cash habits tell the rest of the story. Barbados remains a heavily cash-and-cheque society in everyday life outside the tourist corridor — many smaller transactions, and plenty of larger ones, still happen with physical currency or a hand-written cheque, and the island's ATM network, while functional, is nowhere near as dense or digitally integrated as what most newcomers from larger economies expect. Canada, by contrast, has pushed hard toward a near-cashless everyday economy: tap-to-pay is standard even for a coffee, e-transfers between individuals happen instantly and for free through most major banks, and carrying meaningful amounts of cash is increasingly treated as slightly old-fashioned rather than prudent. Newcomers moving from Barbados to Canada often describe an initial disorientation at how little physical money changes hands day to day, while newcomers moving the other direction frequently find themselves caught out, mid-transaction, by a Barbadian vendor who simply doesn't have card facilities at all, and expects cash, now, with no polite workaround available.

## The Verdict

Barbados's banking bureaucracy is slow because it's personal; Canada's is slow because it's impersonal, and somehow that's worse. At least in Bridgetown you're being inconvenienced by someone who knows your name. In Toronto, you're being inconvenienced by a risk model that has never heard of you and isn't curious to learn. If I had to choose which system treats a new arrival with more actual dignity while making them wait, it's Barbados, by a country mile — just bring snacks and your original documents, twice.

## Conclusion

What both systems are quietly protecting is trust — Barbados protects it through relationship and memory, Canada through data and algorithm. Neither will apologise for making you wait, because neither believes it's being slow; each believes it's being careful. The real adjustment isn't learning new forms. It's accepting that in one country your reputation is the collateral, and in the other, your reputation doesn't exist yet and the system will make you build one from absolute zero, politely, while charging you a monthly fee for the privilege.
