# South Korea Automates Everything Except Trusting Foreigners. Costa Rica Trusts No One, Digitally or Otherwise.

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

South Korea is a country that has built one of the most dazzlingly efficient digital infrastructures on earth and then, with a straight face, tells foreigners they cannot open a bank account without an Alien Registration Card, a specific visa status, and occasionally a Korean phone number that itself requires a bank account to obtain. Costa Rica, meanwhile, has never pretended to be fast — its bureaucracy is unapologetically, gloriously paper-based, and opening an account or securing residency is a process measured in stamped documents, notarised translations, and a patience reserve most newcomers don't realise they'll need until it's already been tested.

I have stood in a Seoul bank branch being told, kindly but firmly, that I did not exist yet as a financial entity. I have also sat in a San José bank waiting for a manager's personal signature on a form that could apparently be provided by no one else in the building. Both moments taught me the same lesson: bring every document you own, and then bring copies.

## Do's & Don'ts: South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your Alien Registration Card (ARC) sorted first — almost everything depends on it | Expect to open a bank account on a tourist visa or before ARC issuance |
| Use banks with dedicated foreigner desks (KEB Hana, Woori) for smoother onboarding | Assume online banking apps will work fully without a Korean phone number |
| Bring your employment contract and passport to every single appointment | Get frustrated at the sequencing — ARC, then phone, then bank, in that order |

## Do's & Don'ts: Costa Rica

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring notarised, translated copies of every document, always, everywhere | Expect a single bureaucratic visit to resolve anything on the first try |
| Use a local lawyer or gestor to navigate residency and banking paperwork | Assume "pura vida" pace applies to how fast your paperwork moves |
| Budget significant time — weeks to months — for account or residency approval | Lose your temper with bank staff — patience and courtesy speed things up |

## South Korea: A Perfectly Oiled Machine That Doesn't Recognise You Yet

South Korea's domestic digital infrastructure is, without exaggeration, one of the most advanced in the world — instant mobile payments, seamless government digital services, banking apps that do more than most countries' entire financial systems combined. And yet the first months of any foreigner's financial life in Korea are defined by a strict, sequential gauntlet that feels almost deliberately at odds with that efficiency: you need an Alien Registration Card to get a Korean phone number, you often need a Korean phone number to open a bank account, and you need a bank account for approximately everything else, from rent to salary deposits to the various subscriptions that make Korean life so convenient once you're actually inside the system.

The Financial Services Commission's regulatory framework, combined with strict anti-fraud banking requirements, means foreigners without an ARC or with an unclear visa status are routinely turned away from account applications, regardless of how much cash they're holding or how sincerely they explain their situation. Banks like KEB Hana and Woori have built out foreigner-specific service desks precisely because this friction is so well known, and expat forums treat "which bank has the English-speaking foreigner desk in your neighbourhood" as genuinely load-bearing local knowledge, passed down like folklore. Once you're through the gauntlet, though, Korea's system rewards you generously — near-instant transfers, comprehensive mobile banking, and a level of digital convenience that makes the initial weeks of bureaucratic limbo feel, in hindsight, like a strange, temporary hazing ritual for an otherwise excellent system.

## Costa Rica: Pura Vida Does Not Apply to Paperwork

Costa Rica's national ethos of "pura vida" — relaxed, unhurried, present-focused — famously does not extend to its bureaucracy, and newcomers who assume otherwise are in for a rude and lengthy awakening. Opening a bank account or securing residency documentation here is a genuinely analogue process, heavy on notarised paperwork, certified translations, in-person visits, and a marked institutional preference for physical signatures over digital verification. The Banco Central de Costa Rica's regulatory environment, shaped partly by strict anti-money-laundering requirements following past international scrutiny, means foreign account applicants face substantial documentation requirements — proof of income, proof of address, sometimes a local reference — processed at a pace that can charitably be called deliberate.

What experienced expats near-universally recommend is hiring a local gestor or lawyer to shepherd the process, not as an indulgence but as a practical necessity — someone who understands which office needs which stamp on which day, and who can navigate the very real possibility that a form accepted on Monday will be rejected on Thursday for a reason nobody can quite articulate. The Costa Rica Ministry of Foreign Trade's own residency guidance is candid about processing timelines running into months rather than weeks, and the community consensus on forums like expat.com is remarkably consistent: bring more documents than you think you need, bring copies of those documents, and bring a level of patience that would make a Zen monk restless. The reward, once through it, is genuine: a country that, bureaucracy aside, delivers on its reputation for quality of life once the paperwork stops being the main event.

## The Verdict

South Korea's bureaucracy is sequential and strict but ultimately fast once you clear each gate — a series of locked doors that, once opened, stay open. Costa Rica's bureaucracy is looser in structure but slower in practice, a genuinely analogue system that tests patience over precision. If I had to choose which frustration to endure, I'd take Korea's front-loaded gauntlet over Costa Rica's open-ended waiting game — at least in Seoul, you know roughly when the ordeal ends.

## Conclusion

South Korea will make you wait at the front door before handing you the keys to one of the smoothest digital economies on earth. Costa Rica will make you wait throughout the entire process, keys included, and call it pura vida the whole time without a hint of irony. Bring documents to both, patience to both, and in Costa Rica specifically, bring someone who's already done this before — the paperwork does not reward optimism.
