# One Country's Currency Barely Exists Online, the Other Just Wants Three Months of Payslips

🇨🇺 Cuba vs 🇨🇿 Czechia — banking and bureaucracy

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Cuba, your foreign card might simply stop working, for reasons nobody will fully explain, in a banking system so cut off from Visa, Mastercard, SWIFT, and PayPal that you may as well be conducting business on the moon. In Czechia, opening a bank account is merely tedious — passport, proof of address, a phone number for verification codes, and, if you're not an EU citizen, a residence permit, followed by a three-month wait if you want a credit card, because apparently every bank in Prague suspects you might vanish back to wherever you came from. One system is broken by design. The other is just bureaucratic in the ordinary, mildly infuriating European way.

I've had a Cuban ATM eat my card out of what felt like pure spite, and I've sat in a Czech Komerční banka branch for forty minutes proving I existed to a clerk who seemed personally unbothered either way. Only one of these experiences left me questioning the actual laws of modern finance.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇨🇺 Cuba**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring cash — genuinely more cash than feels reasonable, given how unreliable card access is | Assume any foreign bank card will reliably work; failures are common and largely unpredictable |
| Understand that MLC (freely convertible currency) cards can't be topped up with cash and expire after two years, balance unrefunded | Expect the 2021 abolition of the CUC to have simplified things; multiple exchange segments still coexist in practice |
| Budget for a genuinely dollarised parallel economy running alongside the official one | Rely on Visa, Mastercard, SWIFT, PayPal, Wise, or Stripe working in any conventional sense — Cuba is essentially cut off from all of them |

**🇨🇿 Czechia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your passport, proof of address, and a Czech phone number to your account-opening appointment | Expect a credit card quickly; it can take up to three months and typically requires three months of payslips as proof of income |
| Know your legal right to a basic account — banks can't refuse you outright for not being a citizen | Assume every bank offers English-language service; UniCredit, Komerční banka, and Raiffeisenbank are the reliably foreigner-friendly options |
| Get a Czech SIM early — verification codes for online banking are sent to it and it's needed almost everywhere | Show up without a residence permit if you're a non-EU national staying over 90 days; it's a hard requirement |

## Cuba: Banking in a Country the Global Financial System Forgot

To understand Cuban banking, you first have to accept that the ordinary assumptions of global finance simply don't apply here. The sector is almost entirely state-controlled, running multiple official exchange rates simultaneously alongside a thriving informal market that most residents and long-term visitors quietly rely on more than the official one. The 2021 monetary reform was supposed to fix this — the Cuban Peso became the sole official legal tender, the old CUC dual-currency system formally abolished — but the lived reality remains stubbornly, almost defiantly complicated, with several exchange segments still coexisting and large parts of the economy still effectively dollarised in practice if not on paper.

For anyone actually trying to move money in or out, the real headline is isolation. Cuba's domestic digital payment infrastructure is advancing, slowly, but remains almost entirely severed from the systems the rest of the world takes for granted: no meaningful Visa or Mastercard functionality, no SWIFT access worth mentioning, no PayPal, no Wise, no Stripe. Foreign bank cards routinely fail for reasons that are rarely explained and never apologised for. State-run import stores frequently require payment via MLC — freely convertible currency — loaded onto a special card that, crucially, cannot be topped up with cash and expires after two years with no refund of any remaining balance. Foreign companies, meanwhile, have reportedly hit real friction trying to repatriate funds held in Cuban accounts, a detail that should concern anyone assuming they can simply wire money out when they're ready to leave.

None of this makes Cuba impossible to navigate financially — cash, patience, and a tolerance for ambiguity get you remarkably far. But it does mean treating Cuban banking like European or even broader Latin American banking is a mistake that will cost you, literally, in fees, failed transactions, and stranded balances you can't recover.

## Czechia: Bureaucratic, Slow, But Fundamentally Fair

Czechia's banking bureaucracy is a different animal entirely — not broken, just thorough, in the specific, form-heavy way continental European bureaucracy tends to be. EU citizens have it relatively easy: an ID card or passport is often sufficient to open a basic account. Non-EU foreigners face a slightly steeper climb, needing a residence permit or visa valid for at least 90 days, plus proof of Czech address, plus — increasingly non-negotiable — a Czech phone number, since verification codes and online banking access both route through it.

The genuinely useful thing to know, and the detail most newcomers miss, is that Czech law actually guarantees a right to a basic account: banks cannot simply turn you away for not holding Czech citizenship. That basic account covers the essentials — deposits, withdrawals, payment orders, direct debits, a debit card, internet banking — which puts a legal floor under an otherwise opaque and occasionally frustrating process. Credit cards are where the real friction lives: approval can take up to three months, and banks typically want to see three months of consistent salary payments before extending credit, a reasonable-sounding requirement that nonetheless leaves plenty of new arrivals stuck without a credit card for their first quarter in the country.

Foreigner-friendly banks do exist and are worth seeking out specifically — UniCredit, Komerční banka, and Raiffeisenbank all offer meaningful English-language support, which cuts through a significant chunk of the frustration. It's slow. It's form-heavy. But it is, at every step, a system that follows knowable rules, applied consistently, with a legal backstop protecting you from outright exclusion — which, compared to Cuba's opacity, counts as a genuine luxury.

## The Verdict

Cuba's banking system isn't merely bureaucratic, it's structurally disconnected from the rest of the financial world, and no amount of patience or paperwork will change that — you simply adapt, carry cash, and accept the ambiguity. Czechia's system, by contrast, is tedious but fundamentally trustworthy: slow, form-obsessed, occasionally maddening over a three-month credit card wait, but ultimately playing by rules you can actually learn and rely on. Give me Czech tedium over Cuban opacity every single time. Predictable bureaucracy, however slow, beats a system where your card might simply stop working and nobody owes you an explanation.

## Conclusion

There's a version of bureaucratic frustration that's actually a sign of a functioning system — slow, form-heavy, occasionally absurd, but ultimately fair and navigable. That's Czechia. And there's a version that reflects genuine structural isolation from the global financial system, where the frustration isn't a design flaw but a geopolitical fact of life. That's Cuba. Neither is going to change to suit your convenience, so plan accordingly: bring patience and a Czech phone number to Prague, and bring cash, more cash, and a healthy tolerance for ambiguity to Havana. Only one of these problems gets solved by waiting three months.
