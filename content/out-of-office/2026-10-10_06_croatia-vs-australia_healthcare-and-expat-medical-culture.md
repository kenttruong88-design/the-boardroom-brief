# Getting Sick Is Cheap And Confusing In One Country, Expensive And Confusing In The Other

🇭🇷 Croatia 🇦🇺 Australia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is no faster way to learn how a country actually feels about its foreigners than to get mildly, inconveniently ill in it. Pleasantries evaporate. What's left is the system — who it was built for, who it tolerates, and who it quietly expects to just go home and deal with it there.

Croatia and Australia both run healthcare systems their own citizens genuinely love, which is rarer than it sounds, and both treat the foreigner-in-the-system question with a bureaucratic shrug that assumes you'll sort it out eventually. One is a small, EU-adjacent public system riding entirely on the HZZO and a patchwork of reciprocal agreements. The other is a vast, well-funded public system with a reciprocal-agreement trapdoor that fewer visitors understand than should.

## Do's & Don'ts: Croatia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether your home country has a reciprocal agreement with HZZO before arrival | Assume EHIC-style coverage from elsewhere automatically transfers without verification |
| Carry private travel insurance regardless, even with a reciprocal agreement in hand | Expect every pharmacist or GP outside Zagreb and Split to speak fluent English |
| Learn the difference between a public (državna) and private (privatna) clinic upfront | Walk into a private clinic expecting public-system prices, or vice versa |
| Keep physical copies of any insurance documentation — digital-only is a gamble | Delay registering with a local GP once you've settled somewhere for longer than a few months |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check immediately whether your country has a Reciprocal Health Care Agreement with Medicare | Assume Medicare covers you automatically just because you're a long-stay visitor |
| Buy private health insurance the moment you arrive if you're not RHCA-eligible | Wait to sort coverage out "once something actually happens" |
| Ask explicitly whether a clinic still bulk-bills before booking — many no longer do | Assume a doctor's visit is free; out-of-pocket gaps have grown considerably |
| Understand the public/private hospital split before an emergency forces the decision | Ignore ambulance-cover gaps — in several states, ambulance use isn't automatically covered |

## Croatia: Small, Public, And Reliant On Who Your Passport Says You Are

Croatia's healthcare system, run through HZZO, is genuinely well-regarded by the people who live inside it permanently — solid primary care, reasonable costs, a public backbone that doesn't bankrupt anyone the way some larger countries' systems can. The catch for a foreigner is that your actual experience of this system depends almost entirely on a document most visitors never think to check: whether your home country has a reciprocal healthcare agreement with Croatia.

The Croatian Ministry of Health's own guidance on this is clear but rarely read before arrival, and the practical result is a two-tier experience that has nothing to do with how sick you are and everything to do with your passport. EU citizens with a valid EHIC generally slot into the public system with reasonable ease. Everyone else is navigating a genuinely more opaque landscape of private insurance requirements, out-of-pocket costs at public facilities, and an expectation — rarely stated outright, universally understood by anyone who's lived there a while — that long-term foreign residents simply carry private cover as a baseline, reciprocal agreement or not.

Outside Zagreb and the major coastal cities, the English-language gap becomes its own real factor. Smaller-town pharmacists and GPs are perfectly competent, warmly attentive, and frequently operating in Croatian only, which turns a simple prescription refill into a small act of improvised translation. None of this makes Croatian healthcare bad. It makes it a system built, sensibly enough, around its own citizens and EU neighbours first, with everyone else slotted in via whatever bilateral paperwork happens to exist.

## Australia: Generous To Its Own, A Trapdoor For Everyone Else

Australia's Medicare system enjoys a reputation, largely earned, as one of the more comprehensive public healthcare systems among wealthy English-speaking countries. What that reputation obscures, and what the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare's own reporting increasingly flags, is a system under real strain — bulk billing (where a doctor charges Medicare directly, with no out-of-pocket cost to the patient) has been quietly retreating in many areas, and a growing number of GP visits now carry a gap payment that would have been unthinkable a decade ago.

For foreigners, the critical variable is the Reciprocal Health Care Agreement, a bilateral arrangement Australia holds with a specific list of countries that grants visiting or temporarily resident citizens of those countries limited Medicare access. If your country isn't on that list — and a great many aren't — you are, functionally, a self-pay patient in a system that was never built with you in mind, and private health insurance stops being a nice-to-have and becomes the only sensible option almost immediately.

What catches people out hardest is the ambulance gap. In several Australian states, ambulance transport is not automatically covered by Medicare even for citizens, and plenty of long-term foreign residents discover this fact only via an invoice arriving weeks after an emergency they assumed, reasonably, would simply be handled. The public-private hospital split adds another layer: public hospitals provide genuinely excellent emergency and essential care, but elective procedures can mean lengthy public waitlists, pushing anyone with the means and the inclination toward private hospital cover, and pushing anyone without either toward a wait that the AIHW's own performance data confirms has been stretching in several specialties.

## The Verdict

Croatia's system is smaller, cheaper at the point of use for those covered, and genuinely warm in its in-person delivery, but it leans hard on a reciprocal-agreement lottery that most visitors never check until they need to. Australia's system is bigger, better resourced, and more reliably excellent in a genuine emergency, but it's been quietly shifting costs onto patients for years in ways that catch long-term foreign residents off guard precisely because the system's reputation hasn't caught up with its current reality. If I had to bet my own health on one system with no insurance and no reciprocal agreement, I'd take Croatia's lower absolute costs over Australia's higher ones. Croatia wins this one, on price alone, which feels like a slightly depressing way to judge a healthcare system, and is nonetheless exactly how most foreigners actually experience the choice.

## Conclusion

Both countries will happily tell you, with total sincerity, that their healthcare system is a point of national pride, and in both cases they're not wrong about the system their own citizens experience. The gap between that pride and your actual experience as a foreigner is measured entirely in paperwork you were never told to read — a reciprocal agreement here, a bulk-billing clause there — and the only real defence is reading it before you need it, not after. Nobody at the pharmacy counter is going to volunteer the information. They assumed, reasonably enough, that you'd already checked.
