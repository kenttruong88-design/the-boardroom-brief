# Mexico Spends the Weekend at the Family Table and Spain Spends It There Too, but Later and Louder

🇲🇽 Mexico vs 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A Mexican weekend is built around food and family. Saturday is for errands, the market and a loose agenda that dissolves into a long lunch. Sunday is for the extended clan, the big comida, the grandmother who disapproves of everything you are wearing and the uncle who has a firm opinion on football. There is music, there are children running underfoot, and somebody will inevitably produce a bottle that was not part of the plan.

A Spanish weekend is built around the street. People go out, because going out is what a weekend is for, and the terrace is the national office. Lunch starts late, dinner starts later, and between them there is a stretch known as sobremesa, the time at the table after the food is gone, in which nobody moves and everybody talks. Two cultures, one language, and an argument about what time it is.

## Mexico

The Mexican weekend is not an escape from life. It is life, at full volume. Families are large, close and geographically knotted, and the weekend is the natural time for the knot to tighten. A Sunday meal can involve three generations, a dozen dishes and a rolling debate about who forgot to bring the tortillas. Mexico News Daily describes the sobremesa tradition as an art form, and it is: the time after eating, when the plates are cleared but the table is not, is where the real conversation happens.

Leisure in Mexico is social by default. There are weekend markets, the tianguis, where you can buy produce, clothes, spare parts and a hot lunch from the same street. There are parks full of families, plazas with music, beaches for those within reach and town fiestas that pop up with the regularity of a rumour. Saints' days, birthdays and anniversaries supply an unending supply of reasons to gather, and Mexicans have never needed much of a reason.

The pace is generous. Plans are loose, time is elastic and the notion of arriving precisely at the agreed hour can be read as slightly eager. For expats from stricter cultures, this is either liberating or infuriating, and the difference usually comes down to whether you have something to do on Monday.

It would be wrong to portray the Mexican weekend as all relaxation. Domestic work, family obligations and the commute to see relatives consume large parts of it. But the point is that the obligations are the pleasure. You do not escape your family on the weekend. You are, gloriously and sometimes exhaustingly, in it.

The money side of a Mexican weekend is also worth a note. Eating out, markets and family events are comparatively inexpensive, and the culture of generosity means the bill is often a matter of competitive hospitality. You will lose the argument over who pays. You will also, without noticing, be expected to host the next one, so plan accordingly.

## Spain

The Spanish weekend is conducted largely outdoors. Terraces fill by midday. Squares are populated with families, teenagers and elderly men in conversation about nothing in particular. The paseo, the evening stroll, is still alive in many towns, and the culture of going out is so ingrained that staying home on a Saturday night can invite concern.

Spanish timetables baffle newcomers. Lunch is a long, substantial meal, often after two. Dinner rarely begins before nine, and in the summer it drifts later still. Expat articles about the country return again and again to the sobremesa, the lingering after a meal that foreigners come to cherish, and to a looser relationship with planning. A Mexican who has moved to Spain recognises much of this and is also surprised by it. The warmth is familiar. The timing is not.

Last-minute plans are common, and a writer based in Seville has observed that spontaneity is a cultural feature, not a flaw. The invitation arrives an hour before and is expected to be accepted. If you insist on a calendar entry two weeks in advance, you will be an object of mild, affectionate suspicion.

Practicalities matter. Many shops close on Sundays or have limited hours, siesta closures still occur in smaller towns, and the working week ends with a rush. Weekend leisure includes day trips to the coast or the mountains, long meals and an astonishing amount of walking. The country seems to run on a combination of coffee, conversation and stubborn refusal to hurry.

Spain's calendar adds fiestas, local saints' days and long weekends known as puentes, which are bridges between a holiday and a weekend and are treated as sacred. Cities empty out, coastal towns fill and everyone suddenly has a cousin with an apartment by the sea. It is wonderful and a little relentless, and by Monday you will want a holiday.

## The Verdict

Mexico wins on warmth. A Mexican weekend wraps you in family, whether it is yours or someone else's. The generosity is unreasonable, the food is unbeatable and the sense of belonging arrives within hours.

Spain wins on freedom. The public life of a Spanish weekend, with its terraces, squares and walkable cities, means you can be sociable without being obligated. You can join in or slip away. Nobody takes it personally.

My vote goes to Spain for the single who values independence and to Mexico for anyone who wants to be adopted. If I had to pick for myself, I would choose Spain on a Saturday and Mexico on a Sunday, which is, naturally, the one thing neither country will let you do.

## Conclusion

Two countries share a language and a love of the table, and they have built entirely different weekends on top of them. Mexico gathers inside. Spain spills outside. Both involve far too much food and an unreasonable amount of talking.

Whichever you choose, surrender the schedule. Neither country will meet you at your deadline. Both will, if you let them, make you wonder why you ever treated the weekend as a place to catch up on emails.
