# One Country Dates in Whispers and Family Committees, the Other Dates Loudly, in Public, on the Dance Floor

🇧🇩 Bangladesh × 🇨🇴 Colombia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Dhaka, a courtship can unfold almost entirely through intermediaries — an aunt here, a family friend there, a carefully engineered "coincidental" meeting that both families absolutely orchestrated — with the couple themselves sometimes speaking directly for the first time only after the broader social apparatus has already given its provisional blessing. In Bogotá, by contrast, I have watched a couple meet at a salsa club, dance for four hours, and by the time the lights came up, appeared to have already introduced each other to half the room as a couple.

Bangladesh's dating culture, particularly outside its most cosmopolitan pockets, remains heavily shaped by family involvement, social discretion, and a courtship pace that prioritises collective approval over individual spontaneity. Colombia's social and dating scene runs almost entirely in the opposite direction — public, physical, unhurried by shame, and built around dancing, shared meals, and an openness about romantic interest that would read as forward almost anywhere else. Neither approach is shy about its own logic.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇧🇩 Bangladesh**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect family involvement even in relationships framed as "modern" | Assume Western-style casual public dating is the norm nationwide |
| Understand introductions often happen through trusted intermediaries | Publicly display affection; it's viewed as inappropriate in most settings |
| Recognise urban, educated circles have more flexibility than rural ones | Assume all courtship follows one rigid, uniform national pattern |
| Approach a serious relationship as something families will weigh in on | Treat parental involvement as unusual or a red flag |

**🇨🇴 Colombia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to dance, at least a little; it's genuinely central to courtship | Expect to sit out the dance floor and still be taken seriously |
| Expect directness about romantic interest, expressed early and openly | Read early enthusiasm as insincere; it's usually genuine |
| Embrace group outings as a normal, common way relationships start | Assume every social gathering is romantically neutral |
| Be comfortable with public affection; it's unremarkable here | Treat visible affection between couples as attention-seeking |

## Bangladesh: Courtship as a Family Project

Dating in Bangladesh, particularly outside university campuses and the most urban, liberal social circles, still operates substantially within a framework where family involvement isn't an intrusion on a relationship, it's an expected part of how a serious relationship comes to exist at all. Even couples who meet independently — at university, through work, through mutual friends — often find that the relationship doesn't feel fully real, socially speaking, until families are informed and, ideally, approving.

Public displays of affection remain broadly frowned upon across most of the country, and open, casual dating in the Western sense — meeting strangers, going on a string of low-commitment dates, being public about who you're seeing — carries a social risk that most young Bangladeshis navigate by keeping early-stage relationships genuinely private, sometimes for months, until they're confident enough in the relationship's seriousness to begin the process of family introduction. This isn't universally rigid; Dhaka's more cosmopolitan, educated younger generation has considerably more room to date on their own terms than a generation ago, and dating apps have quietly gained real traction in exactly these circles. But even there, the endpoint of a serious relationship almost always still runs through family acknowledgment, not around it.

What visitors and expats often misjudge is assuming this reflects a lack of romantic culture altogether — it doesn't. It reflects a different organising principle: romance here is rarely treated as a purely private, individual matter, but as something inherently connected to family and community standing, something to be managed carefully rather than displayed spontaneously. Discretion isn't repression in this framework, it's simply how seriousness is communicated.

## Colombia: Romance as a Public, Physical, Group Activity

Colombia's dating and social culture runs on almost the opposite logic entirely: romance here is loud, physical, communal, and largely unembarrassed by an audience. Salsa, vallenato, and reggaetón aren't just background music to a night out, dancing is genuinely one of the primary ways courtship happens, and a willingness (and reasonable ability) to dance well is taken seriously as a marker of romantic compatibility in a way few other cultures would expect.

Directness is prized over ambiguity. Expressing romantic interest early, openly, and with real enthusiasm isn't considered forward or presumptuous, it's simply how genuine interest is communicated, and the slow-burn ambiguity common in more reserved dating cultures can actually read as disinterest or insincerity to a Colombian partner expecting clearer signals. Group outings — a whole friend circle heading out dancing, to dinner, to a weekend trip — function as a common, low-pressure way new couples form, with the group setting providing exactly the kind of relaxed context where interest can be tested without the heavy formality of a one-on-one first date.

Public affection, similarly, draws none of the social scrutiny it might elsewhere. Couples holding hands, dancing closely, or being visibly affectionate in a park or restaurant are simply part of the social texture, not a statement. Colombia's dating culture, at its core, treats romance as something meant to be lived out loud, in public, among friends — the opposite of something to be managed quietly until it's ready for scrutiny.

## The Verdict

Bangladesh treats a serious relationship as a project requiring family involvement and social discretion before it's considered fully real. Colombia treats romance as a public, physical, communal activity best expressed loudly and directly, ideally on a dance floor. One culture protects a relationship's seriousness through privacy; the other proves it through visibility. Both are entirely coherent on their own terms, and both would find the other's approach faintly baffling.

Given the choice, I have more fun in Colombia's version of things — there's something genuinely freeing about a culture where enthusiasm doesn't need to be hidden — but I have far more respect for the quiet, deliberate seriousness with which a Bangladeshi family approaches the question of whether someone is actually right for their child, rather than just their Friday night.

> *Quora, "How do young people in Dhaka date when public dating is so restricted?" — paraphrased: a respondent explained that most serious relationships operate in a kind of open secret phase, known to close friends but carefully kept from wider family until the couple is confident enough to formalise things.*

> *expat.com Dhaka — paraphrased: an expat described being surprised at how quickly a partner's family became involved once a relationship was disclosed, and realising that was viewed as a sign of seriousness, not an imposition.*

> *Internations Bogotá — paraphrased: a newcomer said they were initially unnerved by how quickly a Colombian date expressed clear romantic interest, before realising the directness was simply the local norm, not a red flag.*

## Conclusion

Bangladesh and Colombia have built two dating cultures around opposite instincts for what makes a relationship feel legitimate — one through careful, family-anchored discretion, the other through open, physical, communal enthusiasm. Bring Colombia's directness into a Bangladeshi courtship and you'll move too fast for a system built on patience and family timing. Bring Bangladesh's discretion into a Colombian dance floor and you'll be mistaken for someone who simply isn't interested. Learn the local signal for seriousness before you assume your own instincts translate.
