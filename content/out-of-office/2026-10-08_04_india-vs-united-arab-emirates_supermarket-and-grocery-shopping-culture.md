# India Will Sell You Rice By Relationship. The UAE Will Sell You Rice By Aisle Number.

🇮🇳 India · 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Grocery shopping reveals more about a culture's values than its parliament does. India's answer to "where do I buy rice" is "which kirana shopkeeper has known your family the longest," a question with a social dimension, a credit dimension, and occasionally a gossip dimension. The UAE's answer is "whichever air-conditioned hypermarket has the better parking," a question with a logistics dimension and, frankly, nothing else, and I mean that as a genuine compliment to both systems.

I have bought vegetables from a man in Jaipur who has sold vegetables to my friend's family for three generations and knows more about their finances than their accountant does. I have also bought vegetables from a Carrefour in Dubai so vast and so cold it functions as an unofficial public park for people avoiding the August heat. Both are grocery shopping. Neither resembles the other in any way beyond the produce aisle.

## Do's & Don'ts: India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a relationship with your local kirana — credit, delivery, and loyalty all flow from it | Assume the posted price is fixed everywhere — small shops often expect a light negotiation |
| Learn which market day or morning hours get the freshest produce in your neighbourhood | Shop for vegetables in the evening if morning-fresh is an option — quality visibly drops |
| Carry cash and small notes — not every stall takes digital payment, even in cities | Expect Western-style fixed aisles and pricing logic at a street market — it runs on different rules entirely |
| Try the local bazaar before defaulting to the nearest supermarket chain — the quality gap often favours the bazaar | Be surprised when the same shop's prices shift with season, festival, or supply — it's normal, not a scam |

## Do's & Don'ts: United Arab Emirates

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Compare Carrefour, LuLu, and Spinneys — pricing and selection genuinely differ by nationality of stock carried | Assume one hypermarket chain covers everything — specialty imports often need a specific store |
| Shop late evening in summer — it's an air-conditioned errand that doubles as heat relief | Expect bargaining at a supermarket chain — pricing is as fixed as it looks |
| Use grocery delivery apps — they're fast, cheap, and genuinely woven into daily life here | Forget that alcohol and pork products are sold only in specific licensed sections or stores |
| Stock up during sale cycles — UAE retail runs frequent, substantial promotional periods | Assume every neighbourhood has a hypermarket within walking distance — car-dependent planning still matters |

## India: Relationship as Retail Infrastructure

The kirana store — the small, family-run neighbourhood shop that still accounts for the overwhelming majority of India's roughly $900 billion retail market — isn't a nostalgic holdout against modern retail. It's the dominant system, full stop, and understanding why requires understanding what it actually offers that a supermarket structurally cannot: memory. The shopkeeper remembers you ran out of sugar last Tuesday and set some aside. He remembers your mother-in-law's dietary restrictions. He extends informal credit during a lean month because he's watched your family pay reliably for a decade, an arrangement no corporate loyalty programme has ever matched because it requires a human who stays in one place and pays attention.

Supermarkets and hypermarket chains have absolutely arrived, especially in the bigger metros, and they're genuinely useful for imported goods, bulk staples, and the specific convenience of one-stop shopping on a Sunday. But research on Indian consumer retail preference consistently finds something modern retail planners find mildly infuriating: price and convenience don't reliably beat trust and relationship, even among urban, income-comfortable shoppers who could easily afford to shift entirely to organised retail. The kirana isn't surviving despite modernisation. It's surviving because it does something modernisation hasn't actually figured out how to replicate.

The shopping experience itself is sensory in a way sanitised retail rarely is — the specific choreography of pointing at vegetables while the vendor weighs, haggles lightly, and tosses in a free bundle of coriander as a relationship gesture rather than a loyalty-points transaction. It's slower. It's also, genuinely, more fun, which is not a word I apply to grocery shopping lightly.

## United Arab Emirates: Grocery Shopping as Climate Control With Extra Steps

The UAE's supermarket culture solves a problem India's doesn't really have to think about: how do you make outdoor-adjacent errands tolerable in a country where "summer" means regularly exceeding 45°C. The answer is the hypermarket — Carrefour, LuLu, Spinneys, Choithrams — vast, deeply air-conditioned retail cathedrals that function as part grocery run, part heat shelter, part genuine social outing, especially for families filling an evening.

What's most striking to a newcomer is the sheer breadth of global stock carried under one roof, a direct reflection of the country's population — the majority of UAE residents are expatriates, and the big chains have built entire aisles around specific diasporas: a substantial South Asian grocery section next to a Filipino goods aisle next to an Eastern European import shelf, all inside the same LuLu. It's retail as demographic mirror, restocked to match whoever actually lives in that neighbourhood.

The system is also almost entirely fixed-price and friction-free in a way that can feel jarringly impersonal after India's negotiated, relational version — there's no haggling, no shopkeeper who remembers your face, just efficient scanning and a loyalty app. Grocery delivery culture has exploded here too; apps promising groceries within the hour are less a convenience than a genuine necessity during the hottest months, when walking even to a nearby hypermarket is a legitimate health consideration rather than mere laziness. The UAE didn't inherit a grocery culture so much as import a dozen of them and air-condition the result.

## The Verdict

India's system runs on memory and relationship; the UAE's runs on climate survival and import logistics. Both are completely rational responses to their own conditions, and neither would work transplanted into the other's context — a kirana-style credit relationship means nothing in a transient expat population that might relocate countries within two years, and a 45°C hypermarket culture would be a baffling overcorrection in a place where mornings are genuinely pleasant for a market stroll. If pushed, I'd take India's version for pure experience and the UAE's for pure convenience, which is, I realise, not a verdict at all. Suki does not do cop-outs often. Today is the exception.

## Conclusion

Grocery shopping, done right, tells you who a place actually is underneath the branding. India tells you it's a country still run substantially on relationship and memory, even as glass-fronted retail rises around the kiranas. The UAE tells you it's a country built, deliberately and recently, to make an unlivable climate liveable through sheer engineered convenience. Both are right. Both will make you reconsider how a bag of rice is supposed to feel, and neither, Suki promises, will ever let you forget which one you're standing in.
