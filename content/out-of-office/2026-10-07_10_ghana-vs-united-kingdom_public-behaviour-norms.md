# A Country Where Skipping the Greeting Is a Genuine Offence and a Country Where Skipping the Queue Is a Genuine Act of War

🇬🇭 Ghana vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom — Public Behaviour Norms

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ghana will teach you, fast and unmistakably, that walking into any interaction without a proper greeting first — a real one, with eye contact and actual warmth, not a mumbled hello — is treated as a small social violation, regardless of how urgent your actual business is. The United Kingdom will teach you, equally fast, that the queue is sacred, personal space is a near-constitutional right, and raising your voice in public is treated with the kind of silent, withering disapproval usually reserved for genuine crimes.

I have been gently, firmly corrected in Accra for trying to rush straight to business without greeting someone's entire extended family first, and I have also watched a perfectly polite Englishman's soul visibly leave his body when a tourist cut in front of him at a bus stop. Both reactions are entirely culturally logical. Neither country will apologise for having strong feelings about it.

## Do's & Don'ts: Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet people properly before any transaction or conversation — this is non-negotiable social etiquette, not optional small talk | Rush straight into business or a request without greeting first; it reads as genuinely rude, not efficient |
| Use the right hand for giving and receiving items — the left hand carries real cultural weight here | Point directly at someone with a single finger; it's considered impolite in many everyday interactions |
| Expect and enjoy lively, warm public interactions — loudness here often signals friendliness, not aggression | Mistake animated conversation volume for anger; context and tone matter more than raw volume |
| Show respect to elders explicitly and visibly in public settings — it is noticed and genuinely valued | Assume punctuality works the same way everywhere; social events often run on a notably more flexible schedule |

## Do's & Don'ts: United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the queue immediately and without question, wherever one has formed — this is close to sacred | Cut in line, even accidentally; the silent judgment you'll receive is more severe than any verbal confrontation |
| Keep your voice down in public transport and enclosed spaces — restraint is the social default | Stand too close in a conversation; personal space expectations here are noticeably larger than in many cultures |
| Say "sorry" liberally, even when something wasn't remotely your fault; it's social lubricant, not an admission | Make loud phone calls on public transport; it is one of the most reliably irritating things you can do here |
| Use polite indirectness for complaints — "this isn't quite right" goes further than direct confrontation | Assume British reserve means coldness; warmth exists, it's just expressed with considerably more understatement |

## Ghana: Warmth as a Non-Negotiable Social Rule

Public behaviour in Ghana is built around a principle that catches a lot of newcomers off guard: the greeting is not a pleasant optional extra, it is the actual foundation that every other interaction depends on. Walk into a shop, a government office, or even a brief roadside exchange without properly greeting the person first, and you will be read, correctly, as someone who either doesn't know better or doesn't care to. The Ghana Tourism Authority's own cultural guidance for visitors leads with exactly this point, because it genuinely matters more than almost anything else you could get wrong.

Public space here tends to run warmer and louder than many Western newcomers expect, and that volume is overwhelmingly a signal of friendliness and genuine engagement rather than conflict — animated conversation, call-and-response market banter, and visible warmth between strangers are completely normal and should not be misread through a quieter culture's lens. Respect for elders is similarly explicit and visible: it is shown actively, through specific gestures, forms of address, and deference, rather than assumed silently. Punctuality, meanwhile, operates on a notably more relaxed social clock for informal and social gatherings than the rigid scheduling Western business culture might condition you to expect, even as formal business settings increasingly hold to Western-style punctuality norms. The right-hand convention for giving and receiving — money, food, any handed object — is one of the fastest ways to either win quiet approval or mark yourself as someone who hasn't done their homework.

## United Kingdom: Restraint as the Entire Social Contract

British public behaviour runs on an almost opposite set of instincts, built around restraint, personal space, and an almost religious devotion to the orderly queue. The UK Office for National Statistics doesn't publish a "queue satisfaction index," but it should, because few cultural institutions reveal British social values as completely as watching a bus stop queue form itself, unprompted, the second more than one person is waiting, with an unspoken but absolutely enforced order of arrival that newcomers violate at serious social cost.

Personal space expectations here run noticeably larger than in plenty of warmer, more physically expressive cultures, and public volume control is taken seriously enough that a loud phone call on a train will earn you pointed looks from an entire carriage without a single person saying anything directly to you — the disapproval is the point, and it is unmistakable despite, or perhaps because of, its complete silence. "Sorry" functions as a remarkably flexible social tool, deployed constantly regardless of actual fault, smoothing almost every minor friction of daily public life. VisitBritain's own cultural briefings for visitors consistently flag queue-jumping and personal-space violations as the two fastest ways to generate genuine, if quiet, local irritation. Warmth absolutely exists beneath the reserve — British hospitality, once you're actually inside someone's trust, is genuine and often very funny — it's simply delivered through understatement, dry humour, and indirect complaint rather than the open, visible warmth Ghana offers as a starting default.

## The Verdict

If you want a public culture where warmth is loud, explicit, and demanded as a baseline courtesy before anything else happens, Ghana delivers that with real, infectious energy. If you want a public culture where restraint, queueing, and personal space form an entire unspoken moral code, the United Kingdom has built a near-religious devotion to exactly that.

I'm calling this one for Ghana, purely because a culture that insists you greet people properly before getting down to business has correctly identified what actually matters. The British queue is a genuine marvel of unspoken social order, but it has never once made me feel as welcomed as a proper Ghanaian greeting does.

## Conclusion

Ghana and the United Kingdom sit at genuinely opposite ends of how a society decides to organise its public behaviour, and both are internally completely consistent about it. One insists that warmth and greeting come first, loudly and visibly, before anything else can happen. The other insists that restraint, order, and the sanctity of the queue come first, silently and immovably, before anything else can happen either. Learn the greeting in one. Learn to queue without question in the other. Get either wrong, and you will find out, politely or otherwise, exactly how seriously each country takes its own unwritten rules.
