# Chile Does Saturday Properly, Argentina Does It Later

🇨🇱 Chile vs 🇦🇷 Argentina

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have eaten in sixty countries and lived in fourteen, and I can tell you that nothing reveals a nation faster than what it does between Friday evening and Monday morning. Chile and Argentina share the world's most dramatic mountain range, a fondness for red meat and a talent for sulking about each other's football. After that, the similarities get thin.

One of them treats the weekend as a ritual with a schedule. The other treats it as a negotiation that begins after dinner and ends after breakfast. I have lived the consequences of both, and my liver files an opinion every time.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇨🇱 Chile ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the invitation to an asado and bring wine or something for the grill | Turn up empty-handed and call it a cultural exchange |
| Join the previa at a friend's house before heading anywhere | Expect the night to start in a bar |
| Take a weekend trip to the coast for seafood and empanadas | Assume Chilean slang will match your textbook Spanish |
| Go to a football match, even if you hate football | Skip the September national holidays, which last for days |

| 🇦🇷 Argentina ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Eat dinner late, then go out later still | Book a table at seven and wonder why you are alone |
| Treat the asado as a long social event, not a meal | Cut the meat before the host does |
| Make time for theatre, bookshops and the cultural scene | Expect cheap steak and wine to be the bargain it once was |
| Say yes to the second invitation, then the third | Plan anything important for the morning after a Saturday |

## 🇨🇱 Chile

Chile does weekends with structure, which I mean as a compliment and a mild accusation. The social calendar runs on rituals: the asado, the empanada, the pisco, the previa, the stadium. Chileans abroad, according to a Matador Network piece, miss exactly these things. They miss sharing a bottle of pisco with friends and counting a third person in by buying a second bottle, the pre-party at someone's house where piscolas flow before everyone heads onward, communal barbecues with salads and wine, and the sprawling three-day September national holiday. They also miss the seafood at coastal markets, the pan con palta and oversized hot dogs loaded with mayonnaise and avocado. This is a country where nostalgia is mostly edible. Even the national holiday is a food event with a flag attached: three days of outdoor stalls, anticuchos, empanadas and enough wine to make the bunting look straight.

For the newcomer, the upshot is that Chile feels organised. You will be invited to something, the invitation will have a time on it, and you will be expected to turn up with something. Beaches and the Andes are both within day-trip distance of Santiago, which makes weekend plans less a question of "what" and more of "which direction". Ski in winter, vineyards in spring, Pacific in summer, repeat. The practical advice from Expats Argentina applies here too: shortlist Santiago neighbourhoods and test them at the hours you would genuinely travel, because a lovely flat that costs you ninety minutes to reach the weekend is not lovely.

Chile also has a quieter edge. LuckyLuke on a BA Expats thread, after four visits, called it commercially and culturally closer to the United States, with little cultural overlap with Argentina. That is not an insult, but it is a warning. Malls and tidy plans are part of the landscape. If your idea of a perfect Saturday is somewhere spontaneous, loud and slightly unhinged, Chile may feel like a very pleasant waiting room.

The drinking culture is warm, but it is bounded. People gather at someone's flat, someone runs the grill, someone's cousin shows up with a guitar. You leave when the pisco leaves. Nobody is rude about it, and nobody is surprised. It is lovely, and I mean that without sarcasm, which costs me something.

## 🇦🇷 Argentina

Argentina does weekends the way a jazz musician does a set list: loosely, late, and with a contempt for the clock. Dinner in Buenos Aires is a late event, and the night out is later. A poster on BA Expats praised Argentina's European feel, its rich cultural offerings, and a social life that works well for singles. All of that is true, and all of it begins when a Londoner would normally be in pyjamas.

The asado is the centre of the universe. It is not a meal, it is a three-to-five-hour event with a hierarchy: someone is the asador, someone is the person who stands near the fire offering unsolicited advice, and you are the guest who should keep quiet and eat. Add theatre, tango halls, bookshops that stay open past midnight, and a football culture that borders on theology, and you have a weekend that can fill a month.

The big caveat is money. The same poster noted that cheap steak and wine used to be a major draw, but the strong peso has erased much of that advantage since 2023. Argentina is no longer the bargain it was, and anyone arriving with a "my euros will stretch forever" fantasy should adjust the fantasy. The culture is still bargain-bin generous. The prices, less so.

Mendoza, with its vineyards and mountain light, offers the slower version of the same national talent for lingering, and the same advice applies: pick your neighbourhood by testing it on a real Saturday, not an estate agent's Tuesday. Argentina's weakness is exhaustion. The late rhythm is glorious for a week and punishing for a year. If you have a nine-to-five, a child or a body older than thirty, you will learn to sleep in shifts and nap in public. The reward, though, is a nation that treats leisure as a civic duty, and treats anyone who says "I'm tired" as having missed the point entirely.

## The Verdict

Argentina wins, and I say that with a hangover and no regrets. Chile is the better-organised country, the one where the weekend is reliable, scenic and safe to schedule. Argentina is the country where the weekend might change your life, or at least your sleep pattern. Reliable is lovely. Memorable is better. Chile is the friend who always texts back. Argentina is the one who turns up at two in the morning with a bottle and a plan, and you forgive them both, but only one gets invited to the wedding.

If you are raising small children, craving the outdoors or simply allergic to chaos, Chile is the sensible choice, and you can stop reading me. If you want theatre at midnight, steak at the pace of a medieval banquet and strangers who treat your arrival as a reason to open another bottle, Argentina has no peer on this continent.

The price caveat matters, and so does the stamina requirement. Chile's football stadiums, by the way, are therapeutic according to the Chileans who miss them, and I believe it, but therapy is not the same as a night you cannot explain to your mother. But you can recover from a weekend in Buenos Aires. It is harder to recover from a weekend in which nothing happened.

## Conclusion

Chile and Argentina are neighbours the way two cousins at a wedding are neighbours: the same family, a completely different table. Chile will give you a calm, predictable and genuinely delightful weekend, with a view of the Andes and a sensible bedtime. Argentina will give you a story and a mild case of regret.

After fourteen relocations I trust one rule above all others: choose the country whose Saturday you will still want in two years. If you like your plans printed and your mountains close, take Chile. If you want your evenings unscheduled and your weekends tangled up in long dinners, take Argentina and buy a better pillow. I will also admit that I have missed Chile's sensible bedtime more often than I have missed anything Argentina ever did to me. Either way, bring wine to the asado. Nobody has ever forgiven an empty-handed guest, and you will be remembered for it.
