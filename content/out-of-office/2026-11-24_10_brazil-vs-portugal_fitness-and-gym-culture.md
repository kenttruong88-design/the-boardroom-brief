# Brazil Builds Bodies, Portugal Builds Excuses

🇧🇷 Brazil vs 🇵🇹 Portugal

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Some countries exercise because they have to and some because they want to be looked at. Brazil is firmly in the second camp and has the shoulders to prove it. The gym there is not a sweaty basement in which you hide your shame, it is a social venue with air conditioning, a trainer who knows your name and a mirror that tells you the truth whether you asked or not.

Portugal speaks the same language, shares half the same surnames and has a coastline that would make a Brazilian homesick, so you would assume the gyms are cousins. They are not. They are distant relatives who meet at weddings and quietly disapprove of each other. One sells you a lifestyle. The other sells you a contract and a towel, if you are lucky.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇧🇷 Brazil ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat the gym as a social event and turn up properly dressed | Hide in a corner with headphones and a hoodie |
| Ask what the plan includes, because trainers and classes often come with it | Assume everything costs extra the way it does elsewhere |
| Walk or cycle to the gym as your warm-up | Drive two blocks and park like it is a pilgrimage |
| Take the nutrition consult seriously | Roll your eyes when someone discusses your breakfast |

| 🇵🇹 Portugal ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the contract for the minimum stay before signing | Sign up in January mood and discover the small print in March |
| Use free outdoor equipment parks and public pools | Pay a premium for a pool you will use twice |
| Bring your own towel and ask about trainers separately | Expect a trainer and a towel to be bundled in |
| Take up surfing, padel or football through a local club | Assume the gym is the only sport on offer |

## 🇧🇷 Brazil

Brazilian gym culture is, to put it kindly, committed. The academia is where people go to see and be seen, and a membership usually buys you far more than a rack of dumbbells. One expat poster on Expat.com described paying around 220 reais a month for two people at an air-conditioned gym a mile from home, and that covered a personal trainer, body-composition testing and nutrition consults. They lifted and did cardio six days a week, and the walk there and back served as the warm-up and cool-down. If a British gym tried to sell that package for the equivalent in sterling, there would be a parliamentary inquiry.

That bundled model is the heart of it. A DN Brasil guide aimed at Brazilians moving to Portugal says it plainly: in Brazil plans usually come with classes and a more complete structure already built in, while in Portugal you pay for the basics and add services as you go. In Brazil you buy a package. In Portugal you buy parts and assemble them yourself, like furniture from a Swedish shop, and about as joyfully.

The culture around it is just as intense. Bodies are visible, discussed and, frankly, a national hobby. Outdoors counts too, and sport spills out of the building onto the beach, the promenade and the street, where the walk to the gym is itself part of the routine, as the same Expat.com poster implied. People dance, swim, run and play football on sand, and nobody calls it exercise because that would sound like work.

Is it superficial? Sometimes. Does it work? Obviously. A population that treats fitness as a social identity rather than a New Year's resolution tends to turn up. Gym attendance in Brazil is driven by pleasure, vanity and a trainer who will notice if you vanish, which are better motivators than guilt will ever be. I do not romanticise it, because the vanity can be exhausting, but I take the bundled personal trainer every time.

There is also the matter of how it feels to be new. A Brazilian gym will assess you, advise you and probably ask what you ate last night, all with a smile that suggests this is care and not an audit. It took me a fortnight to stop flinching. After that I missed it everywhere else, including in countries where the only person who has ever asked about my diet was a customs officer. Warmth is a fitness technology, and Brazil has been quietly selling it for decades.

## 🇵🇹 Portugal

Portugal's gym scene is perfectly adequate and rather proud of it. According to DN Brasil, a gym membership typically costs between thirty and seventy euros a month, with the common range in Lisbon and Porto around forty to sixty. Prices, they note, are similar to Brazil's in the big cities. What is different is the model, because here the base plan gets you the building and the machines, and anything else, personal trainer, nutrition, pool or spa, is extra. Personal trainers reportedly run roughly fifty to ninety euros an hour, which is a very Portuguese way of making you reconsider your abs.

Then there is the contract. Many gyms want a minimum commitment from six months to two years in exchange for a lower monthly fee. No-commitment plans exist but cost a little more, and DN Brasil is careful to say that it does not cover cancellation rules, so read the paper before you sign. This is how a perfectly healthy January resolution becomes a twenty-four-month relationship with a place you will visit twice.

The reviews are honest about the rest. A budget chain like Fitness Hut is cheap but can be crowded and hot in summer, according to expat reviewers quoted by Anchorless. Premium clubs offer saunas, spas and padel courts. Some gyms do not even include towels. Trainers mostly advise on plans rather than hovering over you, which feels refreshing for roughly a week.

Where Portugal genuinely wins is outside the gym. Parks with training equipment, cycle paths and public pools that can cost under two euros a visit give you a perfectly decent routine for free. Coastal sport is everywhere, surfing, kitesurfing and paddleboarding, along with football, rugby, tennis, padel and judo through local clubs. Anchorless also notes that research puts Portugal's exercise levels behind other EU countries, which tracks with what I see at lunchtime: a nation that exercises gently, often on a Sunday, preferably near a coffee. It is not a vice. It is a lifestyle, and a quite sensible one.

## The Verdict

Brazil wins on gym culture, Portugal wins on gym sanity, and I am going to give the point to Brazil because I am tired of paying extra for a towel. A gym should make you want to turn up. Brazil's bundled plans, trainers who care and social energy do exactly that, and nobody has to read the small print to find the exit. Portugal's low-cost, high-commitment structure is financially sensible and emotionally cold. It treats you as a payment schedule that happens to have knees.

To be fair to the Portuguese, their free outdoor equipment and cheap public pools put plenty of richer countries to shame. If Brazil is the better gym, Portugal is the better excuse to never need one. That is a compliment, mostly, and a warning, slightly.

If you want to be fit and sociable, move to Brazil. If you want to be fit and sociable on a budget, move to Brazil and accept that the vanity is part of the membership. If you simply want to surf at dawn and have a pastel de nata for breakfast, Portugal is waiting, and it will not judge you, which is exactly the problem.

## Conclusion

The real difference is not muscles, it is intent. Brazil goes to the gym to look good, feel social and have someone watch. Portugal goes because it signed a contract in a moment of optimism and would hate to waste the money.

Both countries can get you fit. Only one will make you want to go. So pick the one that sells you a package, read the small print on the one that sells you a contract, and for heaven's sake bring a towel. Fitness is simple: turn up, sweat, and ignore the mirror. In Brazil, the mirror is also looking at you, which is why it works.
