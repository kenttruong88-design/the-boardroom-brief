# One Country Will Hug You On Arrival, The Other Will Judge Your Punctuality First

🇨🇴 Colombia vs 🇲🇽 Mexico — Public Behaviour Norms
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

People love to lump "Latin America" together as one big warm, expressive, vaguely late monolith, which is the kind of lazy generalisation that gets you very publicly corrected in both Bogotá and Mexico City, by different people, for different reasons. Colombians and Mexicans are both far warmer in public than the average Northern European, but the specific texture of that warmth — how close you stand, how much time is "acceptably late," how loud is too loud on the metro — diverges in ways that catch newcomers off guard constantly.

I've been gently scolded in both countries for getting it backwards: too formal in Medellín, too familiar too fast in Mexico City. The two are not interchangeable, and treating them as such is how you end up either offending someone's abuela or standing awkwardly far away from a hug that was already happening.

## Do's & Don'ts: Colombia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet everyone individually in a room, even a big one | Give a group wave and call it done — it reads as dismissive |
| Dress neatly in public, even for casual errands | Wear gym clothes to run a quick errand — Colombians dress up for the street |
| Show up close to on time for business, looser for social plans | Assume "Colombian time" applies to a work meeting — it usually doesn't |
| Use formal titles (don/doña) with older strangers | Default to first names with someone visibly older than you |

## Do's & Don'ts: Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a handshake or cheek kiss even in professional settings | Skip the greeting ritual and jump straight to business |
| Soften direct requests with warmth — bluntness reads as rude | Say "no" flatly; indirect refusal is the polite default |
| Give real personal space buffer on the metro despite the crowding | Assume public crowding means personal space norms have relaxed |
| Accept that "ahorita" is a flexible unit of time, not a promise | Panic when "ahorita" turns into ninety minutes — that's normal |

## Colombia: Formality Wrapped in Genuine Warmth

Colombia runs on a social operating system that looks, from the outside, like a contradiction: extraordinary personal warmth delivered inside a fairly formal structural shell. Walk into a room of Colombians and the expectation is that you greet each person individually — a handshake, a cheek kiss, a "buenos días" — rather than tossing out a group hello and finding a seat, which locals experience as faintly cold, even a little rude. I learned this the hard way at a work lunch in Medellín, gave one cheerful wave to a table of eight, and watched each person quietly wait for their individual greeting before the meal properly began.

Punctuality culture splits cleanly by context in a way expats rarely get briefed on. Business meetings run close to schedule, particularly in Bogotá's more corporate circles, where lateness is read as a lack of seriousness. Social plans operate on an entirely different clock — "la hora colombiana" — where arriving on the dot to a dinner party can actually catch your hosts unprepared, since the real expected arrival window is often thirty to forty-five minutes after the stated time. Conflating the two calendars is a classic newcomer error: showing up fashionably late to a client meeting, or arriving painfully on time to a party where the host is still in the shower.

Public presentation matters more here than many Western visitors expect. Colombians, particularly in cities, tend to dress put-together even for mundane errands — leggings-and-trainers as a public uniform reads, to older Colombians especially, as a bit disrespectful of the space you're occupying. Formal address — don, doña, plus a first name — remains the polite default with anyone visibly older or in a service role, and skipping straight to casual first-name terms with an elder can land as presumptuous, even when it's clearly well-intentioned.

## Mexico: Indirectness Is the Actual Politeness

Mexico's public behaviour code runs on a different core principle: harmony over honesty, expressed through warmth, softened language, and an almost allergic avoidance of blunt refusal. A direct "no" is considered borderline rude in many social and even professional contexts; Mexicans will more often signal a no through "vamos a ver" (we'll see), delay, or a change of subject, all of which foreigners raised on directness frequently misread as genuine uncertainty rather than a clear answer politely dressed up.

Greeting rituals matter enormously and get skipped by newcomers constantly, to their detriment. A handshake at minimum, often a cheek kiss between women or a woman and a man, is expected even in professional settings before getting down to business — walking in and launching straight into an agenda reads as cold, occasionally even hostile. I've watched foreign consultants tank a first meeting in Mexico City purely by skipping fifteen seconds of proper greeting in favour of "efficiency."

Public space etiquette holds some genuine surprises. Mexico City's famously packed metro, one of the busiest systems in the world, still maintains a real, if compressed, expectation of personal courtesy — offering seats, minimal eye contact with strangers, quiet conversation rather than loud phone calls, even when physically pressed shoulder to shoulder. And then there's "ahorita," a word that technically means "right now" and functionally means anywhere from five minutes to several hours, deployed with total sincerity every time. Newcomers who take it literally spend a lot of time standing around confused. Everyone else has simply adjusted their expectations accordingly.

## The Verdict

Colombia asks you to be more formal than you'd expect and more socially attentive — individual greetings, appropriate dress, knowing which clock applies to which event. Mexico asks you to be more indirect than you'd expect and considerably more patient with language that means something other than what it literally says. Neither is harder, exactly, just differently calibrated: Colombia's rules are more structural and learnable from a guidebook, Mexico's rules require reading tone and subtext that no guidebook fully captures. If I had to pick a winner for the newcomer's first six months, it's Colombia — the etiquette, once learned, is consistent and rule-based. Mexico's indirectness takes longer to genuinely absorb, and you will, at some point, mistake a soft "no" for a "maybe" and show up somewhere you weren't actually invited.

## Conclusion

The shared warmth of both countries fools a lot of foreigners into assuming the etiquette underneath is basically interchangeable, and it very much is not. Colombia wants formality wrapped around its affection — greet properly, dress properly, know your clock. Mexico wants directness sanded down into something gentler — soften your requests, read between the lines, and never, ever take "ahorita" at face value. Get the shell right in either country and the warmth underneath does the rest of the work for you. Get it wrong, and you'll spend months wondering why everyone's being so nice to your face while quietly finding you exhausting.
