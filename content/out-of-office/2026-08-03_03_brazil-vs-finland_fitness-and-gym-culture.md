# One Country Trains for the Beach. The Other Trains for the Dark.

🇧🇷 Brazil · 🇫🇮 Finland
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Brazil does not have a gym culture so much as a permanent, unspoken national audition, staged year-round on every beach from Ipanema to Barra da Tijuca, where fitness isn't a personal goal so much as a public performance with a live studio audience of several million. Finland, by contrast, trains for an entirely different opponent: six months of darkness, temperatures that make "going outside" a genuine tactical decision, and a culture that has decided the correct response to bodily discomfort is to inflict more of it, deliberately, in a wooden box heated to 90°C.

One country flexes for an audience. The other sweats alone, on purpose, then jumps in a frozen lake to prove a point to nobody.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a beach *futevôlei* or volleyball game if invited — it's the fastest way into a social circle | Show up to Copacabana or Ipanema in gym clothes expecting nobody to notice — everybody notices |
| Try a *funcional* outdoor class — Brazil's answer to CrossFit, run on sand, brutal, and extremely social | Assume the beach bodies are effortless — most involve a strict, unglamorous daily gym routine nobody posts about |
| Respect the beach kiosk culture — post-workout açaí and coconut water are part of the ritual, not optional extras | Skip sunscreen because "everyone else looks fine" — most locals are applying it constantly, just discreetly |

### 🇫🇮 Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say yes to a sauna invitation — it's a core social ritual, not a spa indulgence, and refusing can read as standoffish | Wear a swimsuit in a traditional public sauna unless specifically told to — nudity is the norm and non-sexual |
| Try *avantouinti* (ice swimming) at least once — Finns treat it as both fitness and a genuine mood intervention | Talk loudly or check your phone in the sauna — it's meant to be quiet, meditative, near-silent |
| Sign a gym contract in autumn — most Finns' fitness motivation is highest right as the dark sets in, and classes fill fast | Expect summer gym attendance to hold — Finns famously abandon gyms the second the sun stays out past 10pm |

## Brazil: Fitness as Public Spectacle

In Brazil, and especially in Rio de Janeiro, the beach is not a leisure destination, it's an open-air gym, social club, and unofficial ranking system all at once. The *orla* — the beachfront promenade — fills before 7am with runners, cyclists, and *funcional* class participants doing burpees in sand, which is a specific, quietly brutal form of self-punishment that somehow also looks like fun on camera.

What outsiders read as vanity is, locally, closer to civic participation. Brazilian beach culture runs on visibility — not in a shallow sense, but as genuine communal life. The beach is where friendships are maintained, where romantic pursuits play out in slow motion over volleyball games, where entire extended families spend full Sundays. Being "beach fit" isn't really about a single body ideal; it's about being ready, at any moment, to be part of that stage, because the stage is simply daily life.

The actual training is unglamorous and relentless. Traditional gyms — *academias* — are everywhere and heavily used, often before dawn, because Brazilian heat makes midday workouts a form of self-harm. *Futevôlei*, the Brazilian hybrid of football and volleyball played without hands, is both sport and social glue, played by amateurs and semi-professionals on the same sand, often within shouting distance of each other. Post-workout culture matters just as much as the workout: the beach kiosk, the açaí bowl, the coconut cracked open on the spot — recovery as ritual, performed with the same seriousness as the exercise itself.

Brazil's fitness culture also carries real pressure, especially for women, around body standards that are constant rather than seasonal — there's no "beach body season" when the beach is a year-round backdrop to ordinary life. It is aspirational, exhausting, and, underneath the six-pack economy, a genuinely strong national relationship with movement, sunlight, and community that most gym-bound cultures would kill for.

## Finland: Fitness as Quiet Warfare Against the Dark

Finland's fitness culture makes almost no sense until you understand what it's fighting: roughly 6 hours of daylight in December in the south, far less further north, temperatures that regularly drop below -20°C, and a national psychology that has decided the correct response to all of this is not to hide from it but to walk directly into it, ideally followed by immersion in a frozen lake.

The sauna is the actual centre of Finnish physical culture, and treating it as a wellness spa amenity fundamentally misunderstands it. Finland has more saunas than cars — over 3 million in a country of 5.5 million people — and the sauna functions as a near-sacred social institution: quiet, usually nude, non-sexual, a place for business meetings, family bonding, and post-exercise recovery in equal measure. The etiquette is strict in an unspoken way — no loud talk, no phones, and absolutely no treating it as a novelty photo op.

*Avantouinti*, ice swimming, is the sauna's essential companion — a hot-cold cycle Finns swear by for circulation, mood, and something close to spiritual reset, typically involving cutting a hole in a frozen lake and getting in anyway. It sounds like an extreme sport. To most Finns, it's a Tuesday.

Gym culture proper follows a distinctly seasonal, almost inverted logic: membership and attendance spike in autumn and winter, as darkness sets in and outdoor activity becomes harder, then collapses in summer, when Finns abandon gyms entirely in favour of the brief, precious months of light — hiking, cycling, lake swimming, and making the absolute most of a season that ends faster than anyone's ready for. It's the opposite of most Western gym cycles, and it says everything about a country whose entire fitness culture is really just a very disciplined, very cold negotiation with its own climate.

## The Verdict

Brazil trains to be seen. Finland trains to survive the dark, alone, in near-silence, occasionally submerged in ice. Both are legitimate, deeply embedded national relationships with the body, and both would find the other faintly absurd — a Brazilian would find the sauna's silence unbearable, a Finn would find the beach's visibility exhausting.

If forced to choose, I'd take Finland's version, and not because I enjoy suffering. Because a culture that builds its entire physical identity around resilience rather than appearance has, quietly, built the healthier relationship with exercise — even if it does involve a hole in a frozen lake.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/brazil — a foreign visitor asking if it's normal to feel "constantly underdressed and under-exercised" walking along Ipanema, and the top reply is just "yes, welcome, that's the whole beach."*

> *Reddit r/Finland — an expat asking if it's rude to decline a work sauna invite, and the consensus is that declining once is fine, declining always quietly marks you as someone who doesn't want to belong.*

> *expat.com Helsinki — a long thread on the shock of watching an entire gym empty out in May, with one commenter noting "Finns don't skip the gym in summer, they replace it with an entire country."*

## Conclusion

Brazil and Finland sit at opposite ends of what fitness culture can mean: one built around sunlight, visibility, and community performed in public; the other built around silence, cold, and a private negotiation with darkness. Neither culture would recognise its own reflection in the other's routine, and that's precisely what makes them worth understanding on their own terms. Go to Brazil to remember that movement can be joyful and social. Go to Finland to remember that discipline doesn't need an audience — sometimes it just needs a frozen lake and the nerve to get in.
