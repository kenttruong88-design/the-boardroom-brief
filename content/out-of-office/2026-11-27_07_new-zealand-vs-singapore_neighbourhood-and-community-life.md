# New Zealand Has a Fence and Singapore Has a Void Deck

🇳🇿 New Zealand vs 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A neighbour is the person you share a wall, a fence or a lift with, and every country has an opinion on what you owe them. In New Zealand the answer is a wave, a borrowed ladder and an occasional sausage. In Singapore the answer is a nod in the corridor, a shared noticeboard and a strong awareness that someone, somewhere, will report your noise.

I have lived in towns and towers, and I can say with confidence that the neighbour you get depends less on the country than on the number of metres between your front doors. Even so, New Zealand and Singapore sit at opposite ends of the scale, and the contrast is instructive.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇳🇿 New Zealand ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wave and say hello to people on your street | Walk past a neighbour with your headphones in for a year |
| Take part in street events, school fairs and the local sports club | Assume community only exists on social media |
| Offer help when someone is moving or mowing | Ignore a request to borrow a tool |
| Keep your section tidy and your noise reasonable | Let the hedge grow into your neighbour's view |

| 🇸🇬 Singapore ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep noise down late at night | Hold a loud party and assume the walls are thick |
| Use shared spaces, such as void decks and hawker centres, for community contact | Stay locked in your flat and call it integration |
| Learn the building rules on rubbish, smoking and balconies | Hang laundry or smoke where the rules forbid it |
| Greet neighbours politely and respect their privacy | Ask personal questions in the lift |

## 🇳🇿 New Zealand

The New Zealand neighbourhood is built on space. Most people live in a house with a garden, known as a section, and the pattern of suburban life is a mixture of fences, hedges, driveways and waves. Neighbours know each other by sight and often by name. They swap vegetables, lend equipment, take in parcels and keep an eye on the house when you are away. It is a quiet, practical sort of community, the kind that does not need a committee to function.

The backyard barbecue remains the unofficial town hall. A fence is not a barrier, but a place to lean on and chat. Kids roam, dogs are known by name, and local life revolves around schools, clubs and weekend sport. In smaller towns, the community hall is the centre of things, from quiz nights to fundraisers.

Newcomers often find the warmth real but understated. People are friendly rather than intrusive, and invitations can be slow to arrive. A Kiwi will help you move a sofa and then take six months to ask you for dinner. Patience pays, and so does turning up to the school gala.

There is a downside. Spread-out suburbs rely on cars, and people can be quietly isolated. The famous friendliness does not automatically solve loneliness, especially for an expat who has left a network behind. You must join something, whether it is a rugby team, a walking group or the garden club that meets on Tuesdays.

There is a practical point about meeting people in New Zealand that expats do not always grasp. Community life is organised around volunteering. Surf life saving clubs, school boards, fire brigades, sports teams and church groups all run on unpaid effort, and showing up regularly is the way you become a local. If you offer to help with the barbecue at the school fair, you will be adopted within a year. If you do not, you may find that you are friendly with the neighbour and no one else.

## 🇸🇬 Singapore

Singapore has solved the neighbourhood problem in a way that few places have attempted. About eight in ten residents live in public housing built by the Housing and Development Board, in blocks that are designed with community in mind. The ground floor of a block is the void deck, an open space used for gatherings, weddings, funerals and games of chess. Beside it you will find a hawker centre, a market, a clinic and a playground, all within a short walk.

Policies encourage mixing. Ethnic quotas in public housing are intended to prevent enclaves, which means neighbours often come from different backgrounds. It is a conscious attempt to build a shared society, and it works better than a cynic might expect. Residents' committees organise festivals, health checks and cleanup days, and a lot of social life happens at the hawker centre over a plate of chicken rice.

Expats mostly live elsewhere, in private condominiums with pools, gyms and security guards. These are comfortable, tidy and very sociable in a limited way. You meet other parents at the pool and other tenants in the lift, but contact with the local heartland can stay thin unless you go looking for it. Walk to the nearest hawker centre and you will find it.

The rules are firm. Noise, smoking, rubbish and balcony use are all regulated, and complaints are made through proper channels, often with a certain gleam. Singapore's version of neighbourliness is courteous, orderly and slightly formal, with a clear expectation that you will behave.

The Singaporean hawker centre deserves a longer note, because it functions as a community institution as much as a food court. Residents of all ages and backgrounds share tables, and regulars are known by the stall holders. It is the place where retirees meet friends, families eat together on weeknights, and strangers sit side by side over a bowl of noodles. For an expat it is the easiest route into local life, and it costs less than a cappuccino in a condo cafe.

## The Verdict

New Zealand wins on space and Singapore wins on design. A Kiwi suburb gives you a garden, a fence and a neighbour who will lend you a spade. A Singapore estate gives you a shared deck, a food centre, a clinic and a bus stop within three minutes. For community that works without anyone having to try, the planners have done a better job than the grass.

Still, I would not choose to live without a garden. The loneliness of a tower is a real thing, and a good lawn is a quiet comfort. But I would happily take Singapore's hawker centre to New Zealand's long drive to the supermarket. If you can combine the two, with a small house and a decent food court nearby, you have found the answer, and you should not tell anyone where it is.

Privacy follows a different logic in each. In New Zealand, it is created by space and by custom, since people do not pry. In Singapore, it is created by good manners and by the closing of a door. In both cases, the neighbour knows more about you than you think, and says less than you fear.

A final practical note concerns children and pets, who accelerate integration in both countries. A dog in New Zealand introduces you to every neighbour within a fortnight. A child at a Singapore school introduces you to a parents' chat group with more rules than the Constitution. Both are acts of community, and both will teach you the local etiquette faster than any book.

## Conclusion

A good neighbourhood is one where help is possible and privacy is respected. New Zealand achieves it with distance and goodwill. Singapore achieves it with design and rules. Both can feel lonely if you stay inside and both can feel like home if you step out.

So go outside. Wave at the person over the fence in Auckland. Eat at the hawker centre in Tampines. In either case, learn the names of the people who live closest to you. Everything else, including the noise complaint, becomes simpler once you do.

Say hello first. In either country, it is the cheapest way to improve your address.
