# One Country Works Out for Free in the Park, the Other Pays a Fortune to Avoid the Weather

🇪🇸 Spain vs 🇶🇦 Qatar — Fitness and Gym Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Spain has quietly built one of the most democratic fitness cultures in Europe — free outdoor calisthenics rigs in nearly every municipal park, packed at 8am with retirees, teenagers, and everyone in between, no membership card required. Qatar has built the opposite: gleaming, climate-controlled, obscenely well-equipped gyms inside malls and five-star hotels, because for roughly seven months of the year, going outside to exercise is a genuinely hostile act your body will not thank you for.

I have done pull-ups on a rusted-but-functional bar in a Madrid park next to a seventy-year-old man who was clearly stronger than me, for the price of absolutely nothing. I have also paid the equivalent of a small mortgage payment for a Doha gym membership specifically because the alternative was jogging through 43-degree heat that felt like breathing through a hairdryer. Only one of these built any character.

## Do's & Don'ts: Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the free outdoor calisthenics parks — they're everywhere and genuinely well used | Assume a gym membership is necessary to have a real fitness routine here |
| Try padel — it has overtaken tennis as the social racket sport of choice | Expect 24-hour gyms outside major cities — many still close midday |
| Join a running club (peña de corredores) for a real social scene | Be surprised by the sheer number of older adults who are visibly, seriously fit |
| Exercise early morning or evening in summer — midday heat is genuinely dangerous | Skip stretching in public parks — nobody will look at you twice |

## Do's & Don'ts: Qatar

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check gender-segregated hours before showing up at a public gym or pool | Expect to comfortably run or cycle outdoors between May and September |
| Use the Corniche promenade in cooler months — it's genuinely excellent for walking and cycling | Assume all gyms have the same access rules — hotel, mall, and compound gyms vary widely |
| Budget seriously for a private gym membership if you want variety and off-peak access | Wear minimal athletic wear in mixed public outdoor spaces — modesty norms apply even while exercising |
| Take advantage of world-class facilities left over from major international sporting events | Skip hydration precautions even in air-conditioned gyms — the adjustment period is real |

## Spain: Free, Social, and Aggressively Outdoors

Spanish fitness culture runs on a foundation most countries would kill for: nearly every town, from Barcelona down to villages with a population in the low thousands, has a free public outdoor gym — a cluster of steel calisthenics equipment, usually in a park, usually busy from early morning until well after dark. This isn't a niche subculture; it's genuinely cross-generational, with retirees doing assisted pull-ups next to teenagers filming their sets for social media, and nobody finds this remotely unusual. The result is a fitness culture that's radically more democratic than most of Europe's — you don't need disposable income to be seriously, visibly fit in Spain, you just need a park within walking distance, which is to say, you need to live in Spain.

Running culture has exploded here over the past decade, with peñas de corredores — informal running clubs — turning what used to be a solitary activity into a genuinely social one, often ending in a caña (a small beer) that Spaniards will earnestly argue is nutritionally justified after ten kilometres. Padel, meanwhile, has had an almost absurd rise, overtaking traditional tennis in participation numbers and colonising former tennis clubs at a pace that's left some purists genuinely bitter. What unites all of it is a national relationship with exercise that treats it as inseparable from socialising — you don't go to the gym to be alone, you go to see people, and if you don't see anyone you know, you'll know someone by the third visit.

The one real constraint is climate: a Madrid or Seville summer midday is not a suggestion to exercise indoors, it's closer to a physical law, and Spaniards adjust their entire daily rhythm around it, shifting serious exercise to early morning or the cooler evening hours without a second thought.

## Qatar: Climate-Controlled and Genuinely World-Class

Qatari fitness culture exists almost entirely in negotiation with its climate, and the negotiation has produced facilities that are, without exaggeration, some of the best in the world — a direct legacy of a decade spent building infrastructure for major international sporting events, which left Doha with world-class stadiums, the Aspire Zone's sprawling sports complex, and a density of five-star hotel gyms that would make a Manhattan fitness boutique blush. For roughly half the year, from May through September, outdoor exercise between mid-morning and evening isn't just uncomfortable, it's genuinely dangerous, with heat and humidity combining into a wet-bulb temperature that public health advisories actively warn against exerting yourself in.

This has produced a gym culture built entirely around climate control and, correspondingly, around cost — serious gym memberships in Doha run considerably higher than European equivalents, because you're not paying for equipment, you're paying for reliably not dying of heatstroke. Gender segregation adds another real layer of complexity for newcomers: many public and semi-public facilities operate separate or timed access for men and women, and figuring out the actual schedule at a given gym or pool is a genuine rite of passage for new arrivals, usually involving at least one wasted trip.

The cooler months, roughly November through March, flip the entire equation — the Corniche promenade fills with joggers and cyclists at a volume that rivals any European city, outdoor bootcamps appear seemingly overnight, and the city briefly, genuinely embraces the outdoor fitness culture its climate denies it the rest of the year.

## The Verdict

Spain has built a fitness culture that's cheap, social, and available to literally anyone with a park nearby, powered by a climate that mostly cooperates. Qatar has built a fitness culture that's expensive, segregated by necessity, and available almost entirely indoors for half the year, powered by facilities so good they make the constraint almost worth it. Spain wins on accessibility and soul. Qatar wins if your standard for a gym involves marble floors and an infinity pool, which, credit where due, it usually does.

## Conclusion

Spain proves that a great fitness culture doesn't need a membership fee, just a park, some steel bars, and a willingness to be watched by strangers while you sweat. Qatar proves the opposite case just as convincingly: sometimes the climate writes the rules, and the only sane response is to build something spectacular indoors and wait out the summer. Go to Spain to work out with your neighbours for free. Go to Qatar to work out like you're auditioning for a hotel brochure — just check the schedule first.
