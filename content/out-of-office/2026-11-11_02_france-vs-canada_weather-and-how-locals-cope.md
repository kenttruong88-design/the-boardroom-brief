# France Complains About the Weather. Canada Puts On a Hat and Gets On With It

🇫🇷 France vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The French treat weather as a personal insult delivered by someone they intend to write to. A grey Tuesday in Lille is a social event: it will be discussed at the boulangerie, endured at the office and blamed, vaguely, on the government. Canada, by contrast, has weather that could genuinely kill you, and has responded by developing the national temperament of a man holding a shovel and refusing to discuss it.

I have been rained on in sixty countries and I can tell you that climate shapes character more than constitutions do. The French, whose country is mostly temperate with a Mediterranean south and a few cold mountains, have the luxury of being dramatic about mild discomfort. Canadians, whose winters range from unpleasant to catastrophic, have no such luxury and therefore no such drama. Moving between them is less a change of weather than a change of attitude.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**France**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn that "it's chilly" covers anything between four and fourteen degrees | Expect snowploughs in the south, because there are none |
| Check which region you are in: north, Mediterranean and mountains are different countries | Assume a French summer will be cool because the north is |
| Carry a light scarf as a permanent accessory | Ignore the Mistral if you live near Marseille |
| Complain with elegance, preferably over coffee | Offer cheerful optimism about rain |

**Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy proper winter clothes in Canada, from people who understand them | Import your fashionable European coat and call it winter gear |
| Own two wardrobes: one for the cold, one for the sweat | Believe the whole country shares one climate |
| Check the weather bulletin before you leave the house | Treat -25 C as a reason to stay home |
| Respect snow-day closures | Drive confidently on ice because you own a good car |

## France

France is, climatically speaking, a polite country. One member in Epinal described the weather on an expat forum as temperate, with winters chilly but bearable, little snow and lovely summers. An expat reply on the same page filled in the map: cool summers and mild winters in the north, a Mediterranean south with hot, dry summers and mild, wet winters, and cold, snowy winters in the mountains. Those are not four seasons so much as four negotiating positions.

This is why French weather talk is a genre rather than an emergency. When the worst a winter can do is arrive damp, you can afford to turn complaint into conversation. The heating bill, the grey sky, the sudden heatwave in August when half the country discovers it owns no air conditioning: all of these are cultural touchstones, none of them lethal. A Parisian in drizzle is not suffering. He is performing.

The practical expat advice is correspondingly gentle. Learn your region, buy a decent raincoat, and accept that August may require you to sit very still in a darkened room. The southern heat is real and the old stone houses cope better than the modern flats, which turn into small, expensive greenhouses by July. There is no national plan for a cold snap beyond a sweater and a grumble.

The sting, for a Canadian arrival, is how mild the whole thing feels. You will find yourself mocking a French colleague's agonies over eight degrees, and you will be right, and you should still keep it to yourself. They have, after all, never been asked to start a car at dawn in January.

There is also the matter of the French summer, which catches Canadians out. The south of France is Mediterranean, with hot, dry summers, and the older stone houses cope with the heat better than the newer flats, a point every expat discovers by sweating in a flat that was designed by someone who hated July. Air conditioning is far less common than in Canada, where the summer humidity is matched by units in every office and most homes. The French response is characteristically philosophical: close the shutters at ten, open them at nine at night, and defer all ambition until the heat has passed. It works, and it looks effortless, which is the part that really annoys the rest of us.

## Canada

Canada is a different proposition entirely. A reply in an expat forum thread on adapting to the climate described winters running from about -3 C down to -45 or -50 C in the coldest places, and summers from 20 C to well over 40 C with humidity. Vancouver and Victoria, the reply noted, are milder. The advice was blunt: research your area, own two wardrobes, and buy your winter clothes in Canada. The same reply mentioned snow-day closures, which are a feature of the system rather than a failure of it.

A French interviewee in Montreal, writing in 2017, gave the personal version. She came from Marseille and described winter days of around -30 C under a blue sky, with an outing kit of hat, gloves, double layers and snow pants for a winter festival. She now checks the weather bulletin, something she never did at home, and regards winter boots as a near half-year commitment. That is the quiet revolution: weather stops being a topic and becomes a logistics problem.

What impresses me is the Canadian solution, which is infrastructure plus attitude. Snow is cleared, transit runs, bars stay open late, and people go out. One forum poster put it neatly, saying -25 C and sunny is more pleasant than 3 C, rainy and windy. Dry cold, apparently, is a different animal from wet cold, and anyone who has stood on a Marseille quay in a January gale will see the point.

The sting runs the other way. Canada is not mild in summer, either, and the humidity arrives with a vengeance. You are not choosing a cold country; you are choosing a country that does both extremes, and expects you to have the kit for each.

A word on clothing, since it is the real currency of coping. The Montreal interviewee described a kit of hat, gloves, double layers and snow pants for a festival outing in the deep cold, and called winter boots a near half-year need. That is a different economy from the French one, where a good coat and a decent umbrella serve most of the year. A Canadian winter wardrobe is an investment, and the forum advice to buy it in Canada reflects the simple fact that local shops sell what the local weather requires. Do not arrive in autumn with a stylish European overcoat and expect it to survive a January commute on a windy platform.

## The Verdict

Canada wins on competence, France on comfort. If you are measuring how well a society copes with weather, Canada is the champion: the clothing, the clearing, the planning and the cheerful refusal to be defeated all point to a culture that has made peace with its sky. France has made no such peace, because it has not needed to. Its weather is a drama staged for a gentle audience.

For a relocating executive, the question is what you value. Canada demands more of your wardrobe and your January, but gives you a society that functions in minus thirty and a sense of mild superiority that lasts all spring. France demands almost nothing of your closet and rewards you with a climate that lets you linger outside on a terrace in October. I know which one I would choose for a long posting, and it depends entirely on whether I plan to be in a good mood in February.

If you must decide quickly: bring a decent coat to either, buy the serious one in Canada, and never again describe eight degrees as cold in front of someone from Winnipeg.

## Conclusion

Weather is the one thing neither country can negotiate, so each has built a personality around it. France complains, stylishly and without consequence. Canada prepares, grimly and with excellent boots.

Choose by temperament. If you want to be comfortable, go to France and keep your opinions about the drizzle. If you want to be useful in a blizzard, go to Canada and start shopping in September. Either way, the forecast is not the problem. Your coat is.
