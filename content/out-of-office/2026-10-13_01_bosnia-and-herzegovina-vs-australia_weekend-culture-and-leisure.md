# Bosnians Treat the Weekend Like a Sacred Duty. Australians Treat It Like Marketing Copy.

🇧🇦 Bosnia and Herzegovina vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular smugness that comes over a Bosnian the moment a Saturday morning coffee stretches past its third hour, and there is an equally particular smugness that comes over an Australian the moment they mention, unprompted, that they "did absolutely nothing" over a long weekend except get a tan. Both are performances. Both are deeply sincere. Neither party would survive the other's version of rest for more than one week before either weeping into their fildžan or demanding to know why nobody will just relax and watch the cricket.

I have now sat through enough Sarajevo kahva sessions and enough Bondi barbecues to say this with total confidence: these are two cultures that consider leisure a moral project, just with opposite theses. Bosnia believes the weekend exists to be slowed down until it nearly stops. Australia believes the weekend exists to be optimised into a highlight reel, preferably one involving water, meat, and a esky full of beer that somebody else was supposed to bring.

## Bosnia and Herzegovina: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat Sunday ćevapi lunch with the extended family as non-negotiable | Don't schedule anything, ever, that clashes with that lunch |
| Linger over a single fildžan of kahva for an hour, minimum | Don't gulp your coffee like it's a commute, you animal |
| Join the evening korzo stroll through the old town, even if you have nowhere to be | Don't expect most shops to be open on Sunday — plan your errands accordingly |
| Take a day trip up to Bjelašnica or Jahorina whenever the weather allows | Don't plan anything indoors if there's a football match on; you will be alone |

## Australia: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to the Saturday morning "brekkie" café ritual, flat white in hand | Don't book anything over an AFL or NRL Grand Final weekend — you will be the only soul in the building |
| Get sunburnt in a genuinely humiliating pattern at least once; it's a rite of passage | Don't forget it's your "shout" at the pub — buy the round or face quiet, permanent judgment |
| Turn up to a children's weekend sport sideline even if you have no children, for the social contact alone | Don't wear thongs — or worse, enclosed shoes — to a barbecue; both are wrong in different ways |
| Attempt a long weekend road trip whose entire itinerary is "the beach, then a different beach" | Don't skip the second sunscreen application; the first one does not, in fact, last all day |

### Bosnia and Herzegovina: The Weekend as an Act of Devotion

In Bosnia, the weekend does not begin with an alarm going off later than usual. It begins with the specific, unhurried ritual of making kahva — coffee brewed in a džezva, poured into a tiny fildžan, and then, crucially, not drunk quickly. You sip. You talk. You sip again. An hour vanishes and nobody considers this wasted, because the entire point of Bosnian weekend culture is that time spent with people is not a cost to be minimised; it is the product itself. Try explaining to a Sarajevan friend that you "don't have time" for coffee and watch the genuine confusion cross their face, as though you'd just said you don't have time for oxygen.

Sunday is the fulcrum of the week, and it belongs to family lunch with an intensity that borders on the liturgical. Ćevapi, somun bread, ajvar, a grandmother's opinions about your life choices delivered between courses — this is not optional weekend programming, it is the weekend's entire reason for existing, and expats who try to schedule a brunch date or a gym session over it will simply be informed, gently but immovably, that this is not how Sunday works here. The evening korzo — a slow, deliberate stroll through the old town where half the point is being seen and the other half is seeing everyone else — operates on the same logic: leisure here is communal, performative in the warmest sense, and allergic to efficiency.

Where Bosnians do move fast is up a mountain. Sarajevo's proximity to Bjelašnica and Jahorina (1984 Winter Olympics venues, worn proudly like a family heirloom) means a weekend hike or ski run is a short drive away, and locals treat this not as extreme outdoorsiness but as a basic entitlement of living there. And heaven help you if you plan a quiet Saturday indoors during a football match — Bosnian football support is a weekend institution in its own right, cafés and all, and ignoring it marks you as either an alien or an incredibly boring houseguest.

### Australia: The Weekend as a Branding Exercise

Australians will tell you, often within the first ten minutes of meeting them, exactly how good their weekend was going to be before it even started, and this is the key to understanding the whole enterprise: the Australian weekend is aspirational before it is actual. The Saturday "brekkie" — smashed avo, a flat white, a café with exposed brick that charges inexplicable amounts for toast — is less a meal than a public declaration that one has, in fact, successfully transitioned into weekend mode.

Then there's the beach, which functions less as a location than as a national personality trait. An Australian weekend without at least a passing mention of sun, surf, or sunscreen application technique is barely considered a weekend at all; it's just two days that happened to occur on a Saturday and Sunday. Sideline culture runs just as deep — turning up to watch a children's football match you have no actual stake in is treated as completely normal weekend socialising, mostly because it comes with a sausage sizzle and gossip, which is really all anyone wants from a Saturday morning.

Do not, under any circumstances, attempt to organise anything during an AFL or NRL Grand Final. Restaurants empty, streets go quiet, and the only acceptable activity is being within shouting distance of a television and a esky. The "shout" — the unspoken obligation to buy a round at the pub when it's your turn — is taken with startling seriousness for a country that otherwise prides itself on not taking much seriously; skip it twice and you will simply stop being invited. And the long weekend road trip, Australia's other great leisure export, usually has no itinerary beyond "drive somewhere with a different beach," because the entire philosophy is that rest means motion toward water, not stillness toward anything else.

## The Verdict

If you want your weekend to feel like it mattered, go to Bosnia. If you want your weekend to feel like it happened to someone enviable on Instagram, go to Australia. Bosnians have built a leisure culture around depth — one coffee, one lunch, one conversation, stretched until it's worth remembering. Australians have built one around breadth — more sun, more sport, more mates, spread thin enough to photograph well from several angles.

Neither is wrong, exactly, though I will say that only one of these cultures has ever made me feel guilty for finishing a coffee in under forty minutes, and it was not the one with the beaches. Bosnia wins on substance. Australia wins on tan lines. If forced to choose at gunpoint — and after enough forced small talk about "what did you get up to," I half expect to be — I'd take the long Sarajevo coffee over the long Bondi queue for brunch. Depth outlasts a highlight reel every single time, and nobody in Sarajevo has ever asked me, mid-conversation, whether I've "checked out this new place," because they were too busy actually being somewhere.

## Conclusion

What strikes me most, having now overstayed my welcome in both cafés and beach car parks, is that both of these weekend cultures are quietly defending something they're afraid of losing. Bosnia defends slowness because the alternative — a region racing to modernise, young people leaving for Vienna and Munich — makes the Sunday lunch table feel like the last stable thing standing. Australia defends sunshine-as-identity because the alternative, a country built on commuting and mortgage stress like anywhere else, is simply less marketable. Both are, in their own way, elaborate acts of self-preservation dressed up as fun.

So pick your weekend philosophy accordingly: coffee that teaches you patience, or a beach that teaches you nothing except how badly you need better sunscreen. I know which one left me a better houseguest. It wasn't the one with the esky.
