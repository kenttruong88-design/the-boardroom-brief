# The Germans Will Ask Your Intentions by Tuesday and the Dutch Will Split the Bill by Wednesday

🇩🇪 Germany vs 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Romance in Germany is a procurement process. There is a specification, a timeline and, if the satirical expat guides are to be believed, a menu sent as a PDF twenty-four hours in advance so that nobody is surprised by the soup. Candour is the national love language. If a German wants to know whether you are looking for something serious, they will ask, probably before the starters, and they will expect a complete sentence in reply.

Dutch romance is the same candour with the formality removed, like a German who has discovered flip-flops. Everyone is direct, nobody is flustered, and the bill arrives already divided. Both nations will tell you exactly what they think of you. The difference is that in Germany it feels like an audit, and in the Netherlands it feels like a weather report.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Germany**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say "a drink" if you mean a date | Say "coffee" and wonder why they brought a friend's energy |
| Name the day, time and place | Float a vague "we should hang out" |
| Arrive on time, which means early | Treat punctuality as a suggestion |
| Say what you want, early | Play it mysterious for six weeks |

**Netherlands**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to split or alternate the bill | Insist on paying as a grand gesture |
| Say how you feel, even if it risks a no | Play hard to get |
| Keep compliments sincere and specific | Pile on flattery that sounds rehearsed |
| Suggest a brown café or a bike ride | Dress up like it is the Oscars |

## Germany

IamExpat's guide to dating in Germany is a comic piece, and I will treat it as such, but its jokes are built on a real skeleton. Invite someone for "a drink" and it is a date; invite them for "coffee" and it is friendship. Name the time and the place. Be honest about your intentions early, because Germans would rather risk a hard conversation than a long misunderstanding. Expatica adds the other usual suspects: splitting the bill is common, women may well make the first move, punctuality is close to a moral issue, and flowery compliments are not the national sport.

The people who actually live it give a less tidy picture. A Berlin expat on Expat.com says meeting people is easy and finding a relationship is hard, because so many residents are passing through or looking for something short. She met her husband on a dating site, and her advice is brisk: discuss intentions early, and if one of you will not relocate, end it before it gets serious. Another poster notes that approaching strangers in the street is poorly received; people meet through friends, work, clubs and courses.

Language is the great gatekeeper. A Reddit thread from an American two years into life in south-west Germany, German at B2, found the English-speaking scene small and the apps barren. One commenter described the loneliness of lacking shared references, the childhood television and the in-jokes, and another reported far more matches after switching their profile to German. That is the real tax. Germany will date you in English, but it will only really *keep* you in German.

There is a social logic to all this. Germany compartmentalises life: work friends, club friends, school friends, family. Nobody mixes them without ceremony. Arrive as a stranger and you are not rejected, you are simply not yet filed, and filing takes time. Be patient, turn up to the same Thursday evening for three months, and one day someone will ask whether you would like to come to their birthday. That is the moment you have been admitted. It feels like a ceremony because it is one.

My verdict on German dating is that it is excellent if you value clarity and miserable if you value mystery. Join a choir, a sports club or a volunteer group, because the German social world is built on associations with membership fees and agendas. It is slower to open, but what opens tends to stay open.

## Netherlands

Expat.com's Dutch dating guide describes a culture that is practical, patient and allergic to games. Dating is less structured than in Germany, conversations skip the small talk and get deep very quickly, and the Dutch dislike playing hard to get. Tinder, Bumble, Badoo and Happn are all in use, and bars, sports clubs and a "brown café" evening are the standard habitats. Going Dutch is not a joke; it is policy. Men are not expected to open doors or carry bags, and nobody needs to dress up.

A dating coach quoted by DutchReview, Marie-José Bosch-Kuiper, makes the more interesting point: Dutch women accept courtesy, but they decide which door. The matter-of-fact directness can read as cold to someone raised on romance, and international dating, she says, needs explanation and communication. Her observation that internationals often end up dating other internationals matches what any expat in Amsterdam will tell you after a second glass of wine.

The Netherlands has one big advantage for the non-Dutch-speaker: almost everyone speaks excellent English, which is why one Reddit commenter picked it over Germany. The British Expats forum has a dated 2008 thread in which a man says relationships only became easy once he spoke fluent Dutch, around his fourth year. Another poster says Dutch women can be hesitant about foreign men but that it is "not out of the question". Dated, yes, but the shape of it holds: English gets you through the door, Dutch gets you a seat at the table.

The Dutch also have a talent for the social scene that Germany lacks: the spontaneous plan. A text on Friday afternoon, a table in a bar by eight, and a conversation that skips straight to your opinions on housing policy and your family's quirks. It is warm in a brisk way, and it rewards anyone who can hold an opinion without taking offence when it is disagreed with. Bring views. The Dutch find agreeable people suspicious.

The cost of Dutch directness is a certain flatness. If you want to be wooed, you have come to the wrong country. If you want to be told the truth over a beer and a bitterballen, you have come to the right one.

## The Verdict

The Netherlands wins for the newcomer. It is easier to enter, easier to read and far kinder to anyone who does not yet speak the language. The Dutch will not make you earn your place by passing a German exam first, and a culture that says what it means in English is a mercy.

Germany wins for anyone who wants to be taken seriously. The slow build, the early honesty about intentions and the association-based social life produce relationships with some structure to them. But you will have to learn the language and join the Verein, and nobody is going to feel sorry for you while you do.

I would date in Amsterdam and settle in Hamburg, which is the most German sentence I have ever written. If you want a fling with good manners, go Dutch; if you want a spouse with a pension plan, go German.

## Conclusion

Germany asks what you want. The Netherlands tells you what it thinks. Both are infinitely more honest than the British method, which is to mean something entirely different from what you say and then sulk for a fortnight.

Bring a language course, a thick skin and a sense of humour about the bill. And when a German sends you a menu in advance, or a Dutchman suggests you split a single portion of fries, do not take it personally. It is not coldness. It is respect, delivered the way spreadsheets deliver everything.
