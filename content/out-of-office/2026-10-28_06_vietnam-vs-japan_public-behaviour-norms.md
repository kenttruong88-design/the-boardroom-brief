# The Line Doesn't Exist and the Silence Is Deafening

🇻🇳 🇯🇵 Vietnam vs Japan

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two ways a society can tell you that your body, your voice, and your turn do not matter as much as you think they do. Vietnam does it with an elbow to the ribs and a horn blast that could wake the dead. Japan does it with a silence so total you start wondering if you've died and nobody's told you. Both are, in their own way, a form of discipline. Neither one is interested in your feelings about it.

I have queued — if we're being generous with the word — at a Ho Chi Minh City ATM where the "line" was a scrum of eleven people each convinced they were first. I have also stood on a Tokyo platform at rush hour where three hundred commuters formed a silent, telepathic grid without a single raised voice or a single centimetre of wasted space. Expats arrive expecting one of these countries to be "relaxed" and the other to be "polite," and then spend eighteen months recalibrating what those words were ever supposed to mean.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat any queue as a suggestion, not a contract | Expect anyone to form an orderly line without supervision |
| Match the ambient volume — raise your voice to be heard, not to win | Whisper at a dinner table and expect to be heard, or worse, understood |
| Let strangers brush past you on the street without flinching | Clutch your bag or recoil — it reads as an insult, not caution |
| Use your horn, your elbow, or your voice to claim space when needed | Wait patiently for someone to notice you and let you through |

### Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stand exactly where the platform markings tell you to stand | Talk on your phone, or loudly to a friend, on a train |
| Keep a forearm's length of space from strangers at all times | Touch, bump, or hover near someone without immediate apology |
| Queue even when there is, technically, nothing to queue for | Eat, drink, or make noise while walking down the street |
| Lower your voice the instant you step indoors or onto transport | Assume silence means nobody's judging you — they absolutely are |

## Vietnam

Vietnam's relationship with the queue is less a cultural quirk than an honest account of recent history. Seán Nolan, writing in Viet Nam News, put it plainly: "passive-aggressive nudging, shoving, and blatant queue-jumping are expected and accepted" in supermarkets, petrol stations, and airports — and "expected and accepted" undersells it. It is the system. The line is a polite fiction performed for tourists, and every local knows it. Nolan reaches for Dunkirk as the counter-example — British soldiers queuing for evacuation under mortal threat, where "not even the risk of death or captivity was enough to convince them to jump the line." Vietnam, by contrast, treats the line as something you survive, not something you respect.

The noise is the same philosophy wearing different trousers. Tuoi Tre News contributor Stivi Cooke documented Da Nang and Ho Chi Minh City's attempts to crack down on street karaoke and bar noise, citing sound levels around 120 decibels — "the sound of a jet engine" — tolerated by whole apartment blocks on a Tuesday night. Enforcement, Cooke notes dryly, faced a shortage of noise meters and widespread "defiance" the moment police turned their backs. A shop owner told him loud music brings in customers. A neighbour told him "everyone does it." Nobody told him it was a problem, because collectively, functionally, it isn't one — it's the ambient soundtrack of a country that does its living out loud, in public, on the street, where everyone can see and hear and judge it in real time.

Personal space follows the same logic in reverse: there isn't any, and nobody's hiding that fact. Travel writer Laurel Marshall described being "nearly trampled" by crowds and pushed past in bathroom queues by people who gave her "confused, blank stares" when she tried to signal that a line existed. She's not wrong to notice it. She's wrong to expect an apology. Decades of shared scarcity and multigenerational households never produced queue discipline — they produced exactly what you see on every Hanoi pavement: a crowd that moves as one organism and expects you to keep up.

## Japan

Japan's version of public order looks, from the outside, like the opposite of Vietnam's — and it is, right up until you realise it's equally absolute. The government's own Life and Work Guidebook, issued by the Immigration Services Agency to incoming residents, spells out rules so granular they border on parody: keep earphone audio inaudible to the stranger next to you, don't run a washing machine after dark, never take a phone call on a bus. An immigration expert quoted alongside the guide said Tokyo is worried about "trouble caused by foreigners" disrupting a social order built on exactly this kind of micromanaged courtesy. That's not a guideline. That's a threat wearing a government letterhead.

The train platform is where this gets its purest expression. Hundreds of commuters form silent, invisible queues at marked floor positions, board in sequence, and conduct entire rush hours without a raised voice — because the system only works if everyone complies without needing to be told twice. Tokyo Weekender's etiquette rundown confirms what every tourist eventually learns the hard way: talking on your phone, eating on the move, or simply standing too close marks you instantly, silently, permanently as someone who doesn't belong. Nobody will say anything. That's the punishment.

What nobody warns you about is how exhausting the performance becomes. An Expat.com forum thread on daily life in Japan features poster nadeemjp96 admitting that general "patience levels seem to be at an all-time low" after over a decade in the region — swiftly challenged by fellow member wyngrove60, who'd never noticed any hostility at all. That split is the whole country in miniature: the rules are universal, the interpretation isn't, and the cost of guessing wrong is a wordless, permanent demotion in everyone's estimation. Vietnam will shove you and forget you by lunchtime. Japan will never shove you, and never forget.

Where Vietnam negotiates personal space loudly and constantly, Japan legislates it out of the conversation entirely — the Immigration Services Agency's guidebook even specifies acceptable cough etiquette. There is no Japanese equivalent of "working it out as you go." The rules were decided decades ago, nobody renegotiates them, and your job as a newcomer is to absorb the manual before you embarrass yourself on a platform at 8:15 a.m.

## The Verdict

If you want a culture that tells you the truth about how it functions, Vietnam wins by a landslide — chaotic, loud, occasionally terrifying, but never pretending to be anything else. If you want a culture that quietly catalogues every one of your social failures without confronting you about a single one, Japan has that covered with government-issued precision.

Neither is "ruder." That word assumes a shared standard, and there isn't one. Vietnam's public square is a negotiation conducted at volume; Japan's is a contract signed in silence, and breaking it carries a penalty you'll never see coming because nobody will tell you to your face.

My verdict: Japan wins on comfort, Vietnam wins on honesty, and expats who claim to prefer one over the other have usually only mastered one of them. People who've genuinely lived both will tell you the truth — you don't get to pick. You adapt to whichever one you're standing in, or you spend your posting looking like the fool at the back of a line that was never really a line.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com, Ho Chi Minh City traffic forum — a longtime local resident advised newcomers to "accept it and enjoy even the bad," since individuals can adapt faster than systems ever will.*

> *Expat.com, Japan expat-life forum — one member admitted general social patience "seems to be at an all-time low," only to be told by another poster that they'd never once noticed hostility in the same country.*

> *World Nomads travel journal, Laurel Marshall — recalling a Vietnamese bathroom queue where fellow waiters gave her "confused, blank stares" the moment she tried to confirm a line existed at all.*

## Conclusion

Here is the uncomfortable truth neither guidebook will print: both countries have automated the same human instinct — protect your own interests in public — and dressed it in opposite costumes. Vietnam externalises the negotiation so everyone can see it happening. Japan internalises it so thoroughly the negotiation looks, to an outsider, like it never happens at all. It does. It's just filed as etiquette instead of elbows.

Move to Vietnam and you'll stop flinching at noise within a month, because the alternative is a nervous breakdown by Tet. Move to Japan and you'll learn to read a room so precisely you'll start judging your home country's queuing standards on return. Either way, you won't come back the same person who left — you'll come back someone who's finally stopped assuming "normal" means anything at all. That's not cultural enrichment. That's just what happens when you've been bruised by two entirely different systems and survived both.
