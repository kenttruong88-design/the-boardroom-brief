# One Country Rests Loudly and the Other Rests on a Schedule Someone Else Approved

🇹🇳 Tunisia vs 🇯🇵 Japan

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Weekends reveal what a culture actually believes rest is for. Tunisia believes rest is a communal event, ideally involving coffee, family, and a conversation that will not end for several hours regardless of anyone's plans. Japan believes rest is something you're technically entitled to and will get around to, possibly next year, possibly on a very structured hiking trip you booked eight weeks in advance.

I've spent enough weekends in both to say this plainly: one country will drag you out of bed for a shared pot of coffee whether you like it or not, and the other will let you disappear into a solo ramen queue for three hours and call it self-care. Both are valid. Only one of them will actually leave you rested.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇹🇳 Tunisia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the invitation to a family lunch even if you had other plans — refusing is a genuine faux pas | Rush a café conversation; sitting for two hours over one coffee is the entire point |
| Join the Friday couscous tradition if you're invited, it's a real institution, not a stereotype | Schedule anything important on Friday afternoon during prayer time |
| Expect the beach and souks to be genuinely social spaces, not just tourist backdrops | Assume "let's meet at noon" means noon; it means sometime after noon, with feeling |

### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book popular weekend spots — hiking trails, onsen, izakaya — well in advance | Expect spontaneity to work; Japanese weekend leisure is often meticulously planned |
| Embrace solo leisure culture — solo dining and solo travel are completely normalised | Assume everyone wants group activities; alone time is deeply respected, not pitied |
| Use the weekend to actually disconnect; work contact outside hours is still frowned upon in most workplaces | Be surprised when shops and trains are somehow busier on Sunday than a Tuesday |

## Tunisia: The Weekend Is a Group Project and You Are Now a Member

Tunisian weekends operate on the assumption that rest is something you do with other people, ideally people you're related to, in a space that has more plastic chairs than strictly necessary. Friday holds particular weight — a shorter, calmer workday for many, followed by family lunch, often couscous, often lasting long enough that "lunch" becomes a technical inaccuracy by the time dessert arrives. Refusing an invitation isn't just declining plans, it reads as a small social wound, and Tunisians will absolutely let you know, gently but unmistakably, that you've disappointed them.

Café culture fills whatever gaps the family calendar leaves open. Men and increasingly mixed groups occupy pavement cafés for hours over a single small coffee, watching the street, arguing about football, saying nothing in particular with tremendous commitment. It looks unproductive to an outsider clocking billable hours in their head, and that's exactly the point — Tunisian leisure resists the idea that time needs to be optimised. The beach towns along the coast and the souks in the medinas double as genuine social infrastructure on weekends, not just backdrops for a holiday photo, and you'll notice locals treating a Saturday afternoon shopping trip as a full social occasion, not a chore to be minimised.

The friction for newcomers is almost always about time itself. "Meet at noon" is aspirational. Plans shift, expand, absorb extra people nobody mentioned, and stretch well past when a Northern European schedule would have called it a day. Once you stop fighting it, though, it's one of the more genuinely warm weekend cultures going — nobody is checking their phone wondering if they're free enough to fit you in. You're already assumed to be free. That's rather the point.

## Japan: Rest as a Beautifully Organised Personal Project

Japanese weekends are, on paper, generous — a proper two days off for most salaried workers — and yet the country has built an entire cultural apparatus around the fact that everyone is quietly exhausted anyway. The average worker still logs long hours during the week, and the weekend often becomes less about spontaneous socialising and more about tightly scheduled recovery: a booked onsen slot, a hiking reservation, a solo trip to a specific ramen shop that opens at 11am and will have a queue by 11:03.

Solo leisure is where Japan genuinely leads the world, and it's worth taking seriously rather than treating as a punchline. Solo dining, solo karaoke, solo travel — none of it carries the faint whiff of sadness it might elsewhere. Restaurants build single-seat counters specifically for it. It's less "I have no friends" and more "I have decided today is mine," and honestly, more cultures should adopt that particular confidence.

Where it gets complicated is the planning. Nothing is casual. Popular hiking trails, brunch spots, and even public baths often need booking days or weeks ahead, which turns "let's see what we feel like doing" into a logistical impossibility. Expats used to rolling weekends find this rigid at first and then, slowly, start making their own reservations three weeks out without a flicker of irony. Work contact on weekends is genuinely rare in most companies, which is the trade-off — you get real disconnection, just on a schedule someone, somewhere, approved in advance.

## The Verdict

Tunisia wins on warmth and spontaneity you don't have to book. Japan wins on the genuinely radical idea that solitude is a legitimate leisure activity rather than a failure to make plans. If you want a weekend that finds you, go to Tunis. If you want a weekend that you meticulously design and then execute with military precision, go to Tokyo. Both will leave you tired in completely different ways — one from talking, one from queuing politely for things you booked three weeks ago.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/japanlife — paraphrased: A commenter said their first six months in Japan were spent assuming weekends would be spontaneous, before realising every worthwhile activity needed booking two to three weeks out, and adjusting their entire personality around a calendar app.*

> *expat.com Tunis — paraphrased: A newcomer described accidentally offending a host family by declining a Friday lunch invitation for "just one week," and being told, not unkindly, that saying no wasn't really an option they'd considered offering.*

> *Internations Tokyo — paraphrased: An expat noted that solo dining in Japan felt awkward for the first month and then became the single habit they missed most after moving away — nobody stares, nobody assumes you're lonely, it's simply Tuesday.*

## Conclusion

Neither country is going to hand you a weekend that matches your home country's rhythm, and trying to force one onto the other will just leave you resentful and unbooked. Tunisia will pull you into its orbit whether you planned for it or not. Japan will hand you a beautifully organised itinerary and expect you to have already reserved your place in it. Pick based on whether you'd rather be interrupted by love or exhausted by logistics — because those, dear reader, are your only two options.
