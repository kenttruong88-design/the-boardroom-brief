# Lagos Commutes Like a Contact Sport and Toronto Commutes Like a Polite Apology

🇳🇬 Nigeria vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A Lagos commute is a full-body experience. You negotiate, you gamble, you bargain with a driver, you cross a bridge at an hour when the bridge has given up, and you arrive at work having already survived a day. It is loud, improvised and strangely democratic: everyone from the banker to the trader is in the same traffic, listening to the same radio, composing the same elaborate lie to explain why they are late.

A Toronto commute is the opposite. Everyone is calm. Everyone is staring at a phone. Nobody speaks, nobody bargains, and the only drama is a delayed streetcar and a faint sense of collective disappointment. The two cities are so far apart in their transport philosophies that a Nigerian arriving in Canada experiences it as a luxury and an amnesia at once: the system works, and nobody is talking to you.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Nigeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Leave far earlier than you think you need to | Trust a map app's estimate at rush hour |
| Agree on the fare or route before you board | Get in first and discuss later |
| Use a trusted driver or ride service for important trips | Gamble on a random vehicle at night |
| Keep valuables out of sight | Display a phone in traffic |

**Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a transit card and tap in and out properly | Assume you can pay with loose change everywhere |
| Dress for winter at the stop | Wait for a bus in a thin jacket |
| Let people off before you board | Push in at the door |
| Check service alerts before leaving | Assume the line is running |

## Nigeria

Lagos is a city of ten thousand daily negotiations, and transport is the loudest. The famous yellow danfo buses, the motorbike taxis, the shared tricycles and the ride-hailing cars all compete for passengers on roads that are always slightly too small. A guide published by Expat Arrivals notes that most expats rely on company or personal drivers, and that a foreign licence is valid for a limited period before a Nigerian one is required. It also mentions a bus rapid transit network that runs on segregated lanes but is crowded at peak times, and ferries from the Five Cowries Terminal. That is the polite official version.

The lived version is more theatrical. Traffic is a fact of life. People plan their entire day around it, setting off before dawn or waiting out the worst of it, and the phrase "I'm on my way" has a flexible relationship with geography. Conductors shout the route, passengers bargain, and a system that looks like chaos from outside has an internal logic that regulars navigate effortlessly.

The strengths are real. The informal network is flexible, cheap and reaches almost everywhere. Anybody can start a route, anyone can find a ride, and the system adapts to need with a speed no municipal planner could manage. The BRT lanes show what a more formal approach can do, even if the crowds suggest demand outstrips supply.

The weaknesses are equally real. Safety, comfort, predictability and air quality are all variable, and a bad commute can swallow hours of your life. The expat advice about hiring a driver is practical, not snobbish: in a city where traffic can consume the day, having someone else do the driving is a form of reclaiming it. For locals, there is no such luxury, and the endurance is part of the culture.

## Canada

Canadian city transport is the polite sibling. Take Toronto, where an ExpatExchange guide describes the Toronto Transit Commission's four subway lines and ten streetcar lines, with regional GO Transit trains and a rail link between the airport and downtown. The guide quotes a single fare of three dollars and twenty-five cents, though the page is undated and prices will have changed. It also makes a claim that would be a fantasy in many cities: living without a car is feasible.

That is the headline. A system in which you tap a card, wait a few minutes, board a vehicle that is mostly on time, and arrive close to where you intended is an astonishing thing for anyone raised on improvisation. It is clean, regulated and predictable. Nobody asks you for extra money halfway through the journey.

But the Canadian system has its own character, and it is cold, literally and figuratively. Winter transforms the commute into an endurance test, with a bus shelter at minus fifteen testing your faith in public services. The silence is deep. Strangers do not chat. Everyone has a polite bubble, and violating it with a loud phone call is a social offence. For a Nigerian used to the warm, noisy bustle of a shared ride, the quiet can feel lonely.

A profile published by Zikoko in its "1000 Ways to Japa" series follows a Nigerian woman who moved to Canada in December 2018 for a master's degree, sold shawarma, worked in customer service and eventually became a software engineer, gaining citizenship in 2025. Her story is not about transport, but anyone who has made that journey knows the commute is where the new country first becomes real: the early shift, the cold, the bus that comes when the app says it will. Predictability turns out to be its own kind of dignity.

Outside the major cities, the picture changes sharply. Many Canadian regions are built for cars, and public transport can be thin. If you move to a smaller place, expect to buy a vehicle sooner than you hoped.

## The Verdict

Canada wins on infrastructure, reliability and safety, and this is not a close contest. A system that gets you to work on time without a negotiation or a prayer is a genuine public good. The ability to plan your life around a timetable is a form of freedom that many Lagosians would trade a lot for.

Nigeria wins on flexibility and sheer human energy. The informal transport network is endlessly adaptable, the conversations are more interesting and nobody ever feels invisible on a crowded bus. The system is exhausting, but it is alive, which is more than I can say for a silent Toronto carriage at eight in the morning.

If I were choosing a daily commute, I would take Toronto and its heated waiting room. If I were choosing a story to tell afterwards, I would take Lagos every time. Choose which one you want: the commute you enjoy or the one you survive and write about.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Zikoko, 1000 Ways to Japa (2026) — a Nigerian who arrived in Canada for a master's degree in December 2018 describes working customer-service and street-food jobs before moving into tech, a reminder that the first commute is often to a job you did not plan.*

> *Expat Arrivals, Lagos guide — most expats use company or personal drivers, and a home licence is only valid for a limited period, so the car you do not drive yourself is a standard line item.*

> *ExpatExchange, Toronto guide — the writer says living without a car is entirely feasible in the city, which sounds ordinary until you remember how many cities cannot say it.*

## Conclusion

A commute is a country in miniature. Lagos teaches you improvisation, patience and humour. Toronto teaches you queueing, silence and the quiet pleasure of a timetable that means what it says. Neither is wrong, and both will change the way you think about time.

If you are moving from Lagos to Canada, buy a proper coat before you buy anything else, learn to tap your card, and resist the urge to chat to strangers on the bus. If you are moving the other way, bring a driver, a sense of humour and a very large flask. In both cities, the same truth applies: the journey is never just about getting there.
