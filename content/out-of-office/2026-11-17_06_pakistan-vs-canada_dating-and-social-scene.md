# Pakistan Dates Through Your Mother and Canada Dates Through Your Phone

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In much of Pakistan, romance is a committee decision. There are aunts, there are cousins, there is a family friend with a suspiciously good memory for other people's children, and there is, eventually, a tray of tea carried into a drawing room by a young person who would very much prefer to be elsewhere. It is not as medieval as it sounds and not as romantic as the films suggest.

In Canada, romance is a series of mildly embarrassing text messages followed by a coffee, a second coffee and a long conversation about whether you are "on the same page". Everyone is polite. Everyone is apologetic. Nobody is quite sure whether the evening was a date, and the only truly clear signal is the bill, which will be split to the cent.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Pakistan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat family as part of any serious relationship | Assume a partner's relatives are optional extras |
| Respect modesty and local norms in public | Display affection as you would in Toronto |
| Be honest about your intentions early | Drift along in an ambiguous situation |
| Ask a trusted friend about local etiquette | Rely on a foreign dating-app playbook |

**Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say plainly that you are interested and what you want | Wait for family to arrange the introduction |
| Offer to split the bill or take turns paying | Insist on paying and call it tradition |
| Respect "no" and slow pacing without sulking | Mistake politeness for permission |
| Meet in public the first time and tell a friend | Skip the safety basics because you feel shy |

## Pakistan

Start with the thing most outsiders get wrong: Pakistan is not one dating culture. A Karachi graduate in the corporate world, a Lahore university student, a family in a village in the north and a Pakistani Canadian visiting for the summer will describe four different worlds. The common thread, though, is that family sits at the centre of a relationship. Cultural Atlas, the Australian public-broadcaster guide to national cultures, describes Pakistani society as strongly family-oriented, with obligations running both ways and decisions often made collectively.

Arranged and semi-arranged marriage remains a major route to partnership. But the process is changing. Reporting from Dawn and from regional outlets describes matchmaking moving online, with apps and websites joining the traditional network of aunts. The Asia News Network has reported on young Pakistanis using dating apps to get around arranged marriages, while noting that the cultural impact is uncertain. The Jakarta Post reported on singles who search for spouses in person at organised events. In other words, the old way is not dead, but it has a new interface.

Public life shapes the social scene. Mixed-gender socialising is common among colleagues, university friends and families, but open displays of romance are discouraged in many settings, and a couple who want privacy often need to be creative. Cafés, shopping malls, family gatherings and weddings are the settings where relationships develop, and the wedding season is the unofficial national dating festival. The Express Tribune ran an opinion essay titled "Why I do not date in Pakistan", which captures the sceptical, personal view that dating in the Western sense is complicated by risk, reputation and expectation.

For an expat, the practical advice is to be careful, honest and respectful. Local friends are your best guide. The social cost of misreading a situation can fall on the other person more than on you, and in a society where reputation matters, a careless move can hurt someone who has to live with the consequences long after you have gone home.

## Canada

Canada is, on the surface, easier, and that is part of the problem. There is no family committee, no formal expectation and no shortage of apps. A newcomer arrives to find that dating is individual, flexible and conducted in a vocabulary borrowed from therapy: boundaries, communication, red flags, intentions. Everyone is encouraged to say what they want, and everyone is slightly terrified of saying it.

The social scene is built around activities rather than ceremony. You meet through work, friends, apps, sports leagues, classes and the sort of volunteering that sounds worthy and turns out to be a very good way to meet people. Coffee is the standard first date, followed by a walk, a drink or a hockey game. The bill is often split, the goodbye is polite and the follow-up message arrives with an apology about being busy.

Canadian multiculturalism complicates and enriches all this. In large cities, a Pakistani newcomer will find a visible South Asian community, with restaurants, mosques, community events and networks where familiar customs survive. Canadian academic work on South Asian young people, such as articles in the Canadian Journal of Family and Youth, explores how young people balance family expectations with the dating norms of their peers. Plenty live between two worlds: dating in the Canadian way in public while negotiating very different expectations at home.

The weaknesses of the Canadian system are loneliness and winter. Making friends can be slow, and social circles form around school, work and old friends, which makes the first year a tough one for anyone who arrives alone. Apps help and exhaust in equal measure. Politeness can hide disinterest, and a person who says "we should hang out sometime" may well mean it as a pleasantry rather than a plan. The best advice is to say yes to invitations, issue a few of your own, and wait for the second year.

It would be easy to turn this into a story about tradition versus modernity, and I want to resist that. It is lazy and it is mostly wrong. Plenty of Pakistani families are far more thoughtful about compatibility than a Canadian swiping on a phone at midnight, and plenty of Canadians are far more lonely than their cheerful freedom suggests. Every system has its failure mode. The family-led model can press people into decisions they did not choose, and the individual-led model can leave people alone with too many options and no guidance. The honest question is not which country is more advanced but which kind of support you want when you are choosing a partner. Some people need a push, some need a map, and some just need someone to tell them that it is fine to eat dinner alone for a while.

## The Verdict

Canada wins on freedom, and Pakistan wins on seriousness. If you want to choose your own partner, move at your own pace and not answer to a clan, Canada gives you that, along with the loneliness that comes with it. If you want a relationship that comes embedded in a family and a community, with a clear sense of what it is for, Pakistan offers a structure that many people find reassuring rather than suffocating.

I am wary of declaring a winner, because the winner depends on the person. A woman who wants independence will find Canada liberating. A man who wants guidance may find Pakistan comforting. A couple negotiating both worlds will discover that the real art is not choosing but translating, and it is a harder skill than any app can teach.

If you have to pick, pick honesty. Say what you want, listen to what the other person needs, and do not mistake the rules of one country for the laws of nature. Both countries are full of people who are tired of games and want something real.

## Conclusion

Dating is a country's autobiography in miniature. Pakistan tells you that love is a family matter and that reputation is a shared asset. Canada tells you that love is a private decision and that politeness is a form of protection. Neither is complete without the other.

If you are moving from Pakistan to Canada, be ready to speak for yourself, to ask directly and to hear "no" without drama. If you are going the other way, bring respect for family, patience with ceremony and a very good friend who can translate. In both countries, the people worth meeting are the ones who listen.
