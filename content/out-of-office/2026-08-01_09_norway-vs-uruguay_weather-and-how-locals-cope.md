# Norway Loses The Sun For Months. Uruguay Just Wraps Its Whole Culture Around A Thermos.

🇳🇴 Norway + 🇺🇾 Uruguay

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Northern Norway disappears into polar night for weeks at a stretch every winter, plunging entire cities into a permanent, disorienting dusk that would floor most visitors within days and that locals handle with a specific, hard-won equanimity built from generations of practice. Uruguay's climate poses no such existential threat — mild, temperate, rarely extreme — and yet Uruguayans have still built an entire, deeply ritualised culture around coping with it, centred almost entirely on a thermos of hot water and a gourd of mate carried absolutely everywhere, regardless of season.

I've watched Norwegians combat months of darkness with candles, vitamin D supplements, and a stoicism that borders on the superhuman, and I've watched Uruguayans respond to a mildly overcast, perfectly pleasant afternoon with the same devoted mate-drinking ritual they'd deploy at the height of summer. One country is coping with genuine climatic hardship. The other has just decided a thermos is a personality trait regardless of what the sky is doing.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇳🇴 Norway**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in genuine cold-weather gear before winter — layering isn't optional here | Underestimate how disorienting polar night is; plan light-therapy routines in advance |
| Embrace "koselig" — candles, blankets, cosy indoor rituals — as a real coping mechanism | Schedule anything requiring natural daylight during peak polar night months up north |
| Get outside daily even in winter; locals ski and walk regardless of darkness | Assume the whole country experiences polar night — it's a northern phenomenon only |
| Take vitamin D supplements through the darkest months; it's genuinely standard practice | Isolate indoors for weeks; locals actively fight the instinct to hibernate completely |

**🇺🇾 Uruguay**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry a mate gourd and thermos if you want to fit in — it's a near-universal habit | Refuse a shared mate when offered; it's a genuine social gesture, not just a drink |
| Pack layers; Uruguay's weather shifts quickly and dramatically within a single day | Assume South American climate means guaranteed heat — Montevideo winters get properly cold and damp |
| Enjoy the coastal breeze culture — beach walks happen year-round, not just summer | Expect consistent weather; locals check forecasts obsessively because it changes fast |
| Join the outdoor asado culture whenever weather allows — it's central to social life | Be surprised by humidity swings; the coast and interior can feel like different countries |

## Norway: Turning Darkness Into A Coping System

North of the Arctic Circle, Norwegian towns like Tromsø experience polar night — a stretch of weeks each winter where the sun never rises above the horizon at all, replaced by a soft, extended twilight that never quite becomes day. It sounds, to anyone who hasn't experienced it, like the setup for a horror film, and for a genuine minority of residents it does trigger real seasonal affective disorder that's taken seriously as a public health matter, with light-therapy lamps a common household fixture and vitamin D supplementation practically a national reflex through the darkest months.

What's more striking than the darkness itself is the sheer cultural machinery Norway has built to counteract it. Koselig — a concept close to but distinct from Danish hygge — describes a deliberate cultivation of cosiness: candles lit at 3pm, wool blankets, warm drinks, and a determined resistance to the instinct to simply hibernate through the entire season. Norwegians ski, hike, and get outside through the darkness rather than avoiding it, treating physical activity as non-negotiable regardless of how little daylight is available to do it in. The result, somewhat counterintuitively, is a population that handles genuine climatic hardship with more equanimity and less complaint than I've seen in countries with objectively milder winters — the coping mechanisms aren't an afterthought here, they're a fully developed, actively maintained system.

## Uruguay: Ritual Over Necessity

Uruguay's climate is, by almost any global standard, entirely reasonable — temperate, coastal, with genuine but rarely extreme seasonal swings, closer in character to a mild Mediterranean climate than anything requiring serious adaptation. And yet Uruguayans have still constructed one of the most devoted, all-weather rituals I've encountered anywhere in sixty countries: mate, the bitter herbal infusion sipped through a metal straw from a shared gourd, carried in a thermos tucked under one arm literally everywhere, at every temperature, in every season, entirely independent of whether the actual weather calls for a hot drink at all.

This isn't really about coping with hardship — Montevideo's winters get cool and properly damp, but nothing that would trouble a Norwegian for a moment — it's about ritual as identity. The thermos-and-gourd combination functions as a social object as much as a beverage delivery system, offered and shared between friends, colleagues, and strangers as a genuine gesture of connection, refused only at real social cost. What surprised me most was how the weather itself shifts unpredictably within a single Uruguayan day — a bright coastal morning can turn to a damp, blustery afternoon within hours — which locals navigate with layered clothing and a level of forecast-checking that seems almost excessive for a climate this mild, until you realise it's less about genuine hardship and more about simply refusing to be caught out.

## The Verdict

Norway copes with genuine climatic extremity through a hard-won, actively maintained system of light therapy, cosiness rituals, and stubborn outdoor activity that would floor most other populations within a fortnight. Uruguay copes with an entirely reasonable climate through a ritual — mate — so devoted and so constant that it's stopped being about the weather at all and become a form of cultural identity in its own right. Norway wins on genuine resilience. Uruguay wins on turning a non-problem into one of the most charming national habits I've encountered anywhere. Both taught me something about how much of "coping with weather" is really about the ritual, not the actual temperature.

## Conclusion

Norway faces down months of genuine darkness with a toolkit built over generations — light, layers, and a refusal to let winter win. Uruguay faces down a perfectly mild climate with a ritual so devoted it's hard to tell where the weather coping ends and the cultural identity begins. Buy proper thermal gear and a light-therapy lamp for Norway. Buy a mate gourd and thermos for Uruguay, and accept every shared sip that comes your way. Neither country needs your sympathy about their weather — Norway's got a system, and Uruguay, frankly, was never really coping with anything in the first place.
