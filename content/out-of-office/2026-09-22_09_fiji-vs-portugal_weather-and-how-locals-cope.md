# One Country Has a Meteorological Service Issuing Tiered Cyclone Warnings. The Other Just Tells You to Close Your Shutters and Wait Until Dinner

🇫🇯 Fiji vs Portugal 🇵🇹

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Fiji spends five months of the year on genuine cyclone watch, with an actual tiered alert system and resorts that have refined their preparedness drills over decades of practice. Portugal spends its summers under red heat alerts so severe the government issues formal states of alert, and responds with a national strategy that amounts to: close the shutters, don't go outside between 11 and 5, and reconvene for dinner at a civilised hour once the sun has calmed down. One country prepares for weather that might destroy your resort. The other prepares for weather that will simply make you insufferable if you don't respect it.

I've watched a Fijian resort brief guests calmly through a cyclone watch like it was a mildly inconvenient scheduling change, and I've watched an entire Portuguese town go eerily silent at 2pm in July like the heat itself had declared a public holiday. Different scales of drama, equally serious underneath.

## Do's & Don'ts: Fiji

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Travel with real trust in the Fiji Meteorological Service's tiered alerts — Disturbance, Watch, Warning — they're well-calibrated and taken seriously | Panic at the word "cyclone" without checking which tier is actually in effect — most wet-season trips see nothing worse than heavy afternoon rain |
| Book resort stays during wet season (November–April) with confidence — reputable properties have decades of preparedness experience | Assume constant rain — showers are typically short and intense rather than an all-day washout |
| Ask your resort about their specific cyclone plan if you're travelling January to March, the peak risk window | Ignore a Cyclone Watch notification just because the sky currently looks fine — conditions can shift within the stated 48-hour window |

## Do's & Don'ts: Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Follow DGS guidance during red alerts: stay indoors 11am–5pm, drink at least 1.5 litres of water, skip alcohol and caffeine | Underestimate a "red warning" day — temperatures have approached 46°C during recent heatwaves, and heat safety is a genuine public health matter |
| Keep shutters and blinds closed during the day, opening windows only after sunset to let cooler air in | Leave windows open all day thinking ventilation helps — it just lets more heat in during peak hours |
| Shift your schedule like locals do — quiet, shaded afternoons and a livelier evening once temperatures drop | Plan strenuous outdoor activity for early afternoon during a heatwave — even locals and farmers shift hard labour to night hours |

## Fiji: A Well-Drilled Nation Living Alongside Cyclone Season

Fiji's relationship with extreme weather is defined by genuine institutional competence rather than fatalism. Cyclone season runs from November through May, with the sharpest risk concentrated between January and March, and the country averages two to three tropical cyclones per season — not all of which make direct landfall on the main tourist areas, but enough that preparedness isn't optional infrastructure, it's baseline national practice. The Fiji Meteorological Service runs a genuinely well-calibrated, tiered alert system: a Tropical Disturbance advisory when a system is still forming, escalating to a Cyclone Watch when a credible threat sits within roughly 48 hours, and finally a Cyclone Warning when destructive conditions are expected within 24 hours. This isn't vague or alarmist messaging — it's precise, actionable, and trusted by residents and the tourism industry alike.

What's genuinely impressive is how thoroughly this preparedness has been absorbed into everyday hospitality infrastructure. Reputable resorts have refined cyclone response plans built from decades of lived experience — briefing guests calmly, securing outdoor furniture and equipment, confirming provisions, all executed with a practised, unpanicked efficiency that says more about Fijian resilience than any brochure could. Guests arriving mid-Watch are more likely to be reassured by calm, competent staff than alarmed by the situation itself, and that calibration between genuine risk and calm response is the actual cultural achievement here.

The wet season itself, running November to April, brings the year's heaviest rainfall and highest humidity, but the actual lived experience skews less dramatic than outsiders expect — showers tend to be short and intense rather than day-long washouts, with cloud cover lingering more than constant rain falling. Most visitors travelling during wet season encounter nothing worse than a heavy afternoon downpour that clears within the hour, while the far rarer, genuinely dangerous cyclone events are handled by a system that has had ample practice managing them without theatrics.

## Portugal: When the Government Issues a Formal State of Alert Over Temperature

Portugal's summer heat has moved, in recent years, from "uncomfortably hot" to a genuine recurring public health emergency, with red health alerts covering more than half the country and temperatures pushing toward 46°C in the worst-affected interior regions. This isn't hyperbole or tourist-board spin — the national health authority, DGS, issues specific, formal guidance during these events, and the government has declared states of alert in direct response to the severity of recent heatwaves.

What's genuinely instructive is how methodically ordinary Portuguese life reorganises itself around this heat rather than simply enduring it. The DGS guidance is unglamorous but effective: drink at least 1.5 litres of water daily, avoid alcohol and caffeine, stay indoors specifically between 11am and 5pm, and reapply sunscreen every two hours if you must be outside. Locals close shutters and blinds tight during daylight hours and only open windows after sunset, deliberately keeping cooler night air in and trapping it before the heat of the following day arrives — a genuinely simple, low-tech strategy that outperforms plenty of expensive cooling systems.

The behavioural shift extends well beyond individual households. Cobbled streets in historic towns visibly empty out during peak afternoon heat, with locals and tourists alike retreating to shaded terraces and fan-cooled cafés, while some farmers have shifted physically demanding outdoor labour to nighttime hours entirely, working under cover of darkness rather than risking heat exhaustion during the day. This isn't a temporary emergency adaptation — it's become a recurring seasonal rhythm, one that visitors ignore at genuine physical risk, given how quickly extreme heat can turn dangerous for anyone unprepared for it.

## The Verdict

Fiji wins on sheer institutional sophistication — a tiered, trusted meteorological alert system paired with decades of resort-level preparedness represents genuinely world-class disaster readiness for a small island nation facing real annual risk. Portugal wins on cultural adaptability — an entire country reorganising its daily rhythm around a heat threat that keeps intensifying, without losing its evening liveliness once the temperature actually drops, is its own quiet triumph. If you're travelling during Fiji's risk window, trust the alert tiers and let the resorts do what they've spent decades perfecting. If you're in Portugal during a red-alert heatwave, close your shutters, drink your water, and accept that the entire country agrees with the government on this one: nothing important happens between 11 and 5 anyway.

## Conclusion

Fiji and Portugal both face weather serious enough to reorganise daily life around it, and both have responded with genuinely effective, culturally embedded systems rather than mere resignation. One built a precise, trusted meteorological alert hierarchy backed by decades of hospitality-sector preparedness. The other turned shutters, siestas, and a strict 11-to-5 indoor rule into a national survival strategy against its own increasingly brutal summers. Respect both. Check the cyclone tier before you panic in Fiji, and close your blinds before noon in Portugal — the locals have already told you exactly how this works, whether or not you were listening.
