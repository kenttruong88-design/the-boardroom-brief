# One Country Will Judge Your Bagging Speed. The Other Just Wants You to Enjoy the Tomatoes.

🇲🇰 North Macedonia · 🇩🇪 Germany
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Germany has turned grocery shopping into a discipline with rules, and the rules are enforced socially by everyone in the queue behind you, whether you asked for their opinion or not. North Macedonia, and Skopje's sprawling green bazaar in particular, treats grocery shopping as something closer to a leisurely social occasion where the produce is the excuse and the conversation is the point. One of these countries will make you feel judged for fumbling your reusable bags at the till. The other will make you feel judged only if you don't stop to actually taste the cheese first.

I have been silently, then not-so-silently, hurried along by a German queue for taking eleven seconds too long to bag my groceries, and I have been offered a slice of unripened melon by a Skopje vendor purely so I'd know it wasn't ready yet. Only one of these experiences made me want to come back.

## Do's & Don'ts: North Macedonia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit Skopje's Bit Pazar or a neighbourhood green market for the best produce prices and quality | Expect the same fixed-price certainty as a supermarket; light negotiation on larger purchases is normal |
| Bring your own bags — most market vendors don't provide them and disposable ones cost extra | Rush the interaction; a bit of small talk with your regular vendor usually improves your prices over time |
| Shop early for the best selection, especially for seasonal fruit that sells out by mid-morning | Assume supermarkets like Vero or Tinex offer meaningfully better quality than the bazaar — they mostly don't |

## Do's & Don'ts: Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring exact change or a card and bag your own groceries fast — the cashier is not waiting for you | Forget your Pfand (deposit) bottles at home; the return machines are everywhere and the deposit is real money |
| Bring your own bags every time — plastic bags are rarely free and mildly frowned upon regardless | Chat with the cashier at length during peak hours; efficiency is the unspoken social contract here |
| Shop at Aldi or Lidl without any embarrassment — discount supermarkets are mainstream, not downmarket | Expect Sunday grocery shopping; nearly all supermarkets are closed by law, plan your week accordingly |

## North Macedonia: The Bazaar Is Still the Beating Heart of Grocery Life

Skopje's Bit Pazar and the network of smaller green markets scattered through the city's neighbourhoods remain, even as modern supermarket chains like Vero and Tinex have expanded steadily over the past two decades, the place where most Macedonians actually prefer to buy their fruit, vegetables, cheese, and household staples. The reasons are practical as much as cultural: bazaar produce is typically fresher, often locally grown, and priced lower than supermarket equivalents, particularly for seasonal items at peak harvest, when a kilo of tomatoes at the market can cost a genuine fraction of the supermarket price for a noticeably inferior product.

What distinguishes the Macedonian bazaar experience from a purely transactional errand is the social layer built into it. Regular customers develop relationships with specific vendors over years, and those relationships translate into real, tangible benefits — better cuts, better prices, produce set aside before it's even displayed. Light negotiation remains a normal part of larger purchases, though it's considerably gentler and more relationship-based than the more theatrical bargaining associated with markets further east or south; it's less about winning and more about maintaining a fair, ongoing rapport with someone you'll be buying from again next week.

Supermarkets have absolutely gained ground, particularly among younger, urban Macedonians who value convenience, air conditioning, and predictable hours over the bazaar's more variable experience, and for packaged goods, household items, and imported products, the supermarket is now simply the default. But for the fresh produce that anchors most Macedonian cooking, the bazaar's continued dominance says something real about quality winning out over convenience — a dynamic that plenty of wealthier countries have quietly lost and now pay premium prices at farmers' markets trying to recreate.

## Germany: Bagging Speed as a Moral Category

German supermarket culture operates on a set of unwritten rules so rigid and so widely internalized that violating them, even innocently, produces a genuinely tangible social response — the sigh, the pointed look, the audible foot-tap from the person next in line. Chief among these rules: you bag your own groceries, and you do it fast, because the cashier is scanning items at a pace calibrated to someone who already knows this and has their bags open and ready before the first item hits the belt. Newcomers who fumble this — reaching for bags after items have already piled up, or worse, expecting the cashier to help — quickly learn, through pure social pressure, to move faster next time.

The Pfand (deposit) system for bottles and cans is a genuinely well-designed piece of environmental infrastructure that catches most visitors off guard the first time: a small deposit, often 8 to 25 cents depending on the container, added to the price of most bottled and canned drinks, refundable via return machines present in nearly every supermarket. Germans treat this system with total seriousness — I've watched people carefully sort recyclables into deposit and non-deposit piles with an intensity that borders on ritual, and abandoning a stack of Pfand bottles rather than returning them is regarded as a genuinely wasteful, faintly embarrassing choice rather than a harmless convenience.

Discount chains — Aldi and Lidl chief among them, both German inventions now exported worldwide — occupy a completely different social position here than "discount supermarket" implies elsewhere. There's no stigma whatsoever to shopping at Aldi; it's mainstream across income levels, prized for efficiency and quality-to-price ratio rather than looked down on, and the German export of the no-frills discount supermarket model to dozens of other countries stands as one of the country's quieter and more successful cultural exports. The Sunday closure laws, meanwhile — nearly all supermarkets shut by law on Sundays nationwide — remain a genuine adjustment for anyone from a 24/7 retail culture, forcing a kind of enforced weekly planning discipline that most Germans have long since stopped noticing.

## The Verdict

Germany's system is more efficient by almost any objective measure — faster checkouts, better environmental infrastructure, more consistent pricing. But efficiency isn't the same as pleasure, and North Macedonia's bazaar culture offers something Germany's supermarket aisles structurally cannot: an actual relationship with the person selling you your food, and produce that tastes like it grew somewhere specific rather than everywhere generically.

I'll take the bazaar, tomatoes and all. Germany can keep its Pfand machines; Skopje can keep the melon samples.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations Skopje — a longtime resident wrote that developing a "regular vendor" at Bit Pazar took about three months but permanently improved both prices and produce quality afterward.*

> *expat.com Berlin forum — a Canadian expat described her first Aldi checkout experience as "the single most stressful sixty seconds of my week for the first month," before eventually becoming the fast bagger she once feared.*

> *Quora — a German respondent explaining the bagging-speed culture noted it isn't really about the cashier's schedule at all, but a broader German cultural discomfort with holding up a line unnecessarily, applied here with unusual intensity.*

## Conclusion

North Macedonia will keep letting the bazaar run the country's actual food culture no matter how many supermarkets open around it, and Germany will keep enforcing bagging speed as an unofficial civic virtue whether or not anyone officially wrote it down. If you want a grocery run that doubles as a small social occasion, Skopje has you covered. If you want a grocery run measured in efficient seconds and redeemed bottle deposits, Germany will not apologize for the pace — and it certainly won't slow down for you.
