# One Country Turns the Weekend Into a Mountain Expedition, the Other Just Wants a Boat, a Village, and Absolutely No Plans

🇨🇱 Chile × 🇲🇹 Malta
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ask a Chilean what they're doing this weekend and there's a real chance the answer involves a four-hour drive into the Andes, a hike, and an asado assembled with the logistical precision of a small military operation. Ask a Maltese person the same question and the answer is more likely to be a boat trip to a quiet cove, lunch that takes three unhurried hours, and absolutely no fixed plan for what happens after.

Chile's geography — a country stretched impossibly long between mountains and coastline — has produced a weekend culture built around ambitious outdoor expeditions and the serious, almost sacred ritual of the asado. Malta, a small island where nothing is ever more than a short drive from the sea, has produced the opposite instinct: a weekend built around slowness, proximity, and the quiet luxury of not needing to go far to feel like you've properly left the week behind.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇨🇱 Chile**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take the asado seriously; it has real rules and roles | Rush the grilling process or treat it as casual background cooking |
| Plan proper outdoor expeditions; the landscape rewards ambition | Assume a "quick" weekend hike will actually be quick here |
| Bring wine and contribute properly if invited to someone's asado | Show up empty-handed to a weekend gathering |
| Embrace long afternoons that stretch well into the evening | Expect a tightly scheduled, efficient weekend itinerary |

**🇲🇹 Malta**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get on a boat; a huge amount of Maltese leisure happens on the water | Assume weekend leisure centres on land-based activities only |
| Embrace a slow, three-hour lunch as the actual point of the day | Rush a meal to fit in more sightseeing afterward |
| Explore village festas and local feasts if the timing lines up | Dismiss a village feast as a minor local curiosity |
| Accept that "no plan" is a legitimate, common weekend strategy | Over-schedule a Maltese weekend the way you might elsewhere |

## Chile: The Weekend as an Expedition and a Ritual

Chile's extraordinary geography — over four thousand kilometres of mountains, desert, coastline, and forest packed into one absurdly narrow country — has shaped a weekend culture built around genuine outdoor ambition. Santiago residents think nothing of a multi-hour drive into the Andes for a day hike, treating distance and elevation gain as a feature of the weekend, not an obstacle to it. This isn't a niche enthusiast subculture, it's a broadly shared national instinct that a proper weekend often means leaving the city behind entirely, not just relaxing within it.

Sitting alongside this outdoor ambition is the asado, Chile's version of the barbecue, which carries a seriousness that visitors consistently underestimate. This isn't casual grilling — there are real expectations around who tends the fire (often a specific, semi-ceremonial role), the order in which different cuts of meat are cooked and served, and the appropriate contribution guests are expected to bring, usually wine, sometimes a specific dish. An asado can stretch for an entire afternoon into evening, less a meal than a slow-moving social event structured loosely around food.

What ties both instincts together is a shared resistance to rushing. Whether it's a mountain hike or an asado, the Chilean weekend rewards a willingness to let the day take as long as it needs, rather than compressing leisure into an efficient block. Visitors trying to squeeze a hike and an asado and a city outing into one tight Saturday schedule usually find themselves gently corrected — pick one, do it properly, and let the day breathe.

## Malta: Small Island, Slow Weekend

Malta's weekend culture runs on an entirely different geographic logic. On an island where nothing is ever really far from the sea, the whole concept of "getting away" for the weekend collapses into something much smaller and slower — a short boat ride to a quiet cove, a lazy afternoon at a harbourside table, or simply pottering around a village that's a fifteen-minute drive from wherever you started. Ambition, in the Chilean sense, isn't really the point here; proximity and unhurried repetition are.

Boats occupy an outsized place in Maltese leisure culture relative to the island's size — a huge proportion of weekend leisure time involves getting on the water in some form, whether a proper boat trip to Comino's Blue Lagoon or a more modest potter along the coast. Lunch, similarly, isn't something to be finished efficiently so the day can move on to its next item; a three-hour lunch stretching from midday well into the afternoon, with no particular urgency to leave the table, is treated as a perfectly complete way to spend a chunk of the weekend on its own.

Village festas — the local feast day celebrations honouring a parish's patron saint, complete with fireworks, marching bands, and street food — add another distinct texture entirely, transforming a normally quiet village into the weekend's actual destination for those who time it right. What unites all of this is a fundamentally different relationship with scale: Malta's weekend culture has made peace with smallness, treating "not going far" not as a limitation but as the entire, deliberate point.

## The Verdict

Chile treats the weekend as an opportunity for genuine expedition and ceremonial ritual, best measured by distance covered and the seriousness of the asado. Malta treats the weekend as an invitation to slow down and stay close, finding an entire day's satisfaction in a short boat ride and a long lunch. One culture measures a good weekend by ambition; the other by how little you had to move to feel properly restored.

Given the choice, I have more genuine admiration for Chile's willingness to treat leisure as something worth real effort — there's something satisfying about earning a view — but I won't pretend Malta's three-hour harbourside lunch hasn't ruined me slightly for the idea of eating anywhere quickly ever again.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora, "Why do Chileans treat a weekend asado as such a serious event?" — paraphrased: a respondent explained the asado functions almost like a small ceremony with unwritten rules about roles and timing, and treating it casually is a quick way to seem like you don't understand the occasion.*

> *expat.com Santiago — paraphrased: a resident described being firmly, if politely, corrected after showing up to their first asado without wine, and learning that contributing properly is simply not optional.*

> *Internations Valletta — paraphrased: an expat said it took real effort to unlearn the instinct to over-plan a weekend, eventually realising that a three-hour lunch with no fixed agenda afterward was the whole point, not a gap to be filled.*

## Conclusion

Chile and Malta have built two completely different weekend philosophies out of two completely different landscapes — one long, dramatic, and made for expedition; one small, coastal, and made for staying close. Bring Chile's ambitious multi-stop itinerary to a Maltese weekend and you'll find there's simply nowhere far enough to go to justify it. Bring Malta's unhurried, plan-free instinct to a Chilean weekend and you'll miss the mountain entirely while everyone else is already three hours into a hike. Match your ambitions to your geography before you plan the weekend, not after.
