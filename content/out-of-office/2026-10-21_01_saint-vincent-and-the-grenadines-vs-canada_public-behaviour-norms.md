🇻🇨 Saint Vincent and the Grenadines 🇨🇦 Canada
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nobody warns the Vincentian newcomer that Canada's famous politeness is, in practice, a national agreement to pretend other people don't exist. You can stand shoulder to shoulder with someone on the Skytrain, make eye contact, and both silently agree that acknowledging it would constitute an act of aggression. Meanwhile, back on the Grenadines side, a fifteen-minute wait for a bus is an opportunity, not an inconvenience — somebody's cousin will turn up, a radio will materialise, and by the time the bus arrives you'll know who's getting married in March.

I have lived in both postures. One treats public space as a shared living room; the other treats it as a hallway you are obligated to walk through without touching the walls. Both nations will tell you, completely sincerely, that they are the friendly one. They are both right, which is the part people struggle with.

## Do's & Don'ts: Saint Vincent and the Grenadines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet strangers with a "good morning" before any transaction, even in a shop queue | Walk past someone without a greeting — it reads as open hostility, not efficiency |
| Expect and enjoy "liming" — unhurried, communal hanging-about with no fixed agenda | Rush a conversation to "get to the point"; the point is the conversation |
| Raise your voice freely in a market or on a verandah — volume signals engagement | Assume loud talk means an argument is happening; it usually isn't |
| Join the queue by proximity and vibe rather than a strict single-file line | Panic when the "line" looks more like a loose cluster; it has its own order |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Form a single, silent, almost architecturally precise line — Canadians queue like it's an Olympic sport | Cut, crowd, or stand "too close" behind someone; a half-metre gap is sacred |
| Say "sorry" reflexively, even when someone else bumps into you | Mistake the apology for an actual admission of fault — it's social lubricant, not law |
| Keep public volume low; indoor voices extend to outdoor spaces | Narrate your phone call to the whole bus; Canadians will not tell you to stop, they will just suffer |
| Hold doors, thank bus drivers audibly, nod at joggers — small civilities are currency | Assume silence equals coldness; it's often just conflict-avoidance dressed as serenity |

## Saint Vincent and the Grenadines: the verandah is the village

Public behaviour in SVG runs on proximity and acknowledgement. Walking through Kingstown, you will be greeted by strangers, drawn into brief exchanges with shopkeepers who remember your name after one visit, and expected to greet a room — the whole room — on entry, not just the person you came to see. This isn't quaintness; it's infrastructure. In a small island society, public courtesy functions as a trust network, because everyone is, at most, two phone calls from knowing your family.

Volume is not rudeness. A heated-sounding exchange on a minibus about cricket, politics, or the price of breadfruit is entertainment, not conflict, and bystanders will happily weigh in uninvited — that's not an interruption, it's participation. "Liming," the art of standing or sitting around with no particular destination, is a legitimate and respected use of time, and treating it as wasted time is the fastest way to be quietly filed under "tourist energy, never left."

The flexible queue is the part that unravels newly arrived Canadians (and, frankly, most northern Europeans). What looks like a crowd pressing toward a counter is, to locals, a perfectly legible order based on who arrived when, who's holding whose place, and a general social memory that outsiders simply haven't downloaded yet. Trying to impose a strict single-file line onto this system doesn't produce order — it produces confusion and a faint sense that you think everyone else is cheating.

## Canada: the sacred half-metre

Canadians have turned personal space into something approaching religious doctrine. The queue is not a suggestion; it is a geometric formation, maintained with a precision that would make a parade-ground sergeant weep with pride. Step even slightly out of your allotted gap and you will feel, rather than hear, disapproval radiate outward — nobody will say anything, because saying something would itself be a breach of etiquette. The silence is the enforcement mechanism.

"Sorry" is deployed constantly and means almost nothing in the literal sense — it's closer to a verbal tap on the shoulder meaning "I register that we occupy shared space, let's not make this weird." New arrivals from more verbally expressive cultures often read Canadian quiet as unfriendliness, when it's closer to an exaggerated fear of imposing. Canadians will absolutely help you move a couch; they will also die before telling you your music is too loud, preferring instead to seethe behind a closed door and mention it to their own therapist eighteen months later.

Public transit is the purest lab for this. An entire bus of people will sit in near-total silence, eyes fixed on phones, while one person on a loud call discusses their divorce in detail — and not a single person will ask them to stop. The social contract in Canada isn't "don't be disruptive"; it's "endure disruption silently and complain about it to a friend afterward." Vincentian newcomers who try to politely intervene — "excuse me, could you keep it down?" — report being met less with hostility than with genuine, wide-eyed confusion, as though they'd broken a rule nobody had written down because nobody imagined it needed writing.

Children are where the two systems reveal themselves most honestly. In SVG, a child misbehaving in public is everyone's business — a neighbour, a shopkeeper, or a complete stranger on a bus will correct a child who's out of line, and parents generally welcome this rather than resent it, because disciplining a child is understood as a shared community responsibility, not a private parental matter to be guarded jealously from outside interference. In Canada, the same intervention from a stranger would likely be read as deeply overstepping, possibly even alarming, and parents largely operate on the assumption that their child's behaviour in public is entirely their own domain to manage, for better or worse. Canadian municipal noise bylaws formalise this instinct toward private responsibility even further — many cities set specific legal decibel limits and quiet hours, turning what in SVG would be settled through direct social pressure into something settled through a bylaw officer and, if it escalates, an actual fine. Newcomers from SVG often find this strange in both directions: strange that nobody will simply tell a noisy neighbour to pipe down directly, and stranger still that the eventual remedy, when it comes, arrives as a letter from the city rather than a conversation on the street.

## The Verdict

If you want a public culture that treats you like a person the moment you walk outside, Saint Vincent and the Grenadines wins without a fight — a short walk to the shop is a social occasion, and nobody there has ever died of loneliness in a crowd. If you want to be left entirely, blissfully alone while surrounded by hundreds of people, Canada is unbeatable, and there's real relief in that if you've spent your life being greeted by everyone you've ever met. Canadians will insist their reserve is a form of respect, and honestly, they might be onto something — just don't expect anyone to tell you that to your face. They'd find it awkward.

## Conclusion

Here's the sting: both cultures think the other one is faintly unhinged, and both are, a little. SVG's public warmth can feel, to a reserved Canadian, like an invasion; Canada's public silence can feel, to a Vincentian, like a whole nation holding its breath. Neither is wrong. What's actually happening is two entirely different theories of what strangers owe each other in shared space — one says "acknowledge me," the other says "ignore me, kindly." Move between them enough times, as I have, and you stop looking for the "normal" one. There isn't one. There's just whichever version of being around other humans you were raised to find unbearable, and whichever one you eventually, grudgingly, learn to fake.
