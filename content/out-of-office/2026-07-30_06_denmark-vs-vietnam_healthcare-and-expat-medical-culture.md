# One Country Makes You Wait for a Number, the Other Just Hands You the Medicine

🇩🇰 Denmark vs 🇻🇳 Vietnam
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Denmark's healthcare system is a marvel of egalitarian bureaucracy: free at the point of use, funded by taxes nobody seriously complains about, and gated almost entirely by your assigned general practitioner, who functions as a genuinely powerful gatekeeper standing between you and literally any specialist. Vietnam's system runs on a completely different logic — a thriving, semi-informal pharmacy culture where you can walk in, describe a symptom, and walk out with medication that would require three appointments and a referral letter almost anywhere in Northern Europe.

I've waited two weeks for a Danish GP appointment to discuss a problem that resolved itself out of sheer administrative patience. I've also walked into a pharmacy in Hanoi, mimed a headache with the theatrical commitment of a silent film star, and been handed exactly the right medication in ninety seconds by a pharmacist who'd clearly seen this performance before. Both systems work. Only one of them respects your time.

## Do's & Don'ts: Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for your CPR number immediately on arrival — it's the gateway to literally everything, healthcare included | Show up at a hospital for non-emergencies without going through your assigned GP first — you'll be redirected |
| Choose your general practitioner (læge) carefully — switching later is possible but adds friction | Expect same-day appointments for non-urgent issues; the system prioritises fairness over speed |
| Keep private supplemental insurance in mind for dental and some specialist gaps the public system doesn't cover | Assume the public system is slow because it's broken — it's slow because it's deliberately triaged |

## Do's & Don'ts: Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use private, internationally-accredited hospitals (in Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City) for anything serious | Rely solely on pharmacy self-diagnosis for anything beyond minor, obviously familiar ailments |
| Carry cash for pharmacy and clinic visits — card acceptance is inconsistent outside major private facilities | Assume public hospital standards match private ones; the gap between the two systems is significant |
| Get proper travel or expat health insurance before you need it — private care is excellent but not free | Ignore language barriers at public facilities; bring a translator app or local contact for anything complex |

## Denmark: Healthcare as Egalitarian, Deliberate Bureaucracy

Danish healthcare is, on paper, one of the great achievements of the welfare state: universal, tax-funded, essentially free at the point of use, and built around a fundamental belief that access to care shouldn't depend on wealth. In practice, living inside it as an expat means learning to love your CPR number with the devotion of a cult member, because that ten-digit civil registration number is the actual key to the entire system — without it, you are, healthcare-wise, a ghost.

The general practitioner is the load-bearing wall of the whole structure. You're assigned or choose one near your registered address, and virtually everything routes through them — they're the ones who decide whether you need a specialist, and referrals are not optional shortcuts you can route around with enough insistence. This produces a system that is, by international standards, remarkably fair: your GP doesn't treat you differently based on what you can afford, because affordability isn't part of the equation. It also produces a system that moves at exactly the pace bureaucratic fairness allows, which is to say: not fast.

The adjustment for newcomers, especially those from systems where money buys speed, is recalibrating expectations entirely. A two-week wait for a non-urgent GP appointment isn't a failure of the system; it's the system correctly deciding your issue isn't urgent enough to jump the queue ahead of someone else's. Emergency care, notably, is a different story entirely — fast, excellent, and available regardless of the queue. Denmark just refuses to treat "inconvenient" as "urgent," and that distinction takes some getting used to.

## Vietnam: Healthcare as a Two-Track, Fast-Moving System

Vietnam's healthcare landscape splits cleanly into two tracks that barely resemble each other. The public system, while genuinely functional and widely used by Vietnamese citizens, can be a difficult environment for foreigners to navigate — language barriers, different documentation norms, and inconsistent standards depending on the facility. The private system, concentrated in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City particularly, is a different story: internationally accredited hospitals with English-speaking staff, Western-trained doctors, and standards that rival or exceed care available in wealthier countries, all available at a fraction of the price you'd pay for equivalent private care in Europe or North America.

But the feature that genuinely surprises newcomers is the pharmacy culture. Vietnamese pharmacies operate with a level of accessible, walk-in medical assistance that simply doesn't exist in most Western systems. Describe your symptoms — through language, gesture, or a translation app — and a trained pharmacist will often recommend and dispense medication that would require a full doctor's appointment and prescription elsewhere. It's efficient, it's fast, and it works remarkably well for minor, familiar ailments, precisely because pharmacists here are trusted with more clinical judgment than their counterparts in more heavily regulated systems.

The risk, obviously, is over-reliance. Self-diagnosing anything beyond the obviously minor, or skipping proper care for something serious because the pharmacy route felt easier, is how visitors get themselves into real trouble. The system rewards common sense about where the line sits between "pharmacy problem" and "hospital problem" — and expats who've been here a while develop that instinct fast, usually after one story that ends with "and that's when I finally went to the private hospital."

## The Verdict

Denmark wins on fairness and long-term reliability — nobody falls through the cracks because they can't pay, and the system's patience is a feature, not a flaw, once you accept it. Vietnam wins on sheer responsiveness — need something now, you can usually get it now, provided you know which track to use. For a chronic condition requiring long-term, equitable management, Denmark. For a Tuesday headache you want gone by Wednesday, Vietnam, no contest.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Denmark — paraphrased: a newcomer said they didn't realise how central the CPR number was until they tried to book a routine appointment without one and were told, essentially, to come back once they existed on paper.*

> *Internations Copenhagen — paraphrased: an expat described the two-week GP wait as maddening at first, then oddly reassuring once they understood emergencies get seen immediately, no queue involved.*

> *Reddit r/VietNam — paraphrased: a long-term resident said their first instinct for any ailment is still the local pharmacy, and their Vietnamese friends find it hilarious that this counts as a culture shock story for foreigners.*

## Conclusion

Denmark builds trust through patience and universal access; Vietnam builds trust through speed and a private system that punches well above its price tag. Show up in Copenhagen expecting same-day care for a sniffle and you'll be quietly corrected by a very calm receptionist. Show up in Hanoi assuming every ailment needs a hospital and you'll be gently redirected to the pharmacy two doors down. Learn which system you're in before you need it — the day you actually need healthcare is a bad time to be improvising.
