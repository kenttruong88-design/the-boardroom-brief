# Japan Wipes Down the Barbell. Thailand Hands You a Free Olympic Pool and a Medical Certificate

🇹🇭 Thailand vs 🇯🇵 Japan

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every gym in the world sells the same fantasy: that you will walk in a slob and walk out a person. What differs is the paperwork. Japan wants you to read the rules, change your shoes, cover your tattoos, wipe your sweat, and sign your name on a piece of paper to book the barbell. Thailand wants 1,500 baht a month, or, if you are clever, 40 baht a year and your passport.

I have worked out in sixty countries, which in practice means I have been disappointed by sixty kinds of treadmill. The real divide is not equipment. It is whether the gym is a temple, with rituals and silence and a priesthood of rule-keepers, or a market, with prices that drift depending on how much you want it. Japan is the temple. Thailand is the market. Both will get you fit, and only one will leave you feeling you have been audited.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Thailand**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your passport to government sports centres | Assume the cheap option is the poor option |
| Compare hotel gyms with chains, because the gap is enormous | Sign a long contract at the first desk you see |
| Ask about the doctor's certificate if you want the pool | Show up with swimming trunks and no paperwork |
| Check opening days, since some close on Mondays and holidays | Plan a Monday session at a public centre |

**Japan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for a tour before you sign anything | Rely on Google Maps and sign a year contract |
| Bring clean indoor shoes and ask about the dress code | Walk in with outdoor soles |
| Wipe equipment before and after use | Leave a bench damp |
| Ask coworkers for gyms that online maps do not show | Assume a municipal centre will accept non-residents |

## Thailand

Bangkok gives you a pleasing range of options, and an even more pleasing range of prices. A Bangkok expat writing on a local blog describes a new hotel-attached gym at 5,000 baht a month plus a 1,500 baht signing fee, against a True Fitness membership at 1,500 baht a month. That is a threefold difference for the same basic proposition, which is lifting heavy things while air conditioning hums.

Then comes the best gym-related paragraph I have read in months. The Thai-Japan Youth Centre, in Din Daeng, costs 40 baht a year. It has a boxing and MMA ring, a running track, a football pitch, an Olympic-size pool and squash courts. You bring your passport, and for the pool you bring a doctor's health certificate. It is open Tuesday to Saturday from ten to nine and Sunday afternoons, closed Mondays and national holidays. The writer, who has lived in Thailand for about five years, calls it the best gym in Bangkok for amenities, while admitting a slight guilt at using a government facility for the price of a sandwich.

That is the Thai way: a system with many doors, some of them gilded and some of them nearly invisible. The market sorts itself by price, the state quietly offers a bargain to anyone who asks, and the climate does the rest. Outdoor exercise is possible but not pleasant at midday, so the serious will train indoors, early or late.

The cultural undertone is relaxed and mildly social. Nobody will ask you to change shoes in a ceremonial manner. If you want to be anonymous, you can. If you want a trainer, he is a phone call away. The sting is that the best option takes effort to find, and the effort is not advertised.

One more practical point from the Bangkok writer: opening days matter. The government centre is closed on Mondays and national holidays, and the pool requires a doctor's health certificate, so a visit planned on impulse can turn into a very short outing with a very polite refusal. The lesson applies in both countries: the cheapest gym is usually the one with the most paperwork, and the most expensive is often the one with the least. Ask, bring your passport, and check the calendar before you cross the city in the heat. A bargain that costs you a wasted afternoon is no bargain, though at forty baht a year it is still a lot cheaper than a mistake.

## Japan

Japan makes the opposite offer: infrastructure that is clean, ordered and occasionally maddening. A first-year teacher in Kanazawa describes gyms that are fewer, smaller and usually pricier than in the West. Many are compact studios in high-rises, the clientele skews older, and equipment can be sparse, perhaps a few ten-kilo dumbbells and not much else. His advice is to ask for a tour first, since he signed a year contract after trusting only Google Maps.

The rules are the real character study. You may need to cover tattoos and piercings, wear a dress code, and bring separate indoor shoes. Lockers and showers are common, women-only areas exist, and vending machines sell protein shakes, which is the most Japanese sentence I have ever typed. A software engineer who lived in Tokyo in 2023 and 2024 found the nearest commercial gym at 11,000 yen a month, but a municipal sports centre cost under 3,000 yen, with most requiring residence in that municipality. He wiped down equipment before and after use, and wrote his name on paper to book the barbell.

That booking sheet tells you everything. Japanese gym culture is communal, regulated and courteous to a degree that borders on liturgy. The reward is a place where nothing is broken, nobody leaves a mess, and the machine you want is waiting. The cost is that you must learn the liturgy, and sometimes the forms are in a language you cannot read.

The sting for a visitor is the rain. The same engineer noted fifteen to twenty rainy days a month in the rainy season, which turns outdoor calisthenics into a grim hobby. Japan's answer is the municipal centre and a very good umbrella.

There is a cultural lesson in the Japanese approach to cost. The Tokyo engineer who built from about 55 kilograms to 68 did it with free calisthenics in parks and a cheap gym, and he fuelled it with beef bowls at around 400 yen and a 110 yen shaker from a discount store. That is the other side of the temple: a surprising amount of the system is designed to be affordable, if you know where to look and which rules apply to you. The municipal centre, under 3,000 yen, was a better deal than the 11,000 yen commercial gym down the road, but only for those who live in that municipality. In Japan, as ever, the rule is the product.

## The Verdict

It depends what you want from a gym, and I will not pretend otherwise. If you value order, cleanliness and a sense that everyone has agreed to behave, Japan wins, and its municipal centres are among the best-value fitness options in the world, if you qualify. If you value flexibility, price variety and a climate that lets you wander between pool, track and ring without a second thought, Thailand wins.

For the relocating executive, the practical difference is friction. Thailand has low friction and high variance: you can overpay wildly or underpay absurdly, and the difference is mostly about knowing a person who knows a place. Japan has higher friction and lower variance: the rules are posted, the cost is predictable, and the contract, if you sign one blind, will last a year.

My vote goes to Thailand for the sheer joy of 40 baht and an Olympic pool, with the caveat that you must be willing to hunt. Japan gets my respect, and my shoes, but not my wallet.

## Conclusion

Japan builds a temple and asks you to behave. Thailand builds a market and asks you to bargain. Neither will make you fit without effort, but each will teach you something about the country that built it.

Take your passport to Bangkok, your indoor shoes to Tokyo, and your sense of humour to both. The weights are the same. The etiquette is the workout.
