# One Country Queues Like It's a Suggestion. The Other Treats Queue-Jumping as a Moral Failing.

🇱🇰 Sri Lanka 🇦🇺 Australia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Public behaviour is where a culture's private values get tested in front of strangers, and nothing exposes that faster than a queue, a loud phone call, or someone standing slightly too close to you on public transport. Sri Lanka and Australia have arrived at genuinely different settlements on all three, and the gap catches out newcomers from both directions.

Sri Lanka runs warm, loud, and close — personal space is a flexible, negotiable concept, queues at a crowded bus stop are more of a shared understanding than an enforced order, and a conversation at volume is simply a conversation, not a disturbance. Australia runs considerably cooler and more orderly in public — the queue is sacrosanct, personal space is real estate you don't casually invade, and public transport operates under an unspoken agreement that everyone will suffer their commute in respectful silence. Neither is rude. They're just operating from entirely different assumptions about what strangers owe each other in shared space.

## Do's & Don'ts: Sri Lanka

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept closer physical proximity on buses and in markets as completely normal, not an invasion | Expect a formal, single-file queue at every bus stop — crowding toward the door as it arrives is the actual local system |
| Match the warmth and volume of a friendly conversation rather than keeping a reserved, quiet distance | Mistake loud, animated talking in public for conflict; raised voices here are often just enthusiasm |
| Show visible deference to monks and elders in public space — giving up a seat or stepping aside is expected, not optional | Assume banks and government offices run as informally as bus queues; those lines are orderly and number-ticket based |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Queue in a single, visible line and wait your turn without exception — jumping it is treated as a genuine social offence | Stand closer than arm's length to a stranger; Australians maintain a noticeably wider personal-space bubble than South Asia |
| Keep your voice down on public transport; the norm is quiet, almost library-like restraint | Take a phone call on speaker on a train — it will earn you looks that Australians otherwise reserve for very serious transgressions |
| Say "sorry" reflexively, even when someone else bumps into you — it's a social lubricant, not an admission of fault | Assume Australian friendliness extends to casual queue-skipping for "just one quick thing" — it absolutely does not |

## Sri Lanka: Warm, Loud, and Comfortable in Close Quarters

The first thing that unsettles a lot of newcomers to Sri Lanka isn't the heat or the traffic, it's how physically close everyday public life runs. A crowded bus doesn't really queue in the Western sense — it clusters, a loose crowd converging toward the door as the bus arrives, with boarding determined by a mix of proximity, mild assertiveness, and an unspoken deference to anyone visibly older, pregnant, or carrying a child. Personal space, as a concept with firm boundaries, simply isn't load-bearing here the way it is in cooler, more individualistic cultures — people stand close, sit close, and think nothing of it, and a newcomer who flinches away from casual proximity will, more often than not, just confuse the person standing next to them.

Volume works the same way. Conversations in markets, on buses, between friends across a restaurant, run at a register that would read as heated argument in Australia and is, in Sri Lanka, simply enthusiasm — warmth expressed at full projection rather than held back. What does carry real formality, interestingly, is hierarchy: visible respect for monks, for elders, for anyone senior in a social or professional context, shows up constantly in small, consistent gestures — a seat given up without being asked, a slight bow of the head, a queue that suddenly becomes much more orderly the moment it's at a bank or government office, where ticketed, numbered systems replace the looser bus-stop free-for-all entirely. Context, not a single universal rule, governs how structured public behaviour needs to be.

## Australia: Cool, Quiet, and Fiercely Protective of the Queue

Australia's public behaviour norms run on an almost opposite logic, built around a kind of collective, slightly ironic egalitarianism often summarised as the "fair go" — the idea that everyone deserves an equal, undisturbed shot at their turn, their space, their quiet commute. The queue is the clearest expression of this: form a line, Australians will form it instinctively and precisely, and queue-jumping, even a small, seemingly harmless version of it, registers as a genuine breach of the social contract rather than a minor inconvenience. RMIT's own orientation guidance for international students flags this explicitly, because it trips up newcomers constantly — assuming a line is informal, the way it might be elsewhere, and discovering very quickly that it absolutely is not.

Public transport etiquette runs quiet, almost deliberately so — phone calls kept brief and at a murmur, conversations pitched low, the whole carriage operating under an unspoken agreement to let everyone suffer their commute in peace rather than make it anyone else's business. Personal space, correspondingly, runs wider than in South Asia: Australians maintain a noticeably larger comfortable distance from strangers, and standing too close, even with entirely innocent intent, reads as faintly aggressive. And then there's the reflexive "sorry" — said constantly, often for things that aren't remotely anyone's fault, functioning less as an apology than a kind of social WD-40, smoothing over the smallest frictions of shared space before they can become anything bigger.

## The Verdict

I'm giving this one to Sri Lanka, and I know that'll annoy the queue purists. Australia's system is undeniably more efficient and, in its own cool way, more considerate of strangers' time. But there's something genuinely life-affirming about a public culture that hasn't optimised the warmth out of shared space — where closeness isn't an inconvenience to be managed but simply how people exist near each other, where a loud conversation on a bus is just two people enjoying their day rather than a disturbance requiring an apology. Australia protects your personal bubble beautifully. Sri Lanka never really builds the bubble in the first place, and once you stop missing it, that's oddly freeing.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/srilanka — a returning diaspora visitor admitted feeling genuinely disoriented by how "orderly and far apart" a Colombo bank queue was compared to the bus stop outside it, within the same ten-minute walk.*

> *Reddit r/AskAnAustralian — a thread on queue-jumping included someone recounting a stranger being publicly, politely, but very firmly called out for skipping three people at a supermarket self-checkout, with the top comment simply reading "as it should be."*

> *expat.com Melbourne forum — a South Asian newcomer described their first week on Melbourne trains as "eerily quiet, like everyone was holding their breath," before gradually coming to find the silence restful rather than cold.*

## Conclusion

What these two countries really disagree about isn't noise or queues at all — it's what strangers owe each other in shared public space. Sri Lanka's answer is warmth and flexibility, trusting that proximity and volume are just how humans naturally exist together. Australia's answer is order and restraint, trusting that structure and quiet are the kindest things you can offer someone you'll never speak to. Both answers work. Just know which one you're walking into before you either stand too close or jump a queue that, I promise you, someone is silently judging you for.
