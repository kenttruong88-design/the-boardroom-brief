# Peru Will Fall in Love With You in an Evening; Japan Will Form a Committee

🇵🇪 Peru vs 🇯🇵 Japan — Dating and Social Scene

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two countries, two completely different theories of how romance should be conducted, and both of them think the other is doing it wrong. Peru treats a first meeting like a performance review you didn't know you'd signed up for — eye contact, compliments, a hand on the small of your back before you've exchanged surnames. Japan treats it like a merger between two companies, complete with due diligence, a matchmaking app that functions as an HR portal, and a three-week silence that isn't rejection, it's just Tuesday.

I have been kissed on both cheeks by a Peruvian stranger within four minutes of meeting him at a wedding, and I have waited six weeks for a Japanese man to work up the nerve to suggest a second coffee, by which point I had genuinely forgotten his surname and had to check the group chat. Neither experience was wrong. Both were, in their own cultures, completely normal. That is the horror and the delight of dating abroad: nobody warns you that "normal" is a local currency, and yours doesn't convert.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇵🇪 Peru**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect physical warmth immediately — a cheek kiss on introduction is standard, not a signal | Mistake affection for exclusivity; it's cultural default, not a proposal |
| Bring your A-game conversationally — wit and charm are currency | Show up late without acknowledging it; punctuality is loose but disrespect isn't |
| Meet the family early if things get serious — it's expected, not intense | Assume "we should hang out sometime" is casual — it's often a real invitation |

**🇯🇵 Japan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the apps — konkatsu culture means even serious relationships often start digitally | Push for a label in week two; commitment is a slow, deliberate ascent |
| Respect the silence — a slow reply isn't disinterest, it's consideration | Overshare personal history on date one; build trust incrementally |
| Let group outings (goukon) do some of the early filtering for you | Expect big public declarations; affection here is shown, rarely announced |

## Peru: Loud, Warm, and Not Remotely Sorry About It

Lima does not do subtlety, and neither does Peruvian dating. You meet someone at a party and within the hour you know their star sign, their mother's opinion of their last relationship, and whether they think you're attractive — because they've told you, out loud, unprompted. This is not aggression. This is Tuesday. Peruvians flirt the way they cook: generously, with too many ingredients, and without apologising for the richness of it.

The piropo — the street compliment, the little verbal flourish tossed your way by a stranger — has softened considerably in the last decade under justified pressure from a generation that's tired of being catcalled on the way to buy bread, and good riddance to the worst of it. But the underlying instinct survives in more polite form: Peruvians compliment out loud, often, and mean it. Silence is not romantic here. Silence is suspicious. If a Peruvian likes you, you will know, because they will have told several mutual friends, your hairdresser, and possibly your mother by the following Wednesday.

Family enters the picture fast. Bring someone home in Lima or Arequipa and you are not meeting the parents so much as auditioning for a permanent supporting role in a very warm, very loud ensemble drama. Sunday lunch is not optional once you've been introduced. Neither is having an opinion about the food, offered enthusiastically, regardless of whether you can actually finish the second helping of causa someone's tía has already plated for you.

Where it gets genuinely tricky for outsiders is reading intent. A Peruvian saying "vamos a salir" — let's go out — with real warmth in their voice might mean a date, might mean a friendly hang, might mean an invitation extended in the moment and only half-meant. The generosity of the gesture and the seriousness of the gesture are not always the same size, and that gap catches a lot of foreigners who take every warm word as a green light. Ask a Peruvian friend to translate the subtext. They will, gleefully, and probably add commentary you didn't ask for.

## Japan: A Courtship Conducted at the Pace of Continental Drift

If Peru is a fireworks display, Japan is a kaiseki dinner — a dozen small, deliberate courses, each one arriving only when the last has been properly appreciated, and God help you if you try to rush the chef. Dating in Japan, especially in Tokyo, has industrialised itself: konkatsu (marriage-hunting) events, matchmaking apps with income brackets built into the profile fields, agencies that will vet your prospective partner's family background before the first coffee. Romance, viewed from outside, looks less like chemistry and more like supply chain management.

That's not a criticism, incidentally — it's a response to genuinely brutal working hours and a culture that doesn't organically produce the meet-cute opportunities other countries take for granted. When you're doing twelve-hour days and your commute eats another two, you don't stumble into love at a bar. You schedule it, the way you'd schedule a dentist appointment, and treat it with roughly the same nervous formality.

The pacing will drive impatient foreigners insane. A good first date in Tokyo can end with a polite bow, a "let's do this again," and then absolutely nothing for eleven days, not because anyone's changed their mind but because that's simply the metabolic rate of the thing. LINE messages get shorter, not longer, as affection grows in some circles — brevity read as comfort rather than boredom, which is the exact opposite of how most Western daters are trained to read texting cadence. Public displays of affection remain rare even among established couples; holding hands on the Yamanote line still turns heads in a way that would baffle a Peruvian couple mid-embrace outside a bar in Miraflores.

Confession culture — kokuhaku, the formal "please go out with me" — still exists as a genuine relationship checkpoint, a verbal contract that moves things from "seeing each other" to "dating," explicitly, out loud, on a specific day. Foreigners who skip it and just assume exclusivity because they've been on four dates will find themselves in an ambiguous limbo their Japanese partner assumed was already resolved via unspoken understanding, or not resolved at all. Ask. Directly. It will feel unromantic. Do it anyway.

## The Verdict

Peru wins on joy per hour — nowhere will you feel more instantly, extravagantly desired, and nowhere will that desire evaporate faster if you weren't actually looking for a fling. Japan wins on staying power — the deliberateness that makes early dating feel glacial is the same quality that makes a Japanese partner extraordinarily reliable once the kokuhaku's been said and the thing is real. If you want a passionate few months, book a flight to Lima. If you want someone who will still be quietly, undramatically devoted to you in fifteen years, get comfortable with silence and learn to love Tokyo's patience. Just don't try to import one country's tempo into the other's dating scene. Peru will find you cold. Japan will find you terrifying.

## Conclusion

Neither country is going to adjust its courtship rituals for your comfort, and honestly, why should they — this isn't a customer service industry, it's several thousand years of accumulated cultural instinct about how humans should approach each other. Peru will teach you to say what you feel while you're feeling it, loudly, without waiting for permission. Japan will teach you that restraint isn't the absence of feeling, it's feeling under enormous, deliberate control. Bring the wrong instincts to either country and you'll misread a genuine connection as either overwhelming or nonexistent. Bring the right ones, and you'll understand why people who've dated in both places tend to go a little quiet when you ask them which one they preferred. They're still deciding. So am I.
