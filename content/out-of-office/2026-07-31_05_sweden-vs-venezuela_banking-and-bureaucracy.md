# Sweden Won't Let You Exist Without A Number. Venezuela Won't Let Its Currency Exist For More Than A Year.

🇸🇪 Sweden + 🇻🇪 Venezuela

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Money and bureaucracy reveal a country's soul faster than any tourist board would like to admit. Sweden has built a system so orderly it borders on theological — you do not truly exist in Swedish society until you've been issued a personnummer, at which point every door in the country magically unlocks. Venezuela, by contrast, has spent the last decade running an economics experiment so volatile that locals have developed the financial reflexes of day traders just to buy groceries.

I've waited eleven weeks for a Swedish personnummer while unable to open a bank account, sign a phone contract, or register at a clinic — eleven weeks of existing, technically, as a ghost. And I've watched a Caracas shopkeeper update prices three times in a single afternoon because the bolívar had shifted again before lunch. One system is maddeningly slow. The other is maddeningly fast. Both will leave you standing in a queue wondering what you did to deserve this.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇸🇪 Sweden**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for your personnummer the moment you arrive — the clock matters | Expect to open a bank account without one; it's essentially impossible |
| Use BankID once you have it — it unlocks almost everything digitally | Show up to Skatteverket without a booked appointment |
| Keep every document; Swedish bureaucracy loves paper trails despite being digital-first | Assume cash works everywhere — Sweden is nearly cashless now |
| Be patient; processing times are long but predictable | Chase up constantly; it won't speed up and will just frustrate you |

**🇻🇪 Venezuela**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry US dollars in small bills for daily transactions | Rely on the official exchange rate — use the parallel/black market rate locals reference |
| Check prices daily; they change fast, sometimes hourly | Hold bolívares longer than necessary — spend or convert quickly |
| Use mobile payment apps locals recommend, not foreign cards | Expect ATMs to reliably dispense useful amounts of local currency |
| Ask locals which rate ("tasa") they're using before any deal | Assume price tags in stores reflect what you'll actually pay at checkout |

## Sweden: Bureaucracy As A Rite Of Passage

The personnummer is Sweden's founding myth made administrative: a ten-digit number that, once granted, transforms you from a shadow-person into a fully participating citizen who can open a bank account, get a library card, and be taken seriously by literally anyone. Before you have it, you exist in a frustrating limbo where landlords, banks, and phone companies all treat you with polite, immovable suspicion. Getting it requires registering with Skatteverket, the tax agency, and the wait — depending on your visa category and how busy the office is — can stretch anywhere from a few weeks to several agonising months.

What makes this bearable, eventually, is that Swedish bureaucracy, while slow, is scrupulously fair and almost entirely free of the under-the-table shortcuts you'd find elsewhere. Nobody's expediting your paperwork for a backhander, because that concept barely computes here. Once you're in the system, BankID — a digital identity app tied to your personnummer — becomes the skeleton key to nearly everything: banking, taxes, even signing rental contracts. It's genuinely impressive infrastructure. It's just also completely useless to you for the excruciating stretch before you have it.

The other adjustment is Sweden's near-total abandonment of cash. Buskers take card payments. Church donation boxes take card payments. This is fantastic once you're banked and miserable if you're still waiting on that personnummer, because you'll be stuck relying on whatever payment method you brought from home, often at unfavourable exchange rates, for months.

## Venezuela: Finance As A Full-Contact Sport

Venezuela's economy has been through hyperinflation so severe that the country has redenominated its currency multiple times in recent years, lopping zeros off the bolívar in an attempt to make the numbers manageable again. The practical result for anyone living there, local or expat, is a daily financial life that requires constant vigilance: prices change so fast that businesses often price in US dollars and convert at the moment of sale, using whatever the informal "parallel" exchange rate is that day — a rate that has almost nothing to do with the official government rate and everything to do with what's actually happening on the street.

Cash — specifically US dollars — has become the de facto second currency for anything beyond the smallest purchases, and Venezuelans have gotten extremely good at moving fluidly between bolívares and dollars depending on the transaction. Locals track the parallel rate the way other people check the weather: constantly, reflexively, out of sheer necessity. Turning up assuming your home bank card will work smoothly, or that the official exchange rate bears any relation to reality, is a fast way to overpay for everything.

What's remarkable, and what forums consistently underline, is how normalised this chaos has become. Venezuelans aren't panicking daily over hyperinflation — they've built an entire informal financial literacy around surviving it, one that outpaces what most people in stable economies ever need to learn. It's resourcefulness born of necessity, and it's honestly humbling to watch up close.

## The Verdict

Sweden's bureaucracy is slow, fair, and eventually rewarding — a marathon that ends in a genuinely functional digital-first system. Venezuela's financial reality is fast, volatile, and relentlessly demanding — a sprint that never actually ends. If I had to choose a headache, I'd take Sweden's patient queueing over Venezuela's hourly price recalculations, if only because eleven weeks of limbo eventually resolves into something that works beautifully. Venezuela's system, by contrast, asks you to keep sprinting indefinitely, and that's a much harder ask for anyone, tourist or local.

## Conclusion

Bureaucracy and currency are supposed to be the boring parts of moving somewhere new, right up until they're the only thing standing between you and functioning like a normal human being. Sweden makes you wait, then rewards the wait with a system that just works. Venezuela makes you adapt constantly, with no finish line, and calls it Tuesday. Bring patience for one and a calculator for the other — and in both cases, bring far more paperwork, or far more dollars, than you think you'll need.
