# Hong Kong Hid Its Parks in the Hills, and the Philippines Hid Them Behind a Shopping Mall

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Hong Kong is the most vertical city I have ever lived in, and the cruelest joke it plays is that it is also quietly one of the greenest. Take a ten-minute bus ride from a glass tower and you are on a ridge trail with a view that would cost a billion dollars if anyone were allowed to build on it. The government, in a rare burst of sanity, mostly forbade it. The trails are signposted, the benches are placed with municipal tenderness, and every hiker looks like they have a podcast ready.

The Philippines has the opposite problem: the nature is spectacular and the parks are an afterthought. Manila's idea of green space is whatever the developers have left over between towers, plus a few famous patches kept alive by sheer civic stubbornness. And yet the two places are bound together by one extraordinary fact. On Sundays, a large share of Hong Kong's open public space is quietly annexed by Filipinos, and it tells you everything about who a city's parks are really for.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Philippines**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go early, before the heat turns a park into a sauna | Plan a midday picnic and expect shade |
| Treat the big city parks as social venues, with food and company | Expect a silent contemplative stroll |
| Use the newer privately managed districts for tidy walking routes | Assume every green patch is open to the public |
| Bring water and insect repellent | Count on a park having a working fountain |

**Hong Kong**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Hike on weekday mornings, when trails are empty | Tackle a popular trail on a summer Sunday afternoon |
| Carry water in warm months and check the weather before you go | Treat a hill walk as a stroll in the park |
| Respect the Sunday gatherings in Central and Victoria Park | Complain about the noise like a tourist |
| Pack your rubbish out | Leave it for a bin that is not coming |

## The Philippines

Let me be fair to the Philippines before I am rude. The country has some of the most dramatic natural landscape in Asia, and Filipinos use public space with a warmth most Western cities have forgotten how to produce. A good park there is not an empty lawn. It is a crowd with a snack stall, a guitar, three generations of one family and an enormous speaker. If you expect a hush, you have misunderstood the assignment.

The trouble is supply. In the capital region, land is expensive and contested, and a park without a business case struggles to survive next to a tower with one. The result is a culture in which the mall quietly became the public square. Air conditioning, security guards, clean toilets and a place to sit: the mall has all of it, and it is open when it is too hot or too wet to be outside. Expats from cooler countries tend to mourn the lack of a proper green space for about three weeks, then discover the mall rooftop garden and give up the fight.

The better news is the newer wave of planned districts, where tree-lined walking routes and landscaped plazas are part of the sales brochure. They are tidy and walkable. They are also, let us say it plainly, a shade too curated for anyone who wants a park to feel like a place instead of a product.

Outside Manila, everything improves. Beaches, volcano trails and rice terraces are only a short flight away, and if you can get out of the capital on weekends, the green-space argument largely disappears. What remains is a pattern worth knowing before you move: Philippine park culture is social, hot, loud and unevenly distributed, and the country's best nature is something you travel to, not something you walk to after work.

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong is the reverse. The city is notorious for its density, but a large proportion of the territory is protected country park, and the network of trails is so good that a Friday-night hiking group is a perfectly respectable social event. Nobody in Hong Kong needs a garden because the hills are the garden. You can finish a meeting in Central and be on a trail within the hour, which is a work-life arrangement Singapore would kill for.

The Hong Kong catch is that the green is on the edges, not the middle. Inside the dense urban core, parks are small, hard-surfaced and heavily rule-bound. Signs forbid most of the things people actually want to do. Ballgames, picnics and general loitering are all subject to regulation, and the lawns, when they exist, tend to look at you with suspicion.

Then there are Sundays. According to reporting from Rappler, Filipinos began arriving in Hong Kong in the 1970s, and on their day off they convert streets, overpasses and plazas in Central into pop-up living rooms, marking out territory with cardboard sheets and umbrellas. An Asser Institute piece from 2019 explains why: foreign domestic workers make up roughly ten percent of the city's workforce, Sunday is their only mandatory day off, and many live in employers' homes under a rule that leaves them little private space. Parks and walkways are the only living room they get.

It is the single most revealing detail about Hong Kong's relationship with open space. A city that is willing to preserve mountains is much less willing to preserve a place where its own domestic workforce can sit down. The country parks are for people with the time to hike them. The pavements are for everyone else.

## The Verdict

If you want a place to be alone with a view, Hong Kong wins by a landslide, and I resent how much I enjoyed it. A city that gives you a mountain trail and a ferry ride home for the price of a coffee has solved a problem most cities do not even admit to having. The hiking is world class, the signposting is faultless, and the whole thing feels like a promise that density and nature can coexist.

But the Philippines wins the argument about what a public space is for. A Philippine plaza on a Saturday evening is a crowd that has not been told to move along. Hong Kong's urban parks, by contrast, feel designed to be looked at, with an eye on whoever might be using them wrongly. The Filipino domestic workers who turn Central into a Sunday village know the difference better than any planner.

So: Hong Kong for the hike, the Philippines for the human beings. If forced to choose, I would take the hills, because I am a coward who likes a view. But I will not pretend the winner is the more generous city.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Rappler (Hong Kong feature) — an activist with Filipino community groups says there are now roughly five thousand male domestic workers in the city, compared with fewer than a thousand when he arrived in 1991, and counts around two hundred Filipino organisations that use these Sunday gatherings as their meeting place.*

> *Rappler (Hong Kong feature) — a church worker describes the sting of being called an "eyesore" for sitting on the pavement, and not by a stranger, but by a fellow Filipino.*

> *Asser Institute, Global City Project (2019) — the live-in rule means domestic workers must sleep at their employers' homes, so on Sundays the parks and malls become the only space many of them can use freely.*

## Conclusion

Here is what both places teach you. Green space is never really about the grass. It is about who gets to sit down without being asked to leave, and a city shows its character in the answer. Hong Kong gave the world a country park system that deserves applause, and gave its domestic workers an overpass and a sheet of cardboard.

If you are relocating, pack good hiking shoes for the first and a strong sense of humour for the second. Then, on a Sunday, walk through Central, keep your complaints about the noise to yourself, and watch a city's priorities lay themselves out on the pavement. It is the best free education in urban planning you will ever get, and no one will charge you admission.
