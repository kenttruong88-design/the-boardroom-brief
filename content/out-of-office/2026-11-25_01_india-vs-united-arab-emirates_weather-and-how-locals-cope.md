# One Country Prays for Rain, the Other Air-Conditions the Pavement

🇮🇳 India vs 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every summer, two enormous populations make the same discovery: the sun is not a feature, it is a hostile act. India meets it with fatalism, a ceiling fan the size of a helicopter blade, and a nation-wide fixation on when the monsoon will finally break. The United Arab Emirates meets it with engineering, which is to say it has decided that if outdoors is unbearable, outdoors can be cancelled.

I have lived through both, and I will say this plainly: nobody who describes either place as 'exotic' has stood at a bus stop in June. Heat is not scenery. It is a scheduling system, a dress code, a mood and a medical event, and the way a country copes with it tells you more about its character than any tourist board brochure. India copes with community. The UAE copes with a thermostat.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇮🇳 India ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry water and drink nimbu pani, lassi or coconut water when the heat bites | Rely on a cold fizzy drink to rescue you from heatstroke |
| Plan errands for early morning or after sunset | Cross a city at 2pm in May because the map says twenty minutes |
| Wear loose cotton and cover your head in direct sun | Walk around in synthetic fabric and act surprised |
| Learn the monsoon calendar and carry an umbrella from June | Assume the rain stops the heat; it just adds humidity |

| 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry a thin layer for the arctic indoors | Dress for the street and shiver through every meeting |
| Move your life to evenings between roughly June and September | Plan a long walk, a run or a barbecue at midday |
| Drink water constantly and replace electrolytes after any real exertion | Assume you are fine because the car had air conditioning |
| Give yourself weeks to acclimatise before anything strenuous | Sign up for a half marathon in your first August |

## 🇮🇳 India

India does heat as a participatory sport. The pre-monsoon months are a slow-roasting spectacle in which the entire country discusses the temperature with the intensity other nations reserve for football, and nobody is ever wrong, because it is always hotter than last year. The cultural response is not to hide from the heat but to build a whole economy around surviving it together. Street vendors press sugarcane juice, the neighbour hands you buttermilk without being asked, and the afternoon becomes a negotiated period of horizontal rest that employers, mostly, are wise enough to respect.

What I admire is the sheer improvisation. Wet cloths on the windows, khus mats, water kept in clay pots that cool by evaporation, shutters closed at noon and flung open at dusk. The ceiling fan is not a convenience, it is the national instrument, and its rotation at three speeds is a more reliable measure of the season than the calendar. Air conditioning exists, obviously, and the cities have embraced it with a zeal that will make the electricity grid weep, but a huge proportion of daily life is still lived in the open, in the heat, with a flask of something cold.

Then the monsoon arrives, and India briefly becomes a different country. The first rain is a public event. People stand outside in it. The smell of wet earth is practically a poem, and the pressure that has built for weeks releases in a single downpour. Of course, the next day the streets flood, the trains stop and the humidity makes the preceding heat feel like a dry sauna in a spa, but the emotional arc is magnificent. India's relationship with weather is a long, dramatic marriage with excellent make-up scenes. You suffer, but you suffer in company, and there is always someone with a story about how much worse 1998 was.

India also teaches a useful humility about timing. Offices in the hottest cities shuffle hours, schools close for the worst weeks, and the pre-dawn walk in a park is a social institution complete with gossip, stretching and a man selling fresh juice. Nobody in India imagines they can defeat summer. They negotiate with it, daily, using umbrellas against the sun, kerchiefs, and the sacred afternoon nap. The result is a population that is startlingly unbothered by temperatures that would send a Northern European to hospital, and a street culture that never fully closes, only changes shift. It is an extraordinary piece of collective adaptation, and nobody gets a medal for it.

## 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

The UAE took a different look at the same problem and decided to defeat it. From June to September the country effectively moves indoors, and what you get is a civilisation of malls, metro stations, cooled walkways and cars with air conditioning that could preserve meat. Temperatures can run into the mid-forties, with the sea warm enough to feel like soup, and the sensible adult response is to schedule your entire existence around the sun. Early risers do their exercise at dawn, then retreat. One Dubai expat told Khaleej Times he now wakes around six, leaves for work by seven and trains early, because the afternoon is simply not available.

The trouble is the contrast. Forum members who have lived through a few summers all say the same thing: the heat itself is manageable, the building is the problem. A member from Brisbane described frequent colds and sinus trouble from bouncing between furnace and fridge, and another warned newcomers that glasses fog the second you step outdoors and that indoor air conditioning is often strong enough to demand an extra layer. Doctors quoted in the local press are blunt: stay indoors at peak heat, drink more than you think you need, replace electrolytes and give your body days to adjust before doing anything strenuous.

The upside of this cooled-down civilisation is that winter is glorious. From roughly November the evenings turn gentle, the beaches and parks fill up, and everyone who vanished in August reappears with a tan they did not earn. The social calendar migrates outdoors for a few months in a way that India's does all year round, and one resident put it neatly: summer evenings are for the sofa, winter evenings are for friends and family. It is a very efficient city. It is also slightly eerie to walk through a perfectly cold corridor connecting two perfectly cold buildings and realise you have not breathed actual outdoor air for three days.

Even the medical advice in the Emirates reads like a military briefing. A doctor at a hospital in Al Ain told the local press to stay indoors at peak heat, avoid direct noon sun, keep drinking and replace electrolytes, and said the body needs at least a few days to adjust before anything strenuous. Heat exhaustion, he added, can take a day or two to recover from, and the first aid is a cool room, raised legs, a misting of cool water and fans. It is sensible, repeatable and slightly chilling, because it assumes you will ignore it first. Which, as an expat in your first August, you will.

## The Verdict

India wins on humanity and the UAE wins on logistics, and I refuse to pretend those are equal prizes. If you need a meeting to start on time, a pavement you can survive and a hospital that treats heat exhaustion as an ordinary Tuesday, the UAE is built for you. It knows precisely what the sun does and has a plan for every minute of it.

But a plan is not a culture. India's heat is shared, shouted about and survived collectively, with a street vendor on every corner and a neighbour who will push a glass of something cold into your hand. The UAE's heat is endured privately, behind glass, with a reservation. I know which I would rather be stuck in during a power cut, and I know which I would rather have in July. They are not the same place.

My ruling, then, is conditional. For a working month in August, the UAE wins, because comfort is a legitimate human need and air conditioning is a civilised triumph. For a lifetime, India, because a country that stands in the first rain with its face up has something no thermostat can provide. Just bring a hat in both.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com forum member Ruth, in her second Dubai summer — Don't stand in full sun for more than a few minutes, wear a hat or use an umbrella, and dress in thin, loose natural fabrics; your glasses will fog the moment you leave the air conditioning, and indoors can be cold enough to need a layer.*

> *Expat.com forum member emsyk7, from Brisbane — The biggest problem is living in air conditioning: going from hot outside to freezing inside gave me frequent colds and sinus and chest infections, and it takes months for the body to adjust.*

> *Khaleej Times, expat Firas Hamdan — I now wake around 6am rather than after 7, leave for work about 7 and spend the early hours at the gym, because summer in some parts of the country reaches 50C.*

## Conclusion

Here is what I took from a lifetime of being slowly cooked in two hemispheres. Both countries have understood the same truth, which is that the sun is boss and your timetable is merely a suggestion. India accepts this with a shrug and a glass of buttermilk. The UAE accepts it with a cooling system the size of a small town.

Neither is wrong, and anyone telling you one is 'better' has either never been hot or never been cold in a mall at midnight. Pack a hat for India, pack a cardigan for the UAE, and respect the middle hours of the day in both. The planet is not going to cool down to suit your meeting schedule.
