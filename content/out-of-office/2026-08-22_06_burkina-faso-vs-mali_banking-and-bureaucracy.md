# The Stamp Is More Powerful Than the Signature

🇧🇫 Burkina Faso and 🇲🇱 Mali
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Both countries share a currency, the CFA franc, a colonial hangover pegged to the euro that deserves its own furious article someday, and both share a bureaucratic culture where the physical rubber stamp still outranks any signature, any digital record, and frankly any amount of common sense you'd like to bring to the conversation. If you've arrived expecting the brisk efficiency of a Nordic tax office, you have profoundly misjudged the assignment, and I say that with genuine admiration for how thoroughly analogue this system remains in an era obsessed with digitising everything into oblivion.

Western coverage of both nations fixates almost exclusively on security instability, which tells you nothing about the actual lived experience of trying to open a bank account in Ouagadougou or renew a residency card in Bamako. So let's talk about the stamps, the queues, and the particular species of patience both countries will teach you whether you like it or not.

## Do's & Don'ts: Burkina Faso

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring more photocopies of every document than you think you'll possibly need | Assume one copy of anything is ever sufficient for any office |
| Build a personal relationship with a specific bank teller over repeat visits | Expect the same efficient service from whoever happens to be at the counter that day |
| Budget entire mornings, not hours, for any single administrative task | Schedule back-to-back appointments across different offices in one day |
| Carry cash for "unofficial" processing fees that are widely, quietly expected | Refuse to acknowledge informal fees exist; navigating around them takes local guidance |

## Do's & Don'ts: Mali

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comfortable with the concept of "revenez demain" — come back tomorrow — as a genuine answer | Push back aggressively when told to return the next day; it rarely speeds things up |
| Use a trusted local fixer or facilitator for residency paperwork | Attempt complex bureaucratic processes entirely solo as a newcomer |
| Keep every stamped document indefinitely — you will be asked for it again, years later | Discard "old" paperwork assuming it's been superseded by newer documents |
| Dress neatly and formally for any bank or government office visit | Show up in casual travel clothes expecting to be taken seriously |

## Burkina Faso: The Photocopy Economy

Ouagadougou runs on an economy of paper that would make a German civil servant weep with either horror or nostalgia, I've never been entirely sure which. Opening a bank account at any of the regional banks — Ecobank, Coris Bank, Banque Atlantique — requires an assembly of documents so extensive that photocopy shops cluster directly outside every bank branch, a business model built entirely on bureaucratic inevitability. Bring your passport, your residency documentation, a letter of reference, proof of address, and then bring two extra copies of everything, because whichever clerk you eventually see will invariably need one more copy of something you assumed you'd already provided.

The stamp — the actual physical rubber stamp, inked and pressed by hand — remains the true currency of legitimacy here. A document without the correct stamp, regardless of how many signatures adorn it, is functionally worthless, and the correct stamp is sometimes only available from one specific office, staffed by one specific person, who may or may not be present on any given day. This isn't inefficiency for its own sake; it's a system built over decades where the stamp represents a level of institutional accountability signatures alone have never quite earned in the local context, given how often paperwork has been forged, lost, or quietly "misplaced" for a fee elsewhere in the region.

What foreigners get most wrong is treating a single visit to any office as sufficient. The functional unit of Burkinabé bureaucracy isn't the transaction, it's the relationship — visit the same branch enough times, be polite and patient enough, and you'll eventually be recognised, remembered, and processed considerably faster than any stranger walking in cold. Impatience here doesn't speed anything up; it simply marks you as someone worth deprioritising.

## Mali: Revenez Demain as a Way of Life

Bamako's bureaucratic rhythm centres on a phrase every long-term resident learns painfully early: "revenez demain," come back tomorrow. It isn't necessarily a brush-off, though sometimes it absolutely is one; more often it reflects a genuinely under-resourced administrative system where the specific person, stamp, or file you need simply isn't available today, and pretending otherwise wastes everyone's time. Malians have made a kind of peace with this rhythm that visitors initially find maddening and eventually, if they're paying attention, find rather sensible — why manufacture false urgency around a system that was never built for same-day resolution?

Banking in Mali carries the added complexity of a financial sector recovering from years of instability, meaning documentation requirements for opening accounts or transferring money can shift with little warning, and having a trusted local fixer — someone who genuinely knows which office, which day, which specific clerk to approach — isn't a luxury, it's close to a functional necessity for anyone attempting serious paperwork without local family already in place.

The most important lesson Mali teaches, and one I wish more Western institutions would absorb, is the permanence of paper. A stamped document from three years ago isn't obsolete; it's frequently the exact proof required to resolve a dispute or confirm an identity when a newer digital record has failed, been hacked, or simply gone missing in a system less trusting of servers than of ink. Malians who've lived through periods of real institutional instability have learned, correctly, that the physical document in your own hands outlasts any database somebody else controls.

## The Verdict

Burkina Faso wins on sheer photocopy-shop efficiency — an entire cottage industry has grown up specifically to service the bureaucracy's paper appetite, and once you're plugged into it, the system actually moves with a kind of rhythm. Mali wins on philosophical honesty — "revenez demain" at least tells you the truth about the timeline, rather than pretending a same-day resolution was ever on the table.

Neither country will reward impatience, and both will quietly outlast anyone who tries to rush them. Bring copies, bring cash, bring several unscheduled mornings, and above all bring the humility to accept that the stamp, not your urgency, is the one actually running this show.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a Ouagadougou-based aid worker paraphrased: I brought what I thought was every document required and was sent to a photocopy shop across the street four separate times in one morning before the file was accepted.*

> *expat.com forum — a Bamako-based consultant noted that "revenez demain" became such a running joke among expats that they started keeping a tally, and the record holder waited eleven consecutive "tomorrows" for one residency stamp.*

> *Internations West Africa forum — a longtime resident paraphrased: never throw away an old stamped document in either country, a three-year-old paper proved essential when a bank's own digital records mysteriously failed to reflect an account that very much still existed.*

## Conclusion

Burkina Faso and Mali will not be rushed, digitised, or streamlined for your convenience, and there's something almost bracing about a bureaucracy this honestly analogue in a world drowning in half-working apps and forgotten passwords. Bring your patience, bring your photocopies, and accept the stamp as the final word. Complain about the wait if you must, quietly, to yourself, because nobody behind that counter is remotely interested in your urgency, and they have, by every measure, already outwaited people far more important than you.
