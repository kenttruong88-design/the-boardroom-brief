# Italy Will Give You Free Healthcare Eventually. Pakistan Will Give You Excellent Healthcare Immediately, Assuming You Can Pay For It Yourself.

🇵🇰 Pakistan 🤝 🇮🇹 Italy
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here's the trade nobody explains clearly before you move: Pakistan's private hospitals, in the cities expats actually live in, are frequently excellent — modern, well-staffed, with specialists trained abroad — and almost entirely cash-and-insurance-dependent, because there is effectively no public safety net built for foreign residents to fall back on. ExpatExchange's own crowdsourced survey of fifteen long-term expats on healthcare in Pakistan is remarkably consistent on this point: good care exists, but you are paying for every bit of it, out of pocket or through private insurance, with no SSN-style public backstop waiting underneath you.

Italy runs the inverse arrangement. The Servizio Sanitario Nazionale (SSN) is genuinely universal in principle, and foreign residents who register correctly get access to it — but "access" and "speed" are different promises entirely. ItalyHandbook's own reporting on SSN waiting times is blunt about the gap between public-system availability and public-system punctuality, and the entire expat conversation around Italian healthcare essentially revolves around when to use the free public system and when to simply pay for the private alternative to skip the queue. Pakistan makes you pay for speed from day one. Italy gives you the option to pay for speed, but only once you understand that the free version comes with a wait.

## Pakistan, Deep Dive

The clearest signal from ExpatExchange's own crowdsourced survey — fifteen expats specifically asked about health insurance and healthcare experience in Pakistan — is how consistent the message is across very different individual circumstances: private care, where it's available in major cities, is genuinely good, often staffed by specialists trained in the UK, US, or Gulf states, with modern equipment and facilities that compare favourably to much of the region. The catch, repeated across nearly every account, is that none of this comes with a public safety net underneath it for foreign residents. There's no SSN-equivalent waiting to catch you if your insurance lapses or your savings run short.

Pacific Prime's own country-specific guide, aimed at relocating professionals rather than casual travellers, frames this plainly as a market: health insurance isn't a nice-to-have, it's close to the entire infrastructure, and the specific plan you choose — its network, its cap, its evacuation coverage — matters enormously more here than it would in a country with a public backstop. ExpatExchange's direct Q&A thread on whether healthcare and insurance are "expensive" in Pakistan gets a fairly nuanced answer: costs are moderate by Western standards for most routine private care, but can escalate quickly for anything serious, and without insurance, there's no ceiling.

The practical upshot for an incoming expat is to treat healthcare planning as a day-one administrative task, not a "figure it out once I'm there" afterthought — research specific hospitals in your actual city (quality is genuinely uneven between facilities and between major cities versus smaller ones), lock in appropriate insurance before any gap in coverage, and lean on expat community recommendations, which carry real, specific, hard-won weight in a system without a standardised public alternative to fall back on. Evacuation and medical-repatriation coverage specifically is worth scrutinising line by line rather than assuming it's bundled in by default — several long-term residents report discovering, too late, that an otherwise solid policy didn't actually cover the exact scenario that ended up mattering most.

## Italy, Deep Dive

Italy's SSN is a real, meaningful entitlement — AllianzCare's own overview for foreign residents is clear that once you're properly registered, you get access to a genuinely public, largely free system covering GP visits, hospital care, and much of specialist treatment. The complication, which ItalyHandbook's dedicated reporting on SSN waiting times lays out in detail, is that "access" doesn't mean "promptly." Non-urgent specialist appointments and certain diagnostic procedures can carry waits long enough that a meaningful parallel private-insurance market exists specifically to let people — Italians and expats alike — pay to skip the public queue when timing actually matters.

What's less obvious from outside is how much this varies by city, which ExpatExchange's own regional Q&A threads — asking almost identically-phrased questions for Rome, Naples, and Venice separately — implicitly confirm by existing at all: the "will I have access to public healthcare as a foreigner" question doesn't have one national answer, it has a Rome answer, a Naples answer, and a Venice answer, each with its own registration quirks and local-office friction. ExpatsLivingInRome's ongoing newsletter coverage of SSN enrolment, including pandemic-era access issues, reinforces that the capital's own experience has had real, documented rough patches that didn't necessarily apply elsewhere in the country.

The sensible expat approach, which most long-term residents land on after their first year, is dual-track: register for and use the SSN for routine, non-urgent care, where the cost savings are real and the quality is generally solid, while maintaining supplementary private insurance for anything time-sensitive — a specialist consultation, an elective procedure, anything where a multi-week public wait genuinely isn't acceptable.

## The Verdict

Pakistan hands you excellent care immediately, with the bill attached from the very first visit and no public net beneath you if the insurance runs out. Italy hands you a genuine entitlement to free care, with a queue attached that may or may not matter depending on what you actually need and how quickly. If money is the variable you're most anxious about, Italy's free-but-slow system is the safer long-term bet. If time is the variable, and your wallet can absorb it, Pakistan's private system will see you faster, every time, for a price. Neither system is broken. They've simply decided, at a structural level, whether speed or universality gets priority — and you'll feel the consequences of that choice the first time you actually need a doctor.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *ExpatExchange's fifteen-expat survey on Pakistan healthcare — a strikingly consistent theme across very different respondents: private care quality exceeded expectations, but nobody anticipated quite how entirely the cost burden would sit with them personally.*

> *ItalyHandbook's reporting on SSN waiting times — repeatedly notes that newcomers assume "universal healthcare" means "fast healthcare," and the gap between those two ideas is where most of the frustration actually lives.*

> *ExpatsLivingInRome's newsletter coverage of SSN enrolment — documents specific, city-level friction in Rome's registration process that a newcomer researching Italy's healthcare system nationally wouldn't necessarily anticipate.*

## Conclusion

Both systems are, in their own way, honest about their trade-offs once you understand them — Pakistan never pretends the private system is anything other than pay-to-play, and Italy never pretends the SSN is anything other than free-but-queued. The expensive mistake is assuming either country's headline reputation (Pakistan's "it'll be costly," Italy's "it's universal") tells you the whole story before you've actually needed to use either system for something that mattered. Research the specific city, the specific hospital, and the specific insurance product before you need any of them in an emergency — by then, you've already lost the leverage to shop around.
