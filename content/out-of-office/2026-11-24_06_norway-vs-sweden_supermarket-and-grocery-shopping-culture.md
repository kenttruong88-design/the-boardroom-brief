# Norway Rations Your Wine and Sweden Rations Your Patience

🇳🇴 Norway vs 🇸🇪 Sweden

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Moving between Scandinavian countries sounds easy until you try to buy dinner. The two nations share a border, a taste for cinnamon and a deep suspicion of people who talk loudly in lifts, and yet they have arranged their supermarkets as if they were conducting a feud.

I have shopped for groceries in more than sixty countries, and I can tell you that the best way to understand a nation is to watch someone panic in the cheese aisle. In Norway, the panic usually starts on Saturday afternoon. In Sweden, it starts at the till, when someone realises that the wine is not here at all.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇳🇴 Norway ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Do your main shop before Sunday, because almost everything is shut | Plan a Sunday roast without checking the cupboard on Saturday |
| Join the loyalty apps like Trumf or Rema's Æ for real discounts | Pay full price at the counter and assume that's normal |
| Buy wine and spirits at Vinmonopolet during its short hours | Expect to find a bottle of red at the supermarket |
| Look at Rema, Kiwi and Coop Extra for the cheapest basket | Default to the posh chains and then complain about the bill |

| 🇸🇪 Sweden ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the big chains and shop around, since ranges differ a lot | Assume your favourite foreign brand will be on the shelf |
| Plan wine and spirits around the state shop, Systembolaget | Think strong drink is something you can pick up with the milk |
| Expect smaller pack sizes and adapt your cooking | Show up late and expect a late-night shop on every corner |
| Return bottles and cans for the deposit | Throw the pant money into the bin |

## 🇳🇴 Norway

Norway is a country that has looked at the modern supermarket and decided that it needs to be more like a church. Most shops close on Sundays and public holidays. The exceptions are tiny stores and anything in a tourist area, which is why your first Sunday in Oslo feels like an apocalypse film with better knitwear.

The chains are few and powerful. Norgesgruppen owns Kiwi, Meny, Spar, Joker and others, Coop runs Extra, Mega, Obs and Prix, and Rema 1000 and Bunnpris round things out. According to The Local, Rema, Kiwi and Coop Extra are generally the cheapest, while Meny positions itself as the posh option. The Local also notes that a handful of chains dominate and use exclusivity deals, that Norway produces only about half its own food, and that tolls on imported meat and dairy are high. That is why the bill makes you sit down.

The loyalty apps are not optional extras. Rema's Æ app gives money off fresh produce and your most-bought items, Trumf pays cashback across several chains, and Coop will sell you part-ownership for 300 kroner and send back a small dividend. It is the only country where buying a pepper can feel like joining a co-operative.

Then there is alcohol. Supermarkets can sell only weak beer, and only until 8pm on weekdays and 6pm on Saturdays. Wine and spirits go through the state-run Vinmonopolet, which closes early on Saturdays and not at all on Sundays. Life in Norway puts it kindly, advising newcomers to plan ahead for gatherings and holidays. I would put it less kindly. If you forget the wine, your dinner party is a seminar.

Bargains exist, oddly. Norwegians have long driven across the border to Sweden for cheaper food, the habit known as harryhandel. In early 2023, though, The Local reported that Swedes had started heading the other way, because Norwegian food prices were rising far more slowly than Swedish ones. That is the most Norwegian triumph imaginable: winning on groceries by accident.

## 🇸🇪 Sweden

Sweden does supermarkets the way Sweden does everything: efficiently, tidily and slightly smaller than you wanted. The shops are generally open on Sundays, so you can panic-buy lasagne sheets with dignity. That alone puts it ahead of its neighbour for anyone who works weekdays and shops by instinct.

The catch is selection. A student blogger at Study in Sweden complained that stores often lack the sizes he was used to, that some products come in only one brand, and that staples like dryer sheets are hard to find. When he asked staff, they had never heard the term. His cereal aisle back home was about twice as long. He also noted that many shops close early, and that the late-night options are rare. He still concluded Sweden was worth it, which is the sort of patient generosity I find slightly suspicious.

Wine and spirits have their own temple in the state retailer Systembolaget. I will not pretend the concept is better than the Norwegian version, only that it is less theatrical. You go there, you queue, you buy, you leave, and nobody offers you a loyalty card.

The 2023 reporting that The Local relayed shows how brutal Swedish food inflation got. Swedish food prices rose 21.6 percent over the year to February, against 8.8 percent in Norway. The paper's price check found bananas, broccoli, carrots and oranges cheaper in Norway at that moment, while a border-trade economist cautioned that shopping in Sweden was probably still cheaper overall for people living near the line. It is a snapshot from a particular winter, so do not treat it as a law of nature, but it did dent a long-held national assumption.

Swedes also have the habit of deposit returns, which means your empty bottle is a small piece of money and not rubbish. It is deeply civilised and slightly addictive, and you will soon be carrying cans home like treasure.

One more Swedish truth, which no guidebook admits. The shelves are not bare, they are just edited. Sweden stocks what Swedes buy, in the quantities Swedes buy it, and your preferences are treated as an interesting hypothesis. If you cook from a global cookbook, you will spend your first month substituting, improvising and muttering. After that you will discover that the local bread, fish and dairy are good enough to make the muttering stop, and you will quietly convert, like everyone else.

Also, the staff are not unfriendly. They are simply efficient, which in Scandinavia looks identical to indifference until you need something and they produce it from a back room within ninety seconds.

If you are arriving with children or a large appetite, factor this in early. A family shop in either country is a logistics exercise, and the winners are the people who write lists, know which chain sells what, and never trust a shop to be open when they are hungry.

## The Verdict

For ordinary weekly shopping, Sweden wins. Open on Sundays, a sensible range and a reputation for fair prices in everyday goods make it the easier country in which to feed yourself. If you are a person who forgets things, and I am, Sweden is the more forgiving country.

Norway wins on cleverness, if you are the sort of person who enjoys being told off by a retail system. The loyalty apps can genuinely trim the bill, and the cheaper chains are decent. But the combination of Sunday closures, early alcohol cutoffs and prices that make you do mental arithmetic in the dairy aisle is a lot to ask of anyone who just wants to make a risotto.

The honest ranking: Sweden for convenience, Norway for character, and neither for your wallet.

My own score is Sweden, clearly. I would rather forget the wine in a country that lets me recover on a Sunday than remember everything in a country that shuts at the first sign of a weekend. Norway is the better story, but Sweden is the better supper.

## Conclusion

The border between Norway and Sweden is only a few hundred kilometres of pine trees and passport-free shrugging, yet the shopping cultures could belong to different centuries. Norway is a rulebook with a shopping basket. Sweden is a shop with some rules in it.

My advice is practical. In Norway, do the big shop on Saturday morning, download every app, and treat Vinmonopolet like a flight departure. In Sweden, learn the chains, adapt your recipes to the shelves, and keep your bottles for the deposit.

Wherever you land, keep a tin of something in the cupboard. The apocalypse is coming, it arrives on Sunday, and in Norway it has excellent knitwear.
