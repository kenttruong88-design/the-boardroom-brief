# The Weather Will Break You Before the Bureaucracy Does

🇧🇪 Belgium 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Everyone warns you about the paperwork. The residency permits, the bank appointments that require three other appointments, the municipal official who looks at your file like it personally insulted his mother. Nobody warns you about the sky. Belgium and Switzerland, two of the most meticulously organised countries on the continent, both have a weather problem they have simply decided to be extremely calm about, in the way that only people with excellent healthcare and even better chocolate can afford to be.

Belgium's sky is a mood, not a forecast, and it has essentially one setting: concerned grey. Switzerland, meanwhile, has weather so geographically specific that entire villages can go three months without seeing the sun directly, and separately, a wind that is scientifically documented to make people furious. I have lived through both. I have owned, at various points, four separate umbrellas and one ridiculous light-therapy lamp shaped like a UFO. This is not a travel brochure. This is a debrief.

## Do's & Don'ts: Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a genuinely good raincoat in your first week, not your third month | Describe a single sunny Tuesday as "basically summer" |
| Learn to love a long lunch indoors — the café culture exists for a reason | Schedule any outdoor event without a drizzle contingency |
| Invest in a vitamin D supplement before winter, not during the breakdown | Assume your mood swings in January are personal failings |
| Treat a blue-sky weekend as a national emergency requiring a bike ride | Wait for "better weather" before doing anything, ever |

## Do's & Don'ts: Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check which side of the valley your flat sits on before signing the lease | Buy an apartment in a shadow village without asking locals about winter light |
| Keep painkillers in your bag during foehn season, not just at home | Make big decisions, arguments, or confessions on a foehn day |
| Fit winter tyres even though nobody can technically force you to | Drive through an Alpine pass on summer tyres and blame the insurer later |
| Build a sun-chasing habit — terrace hopping is a legitimate coping strategy | Mistake the postcard Alps for the actual, grinding reality of valley fog |

## Belgium: The Art of Low-Grade Meteorological Despair

Belgium does not really do seasons so much as variations on a single theme, and the theme is damp. The Royal Meteorological Institute's own station at Uccle has the receipts: one recent July delivered 166.5mm of rain across seventeen days, the wettest July in over four decades, while sunshine hours limped in at just over 170 against a long-term average above 203. Belgian meteorologists described it, in their own understated institutional language, as one of the gloomiest months the country had experienced in decades. This is not a one-off sulk. This is the baseline personality of the sky, and Belgians have built an entire emotional infrastructure around managing it.

What's fascinating, and genuinely admirable once you stop resenting it, is how unbothered Belgians are by their own weather compared to the newcomers losing their minds over it. On r/belgium, the national pastime is complaining about the grey with real affection, the way you'd complain about a difficult relative you'd still fight someone for. One user joked flatly that the sun simply does not exist here. Another described the drizzle as a kind of nebula that soaks everything regardless of how prepared you think you are. And yet the same thread is full of practical, unsentimental fixes rather than despair: people swapping notes on vitamin D dosing, several swearing by six thousand international units a day, others recommending light-therapy glasses as casually as you'd recommend a good dentist. A handful of defenders even pointed out that this winter had plenty of properly sunny days, which tells you everything about how low the bar has been set and how happily Belgians have agreed to clear it.

Expats, by contrast, arrive expecting European charm and get a slow-motion psychological experiment instead. A TalesMag post report on Brussels put it bluntly: the weather was the single biggest drawback of the posting, genuinely depressing, and a constant topic of conversation among residents, with only five to ten properly warm days a year to show for it. The coping mechanisms that emerge are almost comically practical — proper rain boots, a sun lamp the contributor wished they'd shipped over sooner, short mercy-dash flights to Portugal for what they called a "sun fix," and a diet that leans heavily on chocolate and beer, which, credit where due, is at least a coping mechanism with excellent production values. The real trick, the one nobody puts in the brochure, is that Belgians don't wait for good weather to live their lives. They just live them anyway, under cloud, and that single adjustment is the whole survival manual.

## Switzerland: Sunlight as a Postcode Lottery

Switzerland's weather problem is less a mood and more a geography exam, and it does not grade on a curve. Because of how narrow Alpine valleys sit relative to the winter sun's low angle, entire villages lose direct sunlight for months at a stretch. Oberterzen goes without it for roughly two and a half months. Emmetten manages 106 days in the shade, Bosco Gurin 83, Hergiswil 81. There is even an officially documented, mercifully uninhabited spot near Nesslau called the Schattenloch — the "shadow hole" — that spends 132 days without a direct ray. This is not metaphor. This is cartography deciding your mental health before you've even unpacked.

Then there's the foehn, the warm Alpine wind that MeteoSwiss notes can blow uninterrupted for 100 to 120 hours, and which the Swiss have medicalised with a straight face. CSS, one of the country's major health insurers, states plainly that roughly half the population reports feeling affected by weather conditions like this, with foehn headaches severe enough that painkillers reportedly don't always touch them, alongside sleep disruption, exhaustion, and a documented rise in accidents and irritability. Switzerland has essentially built a national acceptance that the weather can legally ruin your week, and nobody blinks.

What makes the Swiss response so maddening and so impressive is the sheer administrative tidiness of it. On expat.com's forum thread on adapting to the Swiss climate, posters note that winter tyres aren't technically mandatory, except that your insurer will quietly make your life difficult after an accident if you didn't fit them, which is Switzerland in one sentence: nothing is compulsory, everything is enforced by consequence. Others mention weeks-long autumn temperature inversions that trap fog in the valleys and sour everyone's mood, while a transplant from New Hampshire found Lausanne's winters laughably mild by comparison, crediting Swiss building insulation rather than the actual sky. Locals cope by treating sunlight itself as a resource to be actively pursued — chasing terraces, moving flats for better aspect, timing their whole lives around which side of the mountain gets the light.

## The Verdict

If you want honesty rather than diplomacy: Belgium's weather is worse on paper and easier on the soul. It is relentlessly, democratically grey, which means nobody is quietly winning the weather lottery while you sit in the dark, and the coping culture — chocolate, beer, short flights to Lisbon, a shrug — asks very little of you emotionally. Switzerland's weather is objectively more varied and more beautiful, and that is precisely the trap. The postcard Alps are real, but so is the village down the valley that hasn't seen the sun since October, and so is the wind that gives you a migraine and then gets blamed for your bad mood at a dinner party, as though it's a houseguest rather than a meteorological event.

Belgium wins on honesty. Switzerland wins on scenery and loses on sincerity, because nobody tells you about the Schattenloch until you're already signing the lease.

## Conclusion

Here is the part nobody puts on the relocation pamphlet: you do not move abroad to escape the weather, you move abroad to renegotiate your relationship with it, usually badly, usually in real time, usually while standing in a queue for a permit you don't yet have. Belgium will teach you to stop waiting for the sun and start living anyway. Switzerland will teach you that even paradise has a shadow clause, quite literally, for 132 days a year. Neither country owes you blue skies. Both will hand you extraordinary cheese and an excellent train network as compensation, and if that doesn't feel like enough, you were never cut out for expat life in the first place. Pack the raincoat. Buy the winter tyres. Nobody is coming to apologise for the sky.
