# Sweden Takes the Day Between a Holiday and a Weekend Off. Poland Waits for the First Star Before Anyone Can Eat

🇸🇪 Sweden vs 🇵🇱 Poland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A public holiday is a country's way of telling you what it is willing to stop for. Sweden stops for the summer solstice, for a girl in a candle crown and, with enthusiastic informality, for the gap between a holiday and a weekend. Poland stops for a star. Specifically the first one in the sky on 24 December, which, if you are hungry, is a long time to wait.

Moving between them is a lesson in two distinct national temperaments. One is relaxed and communal and uses schnapps as a diplomatic tool. The other is devout, careful and fond of rituals that begin with a wafer and end at midnight.

## Sweden

Sweden is a country that takes its holidays seriously and its dignity lightly. Midsummer, according to Time Out Sweden's guide, begins with flower crowns woven in the morning, continues with a lunch smorgasbord of pickled herring with sour cream and chives, new potatoes, eggs with roe, Västerbotten cheese pie and strawberries, and then proceeds to a pole, a procession of folk musicians and the ritual frog dance. A small glass of snaps is drunk after a song. The state liquor shop Systembolaget closes for Midsummer Eve and Day, and the big cities empty as everyone heads to the countryside. According to the guide, folk custom has you pick seven flowers, put them under your pillow, and dream of your future partner.

An expat blogger calling herself a swede-in-training wrote a survival guide in 2011 with advice worth keeping: sip a schnapps after each drinking song, which works out at about one shot per three songs, avoid anyone's home-brew (a colleague distilled vodka on a balcony and a father-in-law made absinthe), pitch your tent before you start drinking, and join the maypole dance even if you feel ridiculous. Her advice for the dance, I note, is to commit.

Lucia on 13 December, as The Local's Mandy Pipher reports, is more theatrical. She arrived in Stockholm from Toronto on 13 December 2018 and was taken straight to a church choir concert, where she watched a candle-crowned singer keep singing while hot wax ran down her face. The tradition dates the saint to the old Julian calendar's solstice and the modern national celebration to the 1920s, with a Neapolitan melody and Swedish lyrics from around 1920.

The Swedes also invented a holiday within a holiday. A blogger called Neil Shipley, writing as Watching the Swedes, explains the klämdag, or squeeze day: when a public holiday lands on a Tuesday or Thursday, the weekday between it and the weekend becomes a de facto day off, and many employers go along with it. In 2019, he notes, Ascension on Thursday 30 May made Friday 31 May a squeeze day, and National Day on 6 June fell on a Thursday too. Sweden has decided that the weekend should extend, and the labour market has quietly agreed.

## Poland

Poland's holiday calendar is built on liturgy and food, in that order or the reverse, depending on the family. Expat Exchange's guide to Christmas in Poland says Wigilia, on 24 December, matters more than the 25th, and features a meat-free table of red borscht with little dumplings, fish, pierogi with sauerkraut and mushrooms, poppy-seed cake and dried-fruit compote. The Expat Exchange guide ties the twelve dishes to the apostles, while Culture Trip says twelve months, and I decline to arbitrate. A shared wafer, the opłatek, comes first, with wishes exchanged. Midnight Mass, the Pasterka, comes after.

A Canada-and-Poland member on Expat.com, writing from Wrocław, supplied the practical detail. The meal begins around half past four or at the first star, with up to twelve meatless dishes including beetroot soup with "ears", carp and poppy-seed cake. No alcohol is served, so do not bring wine. Presents come after the meal. Many offices hold an early midday Wigilia, church trees can stay up until 2 February, and the 25th and 26th are days off but less central than in Britain or America.

The rest of the Polish year is gloriously strange. Culture Trip lists Fat Thursday, when pączki and faworki are consumed, the Three Kings blessing of chalk on 6 January, with initials written over doors, and Śmigus-dyngus, the Easter Monday water-dousing with medieval courtship origins. In March, an effigy of Marzanna is burned and drowned to banish winter. On 29 and 30 November comes Andrzejki, with wax poured into water to predict the future and a race with shoes. And a day after a rural wedding comes poprawiny, a party fuelled by vodka.

The theme is repetition and ritual. Every date has a food, a symbol and a mildly alarming custom, and nobody asks whether you would prefer to skip it.

The point about holiday dates deserves a footnote. The Expat Arrivals calendar for Sweden lists Epiphany on 6 January, National Day on 6 June and Midsummer Day in June, all fixed to the calendar and not to the weekend, which is exactly why the Swedes needed the squeeze day. Poland, by contrast, anchors its year in the church calendar and the agricultural one, with St Nicholas on 6 December leaving gifts in shoes and Christmas Eve presents arriving from either Gwiazdor, the Starman, or the little angel. On New Year's Eve, Sylwester brings herring, bigos and a midnight toast of Polish champagne or vodka. A calendar is a character reference.

## The Verdict

Sweden wins on ease, Poland wins on depth. A Swedish holiday is relaxed, outdoorsy and designed for people who like their ritual with a view. A Polish holiday is formal, family-heavy and designed for people who like their ritual with a plate.

If you are a newcomer, Sweden will welcome you faster. You can show up at Midsummer, get handed a flower crown and a snaps, and be one of the group by supper. Poland will ask you to learn the rules before you sit down. That is not a criticism. Poland takes its customs seriously because they are what hold the year together.

I would live in Sweden for the summer and in Poland for the winter, which is an unhelpful verdict but an honest one. Failing that, pick Sweden if you value freedom and Poland if you value belonging. Just remember that in Poland, if you want to eat on 24 December, you must wait for the star.

## Conclusion

Holidays are how a country remembers who it is. Sweden remembers with a pole, a dance and a quiet word with the liquor monopoly. Poland remembers with a wafer, a star and a table that must be exactly right.

Neither asks you to believe in anything, only to show up. That is the real test of integration, and the one no visa will check.

So show up, eat what is placed in front of you, and learn when to lift the glass. In Sweden that is after the song. In Poland, it is after the star.
