# Two Countries That Will Make You Prove You Exist, Repeatedly, in Writing

🇲🇰 North Macedonia 🇨🇦 Canada
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular kind of despair reserved for the moment you realise a government wants a document to prove you have another document, and both countries in today's instalment specialise in it, just with wildly different production values. North Macedonia does bureaucracy the old continental way: stamps, queues, a notary who may or may not be open, and an unshakeable belief that paper, physically held, is the only form of truth. Canada does bureaucracy the polite modern way: a website that looks friendly, a phone queue that plays soothing music, and a system that will still, somehow, take six weeks to tell you that you filled in box fourteen incorrectly.

I have opened bank accounts on five continents and I can report with total authority that nobody enjoys this process anywhere, but some places at least have the decency to be honest about how miserable it's going to be. North Macedonia is honest. Canada smiles at you the entire time it is quietly ruining your week.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇲🇰 North Macedonia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring every document you own, plus photocopies, plus copies of the photocopies | Expect anything to be resolved in a single visit, bring a book |
| Get documents notarised and translated before you need them, not after | Assume English will help you at the counter, bring a Macedonian speaker |
| Build a relationship with one teller, loyalty gets you small mercies | Lose your temper at the counter, it achieves precisely nothing, fast |

**🇨🇦 Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for your SIN before you do anything else, it unlocks everything | Expect same-day anything, "processing time" is a Canadian national pastime |
| Use a newcomer-specific bank package, the fees disappear almost entirely | Assume a polite call centre voice means a fast resolution |
| Keep every reference number, you will need it again in four weeks | Panic when a form asks for a "permanent address" before you have one |

## North Macedonia

Banking in North Macedonia is a deeply analogue experience in a country that otherwise runs surprisingly efficient digital services for almost everything else. Opening an account as a foreigner means collecting a small dossier: passport, proof of residence, sometimes a work contract, occasionally a notarised translation of a document you didn't know existed in your own country until someone asked for it here. The queueing system at most branches has a ticket machine, a number, and a waiting room that moves with the energy of a courtroom recess. Nobody is rushing. Nobody expects you to be rushing either.

What saves the whole system is the human element. Once you've been to the same branch three or four times, the teller starts to recognise you, and recognition in North Macedonia is worth more than any amount of digital convenience — doors open slightly faster, forms get explained slightly more patiently, and you stop being a stranger and start being "the person who comes on Tuesdays." This is a country that still fundamentally trusts relationships over systems, and once you understand that, the bureaucracy stops feeling adversarial and starts feeling like a slow, slightly bureaucratic friendship.

The notary system deserves special mention as its own small ecosystem of Kafkaesque charm — a document isn't real until it has been stamped by someone whose entire job is stamping documents, and you will, at some point, be sent from one building to another building to get a stamp that confirms the first stamp was legitimate. It is maddening. It is also, weirdly, kind of beautiful in its commitment to ceremony.

There's a particular Macedonian institution worth understanding before you arrive: the "šalter," or counter window, which functions less like a service point and more like a small stage where your entire administrative fate is decided by whoever happens to be sitting behind the glass that day. Get a sympathetic clerk and your paperwork sails through; get one having a bad Tuesday and you'll be sent away for a document you were never told you needed. Locals navigate this by building actual rapport over repeat visits, bringing small courtesies — a greeting in Macedonian, genuine patience, the occasional box of chocolates at year-end — that would be considered faintly corrupt elsewhere but here are simply how a slow system gets slightly faster for people it recognises. It's bureaucracy run on relationship capital rather than digital efficiency, and once you've banked the goodwill, it pays dividends for years.

## Canada

Canadian bureaucracy has mastered the art of making you feel cared for while taking forever. Every government website is clean, accessible, translated into both official languages, and built with an almost suspicious amount of empathy copy — "we know this is a big step," "we're here to help" — while the actual processing time for anything, from a SIN card to a permanent bank account upgrade, unfolds on what can only be described as geological time. The call centre hold music is genuinely pleasant. You will have memorised it before your issue is resolved.

The newcomer banking packages are, credit where due, a genuinely good idea — major banks offer no-fee accounts specifically for new immigrants, often with a credit card that doesn't require Canadian credit history, which solves a real chicken-and-egg problem elegantly. But getting to that point requires the SIN, which requires proof of status, which sometimes requires an address, which you can't get without a lease, which some landlords won't give you without a SIN. It is a closed loop that every newcomer eventually breaks through sheer persistence, usually by finding one sympathetic employee willing to bend a rule slightly out of pure fatigue at watching you go in circles.

What I find most distinctly Canadian is the apology built into every delay. Nobody is rude. Nobody raises their voice. The system simply, politely, endlessly asks you to wait, and you will, because the alternative — getting annoyed at a Canadian civil servant — feels roughly as productive as yelling at weather.

What also catches newcomers off guard is how differently banks treat you once you've cleared the newcomer hurdle — credit history, once established, opens doors with a speed that almost apologises for the earlier slowness, as though the system knew exactly how frustrating the entry process was and wanted to make it up to you. Provincial variation sneaks into banking bureaucracy too: a document considered sufficient proof of address in Quebec might be rejected outright in British Columbia, and nobody at the counter will necessarily warn you this discrepancy exists until you've already made the trip. The upside, eventually, is a system that works consistently once you're inside it — the slowness is front-loaded, almost entirely, into those first bewildering months.

## The Verdict

North Macedonia wins on honesty: the system tells you upfront that this will be annoying and a bit old-fashioned, and then delivers exactly that, no surprises. Canada wins on long-term design, because once you're through the newcomer gauntlet, the system actually works well and keeps working. But for raw, immediate, in-the-room experience, I'd rather be told "come back Thursday with the stamp" in Skopje than be told "your application is in progress" by a chatbot in Toronto for the eleventh consecutive day. At least in Skopje, Thursday is an actual deadline.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/Macedonia — paraphrased: get everything notarised before you go, I made four separate trips across town because I assumed a photocopy would be fine, it was never fine.*

> *r/PersonalFinanceCanada — paraphrased: my SIN took nine weeks because of a single typo on the original form and nobody at any stage told me there was a typo until I called for the third time.*

> *expat.com Canada forum — paraphrased: the newcomer bank account is genuinely great once it's set up, the problem is nothing tells you in advance that getting it set up requires three other systems to agree with each other first.*

## Conclusion

Neither country will let you forget that existing officially, on paper, in a new place, is its own small bureaucratic odyssey — one just does it with a stamp and a queue, the other with a smile and a six-week wait. You'll curse both at some point, usually in a waiting room, usually holding a folder of documents you were certain you'd already submitted. And then, eventually, the account opens, the SIN arrives, and you realise the real achievement was never the banking at all. It was surviving the forms.
