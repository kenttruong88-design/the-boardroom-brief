# Islamabad Has a Six-Day Week Problem and Hong Kong Doesn't Even Promise You Two

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is a fun party trick for your first week in Islamabad: ask three different government employees when their weekend starts. You will get three different answers, delivered with three different levels of conviction, because Pakistan's weekend has been politically reassigned more times than a cabinet reshuffle — Friday-Saturday under one regime's Islamization drive, Saturday-Sunday under the next one "to conserve energy," and back toward six-day weeks more recently because apparently leisure is a line item someone keeps trying to cut. Meanwhile in Hong Kong, the city that invented the eighty-hour week as a lifestyle brand, the law doesn't even promise you a weekend at all — just "one rest day in seven," whether paid or not, whichever your contract decided to be stingy about.

I have relocated fourteen times. I have never once asked "what will I do on my day off" with such genuine, structural uncertainty as I did in these two cities, where the very existence of a weekend is treated as a negotiable corporate perk rather than a basic feature of being alive.

## Pakistan: The Weekend as a Rumor

Islamabad's relationship with free time has always been a hostage to politics. The city picked up its Friday-Saturday weekend during Zia-ul-Haq's Islamization push, had it swapped to the globally conventional Saturday-Sunday by the PPP government in 2011 in the name of energy conservation, and has spent the years since quietly drifting back toward six-day weeks depending on which ministry you ask. A 2022 notification had federal offices running six days, 8am to 3pm, Friday a half-day — while the State Bank of Pakistan, because apparently coordination is optional, keeps an entirely different schedule with its own built-in prayer break. You will plan a dinner with a civil-servant friend and discover, mid-text-exchange, that neither of you actually knows if they're working that day.

What there is to do once you've secured the day off is, charmingly, more honest than most places about what actually exists rather than what a tourism board wishes existed. Islamabad has more parks than any other Pakistani city — the Margalla Hills trail network, the Rose and Japanese and Lotus gardens — and locals use them properly, hiking before the heat sets in, which at least gives you something to do with your limbs. What it doesn't have is much else. Nightlife is a near-fiction: alcohol is formally banned in public, and the legal workaround for non-Muslim foreigners involves obtaining an actual government permit from your local Excise & Taxation Officer before you can buy a drink at one of a handful of licensed hotels — bureaucracy as a prerequisite for a beer, which is its own kind of poetry. The Islamabad Club, the obvious colonial-hangover move of "just join the country club," wants roughly $25,000 for a membership it has reportedly stopped issuing anyway, so don't bother dreaming about squash courts.

What fills the gap, and fills it completely, is the wedding circuit. A Reddit thread titled, with admirable directness, "Pakistan is a very boring country" has expats and locals alike converging on the same diagnosis: malls and restaurants are the default outing, green space is scarce and not always comfortable for women alone, and weddings are where actual socializing — meeting people, extending your network, being fed absurdly well — happens. The embassy and diplomatic-enclave party circuit exists as a parallel track, but one columnist for The Express Tribune memorably called it "the height of social climbing," guest-list-gated and allergic to anyone who doesn't already have the right numbers in their phone. Your weekend, in short, is whatever Islamabad decides to give you that week, and you will not be consulted.

## Hong Kong: The Weekend You Have to Earn and Then Schedule

Hong Kong's problem isn't scarcity of leisure — it's that leisure is wedged into a city that has structurally deprioritized it, legally and architecturally. The Employment Ordinance guarantees exactly one rest day in seven. That's it. Whether it's paid is between you and your employer, which means plenty of white-collar jobs here still run close to six-day weeks without anyone needing to pass a special law about it, because the law never promised you anything to begin with.

What survives this hostile scheduling environment is a weekend culture built entirely around compression and escape. The junk boat — chartering a sailing vessel for a day of swimming, drinking, and "junk swapping" between anchored groups — is the closest thing Hong Kong has to a civic ritual, a privatized descendant of a corporate perk that died with the 1997 Asian financial crisis, when companies stopped owning their own boats and employees started renting the fantasy back by the day. A nine-person charter runs about HK$9,000 plus a Thai picnic hamper, which tells you everything about how premeditated "spontaneous" fun has to be here: you are booking your relaxation weeks in advance and splitting the bill like a mortgage.

The geography does the rest of the work. Because the city is small and absurdly well-connected, escape is thirty to forty-five minutes away by ferry to islands like Lamma or Cheung Chau, which locals insist "feel like a different country entirely," and a short flight puts Bangkok or Taipei in weekend-trip range rather than big-holiday range. And then there's Sunday in Central — not the quiet financial-district lull a newcomer expects, but the one contractual day off for the city's 340,000-plus foreign domestic helpers, who fill Victoria Park and Causeway Bay with picnics, dance troupes, and photography meetups that most expats observe from a polite, slightly bewildered distance rather than actually joining.

## The Verdict

Pakistan loses on infrastructure and wins, grudgingly, on honesty — you will never mistake Islamabad for a city that has its leisure sector figured out, and there's a strange relief in that. Hong Kong loses on principle: a city this rich, this organized, this capable of building a working harbor-tunnel network before breakfast, simply never got around to legislating that its workers deserve two consecutive days off. If I have to choose between a country that can't decide what day the weekend is and one that can't be bothered to have a real one, I'll take the hike up Margalla Hills. At least the mountain doesn't bill you by the hour.

## Conclusion

What both cities share, underneath the very different mechanics, is that the weekend is never simply handed to you — in Islamabad it's politically unstable, in Hong Kong it's contractually optional, and in both places you end up building your actual leisure out of whatever informal network you can assemble: a wedding invite, a hiking group, a junk-boat splitting arrangement with five people you barely know yet. The lesson, delivered with the specific weariness of someone who has unpacked in fourteen different countries, is that nobody is coming to organize your fun for you. You will have to do it yourself, on a schedule nobody can quite confirm, in a city that may or may not believe you've earned it. Pack patience. Pack hiking boots. And for heaven's sake, book the boat early.
