# The Desert Built Cathedrals of Commerce and the Outback Built a Trestle Table

🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ask a Dubai resident where they're spending Saturday and the honest answer, nine times out of ten, is "the mall," delivered with zero self-consciousness, because the mall is not a confession, it is a destination, a climate, and occasionally a personality trait. Ask a Sydneysider the same question and they'll say "the markets," then drive forty minutes to a Westfield anyway, buy exactly what they needed, complain about the parking, and never mention it again. Both nations have built entire identities around the acquisition of goods. Only one of them has the decency to admit it.

I have shopped under the same roof as an indoor ski slope and I have haggled over a kilo of king prawns at a trestle table while a man yelled about zucchini. These are not equivalent experiences, but they are both, unmistakably, "going shopping," and the fact that one country turned retail into an air-conditioned civic religion while the other turned it into a slightly resentful Saturday errand tells you almost everything you need to know about how each place actually lives.

## Do's & Don'ts: United Arab Emirates

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the day's gold rate online before you haggle at the souk — the metal price is fixed, only the making charges move | Assume the price tag at Gold Souk is the price. The display gold price is real; everything else is a conversation |
| Dress for the mall like you'd dress for a nice lunch, not the beach — security will notice before you do | Wear beachwear into a mall and act surprised when you're politely redirected to a changing room |
| Treat the mall as a legitimate social plan — cinema, dinner, a walk, it's normal and nobody will judge you | Expect souk vendors to match department-store small talk; haggling is the social contract there, not an insult |
| Use delivery apps for literally everything, including a single lime, during the summer months | Walk anywhere outdoors in July expecting a "charming local market stroll" — you will regret it within four minutes |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Hit the Boxing Day sales if you enjoy crowds, adrenaline, and mild property damage over a parking spot | Expect Boxing Day stock levels to still include your size by 9am — the good stuff is gone before the coffee's cold |
| Learn to love an op shop; the good ones rotate stock weekly and the prices reflect a less ridiculous economy | Dismiss Facebook Marketplace as "just for furniture" — it's the de facto national garage sale and everyone uses it |
| Budget for import-level pricing on anything not grown or made locally — it is genuinely that expensive | Assume Australian retail prices resemble UK or US prices; the maths will mug you in the first fortnight |
| Order internationally when the local markup is absurd, and accept the three-week wait as the cost of sanity | Rage-quit a Sunday market because the parking's full — walk ten more minutes, it's always worth it |

## United Arab Emirates: The Mall Is the Message

Dubai has somewhere north of sixty-five shopping malls, with more under construction, which is either the most honest urban planning decision ever made or a cry for help, and I genuinely cannot decide which. Academics studying the Gulf — NYU Abu Dhabi's Dr Laure Assaf among them — describe these buildings as "de facto community centres and public squares," which sounds like spin until you've actually watched a family spend four unhurried hours there without buying anything beyond a shawarma, because the mall isn't a transaction, it's where the city's social life physically lives when the outdoors is actively trying to kill you for six months of the year. Air conditioning didn't just enable mall culture here; it is mall culture's entire thesis statement, and nobody pretends otherwise.

Underneath the Instagram-bait aquarium-adjacent retail, though, the souk survives on its own terms, and it is the better education in how this country actually works. The Gold Souk's headline trick is that the gold price itself is fixed to the daily international rate, displayed on digital boards, utterly non-negotiable — what you're actually haggling over is the "making charge," the labour and design premium, and a sensible opening gambit is roughly a quarter off that figure, settling somewhere in the middle through a conversation that is meant to be unhurried, friendly, and faintly theatrical. Treat it as a hostile transaction and you'll pay full price and leave annoyed. Treat it as the social ritual it is and you'll walk away with a discount and, improbably, a new acquaintance who insists you come back for his cousin's shop too.

Then there's the third pillar nobody visiting for a week quite registers: the delivery apps. Groceries, electronics, a single missing ingredient at 1am, all of it arrives within the hour, and it has quietly become the actual backbone of daily shopping for residents, with the mall reserved for leisure and the souk for ritual and the apps absorbing everything in between. The oft-repeated line that 71 percent of Western expats, per a survey cited in NYU Abu Dhabi's student press, named the country's "highly materialistic and consumerist society" as the biggest threat to local identity is the sort of thing people say right before ordering a flat white to the beach via an app. The hypocrisy is not subtle. It is also not slowing down.

## Australia: The Trestle Table Economy

Australia's retail culture runs on a split personality it has never quite resolved: enormous, glossy Westfield complexes that function as the suburban town square, sitting about four kilometres from a council car park that turns into a produce and bric-a-brac market every Saturday morning, and the two never acknowledge each other's existence. The Westfield is where you go for the department store, the food court, the cinema, the mandatory errand-running; the market is where you go to feel, briefly, like the country hasn't been entirely swallowed by a handful of supermarket duopolies and a Scandinavian furniture chain.

Then there's Boxing Day, which Australia treats with the civic seriousness other nations reserve for elections. The National Retail Association forecasts spending north of two billion dollars for the single day, with New South Wales and Victoria alone accounting for well over a billion between them and crowds exceeding two million shoppers through Westfield centres nationally — a genuinely enormous, scheduled, almost ceremonial act of consumption that the entire country quietly organises its December around, extended trading hours and all. It is, not coincidentally, the one day Australians will admit to loving a mall without irony.

The quieter, more revealing habit is the op shop and Facebook Marketplace economy, which expats routinely underestimate before discovering, with some horror, exactly how expensive ordinary retail is here. A thread of expats comparing the country's priciest cities lands on a blunt consensus: cost of living is high, rent eats the budget first, and you'd better be "well prepared" before you arrive — advice echoed almost verbatim in TalesMag's real-post interviews, where one Sydney-based expat flatly reported that everyday goods ran "three to four times" what they cost back home, prompting a habit of ordering essentials internationally rather than paying the local markup. Op shops and Marketplace aren't some twee ethical lifestyle choice here; for plenty of residents they're load-bearing infrastructure against a retail market that never got the memo about reasonable pricing.

## The Verdict

The UAE wins this one, and it isn't close, because it committed. A country that built its social infrastructure, tourism economy, and climate-survival strategy into the same sixty-five buildings, then layered a genuinely functional haggling economy and a same-hour delivery culture on top, understood exactly what it was building and leaned in without apology. Australia, by contrast, wants it both ways — the department store and the moral high ground of the Sunday market, the Boxing Day stampede and the quiet shame about how expensive everything is the other 364 days. Commitment to a bit, even a gaudy, air-conditioned, aquarium-adorned bit, beats ambivalence every time. The UAE knows what it's doing. Australia is still pretending the trestle table is the real economy while the Westfield car park overflows every single Saturday.

## Conclusion

Neither country will tell you the truth about its own shopping habits, which is, frankly, on brand for shopping everywhere. The UAE will insist the mall is just "somewhere to go" while quietly building a sixty-sixth one. Australia will insist the market is the authentic experience while the Westfield car park fills by 9am every single Saturday of the year. What actually separates them isn't taste, or even money — it's honesty about the bit. Dubai knows it's performing abundance and does it with full theatrical commitment, gold-dispensing machines and all. Australia performs thrift and modesty while quietly getting fleeced on everything from shampoo to shoes, then blames the market stallholder for the parking. Shop where you like. Just don't, either way, call it humble.
