# The Empty Seat and the Full Carriage: Two Nations at Total War Over Personal Space

🇸🇪 Sweden vs 🇮🇳 India — Public Behaviour Norms

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Sweden has built an entire unofficial social contract around one rule: never sit next to a stranger if an empty row exists anywhere else on the bus. India has built its public life around the opposite assumption: personal space is a luxury concept invented by people who have clearly never needed to catch the 8:12 local to Churchgate. Both systems work, remarkably, for the societies that built them. Neither prepares you for the other.

I once watched a Swede stand for an entire twenty-minute bus journey rather than sit beside the only other passenger, a perfectly pleasant-looking woman reading a book, because doing so would have violated an unspoken code more sacred than most written law. I have also been physically lifted, slightly, by the sheer forward momentum of a Mumbai train platform crowd, with several strangers' elbows in supportive if uninvited contact with my ribs. Both experiences taught me something about personal space. Only one left bruises.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Leave a seat between yourself and a stranger whenever physically possible | Sit directly next to someone on an empty bus unless every other seat is taken |
| Keep conversation minimal and voices low in public spaces | Make loud small talk with strangers; it will be met with visible discomfort |
| Queue precisely, in order, without exception | Cut a queue, even accidentally; Swedes notice and silently, permanently judge |
| Respect silence as a valid, comfortable social state | Fill every pause with chatter; silence is not awkward here, it's normal |

### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect physical proximity as the default in markets, trains, and queues | Expect strict personal-space bubbles; they simply don't apply in crowded public settings |
| Move with purpose and confidence in crowds; hesitation gets you nowhere | Wait for a "polite gap" to board a train; it may never come, so go firmly |
| Accept that noise — horns, calls, conversation — is the ambient soundtrack of public life | Expect silence in public spaces; quiet is the exception, not the rule |
| Use gentle physical contact — a light touch, a nudge — as normal wayfinding in crowds | Take incidental contact personally; it carries no social weight here |

## Sweden: The Architecture of Distance

Swedish public behaviour operates on lagom — the cultural principle of "just enough," applied here to social interaction as rigorously as to everything else. The unofficial "empty seat rule" on Swedish buses and trains is real, observed almost universally, and baffling to newcomers: passengers will stand rather than sit beside a stranger if any alternative seating exists, treating unnecessary proximity as a minor social transgression rather than simple practicality.

Queueing is treated with near-religious precision — numbered ticket systems exist even in small shops specifically to remove any ambiguity about order, because ambiguity invites conflict, and conflict is deeply unwelcome. Silence, meanwhile, is not something Swedes rush to fill. A quiet train carriage, a quiet elevator, a quiet dinner table for stretches of time — all entirely normal, none requiring the nervous small talk that would be expected almost anywhere else.

For newcomers this reads, initially, as coldness. It isn't, quite. It's a system built around respecting other people's space so thoroughly that engagement becomes something offered deliberately rather than imposed by default. The tradeoff is a public life that can feel, to outsiders, almost eerily undemonstrative — efficient, orderly, and largely silent.

## India: The Architecture of Proximity

Indian public life runs on the opposite premise entirely: with well over a billion people sharing space daily, personal space as a strict boundary simply isn't operationally viable, and the culture has adapted with genuine skill rather than mere tolerance. Mumbai's suburban trains carry passenger loads that regularly exceed rated capacity by multiples, and the crowd itself becomes a kind of collective choreography — pushing, boarding, and shifting with a rhythm that looks chaotic to outsiders and is, to regulars, a functioning system.

Noise operates similarly. Horns, vendor calls, overlapping conversations, blaring music from a nearby shop — the ambient volume of Indian public space is high, constant, and rarely commented on by locals, because it's simply the background texture of shared life rather than an intrusion to be minimised. Physical contact in crowds — a hand on a shoulder to move past, gentle jostling to board a bus — carries no particular social charge; it's practical, not personal.

What can look like an absence of boundaries is, in fact, a different and highly functional set of boundaries — ones built around flow, tolerance and adaptability rather than physical distance. Newcomers who arrive expecting Western-style personal space bubbles find the adjustment jarring; those who learn to move with the crowd rather than against it find India's public life has its own generous, almost communal logic.

## The Verdict

Sweden offers order, quiet, and a public life so undemanding it borders on restful. India offers energy, density, and a public life that demands constant engagement whether you want it or not. Neither is objectively better; they're solving entirely different problems, one of abundant space and one of its near-total absence. But if forced to choose which country's public transport experience would make a better dinner party story, India wins by several elbows.

## Conclusion

Sweden will teach you that silence and distance are forms of respect, not coldness. India will teach you that proximity and noise are forms of shared life, not chaos. Visit Sweden and you'll learn to love an empty seat. Visit India and you'll learn to love the crowd that carries you forward whether you're ready or not. Bring earplugs for one and an elbow strategy for the other.
