# Pakistan Shops From a Man Who Knows Your Name, Canada Shops From a Cart the Size of a Car

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Pakistan, the greengrocer knows your order, your mother's opinion of his tomatoes and whether you are on speaking terms with your brother-in-law. In Canada, the supermarket knows your loyalty number, your spending habits and nothing else, which is a different kind of intimacy and a considerably less warm one.

I have pushed trolleys in both countries, and the thing nobody tells you is that groceries are not a chore. They are a daily act of trust. Who you buy from, how you pay and what you are allowed to question tell you more about a society than any museum.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇰 Pakistan ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a relationship with a regular vendor | Switch stalls daily and expect a good price |
| Check the produce and weigh the bag yourself | Assume the first price or the first scale is final |
| Carry small notes for market purchases | Pay with a large note and wait for change |
| Shop early in the morning for the freshest produce | Arrive at noon and complain about the leftovers |

| 🇨🇦 Canada ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your own bags and a loonie for the trolley | Show up without a bag and pay for plastic |
| Check unit prices on the shelf label | Trust the big sign over the small print |
| Look for halal and South Asian grocers in your area | Assume the mainstream supermarket stocks everything |
| Plan a weekly shop around flyers and sales | Shop daily and pay full price each time |

## 🇵🇰 Pakistan

Grocery shopping in Pakistan is a daily, local and deeply personal affair. Outside the big cities especially, there is a vendor for every item: the sabzi wala for vegetables, the fruit seller with a cart, the butcher who sets aside the good cuts, the bakery that does the naan at precisely the right hour. Many households still buy fresh food every day, because refrigerators are small and the produce is better than anything that has been sitting in a depot.

The relationships matter. A regular customer gets a fair price, a fresher batch and the unsolicited family news that comes free with the purchase. A stranger gets the same goods and a slightly less generous smile. Bargaining is part of the culture, and a bit of friendly negotiation can bring the price down, but the shared understanding is that a good vendor and a good customer are in a long game.

Modern supermarkets and chains have grown in the large cities, offering fixed prices, air conditioning and a wide range of packaged and imported goods. They are popular with middle class families and expats, particularly for dairy, frozen goods and household supplies. Weekly markets, bazaars and neighbourhood shops, however, remain the beating heart of the system.

Quality and price follow the season. Mangoes in summer, citrus in winter, spices at any hour, and bread that arrives hot are the quiet luxuries of everyday life. Halal is the default and is not a label but an assumption, which is a comfort until you move somewhere that treats it as a specialty.

The downside is the heat, the dust and the occasional price swing. A tomato shortage can become national news, and inflation has pushed up the cost of staples for many families. But the shopping itself remains a communal ritual, with a conversation at every stall.

Meat and dairy are the most visible differences. In Pakistan, fresh meat is bought from a butcher on the day, and fresh milk is delivered or collected from a dairy, with the quality depending on whom you trust. In Canada, meat is sold in refrigerated packs and milk comes in bags or cartons, and the system is tightly regulated. Both are safe in their own way, and both will surprise you if you were raised in the other.

## 🇨🇦 Canada

Canadian grocery shopping is a weekly mission conducted with the efficiency of an army supply chain. You drive to a large supermarket, fill a trolley the size of a bathtub, load it into the car and return home with enough food to survive a mild apocalypse. The aisles are clean, the shelves are full and every product comes with a brand, a flyer price and a loyalty offer.

The big chains dominate, with warehouse clubs adding to the mix for bulk buyers. Prices have been a talking point for years, and Canadians have become practised at reading flyers, comparing unit prices and planning meals around sales. It is a national pastime to complain about the cost of food while carrying a reusable bag.

The pleasant surprise for many newcomers is variety. Canada's cities have large South Asian, Middle Eastern, East Asian and Caribbean communities, and there are specialist grocers and halal butchers in most urban areas. Pakistani families in the Greater Toronto Area, for example, can find spices, lentils, fresh naan and halal meat within a short drive. The big supermarkets also stock a growing range of ethnic foods, and the Canadian Food Inspection Agency regulates labels, including halal claims.

The surprises are small and consistent. Self checkout is common, you pack your own bags in many stores, and conversation with the cashier is brief and cheerful. Fresh produce is good but depends on the season and the supply chain, and the winter tomato is a polite fiction.

For newcomers, the biggest adjustment is the scale and the car. Without one, a weekly shop becomes a logistical operation, and delivery apps have become a lifeline for city dwellers. The second adjustment is cost, which can feel high until you learn the rhythm of sales and store brands.

The social side of the weekly shop varies as well. In Pakistan, a trip to the market is usually a family affair or a task given to a trusted servant or relative, and prices are discussed with enthusiasm. In Canada, the supermarket is a place to be quick and quiet, and conversations are limited to a polite remark about the weather. People are friendly, but it is not a place to make friends.

## The Verdict

Pakistan wins on freshness and relationship, and Canada wins on consistency and choice. If you want a tomato that was picked this week by someone who will argue with you about it, Pakistan is hard to beat. If you want a guarantee that the shelf will be full, the date will be printed and the refund will be given, Canada is the safer bet.

I would shop in Pakistan for dinner and in Canada for the pantry. The first is a daily pleasure and the second is a monthly necessity, and a household that does both will eat well wherever it lives.

The sting is that both countries have forgotten what the other remembers. Canadians have forgotten that food has a source and a face. Pakistanis, in some cities, are discovering that convenience has a price in time, money and plastic.

For an expat, the lesson is to find the equivalent of the vendor. In Canada, that may be a halal butcher, a corner grocer or a farmers' market where somebody learns your name. It is worth the detour.

Seasonal and festival shopping changes the picture. Ramadan sends prices and queues up in both countries, with dates, flour and fruit in demand, and Eid brings special shopping trips for meat and sweets. Canadian halal stores often run extended hours in the holy month, and many supermarkets add festival sections, a small detail that makes a newcomer feel at home.

## Conclusion

You can learn a country by watching what it puts in a bag. Pakistan puts in a story, a greeting and a good deal. Canada puts in a receipt, a coupon and a reminder of what you forgot.

My advice is to build your own routine. Pick one market for the fresh things, one supermarket for the staples and one person who will remember what you like. That is how you turn a strange country into a neighbourhood.

And do bring a bag. Canada charges for plastic, and Pakistan remembers who forgot.
