# The Philippines Gives You English, China Gives You a Phone

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇨🇳 China

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Everybody has a theory about language and moving abroad, and nearly all of them are written by people who have never been hungry in a place where the menu is a wall of symbols. I have been hungry in a great many such places. I have mimed "chicken, but not the feet" in more dialects than I care to admit, and I am here to tell you that the Philippines and China represent two completely different philosophies of making you feel stupid.

The Philippines flatters you first and humbles you second. China humbles you at once and, if you are not a complete fool, rewards you with something close to respect. One of these is easier to land in. The other is easier to live in. Which is which depends on how honest you are about your own laziness.

I should also confess my bias before the keyboard warriors arrive. I have no patience for the genre of expat who treats a language barrier as an inconvenience the locals have arranged to spite them. Nobody in either country is obliged to speak your mother tongue, and the ones who do are doing you a kindness. Keep that in mind, and the rest of this will sting a little less.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇭 Philippines ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use English for paperwork, contracts and anything official | Assume English will work in every province or with every generation |
| Learn "salamat" and a handful of local words, because they land well | Take offence when people slide into Tagalog or Bisaya around you |
| Ask people to repeat themselves slowly, because accents vary hugely by region | Nod along when you have understood nothing |
| Be patient with the weekday, since fluent English speakers are often at work | Mistake politeness for comprehension |

| 🇨🇳 China ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Install a translation app, a mapping app and a mobile payment app before you land | Expect strangers to answer you in English |
| Learn "this one" and "that one", then point with confidence | Insist on speaking English louder |
| Show the driver your destination in written form, ideally via a ride-hailing app | Bluff your way into a conversation you cannot finish |
| Study some Mandarin properly, because effort is noticed | Expect fluency from yourself within a fortnight |

## 🇵🇭 Philippines

The Philippines is the country where I learned that "I speak English" and "we are communicating" are two entirely separate claims. On paper you are home and dry. Expat forum posters report that official documents, contracts, court proceedings, driving licences and road signs are all in English, and one resident near Davao says he hears more English than Tagalog there. Schools teach in it. Lawyers, utility staff and officials use it without fuss. For a newcomer who is terrified of bureaucracy, this is enormous.

Then you try to make a friend, and the floor shifts. The same forum thread describes a long-term resident who finds English everywhere in print and in shops but remarkably rare in actual conversation, unless the person has spent time overseas. Others say English is not reliably understood in the provinces unless someone has a degree, and that Filipinos will happily switch into Cebuano or Tagalog the moment they are together, leaving you smiling like a hostage. One poster describes relatives and shop staff asking his wife intimate questions about their marriage right in front of him, in a language he could not follow.

Accent is the other trap. One contributor gives a lovely example in which a Bisaya speaker's "eat McDo" turned out to mean "eight McDo". Another says he still struggles to understand his Cebuano partner after five years. This is not a failure of the Filipino, who is generally gracious and often funnier than you, but a failure of the foreigner who assumed the language question was settled at the airport.

The real danger here is comfort. English will carry you for years, and in that time you will never be forced to learn anything, and your friendships will stay politely on the surface. The people who dig deeper, who learn some Tagalog or a regional language, report that even basic phrases are welcomed warmly. That is the whole secret. The door is open. You simply have to bother to walk through it.

## 🇨🇳 China

China does not flatter you. Outside of a university or an international office, the expat essays are consistent that English is thin on the ground and shops often expect at least a little Mandarin. A student writing from Ningbo describes how she began to take the language seriously only once she needed it for ordering food, paying bills and handling visa paperwork. Mandarin shares no word roots with English, has tones that punish the confident, and a writing system that will humble anyone who thinks Duolingo is a personality.

And yet, and this is what drives me mad about the lazy takes, China is easier to survive in than it looks. Forum posters say you can get by with local friends, a translation app and a tiny amount of language, pointing at objects and using the words for "this" and "that". They recommend Alipay for payments, DiDi for taxis, a transit card for buses and metro, and fast-food chains when all else fails. The Shanghai city government's own English portal lists translation tools and essential apps for newcomers, which tells you something about how the country treats the problem. It is not a wall. It is an engineering question, and China is very good at engineering.

There is a social side. One forum poster, a businessman who has studied Chinese for some time, says locals expect foreigners not to speak it, and that he feels you either need real fluency or none at all. I think he is half right and wholly depressing. The essayist says the opposite, that locals rarely expect perfection from foreigners and often help with pronunciation. Having been corrected on a tone by a retired man at a noodle stall with the patience of a saint, I know whose story I believe.

China's gift is that it forces the question. You cannot hide behind your passport, so you learn, or you point, and the pointing has a way of becoming learning.

## The Verdict

If I am being brutal, and I usually am, the Philippines is the easier place to arrive and the harder place to truly belong, while China is the harder place to arrive and the more honest one to live in. The Philippines lets you coast, and coasting is a dreadful habit in a foreigner. You can spend a decade there never once speaking to anyone in their own language, and nobody will say a word, which is both generous and slightly insulting.

China refuses to let you do that. The phone helps, the apps help, and your friends will help, but the expectation hangs in the air that you will try. That pressure, annoying as it is, produces results. People who live there for a year tend to come out with some Mandarin whether they intended to or not.

So my winner is the one that makes you work. China wins on honesty, the Philippines wins on warmth, and warmth without effort is just a very pleasant way of staying ignorant. If you want an easy first month, fly to Manila. If you want to become a slightly better person, buy a ticket to Shanghai and bring a charger.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com forum, Philippines — Residents agree that English covers official business but not everyday talk, that provincial areas and older people can be hard going, and that Filipinos often slip into Tagalog or Bisaya around you, which can leave a foreigner feeling shut out.*

> *Expat.com forum, China — One poster says you can manage with a translation app, a few words and local friends, while another insists that anyone living there should be made to learn Mandarin, and a third thinks it is fluent or nothing.*

> *We Are Resonate — A student in Ningbo writes that English was scarce outside her university, that locals rarely expect perfection, and that expats should stop assuming the world will rearrange itself around their native tongue.*

## Conclusion

I have eaten in sixty countries, and the hardest lesson is the dullest. A language barrier is not a wall built by the locals. It is a door you either open or admire from outside. The Philippines leaves the door ajar and lets you pretend it is a window. China bolts it, hands you a phone, and waits.

Neither is a tragedy. Both are teachers. But if you move to either with the plan of getting by on English alone, you will spend years being politely tolerated by people who would adore you if you simply tried.

Learn ten words. Learn them badly. Say them anyway. The embarrassment fades, the noodles are better, and you stop being a tourist with a lease.
