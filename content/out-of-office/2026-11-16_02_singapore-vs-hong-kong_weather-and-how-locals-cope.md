# Singapore Has One Season and Hong Kong Has Four Moods, and Both Will Make You Cry in a Lift

🇸🇬 Singapore vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Singapore's weather is a single decision made in 1965 and never revisited: hot, wet, sticky, forever. You can plan a year in the city and never once check a forecast, because the forecast is the same. Thirty-one degrees, a violent afternoon thunderstorm, and by evening your shirt has become a personal tragedy. It is the most predictable climate on Earth, and I would say it is restful if I were not constantly wiping my own face.

Hong Kong, by contrast, has actual seasons, which sounds like a luxury until you remember that one of them is summer. The city runs from muggy brutality to a lovely, brittle autumn to a grey, drizzling winter that makes locals behave as though a blizzard is imminent. Both places will test your character, and both have built entire retail and architectural cultures around escaping the sky.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Singapore**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry a compact umbrella at all times | Trust a blue sky at two o'clock |
| Use covered walkways and underground links | Walk uncovered between buildings at midday |
| Layer a thin cardigan for the freezing offices | Dress for the street and forget the lobby |
| Schedule outdoor exercise at dawn or after dark | Run at noon to prove a point |

**Hong Kong**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn what the typhoon signals mean before you need them | Assume the city simply closes for a typhoon without checking |
| Enjoy October to December, the best season | Plan a long hike in July |
| Own a dehumidifier | Leave leather shoes in a wardrobe all summer |
| Pack a proper coat for January | Believe it never gets cold |

## Singapore

Singapore has weather in the way a sauna has weather. The temperature barely moves, the humidity does not take weekends off, and the real variable is whether it is raining. When it rains, it does so with theatrical commitment: a dark sky, a wall of water, forty minutes of pure drama, then sunshine and steam as if nothing happened. Locals have adapted with the efficiency of a city-state that treats climate as an engineering brief.

That means covered walkways. Singapore has built an astonishing network of sheltered links between train stations, malls, offices and housing blocks, so you can move across large stretches of the city without ever taking the full sun. Underground passages, air-conditioned bridges and shaded corridors are part of the urban fabric. Newcomers think of them as conveniences. After a month, they think of them as human rights.

The other local coping mechanism is the air conditioner, which is applied with a zeal that borders on a cold war. Offices, malls, trains and cinemas are set to temperatures that convince you the equator is a rumour. The standard Singapore expat accessory is the thin cardigan you keep in the bag, which makes absolutely no sense outside and saves your life inside. You will go from a ferocious thirty-three degrees to a polar boardroom four times a day.

The expat lesson is acceptance. You cannot out-plan a climate with no variation. You can only dress for two worlds, drink more water than feels reasonable, and accept that you are going to be slightly damp until you move away. The upside is certainty: nobody in Singapore has ever missed a barbecue because of a surprise cold snap.

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong has genuine seasonality, and an expat guide to the city describes it with admirable calm: summer is long, humid and runs up to about thirty-three degrees, typhoons are possible from May to November, and autumn, roughly October to early December, is the delightful stretch with low humidity and little rain. Winter is cloudy, dry and mild, around fifteen degrees, with no snow or frost.

That mildness is a trap, because Hong Kong buildings are not designed for cold. Locals dress for a polar expedition the moment the temperature drops, and the winter indoors can feel colder than outdoors, given the lack of central heating in most homes. You will see people in puffer jackets on the metro while the thermometer says it is the kind of day a Londoner would call a heatwave.

The summer is the real test. Time Out Hong Kong once published a list of the things that make residents furious about the season, and the items read like a confession: insects, umbrellas, unpredictable weather, dripping air conditioners, weekend typhoons, humidity, body odour, and the over-cooled interiors. If you want to know the cultural coping style, it is complaint as a collective sport.

Then there are typhoons, which Hong Kong manages with a graded warning system and the competence of a city that has been through this before. The signals are widely understood and widely respected, and the public-transport network tends to shut down with an orderly efficiency that would embarrass most airlines. A typhoon on a Saturday is an annoyance. A typhoon on a Monday is a gift.

Hong Kong also does something Singapore cannot: it gives you a reason to look forward to a month. The autumn light, the dry air, the first evening you can sit outside without feeling like a wet sponge. You pay for it with a summer that tests your commitment, but the reward is real.

## The Verdict

Singapore is the easier place to live, and I say that with a clenched jaw. A climate with no surprises, a city built around it, and an infrastructure that treats the sky as a personal enemy: that is logistical brilliance. You can wear the same wardrobe for ten years, and nobody has ever been caught out by January.

Hong Kong is the more interesting one, and I would rather be bored by seasons than pickled in humidity. An anonymous poster in a long-running forum thread rated Singapore higher in a comparison of the two climates, and I understand why on a bad drizzly February morning. But Hong Kong gives you a golden autumn, a real winter jumper, and a city that lets you hike on a cool day. That is worth the typhoon season.

Winner: Hong Kong, by a damp nose, mostly because variety is the only thing that makes a sweaty life bearable. Singapore wins on convenience. Convenience is lovely. It is not the same as living.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *City-Data forum thread on Singapore versus Hong Kong climate (2011) — one poster ranked Singapore ahead and listed Hong Kong's winter drizzle, thin sunshine, smog and typhoons as the weak points of the Hong Kong climate.*

> *City-Data forum, same thread — a poster noted swings of roughly nine degrees to twenty-nine degrees within a couple of weeks in Hong Kong, which is exactly the sort of detail the glossy guides skip.*

> *Time Out Hong Kong, summer complaints list — locals blame over-cold air conditioning and dripping units as much as the heat itself, which tells you the air conditioner is a character in the story, not the solution.*

## Conclusion

The oldest truth about expat weather is that you do not adapt to a climate. You adapt to the architecture built to survive it. Singapore gives you the covered corridor and the endless cardigan. Hong Kong gives you the typhoon signal and the stubborn dream of a cool October.

Pick the one that matches your temperament. If you crave certainty, Singapore will love you back with a sauna's loyalty. If you need a change of scene without leaving town, Hong Kong will break your heart every July and mend it every November. Either way, pack a spare shirt. You will need it by lunch, and so will everyone sitting near you.
