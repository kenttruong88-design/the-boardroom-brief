# The Vault and the Ledger: Two Nations, Two Ways to Make You Beg for a Debit Card

🇨🇭 Switzerland vs 🇮🇳 India — Banking and Bureaucracy
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country has a founding myth about its bureaucracy. Switzerland's is that it is precise, discreet, and impossible to rush, which is a polite way of saying it will keep you standing at a counter until you have proven, notarised and re-proven your right to exist. India's is that everything is technically possible, usually today, provided you bring nine photocopies, two witnesses and the patience of a Himalayan hermit. I have opened accounts in both. I have wept, quietly, in both.

What unites them is a shared conviction that a foreigner wanting a debit card is a security threat roughly on par with a foreigner wanting a fighter jet. What separates them is style. Switzerland does its gatekeeping with a raised eyebrow and a filing cabinet. India does it with a stamp, a second stamp, and a man behind glass who has seen it all and is unimpressed by you specifically.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a signed lease, your residence permit, and a reason for existing, all at once | Assume a tourist visa or a smile will get you past the counter |
| Book an appointment — walk-ins are treated as a personal insult | Expect an answer on the spot; "we will review" can mean three weeks |
| Ask about account fees upfront; they are creative and plentiful | Be surprised when a "free" account starts charging you for breathing |
| Use a cantonal bank if you want less snobbery and more patience | Try UBS or Credit Suisse's successor entities as your first stop unless you enjoy being quietly assessed |

### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your Aadhaar and PAN sorted before you do anything else — they are the master keys | Try to open an account with only a passport and hope |
| Bring more photocopies than you think you need, then bring more | Assume the process is the same at every branch of the same bank |
| Use video KYC where available — it has quietly saved millions of hours of national queuing | Lose your temper with the person behind the counter; they did not design this system, they are trapped in it too |
| Keep a folder — physical, tangible, slightly battered — of every document you own | Rely purely on digital copies; someone will always want the original, in ink, in person |

## Switzerland: Discretion as a Load-Bearing Wall

Swiss banking's reputation for secrecy was built on numbered accounts and gnomes who apparently lived in Zurich vaults, but the modern reality for an ordinary expat is less cinematic and more administrative attrition. You cannot simply walk into a branch and open an account the way you might in London or Toronto. You need a Swiss address, which requires a lease, which in most cities requires you to have already beaten off dozens of other applicants in a housing market designed by someone who hates joy. Then you need your residence permit, or at minimum proof that one is being processed, and even then some banks will look at you the way a sommelier looks at a box of table wine.

The appointment itself is a masterclass in unbothered formality. Nobody is rude. Nobody rushes. You will sit across from someone in a spotless blazer who asks precise questions about the source of your funds, your employer, and occasionally your general life plan, and you will answer as though under mild interrogation, because you are. Switzerland does not do banking casually because the entire economy is staked on the idea that Swiss financial institutions are boring, careful, and slow enough that nothing embarrassing ever happens. It works. It is also maddening if you simply want somewhere to put your salary.

Fees deserve their own paragraph of contempt. Accounts that are marketed as accessible will nickel-and-dime you for paper statements, for falling under a minimum balance, for the audacity of using a card abroad. Swiss precision extends, unsurprisingly, to extracting money from you in small, well-documented increments.

## India: Bureaucracy as Contact Sport

India's banking bureaucracy operates on a different principle entirely: nothing is impossible, but everything requires proof, of everything, always. The country has done something genuinely remarkable with Aadhaar, the biometric ID system that has turned account opening from a week-long ordeal into, in the best cases, an afternoon. Video KYC has accelerated this further. If you are an NRI or foreign national with your paperwork in order, India can now move faster than Switzerland ever will.

The catch is "paperwork in order," a phrase that in India expands to fill any available time. PAN card, address proof, passport, visa, sometimes an employer letter, sometimes a local reference, and always, always, more photocopies than seems rational. Every branch appears to interpret the rules with slightly different local flourishes, so the account-opening process that worked for your colleague in Bangalore may not work for you in Pune, for reasons nobody will fully explain. The man behind the counter is not being obstructive for sport; he is following a system layered with decades of regulation, fraud prevention and institutional caution, and he has clearly done this dance ten thousand times today alone.

What India has over Switzerland is warmth inside the chaos. Someone will usually take pity on you, explain the missing form, point you to the right queue. Switzerland's system is frictionless once you clear the wall; India's wall never fully disappears, but people keep helping you climb it.

## The Verdict

If I had to choose which bureaucracy to fight with a hangover, I would pick India's, because at least someone will make eye contact with me while I suffer. Switzerland's process is more efficient once you clear the initial vetting, but the vetting itself is designed to make you feel like a mild liability, and Swiss institutions rarely apologise for that. India's system is messier, more redundant, and occasionally will require the same document four times in four formats — but it comes with humanity attached, and increasingly, with digital shortcuts that put "efficient" Switzerland to shame. Advantage: India, on warmth and momentum. Switzerland wins on polish, and loses on soul.

## Conclusion

Both countries will make you feel, at some point, like banking is a privilege extended to you out of pure institutional mercy rather than a basic service you are paying for. Switzerland does it with a raised eyebrow and a lease requirement that borders on cruelty. India does it with a stack of forms and a stranger's signature you didn't know you needed. Neither is fixing this any time soon, so bring documents, bring patience, and bring the low expectations that have served every expat well since banking was invented. You will get the account eventually. You will simply never feel like you deserved it.
