# A Tale of Two Trolleys: Getting Sick in China vs Ireland

🇨🇳 China 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nothing clarifies your relationship with a country quite like needing a doctor in it. Suddenly the charming inefficiencies you'd been photographing for Instagram — the queueing, the paperwork, the man shouting at a vending machine — become matters of your actual spine. I have broken bones in four countries and been diagnosed with "probably fine, go home" in six, and I can report that nowhere teaches you more about a nation's soul than its waiting room.

China and Ireland present two entirely different philosophies of getting you seen. One treats healthcare as a logistics problem to be solved with volume, cash, and a bit of queue-jumping if you know which floor to ask for. The other treats it as a moral question the state keeps almost, but not quite, answering. Both will leave you lying on something uncomfortable for longer than you'd like. Only one will charge you a trolley fee for the privilege.

## Do's & Don'ts: China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get international health insurance with direct billing before you land | Assume your employer's "insurance" covers more than a sick note |
| Head straight for the VIP or international ward at a major hospital | Expect privacy — people wander into exam rooms mid-consultation |
| Learn the Mandarin for your actual medical history, not just "hello" | Rely on a translation app for anything involving a needle |
| Carry your passport and insurance card everywhere, always | Go to a smaller clinic hoping it'll be "basically the same" |

## Do's & Don'ts: Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a GP within your first month, before you're sick | Wait until you need urgent bloodwork to find a doctor |
| Get private health insurance even if you think you're "healthy" | Assume A&E means "accident and emergency," not "all night" |
| Ask your GP about a GP Visit Card if your income is modest | Expect same-day specialist care outside Dublin, Cork or Galway |
| Keep a European or home-country prescription backup plan | Picture the public system as a safety net — it's more of a queue |

## China: Cash, Clinics, and the VIP Floor

China's healthcare system runs on a split personality that expats learn to navigate fast. On one side sits a vast public system covering roughly nine in ten residents through social insurance tied to employment, administered locally and wildly uneven in quality depending on whether you're in Shanghai or somewhere the hospital still has a 1990s waiting room and a doctor who learned English from a textbook. On the other side sits the parallel universe expats actually live in: VIP wards, international clinics, and private hospitals like Beijing United Family or Shanghai's expat-favoured chains, where the floors are clean, the staff speak English, and the bill reflects both facts generously.

The Shanghai Municipal Government is admirably blunt about the mechanics here. Foreign employees working legally must be enrolled in social insurance — the same package locals get, contributed to by both employer and employee, registered within thirty days of your work permit landing. On paper, that's parity. In practice, expats on ExpatExchange describe a system where the real decision is which hospital, not which insurance. One Shenzhen patient rated Peking University Hospital an A-minus for its sixth-floor VIP ward and dedicated interpreter, then gave a newer, supposedly nicer Hong Kong University-affiliated hospital a flat D because, as they put it, "VERY FEW GENERAL STAFF SPEAK ENGLISH" and what they did speak was, in their words, "Chinglish." That's not snobbery. That's someone who needed to explain a symptom and couldn't.

The genuinely startling part, for anyone arriving fresh from a Western system, is the price transparency and the privacy vacuum existing side by side. Dental crowns and a root canal for around $1,200. Diabetes and blood pressure medication for roughly 400 yuan a month — a fraction of US costs. Glasses quoted at 3,500 yuan in a hospital pharmacy, then found for 1,028 yuan two blocks away, because nobody in China assumes the first price is the real price, least up to and including your hospital. Meanwhile, exam room doors stay open, other patients drift in mid-consultation, and the Western instinct toward sealed, hushed, HIPAA-flavoured medical privacy simply does not exist. You get efficiency and transaction clarity. You do not get to be precious about who sees your chart.

The practical upshot: China's healthcare works brilliantly for expats who treat it like a market — shop the hospital, buy the insurance that actually pays directly, learn enough Mandarin to describe pain without miming it, and never, ever assume the cheap clinic down the road is "basically the same" as the one with the English-speaking cardiologist three subway stops away. It isn't.

## Ireland: Free in Theory, Seven Hours in Practice

Ireland's healthcare pitch sounds, on paper, like the adult in the room: a public system, the HSE, theoretically there for everyone, means-tested medical cards covering GP visits, prescriptions and hospital care for those who qualify, and a GP Visit Card for people who don't quite qualify for a full card but still need subsidised access — available to anyone "living in Ireland," income permitting, no citizenship test required. It is, structurally, a kinder document than anything China's split-tier system produces.

Then you actually get sick, and theory meets the trolley.

The INMO's TrolleyWatch data from February 2025 does not flatter: over 11,000 patients admitted without a bed that month alone, more than 25,000 across January and February combined, including over 250 children, officially the worst February for overcrowding since counting began in 2006. University Hospital Limerick alone logged nearly 2,000 trolley cases in a single month. These aren't fringe anecdotes — they're the HSE's own watchdog organisation, counting bodies on corridors.

Reddit's r/ireland supplies the texture the statistics can't. One poster described waiting 90 minutes for a routine GP appointment only to be sent on to pathology — which had already closed. Another, in a thread titled, with admirable understatement, "Healthcare is a joke… again and again," recounted two separate waits of 18 and 14 hours at a Letterkenny A&E within a single month, summarising the triage philosophy as: "Unless you were basically dying you got sent home with paracetamol." A third commenter's suggested workaround — faint, or pretend to — tells you everything about how expats learn to game a system built on good intentions and chronic underfunding.

ExpatExchange's compiled accounts split cleanly down the public-private line: GPs doing house calls for modest fees, genuinely lovely, personal care — right alongside a Galway expat's description of 30 hours on a trolley with a broken collarbone and ribs at University College Hospital Galway, which they called "old, dirty, and short of doctors." Private insurance, several expats agree, isn't a luxury here — it's "absolutely essential," running around €160 a month, with the caveat that policies exclude plenty and chronic conditions can make coverage a fight.

## The Verdict

If you want transactional clarity and don't mind paying for it, China wins outright — you know exactly what you're buying, the VIP ward exists specifically so money can solve your problem, and nobody pretends otherwise. If you want the moral comfort of universal coverage but can tolerate watching that promise buckle in real time on a hospital corridor, Ireland offers something more complicated: free-in-principle care that frequently becomes very expensive in wasted hours, private top-up insurance you'll feel foolish not having, and a GP system that's genuinely warm when it isn't overwhelmed.

Neither country has solved healthcare. China solved it for people with the right insurance and the right postcode. Ireland solved it for people with the patience of a saint and a spare 18 hours. I'd rather overpay in Shanghai than triage myself in Letterkenny, but ask me again after my next flu season and I may have changed my mind entirely.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *ExpatExchange, Shenzhen healthcare thread — a patient treated at a newer, supposedly higher-standard hospital rated it poorly, writing that almost no general staff spoke English and describing the communication as "Chinglish," despite the facility's modern appearance.*

> *Reddit r/ireland, "Healthcare is a joke… again and again" — a commenter recounted two separate waits of 18 and 14 hours at a regional A&E within one month, summarising triage as: unless you were basically dying, you were sent home with paracetamol.*

> *expat.com China forum — recurring theme across posts is that smaller, cheaper clinics are a false economy, with posters repeatedly steering newcomers toward major hospitals' international or VIP wards despite the higher cost, citing hygiene and staff competence concerns at budget facilities.*

## Conclusion

Here is the thing nobody puts on the tourism board website: healthcare is the only part of expat life where "going local" can genuinely hurt you. Everywhere else, blending in is the goal. In a Chinese clinic or an Irish A&E, blending in means accepting whichever system has been quietly rationing itself for decades before you arrived, and that system does not care how charming your relocation story is. Buy the good insurance. Learn the real words, not the polite ones. And accept that "universal" and "efficient" are, apparently, mutually exclusive concepts that no nation on this list has yet been clever enough to reconcile. Pack paracetamol. Pack patience. Pack a second opinion.
