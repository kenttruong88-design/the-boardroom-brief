# One Country Has a Forest Doing the Work an Entire Park System Should, the Other Turned Its Whole Existence Into a Trailhead

🇽🇰 Kosovo vs 🇱🇮 Liechtenstein
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Kosovo is a young country, in every sense that matters, with a capital that's still figuring out where the green is supposed to go. Liechtenstein is a country so small you could plausibly hike its entire north-south length before lunch and still have time for a fondue, and it has responded to this absurd compactness by turning nearly the whole thing into a trail network. One is improvising civic green space out of a single, beloved, slightly overworked forest. The other has simply made the outdoors the entire point of the country.

I've walked both. Germia deserves more credit than it gets, and Liechtenstein's hiking culture deserves slightly less reverence than the tourist board would like you to believe.

## Do's & Don'ts: Kosovo

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Make the trip out to Germia National Park — it's genuinely Pristina's green lungs and worth the taxi fare | Expect a network of smaller neighbourhood parks to fill the gap; Pristina simply doesn't have many yet |
| Go on a weekend morning to see Germia at its social best, packed with joggers, families, and picnics | Assume the city is short on nature entirely — the hills ringing Pristina offer real, underused alternatives |
| Support newer municipal green initiatives as Pristina slowly catches up on planning | Judge Kosovo's green-space culture by Western European park density; the comparison isn't useful here |
| Bring your own water and shade — Germia's infrastructure is real but not lavish | Expect manicured, over-managed grounds; the appeal here is a working forest, not a groomed lawn |

## Do's & Don'ts: Liechtenstein

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Walk the Liechtenstein Trail — a genuinely well-marked route touching every municipality in the country | Assume "hiking the whole country" is some extraordinary feat; it's a pleasant day, not an expedition |
| Head up to Malbun for proper Alpine scenery without needing to leave the country at all | Expect Swiss-style crowds and infrastructure everywhere; much of it is quieter and more modest |
| Respect posted private land and pasture boundaries — Liechtenstein's access rights are generous but not absolute | Treat every green space as a national park; some of what looks wild is actively farmed land |
| Time Alpine hikes around the weather — conditions shift fast at altitude even on a small map | Underestimate the terrain just because the country itself is tiny; the mountains don't know that |

## Kosovo: A Capital Leaning Entirely on One Forest, and Somehow Making It Work

Pristina's relationship with green space is, to put it kindly, concentrated. The Kosovo Agency of Statistics doesn't paint a flattering picture of formal municipal park coverage per capita, and anyone who's spent a hot July afternoon looking for shade in the city centre already knows this without needing the spreadsheet. What Pristina has instead is Germia National Park, a genuinely substantial forested reserve on the city's edge, and Germia is doing an amount of civic heavy lifting that would be unreasonable to ask of any single green space anywhere else.

And to its credit, Germia delivers. Weekend mornings bring joggers, families with grills, teenagers doing considerably more standing-around-looking-cool than actual hiking, and a genuinely lively, communal atmosphere that plenty of cities with far more formal parkland never quite manage to generate in their own designated spaces. The Ministry of Environment, Spatial Planning and Infrastructure has treated Germia's upkeep as a real priority, and it shows in ways the rest of Pristina's green infrastructure frankly doesn't.

The problem is everything else. Outside Germia, Pristina is a city of concrete, ambitious new construction, and a startling shortage of the small neighbourhood parks and green pockets that make daily urban life bearable without requiring a taxi ride. Kosovo is a young state rebuilding fast, and green infrastructure has understandably not been first in the queue behind housing, roads, and institutions. It's an honest, forgivable gap, but a gap all the same, and Germia can only carry so much of the weight before the seams show.

## Liechtenstein: A Country So Small It Basically Turned Itself Into a Hiking Trail

Liechtenstein solves the green-space question by having so little land that essentially none of it is far from a mountain, a meadow, or a marked trail. The Liechtenstein Trail — a signed route running the length of the country through all eleven municipalities — is the tourist-brochure headline, and it's a genuinely pleasant walk, but the real story is how thoroughly outdoor access is baked into daily life here rather than cordoned off into designated park zones.

Malbun, up in the eastern mountains, gives residents proper Alpine hiking and skiing without anyone needing to cross a border, and the Amt für Umwelt's trail network statistics suggest a density of maintained paths that's almost comic relative to the country's population of roughly forty thousand people. This is a place where "going for a walk" and "leaving civilisation entirely" can happen within the same twenty minutes, and locals treat that as thoroughly unremarkable.

What I'd push back on is the mythology. Liechtenstein Marketing sells the country's hiking culture as some singular, extraordinary offering, and it's good — genuinely good — but it's also just what happens when a small, wealthy, mountainous country with almost no urban sprawl invests properly in trail maintenance. It's less a cultural miracle and more an inevitability of geography plus a functioning budget, and I think the country deserves credit for competent stewardship rather than for having invented the concept of a nice walk.

## The Verdict

Liechtenstein wins on raw provision, and it isn't especially close — a small, wealthy Alpine microstate with a fully maintained trail network touching every corner of the country is always going to outperform a young capital still building its basic civic infrastructure. But Germia gets a real mention here, because a single forest doing that much social and ecological work for an entire city, and doing it with genuine warmth and community energy, is its own kind of achievement, and one Liechtenstein's quieter, tidier trails don't attempt to replicate.

If you want green space that feels alive with people, go to Germia on a Sunday. If you want green space that feels like the entire country was built around it, go to Liechtenstein and start walking. Just don't expect either one to apologise for what it isn't.

## Conclusion

Kosovo and Liechtenstein arrive at green space from wildly different starting points — one a young capital leaning on a single beloved forest while the rest of its infrastructure catches up, the other a tiny, wealthy country that turned its entire modest footprint into a trail network almost by default. Go to Germia for a green space with a genuine social pulse. Go to Liechtenstein if you want to say you hiked an entire country before dinner. Just manage your expectations accordingly, because neither one is pretending to be something it isn't — which, frankly, is more than I can say for most tourist boards.
