# Vietnam Has No Traffic Rules, Only Traffic Faith. Denmark Has So Many Bicycle Rules You'll Get Silently Judged By A Toddler On A Cargo Bike.

🇻🇳 🇩🇰 *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Crossing a street is not, in most of the world, a skill. It's a reflex, learned in childhood and never examined again. Then you arrive in Hanoi, where the crosswalk is a suggestion, the traffic light is a suggestion, and the only reliable law of physics is that if you walk at a slow, steady pace directly into a wall of oncoming motorbikes, they will part around you like water around a very frightened rock. Then you arrive in Copenhagen, where crossing incorrectly against a bike lane will get you a look so cold it functions as its own weather system, delivered by someone's grandmother on an electric cargo bike doing thirty kilometres an hour with three children strapped in the front.

Both cities move an enormous number of people efficiently. Neither city moves them the way your instincts expect, and both will humble you within your first forty-eight hours.

## Do's & Don'ts: Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Walk at a slow, predictable, unwavering pace when crossing — the traffic is calculating your trajectory in real time | Stop suddenly mid-crossing or run; unpredictability is the one thing that actually causes accidents |
| Get a proper helmet before buying or renting a motorbike, even though many locals skip theirs — police checks and hospitals do not care about local custom | Expect painted lane markings, traffic lights, or right-of-way to mean anything close to what they mean at home |
| Use Grab (the regional ride-hailing app) for anything unfamiliar — pricing is transparent and drivers know routes cars physically cannot take | Haggle over a fixed Grab fare with a driver directly; the app price is the price |

## Do's & Don'ts: Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Signal with your hand before turning on a bike, every single time — Danish cyclists do this reflexively and expect it back | Walk, stand, or linger in a marked bike lane, ever, for any reason; this is treated as a minor act of aggression |
| Buy or borrow a bike within your first month — it is genuinely the fastest, most normal way to get anywhere in Copenhagen | Assume bike lanes are casual or optional; rush-hour cycling traffic is as fast and disciplined as a motorway |
| Learn the specific bike-lane overtaking etiquette (ring your bell, pass on the left) before your first commute | Be surprised when a bike carries an entire Danish family, a week's groceries, and a dog, all at once, faster than you |

## Vietnam: Traffic As Negotiated Chaos That Somehow Works

Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City run on an estimated tens of millions of motorbikes between them, a number so large that traffic doesn't so much flow as breathe — expanding to fill every inch of usable road, contracting around obstacles, self-organising without much reference to painted lines or traffic lights, which exist mostly as gentle suggestions rather than enforceable law. To a first-time visitor this looks like guaranteed death. To everyone who has lived there more than a month, it becomes a strangely elegant system: because everyone is moving slowly and everyone is watching everyone else, the actual accident rate for pedestrians who cross with calm, steady confidence is far lower than the visual chaos suggests.

The real skill isn't learning rules — there mostly aren't any that matter in practice — it's learning to read intention. A motorbike edging into a gap is not aggression, it's just the physical language of the road. Horns are used constantly but mean "I am here," not "get out of my way." Sidewalks, confusingly, are often used as parking or extra shop frontage, which pushes pedestrians into the road anyway, which somehow everyone has made peace with.

What genuinely works well underneath the apparent anarchy is the ride-hailing layer: Grab has professionalised what used to be an opaque, haggle-heavy xe ôm (motorbike taxi) system into something with fixed pricing, GPS tracking, and driver ratings, without losing the responsiveness that lets a bike squeeze through gaps no car ever could. It's the best version of Vietnamese urban mobility: informal chaos, professionally intermediated.

## Denmark: Traffic As Civic Religion

Copenhagen has spent five decades deliberately, systematically re-engineering itself around the bicycle, and the result is not just infrastructure — it's a genuine cultural identity. Roughly half of all trips to work or school in the city happen by bike, in all weather, including horizontal January sleet that would send most nations straight back indoors. Cargo bikes, the Danish "christiania bike," ferry children, groceries, dogs, and the occasional sofa through dedicated, physically separated bike lanes that are frequently wider and better maintained than the car lanes beside them.

What surprises newcomers isn't the infrastructure, though — it's the etiquette, which is enforced socially with a rigour that puts most countries' actual traffic police to shame. Hand signals before every turn. Ringing a bell, not shouting, to request a pass. A specific unspoken hierarchy at rush hour where faster commuters filter left and slower riders, tourists, and the confused stay right, understood instantly by everyone except the person visiting from a country where none of this was ever taught. Step into a bike lane by accident and you will not be shouted at — Danes don't really do confrontation — you'll simply receive The Look, a flat, unimpressed stare that somehow communicates centuries of disappointment in under two seconds.

Public transport, where it exists, is efficient, clean, and almost entirely unnecessary if you own a bike, which is itself the point: Copenhagen didn't build a transport system around cars or trains, it built one around the assumption that most people, most days, would simply pedal.

## The Verdict

Vietnam wins on sheer improvisational competence — a traffic system with almost no formal rules that somehow moves millions of people daily through pure collective intuition. Denmark wins on discipline — a traffic system with an enormous number of unwritten rules, all of them enforced with quiet, devastating social pressure. If you want chaos that works, Hanoi. If you want order that will silently shame you into compliance within a week, Copenhagen. I've nearly been clipped by a motorbike carrying a wardrobe in Hanoi and I've been death-stared by an eight-year-old on a cargo bike in Copenhagen, and honestly, the eight-year-old was scarier.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/vietnam — a visitor described standing frozen at a crossing for ten minutes before a local simply took their arm and walked them across at a steady pace, insisting stopping was the only actual danger.*

> *Reddit r/Denmark — an expat recounted accidentally standing in a bike lane while checking a map and receiving what they called "the most devastating silent stare of my adult life" from a passing commuter.*

> *expat.com Copenhagen — a new parent described buying a cargo bike within their first month and realising, three weeks later, they had entirely forgotten what owning a car used to feel like.*

## Conclusion

Neither country will let you keep your old instincts about traffic, and both will replace them with something more honest than what you brought. Vietnam teaches you that rules are optional if trust is universal. Denmark teaches you that freedom is what happens when everyone agrees, without saying so, to follow the same unwritten code. Bring steady nerves to Hanoi. Bring a bell, hand signals, and total humility to Copenhagen. And in both cities, watch out for the eight-year-olds. They will outmanoeuvre you every time.
