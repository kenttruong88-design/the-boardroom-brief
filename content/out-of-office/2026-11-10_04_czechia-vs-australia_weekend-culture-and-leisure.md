# Czechs Flee the City for a Shed in the Woods. Australians Flee the City for a Beach. Both Call It Relaxing

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every Friday afternoon, two nations stage a mass evacuation from their cities. One heads for the coast with an esky, a towel and a sunburn it has already agreed to deny. The other drives to the countryside, pulls on rubber boots and attacks a hedge. Kevin Loo, an Australian-raised writer married to a Czech, put it neatly for Radio Prague: where an Australian asks whether you are going up the coast or down the coast, a Czech asks which cabin you are going to. The question is the same. The destination is a discipline.

The difference, of course, is that an Australian goes away to do nothing and a Czech goes away to do extraordinarily specific things to a garden. If you are an executive relocating between them, you will either be deprived of the sea or deprived of the right to lie still. This is a guide to the weekend, that sacred period in which a nation reveals its true values to anyone watching carefully.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Czechia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the invitation to the chata, and bring something for the grill | Expect to lie in a hammock and be left alone; weeding is the approved activity |
| Eat the buřty and špekáčky sausages with enthusiasm | Be seen to be idle; relaxing is read as a character flaw |
| Pack for ticks, nettles, mosquitoes and a very early night | Expect to open the windows with the lights on |
| Praise the fresh air out loud on arrival | Forget the shared cucumbers and preserves, which are a form of currency |

**Australia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan a trip up or down the coast | Assume the nearest decent beach is a short drive in every city |
| Embrace the barbecue, the esky and the long outdoor lunch | Insist on structure; the weekend is allowed to go wrong |
| Bring sun protection and a hat, as the climate demands | Treat the surf with disrespect or turn your back on the water |
| Make small talk about the weather, the footy or the traffic on the way out | Turn up with a rigid itinerary and a clipboard |

## Czechia: A Nation That Relaxes by Doing Chores in a Cottage

The Czech weekend is built around the chata or chalupa, the cottage or cabin. According to Radio Prague, about 20% of Czech domestic buildings are cabins or cottages, more than half a million in total, and the country ranks fifth for cottages per person, behind the Scandinavians. The tradition has deep roots: it grew after the 1950s, when people resettled houses emptied by expelled Germans, especially in the Sudetenland. And in the communist era, as Kevin Loo notes, the cottage was a place to be yourself, away from the secret police and nosy neighbours. That is not leisure. That is resistance with a barbecue.

The numbers show a nation in slow retreat from the habit. A daily-newspaper poll cited by Radio Prague found that 19% of Czechs spent weekends at a cottage in 2001, and by 2007 the figure was under 8%. Michal Serf, who owns a cottage in the Eagle Mountains inherited from grandparents who bought it in the 1940s, told the broadcaster he has considered selling it but that it is part of his life. Martina Franklová calls her grandmother's cottage near Poděbrady not as relaxing as she would like, but she goes often and plans to take her future child. If that is not the national mood in two sentences, I do not know what is.

For the visiting expat, the real comedy lives in the rules. Elizabeth Zahradnicek-Haas, writing for Expats.cz about her husband's family cottage on the Sázava, lists eleven survival rules including praising the fresher air on arrival and avoiding being seen to relax, because weeding is the expected activity. Dinner features buřty and špekáčky sausages, a Májka pâté, and bublanina cake made in a Remoska. Bedtime is around eight or nine because the windows can only be opened with the lights off, thanks to the mosquitoes. The hazards include ticks, nutria, swans and nettles. It is the most Central European paragraph ever written, and I mean that as praise.

It is worth pausing on how revealing the cottage statistics are about national character. A people who have been told, across generations, that their free time must be productive will take a shed in the woods and give it a to-do list. The Czech weekend is a form of quiet dissent from the idea that rest should be pleasurable. You rest by repairing, tending, stacking and preserving, and the satisfaction at the end is the satisfaction of a thing done. I find this oddly admirable and would not survive it for longer than a long weekend.

## Australia: A Country That Calls Lying on Sand a Lifestyle

Australia's weekend is the mirror image. The coast is the default destination, the beach the town square, and the barbecue the national sacrament. Ask an Australian what they did at the weekend and the answer will involve water, a meat product, and an adverb like "just". The Czech weekend asks you to labour, to contribute, to carry your weight in cucumbers. The Australian weekend asks only that you turn up with a hat and an opinion about the cricket.

I will not pretend the sources dug into Australia as deeply as they did the cottage; the single Australian-raised writer in the file, Kevin Loo, offers a loving, slightly wistful comparison and mourns the ocean views he misses on Czech trips. That sets the tone. Australian leisure is outward-facing, public and weather-dependent. The beach, the park, the pool and the pub are communal spaces, and a good weekend there involves bumping into people you know.

What an executive will notice is how unstructured it feels compared with a chata weekend. There is no drive to a family property with a list of tasks. There is a loose plan, a lot of sunlight and the permission to improvise. The cost, as always, is the sun itself, which is a real hazard, and the long drive to a decent stretch of coast in a big country. But the reward is the sense of being allowed to exist.

There is an obvious temptation to cast the Australian version as shallow and the Czech version as profound, and I resist it. Lying on a beach with friends is not an empty act; it is an affirmation that nothing needs to be earned that afternoon. The two nations have simply decided that rest means different things. For the Czech, rest is what you do once the work is finished. For the Australian, rest is what you do instead of finishing it. Anyone moving between the two should expect a mild identity crisis around three in the afternoon on a Saturday.

## The Verdict

Australia wins for pure relaxation; Czechia wins for sheer character. If you want a weekend that restores you, go to the coast. If you want a weekend that makes you a better storyteller, go to the cottage. I would choose Australia on a Saturday and Czechia on a Sunday, and then resent myself on Monday for having the stamina for neither.

The cold truth is that the chata is a dying institution wearing the costume of an eternal one, and I rather love it for that. The poll data show it fading. The people interviewed are loyal to it out of memory and obligation more than pleasure. Australian leisure, by contrast, has no nostalgic burden. It simply happens, in daylight, on a beach.

## Conclusion

You can learn a great deal about a country from how it spends its Saturday. The Czechs go to the woods and perform manual labour as a form of love. The Australians go to the water and do nothing as a form of freedom. Neither is wrong, and both are wildly misunderstood by outsiders who treat leisure as the absence of effort.

So pick your poison, pack the right clothes, and expect to be misunderstood. In Czechia, bring boots and a taste for sausage. In Australia, bring sunscreen and no ambition whatsoever. And in both, try to remember the golden rule of the weekend: whatever you are doing, do it with people. Everything else is just scenery.
