# Everyone In Nigeria Knows Your Business. In Britain, Nobody Will Admit They Know Your Name

🇳🇬 Nigeria vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Nigeria, your neighbours will attend your child's naming ceremony, borrow your extension cable, and know by sundown that your generator is the loud one with the loose exhaust pipe. In the United Kingdom, your neighbours will achieve a lifelong, almost monastic devotion to never finding out your name, while simultaneously cataloguing your bin day, your recycling habits, and the make of car your "friend" drives on Tuesday nights, via a net curtain twitched exactly four centimetres.

Both countries will insist, with total sincerity, that theirs is a warm and communal culture. One of them is lying. I have lived in both long enough to tell you which, and it will not surprise anyone who has ever tried to borrow a cup of sugar in Surrey.

## Do's & Don'ts: Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join or at least pay into the estate residents' association — it funds the generator, the gate security, and your social standing in one invoice | Skip the service charge and expect the borehole pump to keep running out of civic goodwill |
| Greet the gatemen, the "Baba" who sells recharge cards, and literally everyone on the compound walkway, every single time | Walk past a neighbour in silence — it reads as open hostility, not British reserve |
| Expect spontaneous visits, especially around Sunday church hours and naming ceremonies | Assume a closed gate or locked door is a hint; knock again, louder |
| Keep a UPS and a backup fuel plan — the whole street's social rhythm runs on who has power and who doesn't | Run your generator at 2am without warning the block — that is a declarable act of war |

## Do's & Don'ts: United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wave. A small, economical, non-committal wave. That is the entire friendship | Knock unannounced to introduce yourself — this is considered, at minimum, an act of aggression |
| Join the residents' or parish association if you want planning disputes handled with maximum procedural fury | Mistake a returned "lovely weather" greeting for the start of an actual relationship |
| Mow your lawn, mind your hedge height, and sort your recycling with liturgical precision | Let your bins sit out a day past collection — the street WhatsApp group will notice and will not forgive |
| Accept that borrowing sugar is now a folk legend, not a practice; buy your own | Ask a neighbour anything about their income, relationship, or why the removal van came at 11pm |

## Nigeria: The Estate Is the Village, Whether You Signed Up for It or Not

Nobody in Nigeria lives next to their neighbours; they live *with* them, in the full biblical sense, whether the compound is three bungalows in Surulere or a fortified cul-de-sac on Banana Island. ExpatArrivals' own breakdown of Lagos neighbourhoods tells you everything about the city's social architecture in its place names alone — the "Government Reserved Area" in Ikeja, the walled exclusivity of Ikoyi and Banana Island, the sprawling gated communities rising in Lekki. Nigerian community life is organised, quite literally, around the wall and the gate: who is inside it, who pays for it, and who is allowed through.

That wall does not insulate you from your neighbours — it concentrates you with them. The compound, not the street, is the unit of belonging, and it runs on a parallel system of government that no embassy briefing quite prepares you for: the residents' association. As the Nigerian property platform Ownkey lays out in granular detail, a functioning estate association is a small civic enterprise — registered with the Corporate Affairs Commission as Incorporated Trustees, electing its own officials, drafting a constitution, and setting a service charge that covers security, landscaping, borehole maintenance, and — this is the load-bearing line item — generator fuel and servicing. Ownkey calls a depleted reserve fund "the single most common cause of estate association distress in Nigeria," which is corporate-governance language for: if nobody pays, the whole street sits in the dark together, and everybody will remember who didn't pay.

That shared infrastructure breeds a shared life. A 2015 Foreign Service report from a US diplomatic compound in Abuja, filed on TalesMag, is almost comically on-brand: residents socialise over compound BBQs and borrowed pool access, switch generators on and off "several times a day and night," and describe power as "poor on its best day" — and yet the social calendar, not the power grid, is what holds the place together. Domestic staff, gatemen, and the "Baba" running the kiosk outside are not peripheral figures; they are information nodes in a neighbourhood that runs substantially on word of mouth, church networks, and who-saw-what-at-the-market.

The result is a neighbourhood culture with almost no concept of privacy as the British understand it, and an enormous, well-rehearsed concept of mutual obligation. You will be visited without notice. You will be asked direct questions about your marriage, your salary, and your God. You will also never, under any genuinely dire circumstance, be left to handle it alone — which is more than I can say for the alternative.

## United Kingdom: Community, Theoretically, Viewed Through Glass

The United Kingdom has built an entire architecture of community — parish councils, residents' associations, neighbourhood watch schemes, village fetes with a genuinely alarming number of competitive categories for jam — and then populated it with people who would rather set themselves on fire than knock on a neighbour's door unannounced. It is, structurally, one of the most organised community systems I have lived under. It is, socially, a masterclass in warmth withheld.

The numbers back up what any resident already senses. A 2019 survey of 2,000 UK adults, commissioned by Lottoland and reported by Ideal Home, found that roughly three-quarters of people regard their neighbours as acquaintances at best, more than half have no interest in getting to know the people next door better, and nearly a quarter actively worry about seeming "too keen" if they try. The average adult can name five people on their own street. One in twenty cannot name a single one. And yet — this is the plot twist — 39% have brought in a neighbour's bins, a fifth have fed a neighbour's pet, and nearly a quarter have watered a neighbour's plants on holiday. The British will perform acts of genuine neighbourly care for someone whose name they have never learned. It is community as a set of discreet, deniable transactions, never an actual relationship.

A long-running Mumsnet thread on unfriendly neighbours reads like a sociological field study of this exact contradiction. One poster, thirty years in London, reports she was "never really friendly with any neighbours" despite decades on the same street — just greetings and parcel-minding. Others note the regional fault line sharply: move from the South East, where "not speaking to neighbours was normal," to Yorkshire or Somerset, and the silence itself becomes the oddity. The residents' association, in this landscape, functions less as a social club and more as a tribunal — a body that exists almost entirely to adjudicate hedge heights, parking disputes, and extension planning objections with the icy procedural fury the British reserve for anything involving property lines.

Net curtains are not a design choice. They are a surveillance system that allows observation without the vulgarity of eye contact.

## The Verdict

Nigeria wins, and it isn't close. Not because Nigerian neighbourhoods are easier — compound living with unreliable power and a residents' association chasing unpaid service charges is its own daily negotiation — but because the obligation runs both ways and everyone knows it. You will be known, fed, questioned, and shown up for, in roughly that order, often on the same day.

Britain has built the finest civic infrastructure for community in the Western world and then quietly declined to use it for its stated purpose. The parish council will debate your fence to the inch. Nobody on it will ever learn your first name. That is not reserve; it is a design decision, executed with total commitment, generation after generation.

If your generator is working and your neighbour's isn't, in Lagos, you will hear about it by evening and probably end up running an extension cable to their freezer. In London, if your neighbour's house burned down, you might read about it in the residents' WhatsApp group three days later, filed under "noise complaint, resolved."

## Conclusion

Here is the uncomfortable truth both countries would deny at a dinner party: Nigeria has community without much privacy, and Britain has privacy without much community, and each side has simply decided its trade-off is the civilised one. I am not interested in diplomacy on this point. A culture where your neighbour will show up uninvited with jollof rice when your mother is ill beats a culture where your neighbour will time your bin collection to the minute and say nothing to your face for a decade. Keep your net curtains, Britain. I'll take the extension cable and the unannounced visitor, and I will not be apologising for it at the parish meeting.
