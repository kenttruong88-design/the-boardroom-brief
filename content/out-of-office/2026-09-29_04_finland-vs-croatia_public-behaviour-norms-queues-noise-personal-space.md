# Finland Queues Two Metres Apart in Silence. Croatia Queues in a Cloud of Opinions. Only One Is Civilisation.

🇫🇮 Finland vs 🇭🇷 Croatia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country has a public-behaviour operating system, and nobody gives you the manual. You find out by breaking the rules, being stared at, and piecing together the logic afterwards like a forensic scientist examining your own social crime scene. Finland and Croatia both have strong operating systems. They are simply opposite ones, written by people who would find each other's version baffling.

Finland's public life runs on the principle that other people are a finite resource and must not be wasted. You don't stand near them, you don't speak to them unnecessarily, and you absolutely do not sit next to them on a bus when another seat exists. Croatia's runs on the principle that other people are the entire point of going outside. You stand near them, you discuss the government with them, and if a queue exists, it is a loose social suggestion open to negotiation. Both nations will tell you theirs is the natural, obvious way to behave. Both are wrong. One is less wrong.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇫🇮 Finland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Leave generous space between yourself and others at bus stops and in queues | Start small talk with strangers in a lift, on a bus or in a sauna changing room |
| Take a ticket number (vuoronumero) at pharmacies, post offices and bakeries | Sit next to someone on public transport if an empty double seat is available |
| Keep your voice low in public places, especially on trains (many have quiet carriages) | Mistake silence for coldness — it's respect, not rejection |
| Stand on the right on escalators and let people pass on the left | Arrive late. Punctuality is treated as a basic courtesy |

**🇭🇷 Croatia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet shopkeepers and café staff with "dobar dan" when you walk in | Expect a tidy single-file queue at a bakery or bus door |
| Hold your ground politely at counters — assertive is normal, meek is invisible | Wear swimwear or go shirtless in old-town streets; many cities fine it |
| Accept loud, animated conversation as friendliness, not aggression | Make noise during the afternoon rest hours in residential buildings |
| Linger over a single coffee for an hour — it's a public right | Rush the waiter. The coffee is an event, not a transaction |

## Finland: The Art of Being Politely Absent

The Finnish bus-stop photo is a genre of internet humour in its own right: half a dozen people spaced along a pavement at perfectly equal intervals, as though the bus stop were a chessboard and everyone a piece refusing to be taken. Foreigners laugh at it. Finns don't understand what's funny. Standing that far apart is simply correct.

Personal space in Finland isn't a bubble; it's a small sovereign territory. You don't enter it without cause. On buses and trams, the protocol is iron: fill every empty double seat with one person before anyone sits beside a stranger. In lifts, conversation is not expected and small talk with neighbours in the stairwell can be met with a polite, slightly startled nod. None of this is hostility. It's a deep cultural belief that not bothering people is itself a form of kindness.

Queuing is an exact science. Many places — pharmacies, post offices, bank branches, some bakeries — use a numbered ticket system, and you'll be waiting quietly with your little slip of paper, watching the screen like it holds the lottery results. Where no tickets exist, lines form spontaneously, precisely and without discussion. Cutting in isn't an argument; it's a social catastrophe that will be met with a silence heavier than any shout.

Noise is managed with the same rigour. Quiet carriages exist on trains. Phone calls are taken in a murmur. Loud groups of tourists on the Helsinki tram are observed with the resigned patience of a nation that has seen this before and will see it again. Silence isn't awkward in Finland. It's comfortable. It's the default. Finns will tell you, earnestly, that you only speak when you have something to say — which makes most of the rest of the world look like it's suffering from a serious nervous condition.

## Croatia: Public Life as Participation Sport

In Croatia, public space is a living room shared with everyone else in town. The café terrace, the riva (waterfront promenade), the market, the pekara (bakery) — these are places where life happens loudly and at close range. Conversations fly across tables. Old men argue about football with volume that would trigger a noise complaint in Helsinki. Children run between legs. Personal space shrinks to roughly the width of a newspaper.

Queues exist in theory. At the bakery counter at 7am, however, the arrangement is closer to a polite scrum: people cluster, make eye contact with the person behind the counter, and communicate their turn through a mixture of timing, confidence and the occasional raised hand. At the bus door in summer, it's every tourist for themselves. The trick isn't to be rude; it's to be present. Hang back meekly and you'll simply cease to exist.

But don't mistake the noise for anarchy. Croatia has firm norms, just different ones. You greet people when entering a shop. You respect the afternoon quiet hours in residential buildings. And in recent years, coastal cities have cracked down hard on tourist behaviour: walking the old town of Split, Hvar or Dubrovnik in swimwear or shirtless can earn a fine, and drunken noise at night draws real penalties. Croatians are loud with one another. They have run out of patience with foreigners who confuse "relaxed Mediterranean culture" with "anything goes".

## The Verdict

Finland wins, and I say this as someone who loves a noisy terrace more than almost anything. Finnish public behaviour is built on one elegantly simple principle: don't impose yourself on strangers. The quiet trams, the numbered tickets, the two metres at the bus stop — it all adds up to a society where public space is calm, predictable and respectful by default. You never have to fight for your turn. You never have to raise your voice. You're left in peace, which in a crowded world is the rarest luxury going.

Croatia is more fun, and I'd rather have dinner there. But fun isn't the question. When you're tired, jet-lagged, trying to buy bread and get to work, the Croatian bakery scrum is a small daily battle, and the Finnish ticket machine is a quiet act of mercy.

Croatia is a great party. Finland is a great place to live next to the party.

## Conclusion

Public behaviour norms are the grammar of a society — invisible until you get them wrong, and then suddenly the only thing anyone notices about you. Finland's grammar is spare and exact; Croatia's is expressive, loud and full of exceptions. Both are coherent. Both punish foreigners who assume their home rules travel.

The mistake is to rank them by warmth. Croatians aren't nicer because they stand closer, and Finns aren't colder because they don't. Each has worked out how to share public space with strangers, and each has arrived at a different peace treaty.

But if you're asking me where I'd rather queue for a prescription on a grey Tuesday with a headache, the answer is the pharmacy with the number machine and the silent Finns. Save the Croatian scrum for Saturday morning, when you've had coffee and can fight back.
