# One Country Will Date You for Six Months Before Calling You a Friend. The Other Will Call You Guapo Before You've Ordered a Drink.

🇷🇺 Russia · 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every culture has a social operating system, and most of them are at least loosely compatible with each other — enough overlap in the protocol that you can muddle through with warmth and good intentions. Russia and Spain are running on entirely different hardware, and if you arrive expecting either one to behave like the other, you will spend your first year either freezing in Moscow's social permafrost or sunburnt from the sheer, relentless warmth of a Madrid terraza at 1am on a Tuesday.

Spain will make you feel instantly, deliriously liked. Russia will make you earn every ounce of affection like it's a state secret being declassified in painful instalments. Both, bafflingly, produce some of the most loyal friendships you'll ever have — they just charge wildly different interest rates to get there.

## Do's & Don'ts: Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept that dinner starts at 9 or 10pm and plan your stomach accordingly | Mistake instant warmth and physical closeness for instant intimacy |
| Join a group chat for your barrio — social life runs through them | Expect anyone to leave a party, a bar, or a conversation "on time" |
| Let kissing hello on both cheeks become reflex, fast | Assume flirtatious banter is a proposition — it's often just Tuesday |
| Learn to love the terraza as a genuine social institution, not a novelty | Take a late cancellation personally; plans here are gloriously fluid |

## Do's & Don'ts: Russia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a slow, formal start even with people who'll later be devoted to you | Mistake Russian directness and lack of small talk for hostility |
| Bring something if invited to someone's home — never arrive empty-handed | Push for closeness faster than it's offered; it backfires, reliably |
| Understand dacha culture before you dismiss a weekend invite to "a shed" | Assume dating apps work the same way — courtship here still has real ritual |
| Treat an invitation into someone's home as the actual milestone it is | Expect a smile from a stranger to mean anything at all; it usually doesn't |

## Spain: Warmth as the Default Setting, Not the Reward

Spain's social contract is deceptively simple and will wreck your instincts within about a week: strangers are warm, touch is constant, and the entire rhythm of the day is built around being around other people rather than merely near them. You'll be kissed on both cheeks by someone you met four minutes ago, called cariño by a shopkeeper, and invited to a cousin's birthday by a colleague you've known since Monday. None of this means what it would mean back home. Spanish warmth is ambient, not targeted — it is simply the temperature of the room, and mistaking it for a personal connection is the single most common and most quietly humiliating error newcomers make.

The dating scene operates on a similarly inverted clock to everything else here. Courtship often starts within a friend group rather than a dating app, moves at a pace that looks glacial by swipe-culture standards, and happens almost entirely in public — bars, terrazas, long group dinners that start at 10pm and somehow produce a plan to go dancing at 1am, which is apparently a reasonable Tuesday. Spaniards will tell you, fondly and often, that nothing good in Spain happens before midnight, and they are correct, and your body will hate them for it for roughly the first three months.

What actually builds real friendship here is sustained, repeated, almost absurdly frequent presence — showing up to the same bar, the same five-a-side game, the same Sunday family lunch, week after week, until you simply become a fixture rather than a guest. Spaniards are not short on warmth; they're short on quick exits. The social calendar is dense, loud, and genuinely inclusive, but it demands a level of in-person, late-night, repeated showing-up that a lot of expats, used to scheduling friendship around a calendar app, find more exhausting than the language barrier ever was.

## Russia: Formality First, Devotion Eventually

Russian social life runs on the opposite principle entirely: nothing is ambient, nothing is extended casually, and the warmth that will eventually floor you has to be earned through a slow, almost ceremonial process that newcomers routinely misread as coldness. A Russian stranger will not smile at you on the metro. This is not rudeness; smiling at strangers is, culturally, read as slightly insincere or even unhinged — the famous "Russian smile" rule holds that a smile should mean something, not function as social lubrication, and expats who arrive expecting friendly customer-service energy everywhere spend their first months feeling quietly rejected by a culture that was simply being honest with its face.

Dating and friendship both start formal and stay formal for far longer than Western newcomers expect — proper introductions, a real courtship with actual gestures rather than ambiguous texting, and a social distance that only closes gradually, deliberately, almost tested for sincerity along the way. But once that distance closes — and it does close, usually through shared hardship, shared meals, or the specific bonding ritual of a banya visit — what you get on the other side is startlingly, disproportionately loyal. Russian friendship, once granted, tends to come with real obligation: showing up at 3am if you need help, feeding you until you can't move, defending you in arguments you didn't even know were happening.

The dacha deserves its own mention, because being invited to someone's countryside dacha for a weekend is not a casual gesture — it is one of the clearest signals in Russian social life that you've actually arrived, socially, and expats who treat it as "a weekend at someone's shed" rather than the milestone it is tend to never get invited again. The whole system front-loads scepticism and back-loads devotion, which is precisely the reverse of Spain, and figuring out which mode you're in determines whether your first year here feels lonely or genuinely, deeply connected.

## The Verdict

Spain gives you instant social sugar and makes you work, slowly and repeatedly, for anything deeper. Russia gives you nothing for free and then, once you've earned it, gives you more loyalty than you know what to do with. If you need to feel liked immediately, take Madrid. If you want friendships that would show up at your door at 3am with soup and no questions asked, earn your way into Moscow instead.

I'd rather be cold for six months and genuinely known than warm for six years and never quite sure if anyone actually meant it.

## Conclusion

Neither social system is broken; they're just calibrated for different climates, literal and emotional. Spain hands out warmth like tapas, generously and to everyone, and expects you to keep showing up for the real thing to develop. Russia withholds warmth entirely until you've proven you're staying, then hands you a loyalty that borders on familial. Go in expecting Spain to be shallow and you'll miss the deep friendships underneath the noise. Go in expecting Russia to be cold forever and you'll never stick around long enough to find out it isn't. Adjust your social thermostat accordingly, and stop checking your phone for texts that, in at least one of these countries, were never going to come.
