# One Country's Bureaucracy Runs Like a Perfectly Oiled Machine That Hates You, the Other's Runs on Vibes and Cigarette Breaks

🇯🇵 Japan vs Greece 🇬🇷
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Opening a bank account in Japan required, among other things, a personal seal (hanko) carved specifically for me, a residence card I'd had for less than the mandatory six months, and a level of paperwork precision that meant one misaligned stamp sent me back to the end of an entirely new queue. Opening one in Greece required, functionally, knowing someone who knew someone, showing up at exactly the right hour before the office closed for its extended lunch, and accepting that the actual rules seemed to shift slightly depending on which clerk I got and how their morning had gone.

Japan's bureaucracy is a masterpiece of process — exhaustive, exacting, utterly indifferent to your feelings about how long it takes, but scrupulously fair in that it applies the same absurd standard to everyone. Greece's bureaucracy runs on something closer to institutional improvisation, shaped as much by personal relationships and the specific mood of the ΔΟΥ tax office that day as by the letter of any actual law. Both will test the outer limits of your patience. Only one will do it with a smile and a frappé.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇯🇵 Japan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your hanko stamp made before you need it for anything official | Try to open a bank account before hitting the typical six-month residency mark |
| Bring every possible document to any bureaucratic appointment, twice over | Expect any exceptions to be made, ever, for your specific situation |
| Learn the correct queue and ticket system at each office in advance | Assume online banking will spare you the in-person visit — it usually won't |

**🇬🇷 Greece**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get an AFM (tax number) immediately — nothing else works without it | Show up right before lunch — offices close for hours, unpredictably |
| Bring a local friend or fixer for your first few bureaucratic visits | Expect the same answer twice from two different clerks on the same rule |
| Use gov.gr for anything that's been digitised — it's genuinely improved | Assume a stated deadline is truly fixed — flexibility runs both ways |

## Japan: Precision as a Barrier and a Promise

Japanese bureaucracy operates on the principle that if a process is followed exactly, correctly, every single time, the outcome will be fair and predictable for everyone — a genuinely admirable philosophy that, in practice, means an enormous amount of your early residency is spent gathering exactly the right documents, in exactly the right order, often duplicated across multiple offices that don't share information with each other. The hanko stamp, a small personal seal used in place of a signature for many official transactions, is a perfect example: you need one to open a bank account, and getting one made properly takes its own separate trip and its own separate wait.

Opening that bank account as a foreigner typically requires a residence card that's been valid for at least six months, proof of address, your hanko, and sometimes a Japanese-speaking guarantor or employer letter, a bundle of requirements that catches new arrivals off guard when they discover they can't simply walk in during their first week and set up basic banking. The process, once you clear the threshold, is executed with striking efficiency and near-zero error — Japanese institutions rarely lose your paperwork or contradict themselves, a real virtue after you've adjusted your expectations to the initial wait.

What makes Japanese bureaucracy feel uniquely exacting is the total absence of "just this once" flexibility. There's no charming clerk who'll wave through a missing document because they like your face. The system is the system, applied with total consistency, which is either deeply reassuring or maddening depending entirely on whether you happen to have the right paper in hand that day.

## Greece: Bureaucracy as Improvisational Theatre

Greek bureaucracy has a reputation, richly earned, for a kind of institutional unpredictability that turns basic administrative tasks into genuine ordeals — office hours that shift without notice, extended lunch closures that swallow the middle of the day, and outcomes that can depend heavily on which specific clerk you get and how the conversation goes, in a way that would be unthinkable in Japan's more rigidly standardised system. The AFM, a tax registration number, is the essential gateway document — nothing else, not a phone contract, not a rental agreement, not a bank account, functions without it — and getting your first one is often the first real initiation into how the system actually operates versus how it's described on paper.

The genuinely useful, and slightly unsettling, secret that longtime expats eventually learn is that personal relationships smooth an enormous amount of friction here. A local friend, a recommended fixer, or simply being a known, friendly face at your neighbourhood tax office can shift outcomes meaningfully in ways that have nothing to do with the formal rules and everything to do with the informal, relationship-based texture of how Greek institutions actually run day to day. This isn't corruption in any serious sense, more a cultural preference for human discretion over rigid process, but it disorients newcomers who arrive expecting a rulebook and instead find a negotiation.

There's real, measurable improvement underway — the gov.gr digital portal has genuinely simplified many processes that used to require an in-person queue, and younger Greek civil servants increasingly push for the kind of standardisation Japan takes for granted. But the underlying culture, the extended lunches, the mood-dependent clerk, the sense that today's rule might not be tomorrow's rule, remains a defining and, once you stop fighting it, oddly endearing feature of getting anything official done here.

## The Verdict

Japan will exhaust you with precision and reward you with total predictability once you've cleared its exacting bar. Greece will exhaust you with unpredictability and reward you with the occasional, delightful human shortcut that no amount of correct paperwork could have earned you in Tokyo. If I need something done correctly and I'm willing to wait exactly as long as I'm told, I want Japan. If I need something done at all, on a Tuesday, by someone who might just like me, I want Greece. Bring documents either way. Bring patience for both, but pack it differently.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/japanlife — a newcomer recounts being turned away from a bank because their residence card was four days short of the six-month requirement, and being told, without apparent irony, to simply come back later.*

> *Internations Athens — an expat describes their AFM registration taking three separate visits across two weeks purely because the office closed for lunch fifteen minutes before they reached the front of the queue, twice.*

> *expat.com Greece forum — a poster advises that the single best investment in your first month is a friendly, patient local who can translate not just the language but the actual unwritten rules of the room.*

## Conclusion

Japan's bureaucracy will turn you into someone who triple-checks every form before submitting it, forever, out of a fear conditioned by genuine early consequences. Greece's bureaucracy will turn you into someone who's learned to bring snacks, a book, and low expectations to any government office, anywhere, for the rest of your life. Both experiences leave a permanent mark on how you approach paperwork. I now photocopy everything twice and have developed an almost supernatural sense for which government office clerk is having a good day. Small mercies, hard won.
