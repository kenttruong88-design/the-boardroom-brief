# Singapore Gives You a Holiday Calendar and Hong Kong Gives You a Reason

🇸🇬 Singapore vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two Chinese-majority port cities built on trade, ambition and an aversion to wasting time. You would expect their calendars to match. They do not. Singapore has four official ethnic strands and a Ministry of Manpower that treats them all with the precision of a Swiss watchmaker. Hong Kong has a calendar that hops between Chinese tradition, British leftovers and a handover anniversary, and has somehow made it all feel like a family argument rather than a policy.

I have celebrated Lunar New Year in both, which means I have eaten two kinds of pineapple tart and been handed two different red packets by two different aunties. The lesson was the same each time. A public holiday is not just a day off. It is a snapshot of what a place thinks it is, and these two are lying to you in very different ways.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇸🇬 Singapore ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the Ministry of Manpower list before booking anything | Assume every holiday is a Monday |
| Greet colleagues for Lunar New Year, Hari Raya and Deepavali alike | Only acknowledge the holiday you grew up with |
| Remember a Sunday holiday spills into the next day | Be surprised when 10 August is also a day off |
| Plan banking before the holiday, since banks close | Assume the shops and restaurants being open means everything is |

| 🇭🇰 Hong Kong ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry spare red packets in the first days of Lunar New Year | Show up to a family visit empty-handed |
| Take Ching Ming and Chung Yeung seriously, as family grave days | Treat them as bonus long weekends and nothing else |
| Book Mid-Autumn dinners and hiking routes early | Expect a quiet harbour at fireworks time |
| Learn the date of Establishment Day on 1 July | Joke about the handover to a person who remembers it |

## 🇸🇬 Singapore

Singapore runs festivals the way it runs trains: by plan, with data, and with very little room for spontaneity. According to Expatica's 2026 guide, the Ministry of Manpower sets 11 national public holidays, plus a day off for voting. The 2026 dates cover Lunar New Year on 17 and 18 February, Hari Raya Puasa on 21 March, Labour Day on 1 May, Vesak Day on 31 May, National Day on 9 August, which fell on a Sunday and therefore pushed the holiday to 10 August, and Deepavali on 8 November, with 9 November also off. Christmas on 25 December closes the list. The Sunday rule is a lovely piece of civic engineering: if a holiday lands on a day you were not going to work anyway, you get another one.

What strikes a newcomer is how deliberately the holidays represent each community. Chinese, Malay, Indian and Christian celebrations sit side by side on the official calendar, which is exactly what a multi-ethnic city-state is meant to do. It is not an accident. It is social policy with a long weekend attached.

The practical quirk, and Expatica is right to flag it, is that many shops and restaurants stay open on official holidays but banks and post offices do not. Which means a holiday in Singapore is a day to eat out, not a day to sort out your life. Hawker stalls and malls carry on, because commerce is a form of respect. The festivals are real, the decorations are splendid and the Chinatown crowds are an event in themselves. But the whole thing is coordinated. If you are looking for spontaneity, you will find it queueing for bak kwa.

Even the holidays that look familiar carry local rules. Vesak Day on 31 May is a Buddhist observance in a country where many residents are not Buddhist, and yet it sits on the calendar as a day for everyone, which is the whole philosophy in one line. Deepavali in November comes with its own light-up in Little India, and the crowds there are the clearest proof that the official list is not a formality. People really do show up. They just do it in an orderly line.

## 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

Hong Kong's calendar is looser and a good deal more emotional. According to Expat Living's 2026 guide, Lunar New Year runs 17 to 19 February, followed by Ching Ming on 6 April, Tuen Ng on 19 June, Mid-Autumn on 26 September, Chung Yeung on 19 October and Boxing Day on 26 December. The Lunar New Year parade draws tens of thousands of visitors each year. Establishment Day on 1 July marks the 1997 handover, usually with military parades and fireworks, and it is the one holiday where you read the room before you open your mouth.

The festivals that matter most in Hong Kong are the ones about family and memory. Chung Yeung is typically marked by hiking, kite-flying and visiting family graves, which is a glorious combination of exercise, remembrance and logistics. Ching Ming does the same job in spring. These are not ceremonial days. They are errands with feeling, and every family has a car park story attached to one.

Mid-Autumn is described by Expat Living as perhaps the most popular Chinese festival after Lunar New Year, and anyone who has stood on a hillside with a lantern and a mooncake that weighs as much as a paperback knows why. It is a night for families to sit together outdoors, eat round things and look at the moon. It is also, in my experience, the most reliably pleasant public holiday in Asia.

Where Singapore uses the calendar to balance communities, Hong Kong uses it to hold on to something. A British colonial administration, a handover and a difficult recent history have all left their mark on what gets celebrated, and the result is a calendar with more texture and less tidiness. There is a sense that the holidays matter because people actually do things on them.

Tuen Ng, on 19 June in 2026, is the Dragon Boat Festival, and it brings races, rice dumplings and an unexpectedly competitive streak out of mild-mannered accountants. A calendar with a boat race in it will always have more story than a calendar with a voting day. That is not a criticism of Singapore's voting day, which is admirably civic. It is simply an observation that nobody has ever written a song about a polling booth.

## The Verdict

Hong Kong wins on feeling, Singapore wins on logistics, and I am going to pick feeling. A festival should leave you with a smell, a story and a slight sunburn. Hong Kong gives you that on a mountain with a kite. Singapore gives you a very well organised queue for a very good meal, which is lovely, but not quite the same thing.

To be fair to Singapore, its calendar is the better piece of policy. Four communities, one list, a rule that rescues you when the holiday falls on a Sunday. If I were designing a civil service I would copy it. It is also the more honest one: it says openly that harmony is something a state builds and maintains.

Hong Kong's version is messier and more human. It trusts people to turn up at graves, on hillsides and under lanterns without a Ministry telling them how. If the choice is between a calendar that works and a calendar that means something, I will take meaning every time, as long as someone else checks the bank is open.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat Living Hong Kong, on Chung Yeung — The holiday is typically marked by hiking, flying kites and visiting family graves, so it is part outing and part memorial.*

> *Expat Living Hong Kong, on Mid-Autumn — It is described as perhaps the most popular Chinese festival after Lunar New Year, so book dinners and viewing spots early.*

## Conclusion

Both cities will give you the days off, and both will expect you to work out the rest. In Singapore the rest is administrative: check the list, mind the Sunday rule, do your banking early. In Hong Kong the rest is personal: show up, bring something to eat and do not make jokes on the first of July.

So pack a red packet and a sense of occasion. Learn which holiday belongs to whom, and keep a chilled drink for the hillside. And when someone in either city tells you that festivals are for tourists, smile politely and remember that a public holiday is the only time anyone in these cities is allowed to stop, which is when you find out what they were running toward.
