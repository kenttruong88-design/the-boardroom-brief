# Nigeria Spends the Weekend Celebrating, Britain Spends It Recovering From the Week

🇳🇬 Nigeria vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A Nigerian weekend is a series of invitations, a Briton's is a series of excuses. In Lagos, Saturday arrives with a dress code, a gift envelope and a dozen people telling you the party starts at noon and means five. In London, Saturday arrives with a sofa, a takeaway and a quiet hope that nobody will ring the doorbell.

I have spent weekends in both countries, and I can tell you that the difference is not energy. It is a question of whether leisure is something you do with other people or something you do to escape them.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇳🇬 Nigeria ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the invitation, and bring a gift or a contribution | Decline a wedding invitation with a polite shrug |
| Dress up, since appearance is part of the occasion | Arrive in casual clothes to an owambe |
| Expect the start time to be generous | Plan a second event for the same afternoon |
| Greet the elders first and do it properly | Walk past without acknowledging the senior guests |

| 🇬🇧 United Kingdom ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Text before dropping in on a friend | Turn up unannounced and expect to be welcomed |
| Take your turn buying a round at the pub | Slip off to the toilet when it is your round |
| Book Sunday lunch at a pub in advance | Assume you can walk in at one o'clock |
| Plan outdoor activities with a wet weather backup | Trust the forecast on a Saturday morning |

## 🇳🇬 Nigeria

The Nigerian weekend is social, noisy and extravagant, and there is no gentler way to put it. Weddings, naming ceremonies, funerals, birthdays and graduation parties fill the calendar, and each one is an event with a guest list that would exhaust a small government. In many families, the weekend is the real working week, and the office is the place you go to recover.

The celebration known as owambe, a Yoruba word for a lavish party, has become shorthand for the whole spirit. Guests dress in matching fabric, known as aso ebi, the music is loud, the food is plentiful, and the dancing is taken seriously. Money is sprayed, jokes are made, and no one is in a hurry. Start times are aspirational. The night, in contrast, has no end time.

Religion shapes the rest. Church services on Sunday are long, lively and social, and Muslim families have their own rhythm of Friday prayers and communal gatherings. Faith is not a hobby but a framework, and Sunday afternoon is spent with relatives, over rice, stew and an unsolicited opinion on your career.

Lagos adds its own layer: beach days, lounges, live music, football on a big screen and traffic that makes every outing a strategic decision. Distance, security and infrastructure influence what you plan, so the sensible expat chooses fewer events and plans the journey like a military operation. A generator is a part of the social plan.

The reward is belonging. Nigerians draw you in fast, and a single invitation can turn into a dozen friends and a standing place at the table. You may leave exhausted, but nobody will ever call you lonely.

Transport and timing shape every plan in a Nigerian city. A journey of ten kilometres in Lagos can take two hours, so people choose their events by neighbourhood and their guests by distance. Weddings often cluster in a few hotels and event halls on a given Saturday, which is why a Lagos weekend is partly an exercise in logistics. The party is the reward for surviving the road.

## 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

The British weekend is quieter, more private and much more concerned with the weather. Saturday is for errands, football, shopping, gardening and the sort of home improvement that begins with a shelf and ends with a plasterer. Sunday is for the roast, the walk and the creeping dread of Monday.

The pub is the central institution. It is where friends meet, where news is shared and where the order of buying rounds is a matter of honour. Sunday lunch, with roast meat, potatoes and gravy in lavish quantities, is the weekly ritual that nobody questions. A country walk, preferably muddy, followed by a pub fire is the highest form of British happiness.

Culture, sport and the outdoors fill the rest. There are free museums, historic houses, gardens, markets, parks and seaside towns within reach of most cities, and a surprising number of Britons will travel for hours to see a castle in the rain. Weekend breaks are short and efficient, and most people book them with a spreadsheet.

The social rules are subtler than they look. Plans are made in advance, spontaneous visits are discouraged, and people tend to meet friends in public places before inviting them home. A Nigerian expat can find this chilly at first, as the generous drop-in culture of home gives way to a calendar and a polite text. Many find a community in churches, cultural associations and London's large Nigerian networks, where the weekend reclaims its noise.

The British weekend is not exciting, and nobody pretends it is. It is a chance to be left alone, which for many Britons is the highest luxury.

British weekend leisure is heavily shaped by tradition and by a surprisingly active calendar. Saturday football, village fetes, car boot sales, garden open days, farmers' markets and Sunday car-free walks are all part of it, and a good number of people volunteer on weekends too. For a newcomer, joining a local club, a choir or a running group is the quickest route to a social life and a decent excuse to leave the house.

## The Verdict

Nigeria wins on joy and the United Kingdom wins on rest, and the winner depends on the week you have had. If you want to feel part of something large, loud and slightly overwhelming, a Nigerian weekend is a gift. You will eat too much, dance too long and go home with a pocketful of phone numbers.

If you want to restore yourself with nothing more than a book, a long walk and a pie, the British weekend has no equal. It is predictable, polite and designed to leave you ready for Monday.

I would choose Nigeria for a celebration and Britain for a recovery, and I would not try to do both in the same weekend. The best expats learn to alternate, with a weekend of noise followed by a weekend of silence, and nobody will judge you for either.

The sting is that each culture is a little suspicious of the other's pleasure. Nigerians wonder how Britons survive without being invited anywhere. Britons wonder how Nigerians survive being invited everywhere. Both are right, and both are exhausted.

A final warning for expats. Both countries are changing, with younger Nigerians embracing brunch, gyms and weekends away, and younger Britons rediscovering house parties and dancing. The stereotypes are good for a chuckle, but the person in front of you is more likely to be tired, busy and slightly overbooked, which is the one thing every weekend has in common.

## Conclusion

The weekend is where you find out who you are when nobody is paying you. In Nigeria, you are a member of a crowd. In Britain, you are a person with a rota.

My advice is to bring something of home and borrow something of the host. Throw the party in a rented hall in Peckham, and take the walk in the Peak District. Learn to love both the noise and the quiet, and be suspicious of anyone who claims to prefer only one.

And whichever side of the weekend you land on, ring ahead. In Nigeria, it is courtesy. In Britain, it is the law.
