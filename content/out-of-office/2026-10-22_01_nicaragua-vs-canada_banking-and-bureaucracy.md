# Nicaragua Makes You Earn Your Bank Account. Canada Just Posts You One.

🇳🇮 Nicaragua × 🇨🇦 Canada
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nicaragua does not have a banking system. It has a banking vibe — part cédula checkpoint, part fotocopia economy, part theological negotiation with a teller who has decided, unilaterally, that today is simply not the day. You will queue. You will be asked for documents you do not have, in triplicate, notarized, and ideally blessed by a notary who also happens to be someone's cousin. And when the córdoba quietly slides another notch against the dollar while you wait, nobody in that line will treat it as news. It is simply Tuesday.

Canada, meanwhile, has engineered bureaucracy into a polite, numbing hum you barely notice until you try to do something that actually requires speed. Banks here will let you open an account from your sofa on the other side of the planet before you've touched Canadian soil, hand you a debit card within the week, and then spend the next two years quietly punishing you for not having a Canadian credit history — despite having just handed you a Canadian bank account. It is efficient. It is humane. It is, somehow, still capable of making you feel like an idiot.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Nicaragua:**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Sort your cédula or residency paperwork before you even think about a bank account | Assume a tourist visa and a smile will get you past the teller's desk |
| Keep photocopies of everything, always, in triplicate | Expect a wire transfer to arrive on the day anyone promised |
| Budget extra córdobas for the year's quiet currency slide | Carry only a foreign card and assume every shop or ATM will accept it |
| Befriend a notary — a real one, slowly, over time | Lose your temper at the teller; it costs you more time, not less |

**Canada:**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Open a newcomer account before you land, online, with your passport | Expect a credit card on day one just because you have a bank account |
| Set up Interac e-Transfer immediately; it is the actual national currency | Assume a branch visit will fix your problem faster than the app will |
| Apply for your Social Insurance Number the moment you arrive | Mail anything urgent and assume it will arrive urgently |
| Ask your bank about a newcomer or secured starter credit product | Ignore your credit score — it will haunt you for years |

## Nicaragua

Start with the córdoba, because everyone else eventually has to. Nicaragua's currency is managed by the Banco Central de Nicaragua on a "crawling peg" against the US dollar — a slow, scheduled devaluation that for years ran at roughly five percent annually before the central bank quietly trimmed the slide to one percent. The trim gets framed as stability. In practice it means the ground beneath your savings still moves, just more politely than it used to.

Then there's the part nobody puts in the brochure: since 2018, correspondent banking relationships between Nicaraguan banks and their US counterparts have been thinning out. Washington's sanctions on officials tied to the Ortega government made international banks nervous about touching Nicaraguan correspondent accounts at all, and several pulled back outright. The practical result for an ordinary expat is almost comic in its inconvenience — international wires that vanish into a multi-week fog, foreign cards declined for reasons no human behind the counter can actually explain, and a cash economy that persists not out of charm but out of necessity.

Opening an account as a foreigner is its own small pilgrimage. Non-residents are routinely turned away or given withering stares until they produce residency paperwork; a cédula, proof of address, and bottomless patience are the real prerequisites — a documentation culture that assumes you're hiding something until proven otherwise. Go the residency route instead and you're choosing between pensionado status, for retirees with roughly six hundred dollars a month in guaranteed income, or rentista status, for everyone else living off passive income. Either way, you'll be filing through the Dirección General de Migración y Extranjería, apostilling documents from your home country, paying for notarized translations, and waiting out timelines that move at their own glacial, occasionally reversible pace.

And in a detail that tells you everything about who actually holds the power here: Nicaragua passed a law in 2021 explicitly barring banks from closing the accounts of sanctioned officials, overriding the banks' own compliance instincts by government decree. Bureaucracy, in Nicaragua, isn't an obstacle the state apologizes for. It's a lever the state pulls, cheerfully, in whichever direction suits it that week.

## Canada

Canada's big five banks — RBC, TD, Scotiabank, BMO, and CIBC — have turned "newcomer banking" into a genuine pre-arrival product category. You can open a Canadian chequing account from abroad using nothing but a passport and an immigration document, have a debit card waiting at a local branch, and be tapping it within days of landing. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada backs this with an actual legal right: banks cannot refuse to open a basic account because you lack a job, an income, or a credit history, with only a narrow set of exceptions.

Which makes the next part so perverse. Having the account means almost nothing for your credit. Canada's financial system runs almost entirely on a credit score you cannot build without first having Canadian credit, which you cannot get without a score, which you do not have, because you just arrived. Newcomers routinely spend their first year with a debit card, a secured credit card if they're lucky, and a quiet, simmering resentment toward a system that handed them a bank account like a welcome gift and then withheld everything that account was supposed to unlock.

Beyond banking, Canadian bureaucracy has a specific texture: slow by design, polite by training, and administered almost entirely through forms that assume you have reliable internet, a printer, and the patience to wait on hold with Service Canada for your Social Insurance Number. It works. It is rarely cruel. It is also rarely fast, and it leans heavily on Canada Post, an institution whose relationship with urgency is best described as theoretical.

Spare a thought, too, for Canadians trying to navigate the reverse direction. Global Affairs Canada's own guidance for Managua is a masterclass in diplomatic understatement, routing anything beyond the basics through the consulate in Costa Rica, because Canada's government footprint in Nicaragua is thin enough that its own citizens are quietly told to expect limited help on the ground. Efficient at home. Elsewhere, entirely on your own.

## The Verdict

If you're grading for function, Canada wins, obviously, and it isn't close. Pre-approved accounts, legally protected basic banking, a postal system with tracking numbers that actually track — this is machinery built by people who have never had to bribe anyone for a stamp. But function was never really the question, was it. Nicaragua's bureaucracy is maddening in a way that at least admits what it is: a system run by humans, for better and mostly worse, where the rules bend for the right people and nobody pretends otherwise. Canada's bureaucracy is maddening in a way that hides behind forms, apps, and a veneer of fairness that quietly locks newcomers out of credit for a year regardless of how competent or solvent they actually are.

So here's the sting, free of charge. Canada will process you flawlessly and make you feel invisible while doing it. Nicaragua will make you suffer, visibly, personally, and occasionally in the company of a notary who also happens to be someone's cousin — but at least it looks you in the eye while doing it. Pick your bureaucracy. Just don't pretend either one is doing you a favour.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/expats — a Nicaragua-based poster describes waiting over two months for an international wire to clear after their bank's US correspondent relationship quietly thinned out, with no explanation offered beyond "it's being processed."*

> *expat.com Nicaragua forum — a long-time contributor warns newcomers that opening a non-resident account is close to impossible, and that showing up without a cédula or residency paperwork guarantees a wasted afternoon.*

> *Reddit r/PersonalFinanceCanada — a recent immigrant vents about being handed a shiny newcomer bank account on arrival, then rejected for every credit card that would have actually helped them, on the grounds of having "no credit history" in a country they had lived in for three weeks.*

## Conclusion

Here is what both countries have in common, which is more than either would like to admit: they each assume you already understand their system before you've had any real chance to. Nicaragua assumes you know someone. Canada assumes you have a credit history that, structurally, you cannot yet possess. Neither assumption is fair. Both are completely non-negotiable.

If you need predictability, go to Canada and accept you'll be financially invisible for a year. If you need a story — and, let's be honest, a little chaos you can dine out on for years — Nicaragua will give you one, usually involving a notary, a fotocopia shop, and a wait you didn't see coming. Just don't move to either expecting gratitude for showing up. Bureaucracy doesn't care that you flew in. It never has.
