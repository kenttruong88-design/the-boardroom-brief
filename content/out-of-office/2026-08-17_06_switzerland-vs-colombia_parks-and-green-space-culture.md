# One Country's Parks Are Silent and Immaculate. The Other's Are a Sunday Street Party.

🇨🇭 Switzerland vs 🇨🇴 Colombia — Parks and Green Space Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The Swiss do not "go to the park" so much as engage with nature as a scheduled, well-signposted appointment — trails are marked with such exacting precision that you could hike blindfolded and still know your elevation gain, bins are emptied before they're full, and dogs are, without exception, on leads unless a sign explicitly says otherwise. Colombia, and Bogotá in particular, takes an entirely different position: on Sundays, the city simply removes cars from over a hundred kilometres of major roads and hands the entire network over to cyclists, joggers, dog walkers, and food vendors, turning the city itself into one enormous, chaotic, joyous park.

I have hiked a Swiss trail so pristine I felt guilty breathing near it. I have cycled Bogotá's Ciclovía surrounded by thousands of strangers, a man selling mango on a stick, and a marching band that appeared from nowhere. Both are green space. Only one required a permit in my imagination.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Follow the marked trail signage — the yellow diamonds are not decorative | Go off-trail assuming "it's just nature"; it's often private or ecologically protected land |
| Keep dogs leashed unless a sign explicitly permits otherwise | Leave any trace whatsoever; even orange peels draw disapproving looks |
| Time your hike using the posted trail durations — they're startlingly accurate | Blast music or speak loudly on a quiet trail; silence is the default expectation |

### 🇨🇴 Colombia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join Ciclovía on a Sunday morning — rent a bike if you don't have one | Drive through the closed Ciclovía routes; they're fully car-free by design |
| Buy a snack from a street vendor along the route — it's part of the culture | Expect strict quiet hours in city parks; music and gatherings are the norm, not the exception |
| Bring cash for informal vendors inside parks | Assume every green space is safe after dark; check locally before an evening visit |

## Switzerland: Nature as Precision Instrument

Swiss parks and trails operate with the same underlying philosophy as Swiss trains: everything is measured, everything is marked, and deviation from the plan is viewed with quiet, well-mannered suspicion. The yellow diamond trail markers that crisscross the Alps aren't just helpful, they're load-bearing infrastructure for the entire national hiking culture — distances and estimated times are posted with a confidence that turns out to be, infuriatingly, almost always correct, which either delights or enrages you depending on your relationship with punctuality.

The cleanliness verges on the spiritual. I have watched a Swiss hiker pick up a single sweet wrapper that wasn't even theirs, wordlessly, without breaking conversational stride, as though litter were simply an affront to be quietly corrected rather than commented on. Urban parks follow the same logic at a smaller scale — Zurich's lakeside green spaces are immaculate, quiet, and governed by an unspoken code where loud groups, unleashed dogs, or general rowdiness draw the kind of disapproving Swiss silence that communicates more than shouting ever could.

What surprises newcomers is how much of this order is voluntary rather than enforced. There aren't armies of park rangers policing behaviour; the social pressure to comply is simply strong enough that formal enforcement is rarely needed. It's an incredibly efficient system for anyone who values order, and a slightly suffocating one for anyone who grew up somewhere green space meant a bit of glorious anarchy. Swiss nature is beautiful, generous, and extremely well-behaved — and it expects the same of you.

## Colombia: The Street Becomes the Park

Bogotá took a different, and arguably more radical, approach to green space: rather than only building parks, the city periodically dissolves the boundary between road and park altogether. Every Sunday and public holiday, the Ciclovía programme shuts down more than a hundred kilometres of major arteries to car traffic, and the city's residents pour into the resulting space by the millions — cycling, running, rollerblading, walking dogs, and buying arepas from vendors who've set up shop along a route that, six days a week, is choked with traffic.

It's one of the largest recurring car-free events on the planet, and what strikes visitors most isn't the logistics, impressive as they are, but the atmosphere — this isn't a solemn wellness activity, it's a genuine street party stretched across a city, complete with impromptu aerobics classes, live music at certain intersections, and a level of communal joy that a designated, fenced-off "park" could never replicate. Bogotá's actual green spaces, like Parque Simón Bolívar, follow a similarly loose, welcoming code: picnics, football games, informal vendors, music playing from multiple directions at once, dogs off-lead more often than not.

The trade-off for all this vibrancy is a looser relationship with the kind of pristine order the Swiss prize. Litter is a more visible issue, the infrastructure is less uniformly maintained, and safety varies meaningfully by park and by time of day in a way that requires local knowledge rather than blind trust. But the payoff is a genuine sense that green space belongs to everyone, actively and loudly, rather than being a quiet amenity you visit and leave undisturbed. Bogotá's parks don't ask you to be silent. They ask you to show up.

## The Verdict

Switzerland wins on sheer natural beauty maintained with obsessive care — there is nowhere on earth better suited to a solitary, restorative hike. Colombia wins on community — Ciclovía is one of the great urban inventions of the last century, a weekly reminder that a city can choose joy over convenience if it actually wants to. If you need silence and mountains, book Switzerland. If you need a reminder that public space can be genuinely, chaotically alive, get yourself to Bogotá on a Sunday morning and rent a bike. Just don't try to blast music on a Swiss trail, and don't expect Alpine silence on Bogotá's Séptima.

## Conclusion

Switzerland treats green space as something sacred and precise, a landscape to be protected from you as much as offered to you. Colombia treats it as something communal and loud, a resource to be claimed and used to its fullest. Neither approach is more "natural" than the other — they're both deeply cultural responses to what open space is for. Hike quietly in the Alps. Cycle loudly in Bogotá. Bring snacks either way; only one country will let you buy them from a stranger mid-route.
