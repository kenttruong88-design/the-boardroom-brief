# Two Ways to Pay Too Much for a Roof

🇨🇬 Congo vs 🇸🇪 Sweden — Hotel and Accommodation Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a special kind of expat vertigo that comes from discovering that the two hardest countries on earth to find a bed in have almost nothing else in common. Brazzaville will charge you Kempinski prices for a room with unreliable air conditioning because supply is a rumour. Stockholm will charge you nothing outrageous per month and then quietly inform you that the queue for that reasonably priced flat is eleven years long. Both systems are, in their own way, a scam. Only one of them dresses it up as fairness.

I've paid $300 a night for a Brazzaville hotel room with a view of a construction site, and I've watched a Swedish colleague celebrate landing a rental contract with the sincere, tearful joy usually reserved for a birth announcement. Housing, it turns out, is the one arena where humanity refuses to simply agree on a system and get on with it.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Congo (Republic of Congo)**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book your hotel through a company or trusted contact, not blind | Expect the listed rate to match the invoiced rate |
| Negotiate long-stay rates directly with the hotel manager | Assume "4.5 star" means what it does in Europe |
| Budget for Brazzaville prices that rival Paris | Wait until arrival to sort out where you're actually sleeping |
| Ask about generator backup before you book | Assume villas and serviced apartments are cheaper than hotels — often they aren't |

**Sweden**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for the housing queue (bostadskö) the day you decide to move, even years early | Expect to find a first-hand rental contract quickly without connections |
| Understand the difference between first-hand and second-hand leases before signing anything | Sign a sublet without checking it's registered and legal |
| Budget time, not just money, as your scarce resource | Assume Stockholm prices reflect the rest of the country |
| Network aggressively — word of mouth beats every listing site | Panic-accept a terrible flat just to have an address |

## Congo: Scarcity Dressed as Luxury

Brazzaville does not have a housing shortage in the way Stockholm has a housing shortage. It has an infrastructure shortage that happens to express itself through the accommodation market. There simply aren't that many buildings capable of reliably delivering electricity, water pressure, and a functioning air conditioner all at once, and every hotel that can manage the trick — the Kempinski, the Radisson Blu M'Bamou Palace, the Grand Lancaster — knows exactly how rare that makes them. The result is a pricing structure that would make a Zurich concierge blush, applied to a city where the median local resident could not conceive of paying it.

For expats, this creates a genuinely strange rhythm of life. Long-term visitors quickly learn that the "hotel" and "serviced apartment" markets aren't separate tracks so much as the same small pool of reliable landlords and hoteliers, all of whom know each other, all of whom set prices accordingly. The savvy move — and every oil-and-gas expat I've met in Pointe-Noire or Brazzaville eventually learns this — is to negotiate a long-stay corporate rate directly rather than paying rack rate for six months running. The unsavvy move is assuming your employer's per diem was calculated by someone who has actually seen a Brazzaville invoice.

What genuinely surprises newcomers is how much status is baked into where you're staying. Which hotel bar you're drinking in on a Thursday says more about your position in Brazzaville's small expat and diplomatic circuit than your actual job title does. It's less "accommodation" than "social address," and once you understand that, the pricing starts to make a certain grim sense. You're not paying for the room. You're paying for the relief of a reliable ceiling and a generator that doesn't quit at 2am, and apparently that relief has a very specific market value.

## Sweden: Fairness as a Full-Time Job

Sweden, meanwhile, has built one of the most sincerely egalitarian housing systems on the planet and then rendered it almost unusable through sheer, well-intentioned bureaucracy. The bostadskö — the housing queue — is not a metaphor. You genuinely register, you genuinely wait, and in Stockholm you genuinely need years, sometimes over a decade, of queue time to land a decent first-hand rental contract in a desirable neighbourhood. This is a system designed to prevent exactly the kind of price-gouging chaos Brazzaville runs on, and it succeeds magnificently at that single goal while creating an entirely different, quieter form of despair.

Expats arriving without a decade of foresight are funnelled into the second-hand market — subletting from someone else's first-hand contract — which is legal, common, and requires you to develop a genuine talent for vetting who's-subletting-what-to-whom through Facebook groups and word of mouth, because the official listing sites move slower than the demand. The golden rule, repeated by every expat forum with the weary consistency of scripture, is to make sure any sublet is actually registered with the landlord. The unregistered ones evaporate the moment the original tenant decides they want their flat back, sometimes with two weeks' notice.

What nobody explains before you arrive is that Swedish housing culture treats "having a first-hand contract" as roughly equivalent to holding a winning lottery ticket, and people discuss their contract status with the same mix of pride and slight embarrassment usually reserved for talking about inheritance. Once you're in, you're extraordinarily protected — Swedish tenant law makes eviction genuinely difficult. Getting in is the entire battle, and no amount of salary solves it. Money, refreshingly and infuriatingly, is not the bottleneck. Time and connections are.

## The Verdict

Sweden wins on principle, Congo wins on speed, and I'd rather have my principles tested than my patience. Give me the country where I can, with enough cash, simply solve the problem this afternoon. Brazzaville's system is unashamedly unfair, but at least it's honestly unfair — you know exactly what you're buying and exactly what it costs. Stockholm's system pretends fairness is achievable and then makes you wait eleven years to discover it mostly is, just not for you, not yet, and possibly not for your children either.

## Conclusion

Both countries will make you pay for shelter in a currency other than money — Congo taxes your bank balance, Sweden taxes your patience — and neither will apologise for it. If you're the sort who likes to solve a problem by throwing resources at it, Brazzaville will reward you handsomely and briefly resent you for it. If you're the sort who believes queues build character, Stockholm has an eleven-year character-building programme with your name already quietly, glacially, moving up the list.
