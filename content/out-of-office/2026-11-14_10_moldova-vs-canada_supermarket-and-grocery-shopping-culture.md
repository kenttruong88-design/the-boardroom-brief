# Moldova Buys Its Tomatoes From a Woman Named Elena. Canada Buys Them From a Corporation With a Loyalty Programme

🇲🇩 Moldova vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

You can learn everything you need to know about a country by following its inhabitants into a supermarket. Do they bargain? Do they bring their own bags? Do they apologise to the person who just ran a trolley into their ankle? Groceries are a culture compressed into a single trip.

Moldova and Canada are about as far apart on the grocery spectrum as it is possible to be while both selling potatoes. One has markets that have been functioning for generations, in the open air, with weights on a scale and a conversation attached. The other has a landscape of chain stores, points cards and the quietly crushing efficiency of the weekly shop.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Moldova**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go to the central market early for the best produce | Arrive at closing time and expect a choice |
| Bring cash and your own bags | Expect every stall to take a card |
| Chat with the sellers and ask what is in season | Rush the transaction |
| Learn the market opening hours, which vary by day | Turn up on a Sunday at the wrong market |

**Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the loyalty programmes of your local chain | Pay full price when there is a points deal |
| Compare chains, since discount banners are far cheaper | Shop at the nearest one out of habit |
| Check provincial rules on alcohol before you plan dinner | Assume you can buy wine with your groceries |
| Watch for tax added at the till | Be surprised by the total |

## Moldova

Chisinau's markets are the heart of the grocery culture, and Expat Exchange gives the useful details. Piata Centrala, on Stefan cel Mare si Sfant Boulevard, is open daily from 7 in the morning to 6 in the evening. The Malldova farmers market on Arborilor Street opens on Saturdays from 8 to 3. Zeleny Market on Decebal Boulevard is open daily from 7 to 5. And the flea market at the central station runs on Sundays from 7 to 2. That is a week's worth of shopping organised around a calendar older than any app.

The supermarkets exist too. The same source names chains such as Fidesco and Nr1 as options, though I would treat the details with caution. But the soul of Moldovan food shopping is the piata, the open market, where the seller is often the grower, the produce is seasonal and the price is subject to conversation. It is not a nostalgic act. For many households, it is simply the cheapest way to eat well.

I find this system hard to resist. The tomatoes taste of tomatoes. The cheese has a story. The seller remembers you after two visits, and by the fifth she is slipping in a handful of something for free and asking after your mother. This is not a service you can buy at a chain. It is a relationship, and it is built over repeated visits and small courtesies.

The costs are time, cash and inconsistency. Markets close in the afternoon, selection varies by season, and not every stall has a card machine. You plan your shopping around opening hours, not around your convenience. You carry the bags home. And if you are in a hurry, you pay for the supermarket's predictability.

For a newcomer, the best advice is to treat the market as a social event as much as a shopping trip. Go early, go often, and learn the vendors. You will eat better and you will meet your neighbours.

## Canada

Canada is built for the weekly shop. According to Immigrant.Today, No Frills operates in every province except Quebec and is owned by Loblaws, with the PC Optimum loyalty programme attached. Freshco is owned by Sobeys and operates in British Columbia, Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba and Ontario. Maxi, also owned by Loblaws, is Quebec only. Save-On-Foods runs 1.49 dollar deals on Tuesdays, according to the article, and a Dalhousie study cited there found that nearly two thirds of Canadians changed their main grocery store in the past year.

That last figure is the whole story. Canadian shoppers are not loyal to a place. They are loyal to a price, and they will cross town for a discount. The loyalty cards are the true currency, and the chain is an interchangeable vessel.

A British writer in Calgary, Charlie Hart, wrote a charming list of things that surprised them after moving from the UK. "When I moved to Canada from the U.K., I knew I was definitely going to be in for some big culture shock," the piece begins. The writer noted that Alberta grocery stores do not sell alcohol, so you need a separate liquor store, and that they envied people in British Columbia, who "can buy wine, beer, and other alcoholic drinks while grocery shopping." Eggs are kept in the fridge, the writer adds, and tax is added at checkout.

The writer also lamented the choice. "I'd definitely love to see more options in Canada because whenever I'm at the grocery store, I do feel like it's lacking." Prices were another surprise: the piece cites three chicken breasts at about 14 dollars at Walmart against about 5 pounds at home, based on a price comparison site. The piece is an opinion, not a study, but it captures the newcomer's shock.

What Canada does well is consistency and scale. Stores are clean, well stocked and open long hours, and a shopper can find almost anything. What it does less well is intimacy. Nobody asks after your mother. The vegetables have travelled a long way, and the receipt is longer than the conversation.

One last observation concerns what each system does to your wallet and your waistline. A Moldovan market shopper buys what is in season and tends to cook it, because that is what the stall offers. A Canadian chain shopper is tempted by promotions and ready meals, because that is what the aisle offers. Neither habit is virtuous in itself, but the environment nudges you, and after a year you will find your diet has been quietly rewritten by where you shop. If you want to eat the way you used to, you will have to fight your supermarket, and the supermarket has a loyalty scheme on its side.

## The Verdict

Moldova wins on freshness and character, Canada wins on convenience and consistency, and prices depend on what you buy and where. I would not want to give up the market, but I would not want to give up a late-night supermarket in a Canadian winter either. A tomato from a stall is wonderful in August. In January, I want a heated aisle.

The honest comparison is that Moldova asks you to invest time and Canada asks you to invest planning. Neither is easy, and both reward the shopper who understands the system. If you are the kind of person who enjoys a conversation with the person selling your lunch, Moldova will feel like home. If you are the kind who wants to be in and out in twelve minutes with points, Canada is your country.

My ruling is a split. Moldova for the food, Canada for the logistics. And if you move from one to the other, bring a list of what you will miss, because it will be longer than you think.

## Conclusion

A grocery shop is a small ritual repeated a thousand times, and the ritual shapes the life around it. In Moldova it is a conversation, a stroll and an exercise in seasonality. In Canada it is a plan, a points balance and a slightly cold efficiency.

Take what each does best. Buy the tomatoes from Elena in summer, and the loyalty points from the chain in winter. And whichever country you are in, carry your own bag. The cashier will not ask, but everyone is judging.
