# Switzerland Shuts the Shops on Sunday. Vietnam Never Really Closes At All.

🇨🇭 Switzerland · 🇻🇳 Vietnam
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Switzerland treats retail like a Protestant work ethic wearing a very expensive watch: precise opening hours, fixed prices to the centime, and a near-religious devotion to Sunday closure that will strand you, hungry and sockless, if you didn't plan your errands like a military campaign. Vietnam treats retail as a 24-hour improvisational performance where the price is a starting bid, the shop is also someone's living room, and the market never really stops, it just quietly dims after midnight before roaring back at 5am with fresh fish.

I have been turned away from a Zurich pharmacy at 6:31pm by a clerk visibly relieved to be closing on schedule. I have also bought a mango, a phone charger, and what I'm fairly sure was a live chicken from the same woman in Hanoi's Old Quarter at 9pm on a Tuesday. Only one of these experiences left me feeling remotely resourceful.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇨🇭 Switzerland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stock up before Saturday closing — Sundays are genuinely dead | Expect to haggle anywhere, ever, under any circumstances |
| Bring exact change or a card — rounding disputes are taken seriously | Assume "corner shops" exist like they do elsewhere; petrol stations fill the gap, badly |
| Enjoy the fixed, transparent pricing — what you see is what you pay | Complain about prices out loud in a shop — it reads as gauche |

**🇻🇳 Vietnam**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Haggle at markets, cheerfully, as a form of social ritual | Pay the first price quoted — it's practically an insult to the seller's expectations |
| Shop early morning for the best produce and the calmest crowds | Assume fixed prices apply outside of malls and convenience chains |
| Build a relationship with a regular vendor — the discounts follow the loyalty | Rush the transaction — a bit of banter is part of the deal |

## Switzerland: Retail as an Act of National Discipline

Switzerland's shopping culture makes sense only once you accept that the entire country runs on the assumption that everyone planned ahead, and if you didn't, that's a personal failing the Swiss have no obligation to accommodate. The Swiss Federal Statistical Office confirms what every bleary-eyed expat learns within their first fortnight: Sunday closing isn't a quaint tradition, it's near-total. Supermarkets, boutiques, department stores — dark, locked, silent, save for the odd bakery or the train-station Migros that exists purely to remind you of your poor planning.

What's remarkable is how little anyone seems to mind. Switzerland Tourism's own guidance to visitors essentially says "plan around it," with the same brisk efficiency the Swiss apply to everything else. There's no sense of frustration from locals — Sunday is for hiking, family, or staring meaningfully at a lake, and the closed shops are simply part of that rhythm, not an inconvenience to be resented. Prices, meanwhile, are exactly what they say on the label, to the centime, with a rigidity that borders on the spiritual. Haggling isn't just unwelcome, it's borderline incomprehensible as a concept — I once jokingly asked a Geneva shopkeeper for a "better price" and received a look somewhere between confusion and mild concern, as though I'd asked him to renegotiate gravity.

The retail experience itself is immaculate, unhurried, and eye-wateringly expensive — a coffee-table book that would cost twelve pounds in London costs forty francs in Zurich, and nobody blinks. That's simply the price of Swiss quality, or Swiss rent, or Swiss everything. The shopping culture here isn't about the thrill of the transaction. It's about trust — trust that the price is fair, the quality is real, and the shop will be exactly where you left it, at exactly the hours it promised, forever.

## Vietnam: Retail as Living Theatre

Vietnam's shopping culture operates on a completely different metabolism — faster, louder, more personal, and running roughly around the clock. Vietnam's General Statistics Office data shows the extraordinary scale of informal and market-based retail still dominating daily commerce, alongside the rapid rise of convenience chains and malls in the cities. But the soul of Vietnamese shopping remains the market — Bến Thành in Ho Chi Minh City, Đồng Xuân in Hanoi — sprawling, chaotic, wonderful theatres where the quoted price is an opening move in a conversation, not a fact.

Haggling here isn't rude, it's expected, almost obligatory — failing to negotiate is treated less as politeness and more as a missed social cue, like refusing to shake hands. Vendors will quote you double or triple the "real" price to a foreigner on instinct, not out of malice but out of habit, and the entire transaction becomes a small, good-humoured performance where both parties know roughly how it will end and enjoy the theatre of getting there anyway. Ho Chi Minh City's Department of Industry and Trade still actively regulates and supports these traditional markets precisely because they remain economically central, not a tourist curiosity.

What genuinely charmed me was the loyalty economy underneath it all — return to the same vendor three times and the price softens, the portions grow, the banter gets warmer. It's a relationship, not a transaction, and once you're recognised, you're basically family, at least for pricing purposes. And the hours — genuinely, functionally endless. Street stalls selling banh mi at 6am give way to noodle carts at midnight, convenience stores never close, and the idea of a mandated day of rest for retail would strike most Vietnamese vendors as faintly absurd. Commerce here doesn't sleep because life here doesn't really sleep either.

## The Verdict

Switzerland wins on trust, transparency, and not having to perform social combat over the price of a t-shirt. Vietnam wins on everything that actually makes shopping enjoyable rather than merely functional — the theatre, the relationships, the sheer availability of anything, anytime. If you want to know exactly what you'll pay and exactly when the doors close, Switzerland has you covered with Teutonic precision. If you want shopping to feel like a small daily adventure with a slightly inflated foreigner tax you can talk your way out of, Vietnam wins, and it isn't close.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/VietNam — a commenter admitting they paid triple for a phone case in their first week and only found out months later when a Vietnamese friend saw the receipt and laughed for a full minute.*

## Conclusion

Switzerland and Vietnam represent two entirely opposite philosophies of commerce: one built on the assumption that everyone should be able to trust a fixed system without ever needing to think about it, the other built on the assumption that shopping should be personal, adaptive, and a little bit of a game. I trust the Swiss system more. I enjoy the Vietnamese one infinitely more. That, in the end, tells you everything about the difference between a country that's efficient and a country that's alive.
