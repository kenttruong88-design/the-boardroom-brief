# China Exercises in Public at Dawn and Singapore Does It Indoors Under Air Conditioning

🇨🇳 China vs 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Chinese fitness is a public performance. At dawn the parks fill with tai chi practitioners, sword dancers, badminton players, walkers going backwards for reasons of their own and, in the evening, groups moving in unison to music that can be heard two streets away. Exercise is not a private improvement project. It is a neighbourhood activity, shared across generations, and nobody is embarrassed to be doing it where everyone can see.

Singapore fitness is a private appointment. The gym is in the condominium, the mall or the community centre, and it is air-conditioned to a degree that would make a polar bear reach for a jumper. People train with earphones on and eyes forward, in a country where the heat is relentless and the indoor option is a refuge. It is efficient, organised and somewhat solitary, like most things in Singapore that work properly.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**China**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a park group or class and learn by watching | Stand and stare, then complain that no one invited you |
| Exercise early, before the day heats up | Plan a big outdoor session at midday in summer |
| Use local apps to book classes and courts | Turn up expecting a drop-in at a busy facility |
| Be ready to be noticed and gently corrected | Take advice from strangers as an insult |

**Singapore**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use your condominium gym or the public ActiveSG facilities | Assume you need an expensive private club |
| Wipe down equipment and rack your weights | Leave your sweat for the next person |
| Run early in the morning or after dark | Jog at one in the afternoon in full sun |
| Carry water and rest when the humidity hits | Treat heat exhaustion as a personal challenge |

## China

Exercise in China is rooted in a long tradition of collective health. Mornings in parks reveal a cross-section of society in motion: retirees practising slow, precise tai chi, groups stretching and chatting, individuals walking laps with a purposeful stride. The evening brings group dancing in squares, which is exercise, social event and community theatre at once.

The country has also embraced the modern gym with enthusiasm. Big-city fitness clubs, boutique studios, climbing walls and cycling classes sit alongside the parks, and a generation of young professionals treats the gym as an essential part of life. Apps handle booking, tracking and sharing, and fitness content is a major part of social media. The result is a culture in which exercise is both ancient and aggressively up to date.

For a newcomer, the openness can be startling. People observe each other's form, offer advice and join conversations without introduction. It is not intrusive, but it is not private either. If you do something unusual, somebody will notice, and somebody will tell you what you should be doing instead.

Air quality and weather shape the routine. On heavy-pollution days, many people move indoors, and the gym becomes a refuge. In summer the heat pushes activity to the early morning and late evening. A resident learns to read the day before deciding where to exercise.

The pace of life affects fitness in both places. Chinese cities have long working hours and a strong culture of ambition, yet the early-morning ritual survives because it is cheap, social and good for the joints. People exercise before work, after dinner and in the gaps in between, and the parks are rarely empty. It is a lesson in how to keep moving without spending money.

## Singapore

Singapore is hot and humid all year, and the country has built its fitness culture around that fact. Condominiums come with gyms and pools, public sports complexes run by the national sports authority offer affordable access and shopping malls are full of studios offering everything from yoga to boxing. The air conditioning is not a luxury. It is the point.

Outdoor exercise exists and flourishes at the right hours. Parks and the waterfront fill at dawn and after sunset with runners, cyclists and walkers. Evenings are busy, and the paths hum with athletic efficiency. The national passion for running, group classes and organised events is strong, and the island is small enough that a good facility is rarely far away.

Gym culture has its own flavour. A Singaporean magazine essay on body culture in the country's gyms describes a world of dedicated lifters with an eye on aesthetics, and a Chinese visitor was reported in local media as surprised by shirtless men working out at a public stadium, a reminder that norms about modesty and exercise vary even between neighbours. Singaporeans are generally respectful and rule-abiding in gyms: equipment is shared fairly, etiquette is observed and nobody wants to be the person who leaves a weight on the floor.

Expat life tends to revolve around the condo facilities and a few favoured studios, and community is built through classes and clubs more than through chance encounters. It is a polite, self-contained culture that rewards people who like to book ahead.

Cost is the other difference. Singapore's private gyms can be expensive, though the public facilities are inexpensive by comparison, and many residents rely on the free facilities in their building. Fitness trends move fast, with boutique studios appearing and vanishing like pop-up shops. If a class feels crowded, there is usually another one around the corner within a week.

## The Verdict

China wins on community. The park, the square and the group class turn exercise into something that is inseparable from social life, and the result is a population that moves, talks and ages with enviable energy.

Singapore wins on infrastructure. The facilities are clean, accessible and climate controlled, and a person who wants to train consistently will find nothing standing in their way except their own excuses.

I will give the nod to China for atmosphere and to Singapore for convenience. If you want to feel part of something, move to China and join a group. If you want to get fit without leaving the building, move to Singapore and use the lift. Either way, the heat will be doing most of the persuading.

## Conclusion

The two countries disagree on whether fitness is a spectacle or a secret. In China, you exercise where everyone can see, and the audience becomes the community. In Singapore, you exercise where nobody can see, and the community becomes whoever is on the next treadmill.

Move to either and you will end up fitter, if only because the alternative is sweating through your shirt on the way to the shops. Pick the environment that suits your temperament, and then show up on a Monday. That is the only fitness secret that has ever survived translation.
