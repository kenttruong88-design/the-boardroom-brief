# In Nigeria You Pay Before They Look at You. In Britain They Look at You Eventually, and It's Free, and Please Hold

🇳🇬 Nigeria vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Nigeria, healthcare is a transaction with a pulse. You pay, you are seen, and the speed of the seeing is roughly proportional to the size of the paying. People who can afford it use private clinics, people who cannot use whatever is nearest, and everyone has a cousin who is a nurse, a pharmacist or somebody who knows somebody.

In Britain, healthcare is a national religion that has not fully delivered on its promises. It is free at the point of use, which is wonderful, and slow at the point of access, which is a national sport. You will be told to register with a GP, ring at eight o'clock, hold, try again and, if you are still alive, wait. The system is a miracle held together by queues, patience and nurses who often came from somewhere with weather far better than this.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Nigeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep cash or a card for the clinic before you need it | Arrive at a private hospital expecting care before payment |
| Ask a relative in the field for a recommendation | Pick a clinic on the sign alone |
| Check what the pharmacist is actually giving you | Treat every tablet as harmless |
| Keep your medical records on you | Assume the next hospital has them |

**United Kingdom**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a local GP as soon as you arrive | Wait until you are ill |
| Ring early and be prepared to hold | Expect a same-day slot for a minor problem |
| Learn what the pharmacist can sort out | Go to A&E for a sore throat |
| Report racism at work, as the colleagues below did | Assume it will go away if you stay quiet |

## Nigeria

I will be careful here: the Nigerian half of this comparison draws on general knowledge rather than a single fetched source. Most people in Nigeria pay for healthcare out of their own pockets, which shapes everything: what you do when you feel unwell, how long you wait before going to a clinic and how seriously you take a small problem that might become a large bill. The pharmacy and the patent medicine seller do a great deal of unofficial primary care, and a relative in the profession is the closest thing the country has to a universal health plan.

The remarkable thing is how many of those professionals leave. The Zikoko Abroad Life series is full of Nigerian nurses and healthcare staff who have crossed to the NHS, usually after a long, expensive and exhausting route. One interviewee, Kainene, 28, left in mid-2023 for an NHS job. She says registration in England took about six months, with documents posted from Nigeria and fees at every step, and that she sent about 120 applications for four interviews and two offers. She also had to learn to drive a manual car in the UK, which ate into her savings.

Another, Bambs, 31, an ICU nurse who left in 2021 during the tail of COVID, says she sent 20 applications a day, sometimes 30, and got hundreds of rejections before four offers arrived in January. One interview was taken in a work toilet because it was the only quiet place. That is not a migration story. It is a sports story: stamina, repetition and a stranger's yes at the end.

The money side of leaving is its own plot twist. Bambs says she paid for her move herself on a corper's salary, which anyone who knows the Nigerian national service scheme will recognise as a feat of arithmetic. When the offers finally came, the hospital booked the flights, the nurses arrived as a group, and they spent ten days isolating in hospital-provided accommodation. She deferred her start from March to April. That sequence of bureaucratic waiting, sudden speed and a plane full of colleagues is the usual route, and it is why so many Nigerian nurses first meet their British workplace as a corridor in quarantine.

## The United Kingdom

On arrival, the NHS is both a relief and a lesson in patience. Bambs says the UK felt cold and the accent was fast, and that she was surprised by how late it stays light in summer and by how many people walk around shirtless in the sun. She now rates her happiness at eight out of ten, and calls winter the thing she hates most. Kainene is less cheerful. She rates hers at about 6.6 out of ten, says she has not been back to Nigeria since she left because of ticket prices and money sent to family, and describes the loneliness of having few friends.

The workplace is where the NHS shows its other face. Both women describe racism from patients. Kainene says it ranges from direct comments to the quieter kind, such as being told her English is good, and says her ideas are dismissed unless a local says them. Bambs says a patient was racist to her and she reported it. These are the people the system relies on, and the system is frequently unkind to them.

Then there is the human side of arriving. A Nigerian couple, Yusuf and Nimat, writing on Substack, settled in Leicester, where there is an established Nigerian community. Yusuf was a doctor who arrived first and lived alone for over a year. Nimat, who did not yet have her UK licence, worked during the pandemic as an NHS helpline respondent from home. They say being away from family is the hardest part, and that video calls are a lifeline. Their son eats amala at home and a jacket potato at nursery, which is the most British sentence I have read all week.

As a patient, the practical advice for a new arrival is dull and essential. Register with a GP before you are ill, learn what the pharmacist can sort out and do not treat A&E as a walk-in clinic.

The small shocks are what stay with people. Kainene says British buses have to be planned in advance, that pubs are mainly for drinking, and that smoking is common, including among teenagers who vape. Her best British memory is Hull Fair, a roller coaster and a Ferris wheel, which tells you how little a person can ask of a country and still be grateful. Bambs mentions that nurse careers in the UK have a ceiling and that she has passed US nursing exams, which suggests that for many the NHS is not a destination but a waypoint. She also says travel, which Nigerians treat as a flex, becomes ordinary in the UK, where a weekend in Italy can cost about three hundred pounds. The health system you join is also the one that lets you leave.

## The Verdict

Britain wins, but I would like to be clear about what it is winning. The NHS is a better deal than paying cash for care, and nobody should romanticise the alternative. A system that treats you first and asks about money never is a moral achievement, even when it is run on goodwill and overtime.

But I will not hand it the full trophy. It is a system whose staff, many of them Nigerian, are tired, sometimes mistreated and often far from home. A healthcare culture is not just whether you get treated. It is whether the people doing the treating are treated decently.

The sting is this. Nigeria exports its nurses and Britain imports them, and then both complain about the shortage.

## Conclusion

Healthcare tells you what a society thinks a person is worth. Nigeria asks what you can pay. Britain asks what you need and then asks you to wait.

Neither answer is perfect, and the people carrying the system on their backs know it better than anyone. If you move, register with a GP, learn the phone queue and keep your friends close.

And if a colleague tells you your English is good, you have my permission to say it back.
