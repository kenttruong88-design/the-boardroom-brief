# One Country Makes Every Neighbour Your Aunty. The Other Makes Every Town Its Own Tiny Nation.

🇬🇭 Ghana 🇮🇹 Italy
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Neighbourhood life is where you find out whether a culture believes community is something you're born into by default or something you build, slowly, over decades of showing up to the same piazza. Ghana and Italy have built two of the warmest, most specific answers to this question I've encountered in sixty countries of looking, and they couldn't be more differently shaped.

In Ghana, your neighbour isn't just your neighbour — within about a week of moving in, they are your aunty, your uncle, an honorary relative with the standing to comment on your cooking, your visitors, and occasionally your life choices, whether you asked for the input or not. In Italy, community runs through geography with an intensity that borders on tribal — not your neighbour specifically, but your town, your piazza, your particular corner of a country where loyalty to your own campanile, literally your own bell tower, outranks loyalty to the nation itself more often than Italians will comfortably admit. One builds community through instant, almost familial closeness. The other builds it through centuries of fierce, specific, geographic pride.

## Do's & Don'ts: Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet every neighbour you pass, every single time, by name if you know it — skipping this reads as genuinely rude, not just distant | Treat "aunty" and "uncle" as literal kinship terms you need to verify; they're honorific, applied warmly to any respected elder or close neighbour |
| Expect communal involvement in your household affairs — compound living means shared space, shared childcare, and shared opinions | Be surprised when a neighbour disciplines or looks after your child without being asked; it's viewed as a shared communal duty, not overreach |
| Contribute to neighbourhood events — funerals, naming ceremonies, church functions — even for people you don't know personally | Assume privacy works the way it might elsewhere; compound and close-neighbourhood living means very little stays unnoticed |

## Do's & Don'ts: Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the evening passeggiata — the unhurried stroll through the piazza — it's where real neighbourhood social life actually happens | Badmouth a neighbouring town, even jokingly, until you understand the specific, often centuries-old rivalry you've just stepped into |
| Shop at the local alimentari and bar regularly, not just the supermarket — becoming a known face there is how you actually get accepted into the neighbourhood | Assume Italian friendliness in the piazza extends to being let off the hook on local gossip; everyone genuinely does know everyone's business |
| Learn your specific town or neighbourhood's own saint, festival, and football rivalries; identity here is intensely local before it's national | Expect fast friendship; Italian neighbourhood warmth is real but built slowly, through visible, repeated presence over months and years |

## Ghana: Community as Instant, Informal Family

The organising unit of Ghanaian neighbourhood life is, for many families, the compound house — a shared courtyard arrangement where multiple households live in close, genuinely communal proximity, and the Ghana Statistical Service's own housing data reflects just how common this living arrangement remains, especially outside the wealthiest urban enclaves. What that proximity produces, socially, is a neighbourhood culture where the boundary between "family" and "neighbour" is far more porous than most newcomers expect. Calling an older neighbour "aunty" or "uncle" isn't a figure of speech so much as the correct, expected form of address — a small daily acknowledgment that this person has real standing in your life, with the accompanying right to comment on your choices, check in on your wellbeing, and occasionally discipline your children in a manner that would raise eyebrows elsewhere and is, here, simply shared communal duty.

Greeting is not optional, and this is the single thing that trips up newcomers fastest: walking past a neighbour without a greeting, even a brief one, lands as a genuine social failure rather than a minor oversight, because the greeting itself is the daily maintenance work that keeps the whole communal fabric functioning. Church, too, often anchors this further — attendance isn't just personal faith, it's neighbourhood infrastructure, the place where the same compound and street residents reinforce their ties weekly. The upside of all this is a depth of mutual support that's genuinely hard to overstate: illness, childcare, financial hardship, a funeral — the neighbourhood absorbs these collectively, as a matter of course, in a way that more privacy-oriented cultures simply don't replicate. The cost, and it is a real cost for some newcomers, is that very little about your life stays actually private.

## Italy: Community as Fierce, Specific, Geographic Loyalty

Italian neighbourhood identity runs on a logic the Italians themselves have a word for: campanilismo, loyalty to your own bell tower, your own specific piazza and parish, often outranking any broader regional or even national identity in daily emotional weight. This isn't a quaint historical footnote — it's alive in the rivalries between neighbouring towns over football, over who makes the better version of a regional dish, over which town's patron saint festival is more impressive, rivalries that can run for generations and that outsiders dismiss as charming until they accidentally praise the wrong town's cooking at the wrong dinner table.

The actual daily mechanism of Italian neighbourhood life is the passeggiata — the evening stroll through the piazza, unhurried, often undertaken by entire families, where greetings, gossip, and genuine social bonds get maintained in public, in the open air, rather than behind closed doors. The local bar and the alimentari, the small neighbourhood grocer, function as genuine social infrastructure here too — becoming a recognised regular at either is a real, if slow-building, form of neighbourhood acceptance, the Italian equivalent of being handed an honorary "aunty" title, just earned through repeated quiet presence rather than instant warmth. What's notable, and what catches newcomers from more guarded cultures off balance, is how much everyone genuinely does know everyone's business in a tight-knit Italian neighbourhood — not maliciously, usually, but as an inevitable byproduct of a community that still conducts so much of its social life in full public view.

## The Verdict

Ghana, by a decent margin, and here's why: Italy's neighbourhood culture is beautiful, genuinely, but it takes years to actually earn — you stroll the same piazza for a long time before you're truly inside it. Ghana's version hands you provisional membership almost immediately, the moment you move in and start greeting your neighbours properly, and that immediacy matters enormously if you're an expat without the luxury of a decade to slowly earn your place. Both cultures will eventually absorb you into something that functions like family. Ghana just does it on week one instead of year five, and when you're far from home, that speed is its own form of kindness.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/ghana — a newcomer asked, slightly alarmed, why an elderly neighbour had scolded their child for running near the road, and the top reply was simply: "that's not overstepping, that's just what aunty does, say thank you."*

> *Internations Rome — a longtime expat described taking nearly two years of daily visits to the same local bar before being genuinely, visibly welcomed as a regular, calling the moment "oddly one of the proudest of my life abroad."*

> *expat.com Accra forum — a foreign resident admitted forgetting to greet a neighbour once, in a rush, and spending the next week actively repairing the relationship, noting "I did not realise how much that one missed greeting would matter."*

## Conclusion

Both of these countries are, in their own very different ways, arguing against the idea that your neighbours are strangers you happen to live near. Ghana collapses that distance almost instantly, through greeting, through compound living, through a vocabulary of kinship extended generously to people who share no blood with you at all. Italy makes you earn it, slowly, through piazza strolls and years of showing up at the same bar, but the loyalty that results runs centuries deep once it's yours. Neither approach is for the person who genuinely wants to be left alone. Good news: that person was never going to enjoy living abroad anyway.
