# One Country Has Two Seasons, Both Wet. The Other Has Four, and One of Them Is Trying to Kill You.

🇲🇾 Malaysia 🇰🇷 South Korea
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Malaysia's weather forecast is less a forecast than a formality — hot, humid, and a strong chance of rain, repeated 365 days a year with only the monsoon's intensity dial turning up or down. South Korea, by brutal contrast, runs the full four-season gauntlet with a vengeance: summers thick enough with humidity to rival Kuala Lumpur, winters cold enough to freeze the Han River solid, and a national coping infrastructure built to survive both extremes within the same calendar year.

I have sweated through a Kuala Lumpur afternoon so humid my notebook paper curled, and I have stood on a Seoul street in January where the wind made my face physically hurt within ninety seconds. Both countries have built entire ways of life around weather extremity. Only one of them gets to complain about variety.

## Do's & Don'ts: Malaysia 🇲🇾

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry a compact umbrella everywhere, always — monsoon downpours arrive with almost no warning, even outside the official wet season | Schedule anything tightly around outdoor plans during the Northeast Monsoon (November–March), especially on the east coast |
| Embrace the air-conditioned indoor life — malls, offices and transit hubs are kept aggressively cold specifically to offset outdoor humidity | Underestimate flash flooding in Klang Valley — "a bit of rain" can close roads and strand cars within the hour |
| Dress in breathable, light layers regardless of season — there effectively isn't a cold season to plan around | Expect seasonal wardrobe changes the way you might elsewhere — most Malaysians own one climate wardrobe, adjusted only for rain |

## Do's & Don'ts: South Korea 🇰🇷

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in proper winter layers — thermal base layers, a real coat, not a fashionable one — before your first December | Assume Korean summer is mild because the country feels temperate on a map — Seoul summers run brutally hot and humid, monsoon rains included |
| Use heated flooring (ondol) and government cold-wave shelters if you're genuinely struggling with the cold snaps | Ignore yellow dust (hwangsa) and fine dust advisories in spring — mask-wearing on bad air days is a real health precaution, not an affectation |
| Lean into seasonal culture — cherry blossom season, ice fishing festivals, summer bingsu — Koreans structure entire social calendars around the shift | Pack a one-season wardrobe and assume you'll "figure it out" — the temperature swing between July and January can exceed 50°C |

## Malaysia: Mastering the Art of Permanent Humidity

Malaysia doesn't really have seasons in the sense most of the world understands them; it has a wet season and a slightly-less-wet season, modulated by two monsoon systems — the Southwest Monsoon roughly May to September, and the more disruptive Northeast Monsoon roughly November to March, which batters the east coast of Peninsular Malaysia and parts of Borneo with serious flooding risk. Temperatures hover in a narrow, relentlessly warm band year-round, typically 25–33°C, with humidity sitting so consistently high that Malaysians have essentially stopped discussing it, the way you stop noticing a hum after enough years.

What's genuinely impressive is the national coping infrastructure built entirely around this permanent humidity. Malaysian architecture, both historic and modern, leans hard into shade, airflow and elevation — the traditional kampung house on stilts wasn't an aesthetic choice, it was climate engineering. Modern Malaysia has simply swapped stilts for industrial-strength air conditioning, and the result is a country where indoor spaces are kept noticeably, almost aggressively cold specifically to create relief from the heat outside, a contrast so stark that carrying a light jacket for mall visits is standard local practice despite the tropical climate just beyond the doors.

The real seasonal hazard isn't heat, it's water. The Northeast Monsoon brings genuine flooding risk to Kelantan, Terengganu, and parts of Johor virtually every year, sometimes severely enough to require evacuations, while Klang Valley's notorious flash floods can turn a routine Kuala Lumpur commute into a stranded-car crisis within thirty minutes of unexpected downpour. Locals read the sky with a practised wariness tourists rarely develop in time, and the national umbrella-carrying habit isn't superstition, it's lived experience of how fast "sunny" becomes "biblical" here.

## South Korea: Four Seasons, Each With an Attitude

South Korea occupies a genuinely punishing climatic position — far enough north for brutal continental winters, far enough into the East Asian monsoon belt for genuinely tropical-feeling summer humidity, and positioned to catch both Siberian cold fronts and seasonal fine-dust blowing in from industrial regions further west. The seasonal swing is extreme by almost any global standard: Seoul winters regularly drop below -10°C with biting wind chill, while summer highs push past 33°C with humidity thick enough to make a Malaysian feel briefly at home, all within the same twelve months.

Korean culture has responded by building seasonality deep into both infrastructure and social life. Ondol, the traditional underfloor heating system, has been so successful it's now standard in virtually all modern housing, meaning Koreans historically and still today sit, eat and sleep on heated floors through winter rather than relying purely on ambient room heat. Public cold-wave shelters open during the harshest snaps for anyone without adequate heating, a genuine social safety net response to weather Seoul's government treats with real seriousness, not just advisory language.

Spring brings its own hazard entirely unrelated to temperature: hwangsa, fine dust and yellow dust blown in seasonally, bad enough on certain days that mask-wearing (already culturally normalised well before any pandemic) becomes a genuine respiratory precaution rather than habit. But Koreans have also built an entire aesthetic and emotional culture around the seasonal shift that Malaysia's climate simply doesn't offer the raw material for — cherry blossom (beotkkot) season draws crowds to specific viewing spots with near-religious devotion, autumn foliage trips are a defined social ritual, and summer bingsu (shaved ice dessert) culture exists specifically because the heat demands a ritual response. Weather here isn't just survived, it's narrated, anticipated, and built into the national calendar as a recurring character.

## The Verdict

Malaysia wins on sheer predictability — once you've adapted to the humidity, every day asks roughly the same thing of you, and the coping strategy barely changes year to year. South Korea wins on resilience and culture-building — surviving and even romanticising a fifty-degree annual temperature swing takes a kind of adaptive sophistication that a permanently warm country never has to develop. If you want weather that asks little variation of you, Malaysia's monotony is oddly restful. If you want weather that forces genuine seasonal reinvention, Korea wins, though it will cost you a winter coat budget Malaysia never makes you spend.

## Conclusion

Malaysia asks you to make peace with permanence — the same warm, wet air, every day, forever, until you stop noticing it entirely. South Korea asks you to survive a genuine seasonal war four times a year and then, somehow, to love the battle enough to photograph the cherry blossoms. Both are legitimate responses to an unreasonable planet. Neither country is interested in your opinion about which approach is correct, and honestly, that's probably the healthiest attitude either of them could have.
