# A City That Trains Like It's Late for a Meeting, and a Country That Trains Like It Has Nowhere to Be

🇭🇰 Hong Kong vs 🇨🇦 Canada — Fitness and Gym Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Hong Kong does not have a fitness culture so much as a vertical cramming exercise with resistance bands attached. I lived eleven months in a flat above Sai Ying Pun where the nearest "gym" was a converted office unit with six squat racks, one mirror, and a queue system enforced entirely by passive-aggressive sighing. Canada, by brutal contrast, treats exercise as something that happens to you accidentally while you are simply trying to get a coffee four hundred metres away, because someone built the city with the apparent assumption that oxygen is free and time is infinite.

I have had both these countries' relationship with exercise explained to me with total sincerity as "basically the same, just outdoors versus indoors," by someone who had clearly never tried to find floor space in Causeway Bay at 7pm on a Tuesday. It is not the same. One is a logistics puzzle. The other is a lifestyle you can apparently just download for free, assuming you own a decent coat.

## Do's & Don'ts: Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book your gym slot the second your membership unlocks it — the good machine times vanish by 8:01am | Assume "24-hour gym" means spacious; it means small and occupied at every single one of those hours |
| Try a hiking trail on Hong Kong Island — the city produces genuinely excellent weekend hikers | Wear your gym kit on the MTR and act surprised when someone side-eyes you for the smell |
| Learn the unspoken circuit-training etiquette before you touch someone's "claimed" bench | Expect a personal trainer's "free consultation" to stay free past minute eleven |
| Join a dragon boat or running club — the social fitness scene here is genuinely excellent | Complain loudly about queueing for equipment; everyone is doing it, silently, with more dignity than you |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in actual winter gear if you intend to keep running outdoors past October | Assume the gym closes for weather — Canadians will not notice a blizzard has started |
| Try a community rec centre — astonishingly cheap, astonishingly well-equipped | Expect anyone to comment on your effort, your form, or your existence; the silence is the culture |
| Get outside for the three months of good weather like your social calendar depends on it, because it does | Skip the handshake-firm post-workout nod to the regulars; it is the entire social contract of the gym floor |
| Try a YMCA membership before committing to a boutique studio — the value gap is enormous | Underestimate how seriously a "casual" beer-league hockey game is actually being taken |

## Hong Kong: Where Floor Space Is the Actual Luxury Good

Hong Kong's fitness culture makes perfect sense the moment you accept one fact: real estate here is so violently expensive that gyms are built to the same square-footage logic as the flats. A "spacious" studio means you can do a lunge without clipping someone's protein shaker. The big international chains — Pure Fitness, Physical, California Fitness in its various reincarnations — charge prices that would get you a reasonably serious personal trainer back home, for the privilege of sharing a squat rack with forty of your closest strangers during peak hours, which in Hong Kong means roughly eighteen hours a day.

And yet — and this is the part that converts even the most committed skeptic — Hong Kong produces some genuinely superb amateur athletes, because the compensation for terrible gyms is terrific terrain. The hiking trails threading Hong Kong Island, the Dragon's Back, the MacLehose Trail sections that make you forget you are twenty minutes from a Chanel boutique, are used relentlessly and well. Dragon boat training starts absurdly early and takes itself more seriously than most people's actual jobs. The city's runners treat the waterfront promenades like a free, humidity-assisted sauna circuit, which, in July, they genuinely are.

The Leisure and Cultural Services Department operates public pools and sports centres that are cheap, clean, and permanently full, because everyone in this city has worked out that paying HK$5,800 a month for a gym the size of a hotel room is a mug's game when the government will rent you a lane for the price of a coffee. Book two weeks ahead or don't bother. The queue culture extends to the equipment itself: there is an entire unspoken choreography around how long you are permitted to occupy a bench before someone starts hovering with the specific, wordless intensity only Hong Kong commuters and gym-goers seem to master. Learn it fast. Nobody will explain it to you, and nobody will forgive you for not knowing it.

## Canada: The Gym Is Optional When the Country Itself Is the Equipment

Canada's approach to fitness can be summarised as: why would you pay to sweat indoors when there is an entire uncomplaining continent outside doing it for free. This works beautifully for exactly the four months a year when the weather agrees to cooperate, and becomes a genuine character test for the other eight, which is precisely when you find out who the real Canadians are — the ones who keep running, cycling, and attending outdoor boot camps at minus fifteen degrees with an expression of mild, cheerful defiance that I personally find unsettling.

Community recreation centres are the unsung triumph of Canadian civic life — astonishingly well-funded, astonishingly cheap, and stocked with everything from Olympic pools to climbing walls, usually for an annual fee that wouldn't cover six weeks at a Hong Kong boutique studio. ParticipACTION's decades of public nagging about physical activity has clearly landed somewhere, because Canadians treat movement as a civic duty rather than a vanity project, which is refreshing until you realise it means nobody is going to compliment your deadlift. Ever. The gym floor here runs on a very specific social contract: minimal eye contact, a firm nod of acknowledgment to the regulars, and an almost religious respect for not asking anyone what they're training for.

Winter sport is not a seasonal hobby in Canada, it is closer to a second national identity. Beer-league hockey is played with a level of tactical seriousness that would embarrass a Bundesliga scout, and cross-country skiing is treated as a perfectly reasonable commute option in towns that take it that far. The rest of the year gets reclaimed violently: paddleboards, trail runs, lake swims, and an entire culture of people who will tell you, unprompted, about a "great little hike" that turns out to be eleven kilometres with a vertical gain that should legally require a permit.

## The Verdict

If you want to be forged into a disciplined, resourceful, queue-tolerant athlete who can do a full workout in the square footage of a parking space, Hong Kong will build that person out of you within six weeks flat, whether you asked for it or not. If you want to rediscover the actual, unmonetised joy of movement — the kind that doesn't require a membership card, a towel fee, or a stranger's elbow in your ribs — Canada hands you that on a plate, assuming you own a coat that can survive minus twenty.

Canada wins this one, and it isn't close. Hong Kong's fitness scene is a masterclass in making do; Canada's is an entire country quietly, smugly, correctly convinced that the best gym was always outside. Pay for the convenience in Hong Kong if you must, but don't pretend it's the same as having actual room to move.

## Conclusion

Here is the uncomfortable truth neither city wants printed on a membership brochure: Hong Kong's fitness culture is a direct symptom of its real estate market, and Canada's is a direct symptom of having more land than it knows what to do with. Neither is really about fitness. One is about making peace with scarcity with impeccable queueing manners; the other is about making peace with a climate that is trying, politely but persistently, to kill you nine months of the year. Choose your punishment. Just don't move to Hong Kong expecting California Fitness to feel like California, and don't move to Canada expecting anyone to notice your gains — the only thing Canadians will notice is whether you held the door, and whether your boots are actually rated for the cold, because apparently that's the metric that matters here.
