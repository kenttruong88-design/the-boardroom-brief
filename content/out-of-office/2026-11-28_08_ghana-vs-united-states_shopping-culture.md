# Ghana Haggles Like a Diplomat, America Shops Like It Is Under Siege

🇬🇭 Ghana vs 🇺🇸 United States

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The day after American Thanksgiving, a nation of three hundred million people pursues a television as though it were a lost relative. A few thousand miles away, in the markets of Accra, a woman and a stranger negotiate the price of a bunch of plantains with the poise of two ambassadors. Both are shopping. Only one of them is doing it with dignity.

I have bought a bag of onions in Makola and a bag of anything in a suburban mall in Ohio, and I can tell you the products are the same and the theatre is entirely different. In Ghana, shopping is conversation. In America, it is a contact sport with a loyalty card.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇬🇭 Ghana ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet the seller before you ask the price | Launch straight into negotiating without a hello |
| Bargain with a smile and a sense of humour | Insult the goods or storm off dramatically |
| Carry small notes and coins, as change is scarce | Hand over a large note for a tiny purchase |
| Use mobile money where vendors accept it | Assume everyone takes a foreign card |

| 🇺🇸 United States ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Remember that the sticker price excludes sales tax | Be surprised by the total at the register |
| Keep your receipt, since returns are generous | Open a product and then lose the proof of purchase |
| Join the store rewards programme for discounts | Ignore the app, which is where the savings live |
| Be polite to the cashier, who will chat unprompted | Stare at your phone and say nothing |

## 🇬🇭 Ghana

Ghanaian shopping begins with a greeting, and anyone who skips it has already lost the argument. The market is a social place, with vendors who know their regulars, who offer a good price to a friend and a different price to a stranger. Makola in Accra, Kejetia in Kumasi and the neighbourhood markets of every town are sensory experiences: colour, smell, noise and an astonishing range of goods, from fabric and spices to second hand clothes and plumbing.

Bargaining is not rude. It is expected, and a first price is simply an opening bid in a conversation. Foreigners are sometimes quoted a higher price than locals, which is a custom rather than an insult, and the best cure is to take a local friend or to learn the going rate and settle with good humour. The goal is a price that both of you can live with, and a story to tell.

The modern retail sector is growing. Malls such as Accra Mall, West Hills Mall and Kumasi City Mall, together with supermarkets and chains, offer fixed prices, air conditioning and imported goods at a premium. Expats often use these for groceries and household items, and turn to the markets for fruit, fabric and anything worth haggling over. Local press reports suggest that most Ghanaians still do much of their shopping in traditional markets, which says as much about price as about tradition.

Street selling is an art form. Hawkers walk between cars in traffic, balancing trays of water, plantain chips, phone chargers and, for reasons nobody has explained, a full-sized mirror. You can buy a surprising amount without leaving the back seat, and the vendors are rarely pushy.

Payment is increasingly by mobile money, and the system is widely used, from market stalls to big shops. Cash still matters, particularly in small markets, and small notes are a currency in their own right.

The role of the informal economy cannot be ignored. In Ghana, a vast network of small traders, seamstresses, tailors, cobblers and market women makes goods and repairs them on the spot. Fabric is bought by the yard and sewn to measure, shoes are mended on the pavement, and a good tailor is a family treasure. The idea of throwing something away because a button fell off would strike most Ghanaians as a lack of imagination.

## 🇺🇸 United States

American shopping is built on abundance, convenience and a sacred belief in the return policy. The big box stores, supermarkets, malls and online giants offer a stunning range of products at prices that vary by the hour, and the whole system is designed to make buying as frictionless as possible. You can purchase a washing machine, a guitar and a goldfish in a single trip, and return all three the following week.

Prices are fixed and the sticker is the starting point, with sales tax added at the register in most states, a quirk that continues to baffle visitors. Discounts are everywhere: coupons, loyalty apps, seasonal sales, clearance racks and a calendar of shopping events that culminates in Black Friday and Cyber Monday. The theatre of the early morning crowd is partly myth and partly tradition, though more of the frenzy has moved online.

The culture is chatty. Cashiers ask how your day is going, and the correct answer is a cheerful, brief one. Customer service is a point of national pride, and "the customer is always right" is treated as something close to scripture. Returns are generous by global standards, which an expat from a haggling culture will find strange and wonderful.

There are costs. Distances are large, so shopping is often a car journey to a vast car park. Fast fashion, packaging and overbuying are part of the landscape, and the speed of delivery can turn impulse into habit. Many newcomers find that they own more than they ever intended within a year.

For a Ghanaian arriving in the United States, the absence of bargaining is the first culture shock, and the abundance is the second. The third is discovering that you can no longer buy a single egg.

American shopping has its own informal rules. Thrift shops, garage sales, marketplace apps and outlet malls give shoppers more bargaining room than they realise, and a polite question about a price tag is not always refused. Tipping is not expected in most shops, but it is for services such as hairdressers, delivery drivers and restaurants, and the line between the two trips up many newcomers.

## The Verdict

Ghana wins on character and America wins on convenience, and I would not want to live without either. A market in Accra is a place where you can be treated as a person, where a transaction can become a friendship and where the price depends on charm. An American superstore is a place where you can be treated as a customer, where the price is fixed and where the refund is guaranteed.

If I want a good story, I go to Makola. If I want a good return, I go to a mall in Ohio. If I want to understand a country, I go to both, and watch what people do when someone tries to take the last item on the shelf.

The sting is that each country is proud of exactly what the other finds exhausting. Ghanaians find the American system sterile and slightly lonely. Americans find the Ghanaian market loud and slightly confusing. Both are shopping, and both think the other has misunderstood the point.

The practical lesson is to bring the right tool. In Ghana, bring patience, small change and a sense of humour. In America, bring a card, a receipt folder and a calculator for sales tax.

Seasonal rhythms matter too. Ghanaian markets peak around Christmas, Eid and funerals, when fabric, food and gifts are in demand, while American retail peaks in November and December, then slumps into the January sales. In both countries the smart shopper knows that the best time to buy something is not when the crowd is buying it.

## Conclusion

Shopping is the quickest way to learn a country's manners. In Ghana, you learn that a relationship comes before a purchase. In America, you learn that a purchase can be undone if you change your mind.

Take the best of both. Greet the person behind the counter, whichever country you are in, and keep your receipt in case the story goes wrong. Be willing to bargain with a smile, and be willing to leave when the price is not right.

And never, under any circumstances, get between an American and a half-price television. Ghana will teach you to negotiate. America will teach you to run.
