# Singapore Will Bill You For The Air You Breathe. Denmark Will Just Make You Wait Three Years For A Flat.

🇸🇬 Singapore 🇩🇰 Denmark

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Singapore has turned renting into a luxury retail experience, complete with a concierge, a lap pool, and an invoice for things you didn't know could be invoiced. Denmark has turned renting into a bureaucratic endurance sport in which the prize, after years of patient suffering, is a slightly damp room above a bicycle shop in Nørrebro. Both countries will tell you, with a straight face, that their system is the sensible one.

I've signed fourteen leases on four continents, and I can tell you precisely where each of these countries will try to take your money: Singapore does it upfront, with a smile, via an agent who vanishes the moment the air conditioner breaks. Denmark does it slowly, over years, via a waiting list so Kafkaesque it makes you nostalgic for capitalism. Pack accordingly.

## Do's & Don'ts: Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Insist on a diplomatic clause in every tenancy agreement before you sign anything | Assume your agent is working for you — most represent the landlord, not you |
| Photograph every scratch, stain, and dead lightbulb on move-in day | Pay a cent of deposit before you've actually seen a signed Letter of Intent |
| Budget for a full month's rent in agent commission on top of your deposit | Expect a used-air-conditioner replacement to count as "like for like" |

## Do's & Don'ts: Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for Studiestartlisten or your city's housing queue the day you decide to move | Hand over three months' deposit and three months' advance rent without a signed lease in hand |
| Request the standard Danish lease template (standardlejekontrakt) in English | Assume "fully furnished" means anything beyond a bed frame and a lamp |
| Report every defect in writing within 14 days of moving in | Expect your deposit back quickly — one to two months is normal, and "refurbishment" deductions are the house style |

## Singapore

Singapore's accommodation market runs on the same principle as its airport lounges: everything looks effortless because someone is being paid enormously to make it look that way. About eighty percent of the housing stock is public HDB flats that foreigners mostly can't buy, which pushes the entire expat population into a rental market stalked by licensed agents who, per the Council for Estate Agencies' own rulebook, are supposed to represent one side of the deal but frequently behave as though they represent only themselves. On the Expat.com forum, a long-time contributor going by beppi put it with the weary precision of someone who's filed the complaint before: property agents have "a reputation for dishonesty and unprofessional behaviour," and the smarter move, in their experience, is to deal with landlords directly and skip the middleman theatre entirely.

The money works like this. You'll pay a security deposit equal to roughly one month's rent per year of the lease, and if the tenancy clears S$3,500 a month, you're also on the hook for an agent's commission of about a month's rent — a fee structure gift-wrapped to feel like a toll booth on your own address. A three-bedroom condo near the CBD runs S$4,000 to S$12,000 a month; the equivalent HDB unit, if you can find a landlord willing to show you the government approval letter you're legally entitled to see, comes in meaningfully cheaper. Serviced apartments — glossy, furnished, gym-and-pool included — exist specifically so relocating executives never have to learn any of this, at S$7,000 to S$14,000 a month for the privilege of not dealing with a landlord at all.

The one genuinely useful piece of paper in the entire system is the diplomatic clause, which lets you break a lease early if your employer transfers you out of the country, and which every single relocation guide tells you to demand and which an alarming number of first-timers forget to ask for. On r/askSingapore, a tenant going by Chaourceandwine learned the alternative the hard way: five years in one flat, a handover where the landlord signed off that no repairs were needed, and then a surprise S$700 deduction from the deposit two weeks later with zero supporting evidence. Other users' blunt verdict — this is depressingly common, foreigner or not. Singapore's rental culture isn't hostile, exactly. It's just priced, itemised, and entirely uninterested in your feelings about either.

## Denmark

Denmark's accommodation culture operates on the opposite philosophy: nothing should move quickly, and everyone should suffer the queue equally, which the Danes consider fair and everyone else considers a form of soft punishment. The country's rental law, the Lejeloven, is genuinely protective once you're inside a lease — rent increases are capped and tied to the property's value, evictions require a court order, and a tenant tribunal called the Huslejenævn exists specifically to referee deposit disputes. The problem has never been the rules. It's getting a key in the first place.

The official government housing portal is admirably honest about the obstacle course: housing-association flats are cheaper but allocated from waiting lists that can run for years, there's no central register for private rentals, and the going advance payment is brutal — commonly three months' rent as deposit, three months' rent paid up front, plus the current month, all before you've unpacked a single box. Emma Pouncey, writing for The Local about her own Copenhagen housing saga, moved eight times in two years and three months, sleeping on sofas between sublets, and described handing over a 45,000-kroner deposit as "money we knew we were unlikely to see again." She wasn't being dramatic. Landlords routinely claim "refurbishment" costs against deposits, and getting the balance back takes one to two months on a good day.

What actually works, per the people who've survived it, is unglamorous: register for the Studiestartlisten queue the moment you know you're moving, use paid listings on Boligportal because the free ones are scam-infested and oversubscribed, and accept that your first Copenhagen address will be temporary, probably sublet, and possibly someone else's guest room. The housing itself, once secured, tends to be lovely — older buildings with genuine character rather than the generic shoebox sheen you get elsewhere. You just have to out-wait half the city to get the keys.

## The Verdict

Here is the uncomfortable truth neither country wants printed: Singapore's system punishes you financially, and Denmark's punishes you temporally, and which one you'd rather suffer depends entirely on whether you have more money or more patience — most relocating professionals, notably, have neither. Singapore at least moves fast; you can be unpacked and resentful within a week. Denmark makes you audition for housing like it's a competitive postgraduate programme, except the acceptance letter is a 1-bedroom above a bakery and the interview process took fourteen months.

If I'm picking a winner, it's Singapore, barely, and only because speed beats dignity when you're the one paying rent either way. At least in Singapore the extortion is itemised on an invoice you can actually read. In Denmark, the extortion is a waiting list, and nobody will even tell you where you rank on it.

## Conclusion

Neither of these countries will apologise for how they do accommodation, and that's honestly the most interesting thing about both of them. Singapore has built a rental market as efficient and impersonal as its transit system, where everything is available instantly provided you accept that someone, somewhere, is clipping a fee off every step. Denmark has built one as fair and glacial as its bureaucracy, where the rules protect you beautifully once you're inside the fortress and do absolutely nothing to help you climb the wall. Expats arriving in either place make the same rookie mistake: assuming the accommodation culture will bend around their timeline. It won't. Singapore will cash your deposit before you've found the light switches. Denmark will still be processing your housing-queue application after you've been promoted twice. Plan your relocation budget — and your patience — accordingly, and never, under any circumstances, believe an agent who tells you the fee is "negotiable."
