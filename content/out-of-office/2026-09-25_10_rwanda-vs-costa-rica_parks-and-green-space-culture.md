# Rwanda Makes Green Space a Monthly Civic Duty, Enforced by Law and a Broom. Costa Rica Just Built a Whole National Identity Out of Standing Near a Tree and Feeling Grateful About It.

🇷🇼 Rwanda 🇨🇷 Costa Rica
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

On the last Saturday of every month in Rwanda, from around 8am until noon, the country essentially stops. Shops close. Traffic clears. And nearly every able-bodied citizen — plus any foreign resident who's been living there long enough to be noticed — heads outside to sweep streets, plant trees, clear drainage, and generally maintain shared public space, because Umuganda, mandatory community labour, is written into national policy and taken with total seriousness. Costa Rica's relationship with green space is built on the entirely opposite philosophy: no mandate, no monthly civic obligation, just a slow, decades-long cultural and political commitment to conservation that's made roughly a quarter of the country protected land, and turned "Pura Vida" from a casual greeting into something close to a national environmental ethos.

I have swept a Kigali street at 9am on a Saturday, handed a broom by a neighbour who assumed, correctly, that I'd want to participate rather than watch from a balcony. I have also stood in Costa Rica's Monteverde cloud forest in reverent silence, surrounded by other tourists doing the exact same thing, all of us performing a kind of quiet ecological gratitude that's become, itself, a genuine national export.

## Rwanda: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Participate in Umuganda if you're a resident — it's expected, and skipping it is genuinely noticed | Bring or use plastic bags; Rwanda's ban is strictly enforced, including at the airport on arrival |
| Book gorilla trekking permits well in advance — Volcanoes National Park access is tightly regulated | Litter, even minimally; Kigali's reputation as Africa's cleanest capital is a point of real national pride |
| Join a neighbourhood clean-up even informally if you're only visiting short-term — it's well received | Assume Umuganda is purely symbolic; actual infrastructure work gets done and is taken seriously |

## Costa Rica: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book national park entry in advance for popular sites like Manuel Antonio — daily caps are enforced | Stray off marked trails; conservation rules are strict and rangers do enforce them |
| Say "Pura Vida" and mean it as a genuine value, not just a tourist phrase — locals notice the difference | Assume every green space is a national park; plenty of protected land is private reserve with separate rules |
| Support eco-certified lodges and tours — the certification system here is genuinely rigorous | Expect low-cost, unplanned access everywhere; conservation infrastructure comes with real entry fees |

## Rwanda: Green Space as Collective Duty, Legally Mandated and Genuinely Effective

Umuganda — the word translates roughly to "coming together for a common purpose" — is community labour enshrined in Rwandan law, performed nationwide on the last Saturday of every month, and it is not, contrary to what a skeptical outsider might assume, a hollow, performative gesture. Roads get built. Drainage gets cleared. Trees get planted at a scale that's directly measurable in Kigali's now-famously green, orderly urban landscape — a city regularly cited as one of Africa's cleanest and best-organised capitals, a transformation that traces directly back to decades of this kind of consistent, mandatory collective labour combined with genuinely strict environmental policy, including one of the world's most rigorously enforced plastic bag bans, checked at the airport on arrival.

For foreign residents, Umuganda isn't legally mandatory in the same way it is for citizens, but the social expectation is real and consistently observed — long-term expats who skip it repeatedly are noticed, gently but unmistakably, and those who participate report a level of neighbourhood integration that's hard to achieve any other way, precisely because you're doing physical, shared labour alongside people rather than just socialising near them. Rwanda's broader conservation story extends well beyond the cities: Volcanoes National Park's mountain gorilla population has been a genuine conservation success story, rebuilt through a combination of strict protection, tightly regulated (and expensive) trekking permits, and a national tourism strategy that treats conservation revenue as directly reinvested rather than extracted. The whole system, urban and wild, reflects the same underlying philosophy — green space isn't a passive amenity here, it's an actively maintained civic project everyone's expected to contribute to.

## Costa Rica: Conservation as National Identity, Built Through Policy Rather Than Mandate

Costa Rica took a different route to a similarly green outcome: no mandatory monthly labour, but a sustained, decades-deep political and cultural commitment to conservation that's resulted in roughly a quarter of the country's land under some form of protection, a reforestation programme that's genuinely reversed decades of deforestation (forest cover has climbed back from a low point in the 1980s to over half the country today), and an ecotourism industry so successful it's become the primary economic argument for further conservation rather than against it. The "Pura Vida" ethos, often flattened by outsiders into a casual greeting or tourist marketing phrase, actually reflects something closer to genuine national self-conception — a country that abolished its military in 1948 and has spent the decades since positioning environmental stewardship as a core part of what Costa Rican identity even means.

The practical experience of this for visitors and residents alike involves considerably more structure and cost than Rwanda's community-labour model — national parks like Manuel Antonio and Corcovado enforce daily visitor caps and require advance booking, eco-lodges operate under a genuinely rigorous certification system (the Certification for Sustainable Tourism programme is taken seriously, not just marketing), and access to the country's most spectacular green spaces often comes with real entry fees that fund the conservation system directly. It's less a shared civic duty than a carefully managed, monetised conservation economy — but one that's demonstrably worked, producing outcomes environmental policymakers elsewhere study specifically because Costa Rica proved robust conservation and a thriving tourism economy aren't actually in conflict.

## The Verdict

Rwanda achieves its green outcomes through collective obligation — everyone contributes, monthly, by law and by norm, and the results are visible in every swept street and planted tree. Costa Rica achieves comparable outcomes through sustained policy and a genuinely internalised national identity, funded by the tourism it protects rather than mandated labour. I find Rwanda's model more immediately moving to witness — there's something genuinely rare about an entire capital city stopping, together, to sweep its own streets. But Costa Rica's decades-long policy discipline, sustained across multiple governments without ever requiring a single citizen to pick up a broom on command, is the harder trick to pull off. Rwanda wins on collective spirit. Costa Rica wins on proving you don't need a mandate if the policy holds long enough.

## Conclusion

Somewhere between a swept Kigali street on a Saturday morning and a silent, reverent walk through a Costa Rican cloud forest, you'll realise both countries reached genuinely green, genuinely liveable outcomes by refusing to leave it to chance. Rwanda mandated the effort and made it communal. Costa Rica built the policy and made it identity. Show up ready to actually participate in Rwanda, and ready to book, pay, and follow the rules properly in Costa Rica — neither country's green space is an accident, and neither one is interested in a visitor who treats it like one.
