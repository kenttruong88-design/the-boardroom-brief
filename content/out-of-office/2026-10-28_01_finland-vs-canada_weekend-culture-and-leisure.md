# The Quiet Wars: How Finland and Canada Turned Doing Nothing Into a National Sport

🇫🇮 Finland 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is a sentence that will mean nothing to anyone who grew up somewhere loud: the best weekend is the one where nobody can reach you. Finland and Canada have each built entire national identities around this principle, and they have arrived at it through almost opposite temperaments — one through silence enforced by law and shame, the other through geography so enormous that silence simply happens to you, whether you wanted it or not.

I have lived in both. I have been the idiot standing outside a shuttered Helsinki supermarket at 6:04pm on a Saturday, and I have been the idiot in Muskoka who agreed to "just a quick hike" and returned four hours later having seen one (1) other human being. Both countries will tell you, completely straight-faced, that this is relaxing. Both countries are correct, which is the most annoying part.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇫🇮 Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book your sauna slot like you'd book a doctor's appointment — it's that sacred | Assume shops stay open past 6pm on a Saturday; the law says otherwise |
| Say yes to a mökki invitation the first time it's offered; it won't be offered twice | Talk through the silence on a nature walk, or on the bus, or ever |
| Learn to love the cold plunge; it's non-negotiable, not a dare | Expect anyone to text back before Monday |
| Treat "maybe" as a real answer and stop chasing a yes | Bring a group of six to someone's cottage uninvited |

### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy the flannel; you will need it, and also everyone will judge you if you don't | Assume a "sure, let's hang out sometime" is an actual plan |
| Learn one sport well enough to be invited to play it, ideally hockey or curling | Expect the friend group that formed in grade six to have room for you |
| Get a cottage friend, even a fake one, by any means necessary | Confuse "nice" with "interested in seeing you again" |
| Pack bear spray before you pack snacks | Underestimate how far "just up north" actually is |

## Finland

Finland's weekend culture runs on two engines: the sauna and the mökki, and both are protected with the kind of reverence other nations reserve for constitutions. There are roughly 3.3 million saunas in a country of 5.5 million people — practically one each, which UNESCO found compelling enough to inscribe sauna culture onto its Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2020. This is not a spa amenity. It is closer to a civic duty, performed weekly, often silently, occasionally with a beer balanced on the bench, and absolutely never while discussing work.

Then there's the cottage. Statistics Finland counts close to half a million registered summer cottages, a figure that undercounts reality because it excludes anything under 40 square metres, which in Finland apparently doesn't qualify as a real structure. From roughly May to September, the entire country performs a disappearing act: cities empty, offices run on skeleton staff, and everyone who can get to a lake does. A Russian-Finnish yoga instructor quoted in coverage of the phenomenon put it plainly — Finns go to the cottage "to relax, sauna, swim, and spend quality time with relatives or friends," and the point isn't the swimming so much as the absence of everything else.

The flip side, and the part every newcomer discovers the hard way, is that Finnish weekends are legally quiet. Shops under roughly 400 square metres can set their own hours, but larger stores operate under hour restrictions that keep most things shuttered by early evening on Saturdays — a Helsinki-forum regular described it flatly as simply "the law," full stop, no further explanation needed, because to a Finn none is required. Newcomers post bewildered forum questions about why the mall went dark at 6pm; the standard answer amounts to a shrug. Combine that with a culture where social plans are rarely improvised and rarely renegotiated, and a Finnish Sunday can feel less like rest and more like house arrest with better lighting — right up until you understand that this is precisely the point. Nobody is available because nobody is supposed to be. The emptiness isn't a bug. It's the entire product, exported at considerable profit via "world's happiest country" rankings that conveniently never ask whether anyone there has had a spontaneous Tuesday-night drink with a friend in a decade.

## Canada

Canada's weekend culture is Finland's with the volume turned up and the admission price turned down, which sounds generous until you realise the price is actually your entire Saturday, lost to traffic getting "up north." Cottage life here isn't a niche hobby — surveys from Ipsos and cottage-industry outlets consistently find a majority of Canadians would rather spend a vacation at a cottage than fly anywhere exotic, and an Ipsos long-weekend poll found the top answer to "what do you want to do" wasn't an activity at all, just "be with family and friends," at 38%, ahead of hiking, camping, or doing absolutely nothing.

That sounds wholesome, and it is, provided you were issued the family and friends at birth. This is Canada's quietly brutal secret: the social infrastructure assumes you already have a crew, assembled sometime around 2003 and never updated since. On expat forums, the same complaint recurs with almost comic consistency — one long-term newcomer, after years in the country, wrote that Canadian friendships felt "virtually impossible" to break into, observing that people "stick with childhood friends and that is it," with newcomers politely gestured toward the perimeter. Another arrival outside Montreal, barely six weeks in, described becoming a "total homebody" simply because nothing else presented itself. Suburbs make this worse, not better — nice lawns, friendly waves, and absolutely nobody who will ever invite you inside for dinner.

What Canada does have, generously, is size. Statistics Canada's own satisfaction data shows over a third of adults rate their free time as unsatisfying, worst among the 25-to-54 bracket who are drowning in roughly nine hours a day of work and chores — so when the weekend finally arrives, Canadians don't so much relax as flee, toward a lake, a trail, a cabin three provinces away, anywhere the geography can do the emotional labour the social scene won't. It's a gorgeous country to be alone in. That's not always a compliment.

## The Verdict

If you want a weekend that restores you, go Finnish: book the sauna, accept the cottage invitation the first time, and trust that the silence is affection wearing a very stern face. If you want a weekend that merely distracts you, go Canadian: drive two hours, look at something enormous and indifferent, feel briefly important by comparison.

Finland wins on honesty. It never pretends the quiet is anything other than deliberate, and once you stop fighting it, the whole system clicks into place like a well-oiled door you were told not to slam. Canada wins on scenery and loses on candour — it sells you "friendly," delivers "pleasant," and leaves you to discover, usually around month four, that pleasant and included are not synonyms. Both countries will insist, entirely sincerely, that this is the best place in the world to switch off. Both are lying only slightly: it's the best place in the world to switch off alone, which is a different, colder sentence that nobody puts on the tourism website.

## Conclusion

Strip away the saunas, the lakes, the flannel, and the loons, and both countries are selling you the same product: permission to disappear. Finland just makes you sign the paperwork up front, with legally enforced shop hours and a cottage culture that expects your silence as a matter of course. Canada lets you find out gradually, usually alone, usually outdoors, usually a very long drive from anyone who might have noticed you'd gone. Neither is wrong. Rest is rest, however it's delivered. But if you're moving abroad hoping the new country will hand you a ready-made social life along with the new postcode, I have appalling news for you twice over. Pack a book. Pack bear spray too, depending on which flag you're under. Nobody's coming to get you either way, and that, apparently, is the whole appeal.
