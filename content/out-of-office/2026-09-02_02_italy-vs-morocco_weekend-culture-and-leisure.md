# One Country Naps Through the Weekend, the Other Steams Itself Clean

🇮🇹 Italy vs 🇲🇦 Morocco — weekend culture and leisure

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Italians spend their weekend walking in circles, on purpose, dressed impeccably, achieving nothing, and calling it a national pastime. Moroccans spend theirs being scrubbed raw by a stranger in a steam room while catching up on three generations of family gossip. Both consider this leisure. Both are correct, and both would be horrified to learn what the other calls relaxation.

I have lived through enough riposos and hammams to say this with total confidence: nobody on earth takes their weekend as personally as Italians and Moroccans do. Everywhere else, "weekend plans" means a vague shrug and maybe brunch. In these two countries, weekend plans are a ritual, inherited, non-negotiable, and if you try to reschedule them for your own convenience you will be gently but firmly informed that you have misunderstood the assignment.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇮🇹 Italy**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress properly for the passeggiata — it is a performance, not a stroll | Rush anyone through Sunday lunch; it is scheduled to run for hours and you will not be the exception |
| Expect small towns to shut down for riposo, especially outside the big cities | Try to run errands between 1pm and 4pm on a Sunday and expect sympathy when everything's closed |
| Treat the evening walk as the social event of the day — that's the actual point | Walk with obvious purpose or destination; aimlessness is the whole aesthetic |

**🇲🇦 Morocco**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit the souk before the hammam — buying your own black soap and kessa glove is part of the ritual | Go to a hammam alone expecting a quiet, solitary spa experience; it's a social event first |
| Budget a genuinely long chunk of the day for a proper hammam visit, dry heat through to oil massage | Rush the three-room rotation — dry heat, steam, cool — it's sequenced for a reason |
| Embrace the weekly rhythm — many locals treat the hammam as a non-negotiable weekly routine | Assume Friday functions like a Western Sunday; the rhythm of the week is genuinely different |

## Italy: The Republic of the Purposeless Walk

The passeggiata is, on paper, the least productive activity a human being can schedule into their week: a walk with no destination, undertaken between 5pm and 8pm, for the sole purpose of being seen walking. On Saturdays and Sundays entire families turn out for it, three generations deep, grandmother included, dressed as though auditioning for a role they already have. There is no rush. There is, notably, no destination — which is the part outsiders find hardest to accept. You are not walking to somewhere. You are walking to be walking, in public, among people who are also walking to be walking, and everyone silently agrees this is a perfectly reasonable way to spend an evening.

Sunday itself, domenica, is sacred in a way that has nothing to do with church attendance and everything to do with the table. Long lunches metastasise into dinners. Conversations run for hours without anyone checking a phone. In smaller towns the old tradition of riposo — the afternoon pause, sometimes a nap, sometimes just a slow unwinding — still shuts the place down between roughly 1pm and 4pm, to the eternal frustration of tourists who assumed "closed for lunch" was a metaphor. It is not a metaphor. The shutters come down, literally, and no amount of jiggling the door handle will change anyone's mind.

What I find genuinely admirable is the refusal to apologise for any of this. Italians are not confused about productivity; they've simply decided the weekend isn't where it belongs. You want a country that treats leisure as a discipline requiring its own etiquette, its own dress code, its own liturgy — Italy built one, and refined it over several centuries, and shows zero interest in updating it for your convenience.

## Morocco: Steamed, Scrubbed, and Thoroughly Caught Up

Morocco's answer to the leisurely weekend is less a stroll and more a full-body reset. The hammam — the public bathhouse, tiled in that unmistakable zellige pattern, humid enough to fog your sense of time — is not a spa in the Western sense of a private, silent indulgence. It's a social event. People rarely go alone; they go with mothers, sisters, friends, using the enclosed heat and steam as cover for hours of unhurried conversation. The full ritual moves through three rooms — dry heat, then steam, then a cooler space — while you're exfoliated with savon beldi, a traditional black soap, and a rough kessa glove that removes a genuinely alarming amount of skin you didn't know you had, finishing with a rinse and, if you're doing it properly, an oil massage.

The tradition connects directly to the souk: historically, and still today, a hammam visit begins with a trip to the market to buy your own soap, glove, and towel, turning the whole afternoon into a single, unhurried errand-slash-ritual rather than two separate tasks. Many Moroccans still treat this as a genuinely weekly fixture, not an occasional indulgence — which tells you something about how central the hammam is to how the week resets.

What outsiders misread as simply "a bath" is, in practice, a piece of infrastructure for maintaining a social fabric — the place where business gets quietly discussed, family updates get exchanged, and community gets reaffirmed, all while nobody's checking their phone because nobody can, because it's in a bag outside, wrapped in a towel, where it belongs.

## The Verdict

Italy has weaponised idleness into performance art; Morocco has weaponised cleanliness into community infrastructure. If forced to choose, I'll take the hammam — there is something almost radical about a leisure ritual that leaves you both spiritually and literally scrubbed clean, versus one that leaves you merely well-dressed and slightly more caffeinated. But I'll concede the passeggiata wins on spectacle. Nowhere else on earth will an entire town turn out, dressed to the nines, purely to watch each other walk in a loop. Morocco relaxes you. Italy performs relaxation at you. Choose accordingly.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — "What do Italians actually do on a typical Sunday?" — paraphrased: a respondent describes lunch starting at 1pm and "somehow" not ending until the sun goes down, with dessert, coffee, and a digestivo treated as three separate, non-optional courses.*

> *Expat.com Marrakech forum — paraphrased: a new resident admits their first solo hammam visit was "the loneliest hour of my life" until a local woman took pity and started scrubbing their back unprompted — apparently standard practice, not a special favour.*

> *TheLocal-style expat blog — paraphrased: a foreigner living in rural Tuscany recalls genuinely panicking the first time every shop shut mid-afternoon, before realising the entire town had simply gone home to eat and nap, unbothered by their emergency.*

## Conclusion

Both countries have quietly decided that the weekend is too important to leave unstructured, and both have built rituals sturdy enough to survive globalisation, tourism, and every attempt to modernise them out of existence. Italy's passeggiata and Morocco's hammam ask nothing of you except time — real, unhurried, undistracted time — which is precisely the one resource most visitors arrive completely unwilling to spend. Learn to spend it anyway. You'll leave both countries walking slower, complaining less, and, in Morocco's case, several layers of skin lighter. Consider it a professional development course in doing absolutely nothing, properly.
