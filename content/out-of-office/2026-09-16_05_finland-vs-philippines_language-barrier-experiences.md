# One Country Makes You Feel Illiterate in Its Impossible Language, the Other Won't Even Let You Practise

🇫🇮 Finland vs Philippines 🇵🇭
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I spent four months diligently learning Finnish — fifteen noun cases, a vocabulary that shares almost nothing with any language I'd previously encountered, verbs that fold in on themselves like linguistic origami — only to have every single Finn I attempted it on switch, within roughly two words, to flawless English, out of what I eventually understood was genuine kindness rather than judgment of my terrible accent. In Manila, I tried the opposite tack, arriving with zero Tagalog and assuming I'd be forced to learn fast. Instead I discovered Taglish, a fluid, code-switching hybrid that meant everyone understood me perfectly while I understood roughly sixty percent of any given sentence back.

Finland presents a language so structurally alien that locals have collectively decided it's kinder to just speak yours instead. The Philippines presents a linguistic landscape so fluidly bilingual that the barrier isn't comprehension, it's ever finding a stable target to actually practise against. Both leave you, in different ways, permanently unable to order a coffee in the local tongue with any real confidence.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇫🇮 Finland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic greetings and pleasantries, they're genuinely appreciated | Expect much small talk in either language — silence is comfortable here |
| Persist in Finnish even when they switch to English, if you want to actually learn | Take the switch to English personally — it's efficiency, not judgment |
| Use apps and classes designed specifically for Finnish's case system | Assume knowing Swedish or Estonian gives you a real head start |

**🇵🇭 Philippines**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn key Tagalog phrases even knowing English will usually work | Assume English fluency means Western cultural context is shared |
| Embrace Taglish rather than fighting to keep languages separate | Expect the same dialect nationwide — over 180 languages exist across the islands |
| Ask locals to correct your Tagalog, they enjoy teaching it | Rely solely on Manila Tagalog if you're moving to Cebu or Davao — different regional languages dominate |

## Finland: A Language That Doesn't Want You to Bother, Kindly

Finnish is a Uralic language, unrelated to the Indo-European family that covers most of Europe, and this isn't pedantic trivia, it's the entire reason it feels so viciously difficult to a newcomer — the vocabulary offers almost no cognates to hang onto, the fifteen grammatical cases restructure nouns depending on their role in a sentence in ways that took me actual months to stop finding personally insulting, and the compound words stack meanings together into constructions that look, on paper, like a cat walked across a keyboard.

What makes the barrier uniquely Finnish, though, isn't just the language's difficulty, it's the response to your attempts at it. Finland has one of the highest English proficiency rates in Europe, taught rigorously from primary school, and the near-universal instinct upon hearing a foreigner stumble through Finnish is to switch to English immediately, smoothly, without fanfare — a genuine act of hospitality that nonetheless makes it extraordinarily hard to actually get the practice reps you need. You end up needing to explicitly, sometimes repeatedly, ask people to please continue in Finnish, which feels oddly confrontational in a culture that otherwise prizes not making anyone uncomfortable.

Layered onto this is Finnish conversational culture itself, which treats silence not as an awkward gap to fill but as a comfortable, normal part of interaction — the stereotype of Finns being comfortable with long pauses is, in my experience, entirely true and genuinely restful once you stop reflexively trying to fill every silence with nervous chatter. Learning Finnish, in the end, becomes less about vocabulary and more about learning when not to speak at all.

## Philippines: Fluent in Everything, Anchored to Nothing

The Philippines has two official languages, Filipino and English, plus somewhere north of 180 distinct regional languages across its more than seven thousand islands, and the practical daily result of this in urban centres like Manila is Taglish — a genuinely fluid code-switching between Tagalog and English within the same sentence, sometimes the same clause, that native speakers navigate without a second thought and that newcomers find almost impossible to pin down as a stable target for language learning.

The English proficiency here is high and historically embedded, a legacy of American colonial-era education policy, which means you can function entirely in English in Manila for years without ever being forced into Tagalog the way a stricter monolingual environment might push you. This is, in its own way, a barrier of comfort — the absence of necessity removes the pressure that usually forces language acquisition, and expats who arrive intending to "pick up Tagalog naturally" often find, five years in, that they've picked up remarkably little beyond food vocabulary and taxi directions.

Compounding this, "Tagalog" itself isn't a single stable target once you leave Luzon — Cebuano dominates the Visayas, and dozens of other regional languages hold sway elsewhere, so the Tagalog phrases you diligently practise in Manila may earn you a blank, faintly amused look in Cebu. The country's linguistic identity is genuinely, structurally plural rather than singular, and the honest lesson for a newcomer is that fluency here means comfort with constant, cheerful code-switching rather than mastery of any one fixed system.

## The Verdict

Finland's barrier is a wall you can see clearly and are simply too polite to climb, because everyone keeps offering you a perfectly good door instead. The Philippines' barrier is more like fog — you can walk through it perfectly well, understood at every step, without ever quite knowing where the edges of the language actually are. I respect Finnish for its honesty about being hard. I respect Filipino code-switching for making the whole concept of "a language barrier" feel almost quaint. Neither will hand you fluency without real, deliberate effort, no matter how kind the locals are about your attempts.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Finland — a language learner describes the specific frustration of a shopkeeper switching to flawless English the instant they detected the faintest foreign accent, undoing weeks of hard-won practice in a single sentence.*

> *Internations Manila — an expat explains that after three years they realised their "conversational Tagalog" was really conversational Taglish, and that trying to speak "pure" Tagalog to older relatives in the provinces sounded oddly formal and stilted.*

> *Quora — a respondent to "Is it hard to learn Finnish as a foreigner?" notes that the grammar isn't actually the hardest part, it's finding enough real-world Finnish-only conversation to practise on before someone takes pity and switches to English.*

## Conclusion

Finland will make you a more careful, deliberate language learner, purely because the language demands it and the locals won't push you. The Philippines will make you a faster, looser communicator who's genuinely comfortable mixing two or three languages mid-sentence without embarrassment. Both experiences quietly ruin you for monolingual environments afterward — I now default to code-switching English into conversations that don't call for it, and I still, four years on, flinch slightly at the word "kolmetoista." Some scars are linguistic. These are mine.
