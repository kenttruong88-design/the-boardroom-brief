# One Country Turns a Park Into a Family Reunion. The Other Turns It Into a Traffic System.

🇹🇷 Turkey vs 🇳🇱 Netherlands — Parks and Green Space Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Walk into a Turkish city park on a Sunday and you'll find multi-generational families camped out for entire afternoons, samovars of tea brewing on portable stoves, grills going, and enough food laid out on picnic blankets to suggest nobody is leaving before sunset. Walk into a Dutch park on any given afternoon and you'll find an almost eerily well-organised ecosystem of joggers, cyclists, and picnickers, all moving through clearly delineated paths with the quiet efficiency of a country that has genuinely optimised leisure the way it optimised its flood defences.

I've been folded into a Turkish family's tea-and-grilled-corn afternoon in Gülhane Park purely by sitting too close to their blanket and being handed a glass before I could even introduce myself, and I've been silently, correctly redirected off a cyclist's path in Vondelpark by a Dutch grandmother's single unimpressed glance. Both parks are full of people enjoying green space. Only one of them will feed you unprompted.

## Do's & Don'ts: Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a full picnic setup — tea, food, and blankets are the norm | Expect a park to be quiet or lightly used on weekends |
| Accept food or tea if offered by a neighbouring family — it's genuine hospitality | Refuse hospitality outright; a polite taste is expected |
| Arrive early for a good shaded spot in summer | Assume grilling or open flames are banned everywhere — many parks allow it |
| Bring the whole extended family — parks are built for multi-generational use | Rush through — park visits are meant to last hours, not minutes |

## Do's & Don'ts: Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stay off designated cycle paths when walking or picnicking | Assume you can grill or light a fire — it's heavily restricted |
| Keep dogs leashed where signage requires it | Play loud music — parks are shared quiet spaces by default |
| Bring your own bike; most parks are built around cycling routes | Leave rubbish behind — locals police this socially, not just legally |
| Respect quiet, sunbathing-friendly zones separate from sport areas | Assume a "park" here means space for large group gatherings — many are compact |

## Turkey: The Park as Extended Living Room

Turkish parks function less like municipal green spaces and more like a shared, unofficial extension of the home — a place where entire extended families relocate for the day, bringing tea sets, grills, folding tables, and enough food to survive a small siege. This isn't incidental; it's a deeply embedded cultural pattern rooted in the centrality of family gatherings and hospitality (*misafirperverlik*) to Turkish social life. A city park on a warm Sunday in Istanbul, whether it's the sprawling Emirgan Korusu or the more central Gülhane Park, will be full of blankets, samovars hissing away, and grandparents supervising grandchildren while parents grill corn and köfte, often for hours at a stretch.

The hospitality extends outward, too, in a way that catches visitors off guard. Sit near a Turkish family's picnic spread and there's a real chance you'll be offered tea, food, or both, not as a transaction but as an automatic extension of a cultural norm that treats a stranger nearby as, at minimum, someone worth feeding. Refusing outright can read as rude; the polite move is to accept a small taste and express genuine appreciation, even if you weren't remotely hungry.

Turkish municipalities have responded to this cultural pattern by building parks explicitly for it — wide grassy areas, designated grill zones, ample shade, and infrastructure clearly designed around groups staying for hours rather than joggers passing through. The trade-off is that Turkish parks rarely offer the kind of quiet solitude some visitors might expect from green space; weekend crowds are dense, loud, and joyfully social, which is either a delight or an overload depending on your tolerance for other people's extremely good time.

## Netherlands: The Park as Infrastructure

Dutch parks operate with the same underlying logic as the rest of Dutch public infrastructure: everything has a designated lane, a designated use, and a set of unwritten but rigorously enforced social rules that keep a small, densely populated country functioning smoothly. Vondelpark in Amsterdam is the most famous example — a beautiful, heavily used green space that nonetheless maintains an almost choreographed order, with clear separation between cycling routes, walking paths, sunbathing lawns, and picnic zones, all silently respected by locals who would never dream of picnicking in the middle of a cycle path.

This orderliness reflects a broader Dutch relationship with shared space that prizes *gezelligheid* — a kind of cosy, low-key conviviality — over the louder, more expansive social gatherings you'd see in Turkey. Dutch park culture leans toward smaller groups, quiet sunbathing, reading, or a casual picnic among friends, rather than sprawling extended-family affairs with grills going for hours. Open flames and grilling are, in fact, heavily restricted or banned outright in most Dutch parks, both for safety and because it simply doesn't fit the more contained, tidy way green space gets used here.

Cycling infrastructure runs directly through Dutch parks as functional transit corridors, not just recreational paths, which means pedestrians who wander absentmindedly onto a cycle lane inside a park will be reminded, briskly and without much patience, that this is still a route Dutch commuters use daily. Combined with strict social norms around litter (locals will genuinely give you a look, or say something, if you leave rubbish behind), Dutch parks feel less like a place to sprawl out and more like a beautifully maintained shared resource everyone has silently agreed to use responsibly.

## The Verdict

Turkey wins on warmth and social abundance — no park experience matches being unexpectedly fed tea and grilled corn by strangers who simply enjoy sharing their afternoon with you. The Netherlands wins on function and fairness — a small, densely populated country has built green space that genuinely works for everyone, cyclists and picnickers alike, through sheer disciplined order. If you want a park experience that feels like being adopted by a family for an afternoon, Istanbul delivers. If you want a park experience that runs like clockwork and never once interrupts a cyclist's commute, Amsterdam has already thought of everything.

## Conclusion

Turkey and the Netherlands have built two of the most fully realised, culturally coherent park systems in the world, and neither would recognise the other's version as remotely correct. Turkey treats a park as a living room extended outdoors, full of tea, family, and generosity toward strangers. The Netherlands treats a park as shared civic infrastructure, orderly and efficient, where everyone's convenience matters equally and grilling is simply not on the table. Bring an appetite to Istanbul. Bring a bicycle, and impeccable lane discipline, to Amsterdam.
