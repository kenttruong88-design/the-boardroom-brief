🇮🇹 **Italy** vs 🇲🇹 **Malta**
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Italy and Malta are separated by a short flight and a wildly different relationship with sweating in public. Italian gym culture is surprisingly formal and image-conscious — you will be judged, subtly but definitely, on your outfit before anyone cares about your deadlift. Maltese fitness culture, by contrast, barely bothers with the gym at all when there's a coastline this good; outdoor running routes, sea swims, and a small but devoted CrossFit scene matter more than any indoor facility ever will.

I've been side-eyed in a Milan gym for wearing the wrong leggings. I've also watched a Maltese CrossFit box run an entire class outdoors because, frankly, why wouldn't they. Only one of these cultures understands that fitness and vanity were always going to be the same sport.

## Do's & Don'ts: Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress properly for the gym — coordinated, well-fitted activewear is genuinely expected | Show up in mismatched, worn-out gear and expect nobody to notice — they will |
| Say "buongiorno" to the front desk and regulars — gym etiquette includes basic social ritual | Skip the greeting and head straight to the machines; it reads as oddly rude |
| Book group fitness classes in advance — popular ones fill up fast in cities like Milan and Rome | Assume drop-in culture works everywhere — many clubs expect membership commitment |
| Expect a genuine emphasis on form and technique, often with attentive trainers | Rush through a workout without proper technique — it will get commented on |

## Do's & Don'ts: Malta

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take advantage of sea swimming and coastal running routes as the default fitness scene | Assume indoor gyms are the main fitness culture — outdoor and sea-based activity rules here |
| Try a CrossFit box if you want the closest thing to a structured fitness community | Expect a huge range of gym choices — the scene is small, so good ones fill up and get loyal regulars |
| Embrace early-morning or evening workouts to dodge the heat for much of the year | Schedule serious outdoor training for midday in summer — the heat is genuinely no joke |
| Join a local running or swimming group for the social side — it's a real way to make friends fast | Expect gym membership prices to be as cheap as nearby mainland Europe — islands cost more |

## Italy: Fitness as Performance Art

Italian gym culture inherits the same aesthetic seriousness that governs Italian fashion more broadly, and it shows up immediately in the unspoken dress code: coordinated activewear, properly fitted, is close to mandatory in any gym with self-respect, and showing up in mismatched old gear genuinely draws attention in a way it simply wouldn't in most Anglophone countries. This isn't shallow vanity so much as a broader cultural conviction that how you present yourself matters everywhere, including, perhaps especially, somewhere everyone can see you struggling through a set of squats.

Beneath the aesthetics, the actual training culture is notably technique-focused. Trainers correct form attentively, classes tend to emphasise proper execution over sheer intensity, and there's a real emphasis on doing an exercise correctly rather than doing it fast. Social ritual matters too — greeting the front desk, acknowledging regulars, treating the gym slightly like a extension of the neighbourhood rather than an anonymous facility you move through silently. Group classes in cities like Milan and Rome fill up quickly and often require advance booking, reflecting both popularity and a membership culture that favours loyal regulars over casual drop-ins. The overall effect is a fitness scene that takes itself seriously on two fronts simultaneously — how you look doing it, and how correctly you're doing it — which can feel intense to newcomers used to gyms as purely functional, sweat-and-leave spaces.

Outdoor fitness exists too, particularly running along historic city centres or parks like Rome's Villa Borghese, but it occupies a smaller cultural footprint than in many northern European countries, partly because Italian cities weren't built with joggers in mind and partly because the gym's social and aesthetic rituals have simply absorbed more of the cultural energy. Seasonal variation matters as well — August sees gyms across the country empty out dramatically as everyone decamps for the coast during the traditional summer holiday period, to the point where some clubs simply reduce their hours or close for stretches, assuming correctly that nobody serious about their social calendar is staying in the city to lift weights in 35-degree heat.

## Malta: The Sea Is the Gym

Malta's small size and genuinely stunning coastline have shaped a fitness culture that treats the indoor gym as a secondary option rather than the default. Sea swimming, coastal running routes, and outdoor training dominate how most residents actually stay active, helped enormously by a climate that makes outdoor exercise viable most of the year, provided you dodge the brutal midday summer heat by training early or late. Indoor gyms exist and are perfectly serviceable, but the scene is noticeably smaller and pricier than nearby mainland Europe, a predictable consequence of running any fitness business on a small island with limited real estate and import costs on equipment.

What Malta lacks in gym variety, it partly makes up for in community intensity among the options that do exist — the island's small CrossFit scene, for instance, is tight-knit and genuinely committed, drawing in both locals and the country's substantial expat population looking for structure and a social circle in one package. Expat forums consistently point newcomers toward outdoor-first options before indoor ones: join a running club, find a sea-swimming group, try a coastal CrossFit box, and treat the traditional gym as a wet-weather backup rather than a first resort. The underlying cultural logic is simple and hard to argue with: when the coastline itself is this good, building an indoor box to replicate it feels almost beside the point.

Walking itself does a lot of quiet fitness work in daily Maltese life too, given how compact most towns are — errands, commuting, and socialising often happen on foot across old, narrow streets never designed for cars, meaning a fair amount of incidental exercise gets absorbed into routine life without anyone framing it as "fitness" at all. Winter, mild by most European standards, keeps outdoor training viable nearly year-round, which is part of why so few residents feel the seasonal pull toward an indoor gym membership that colder countries take for granted. The entire culture, in other words, treats structured fitness as something you bolt on if you want more intensity, not something you need in order to move your body regularly in the first place.

## The Verdict

Italy wins on structure, technique, and sheer aesthetic commitment to the bit — you will leave an Italian gym better dressed and better trained, even if mildly self-conscious the whole time. Malta wins on sheer enjoyment and accessibility — nothing beats a sea swim before work, and the outdoor-first culture removes most of the performance anxiety Italy quietly builds in.

Given the choice, I'll take Malta's sea over Italy's mirror. Fitness should feel like a reward, not an audition, and only one of these two cultures has figured that out.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Malta forum — several posters agreed that finding a gym with availability during peak hours in Valletta or Sliema can take real legwork, since memberships at the good ones rarely turn over.*

## Conclusion

Both cultures have simply built fitness around what their geography and self-image reward most. Italy rewards presentation and precision because that's the national language already. Malta rewards the outdoors because, frankly, it would be absurd not to use a coastline that good. Neither approach is more "serious" than the other — one just happens to require better leggings, and the other requires knowing which cove has the best current.
