# Panama Sells You Healthcare Like a Resort Amenity; Estonia Just Logs You In

🇵🇦 Panama vs 🇪🇪 Estonia — Healthcare and Expat Medical Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Panama has built a genuinely thriving medical tourism industry around private hospitals that look and feel more like upscale hotels than clinical facilities, marketing world-class specialists and short wait times directly at the expat and retiree market with the confidence of a country that knows exactly what it's selling. Estonia has built something almost architecturally opposite: a healthcare system so thoroughly digitised that most of the friction — appointment booking, prescriptions, records access — happens through a single, remarkably competent e-health portal, with the actual human interaction kept efficient and minimal by design.

I have sat in a Panama City private hospital waiting room that had better coffee and a nicer view than most business-class airport lounges, waiting for a same-day specialist appointment that would have taken months to arrange back home. I have also logged into Estonia's e-health portal from a café in Tallinn, renewed a prescription in about ninety seconds without speaking to a single human being, and felt a genuine, slightly eerie appreciation for a government that had apparently decided bureaucratic friction was simply a problem to be engineered away.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇵🇦 Panama**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get private health insurance early — the public system exists but isn't built for most expats | Assume all private hospitals are equal; research reputations for your specific medical needs |
| Use Panama City's top-tier private hospitals for anything specialist or urgent | Wait for a medical issue to become serious before sorting out insurance; do it on arrival |
| Ask other expats for specialist recommendations — word of mouth is genuinely reliable here | Expect rural or smaller-town healthcare to match Panama City's private hospital standard |

**🇪🇪 Estonia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Set up your e-health portal access and digital ID immediately upon residency | Skip registering with a family doctor (perearst) — it's the required entry point to the system |
| Trust the digital prescription system; pharmacies access it directly, no paper needed | Assume you can walk into a specialist without a referral; the family doctor gate keeps this structured |
| Take advantage of remote consultations through the portal for straightforward issues | Expect the same private-hospital luxury experience Panama offers; Estonia's strength is efficiency, not amenities |

## Panama: Healthcare as a Premium, Marketed Experience

Panama's private healthcare sector has spent the last two decades building itself deliberately and successfully into a genuine medical tourism destination, and the strategy shows in every polished, resort-adjacent detail of the experience. Hospitals like Punta Pacifica (affiliated with Johns Hopkins) and Hospital Nacional market themselves explicitly to the international retiree and expat community, offering English-speaking staff, US-trained specialists, and appointment turnaround times that would be genuinely unthinkable in most public healthcare systems, including many wealthy countries' own.

This isn't accidental. Panama's substantial expat and retiree population, drawn partly by the country's long-running Pensionado visa programme, represents real, ongoing demand for exactly this kind of premium healthcare experience, and the private hospital sector has built its entire business model around meeting it. The result, for anyone with adequate private insurance, is genuinely excellent — specialists available within days rather than months, facilities that feel closer to hospitality than clinical care, and a level of attentiveness that spoils you for whatever public system you came from.

The catch, and it's a significant one, is that this experience is almost entirely gated behind private insurance, and Panama's public healthcare system, while it exists and serves the domestic population, isn't realistically built around expat needs or expectations. Arriving without private coverage, assuming the public system will function as an adequate safety net the way it might in a country with stronger universal healthcare infrastructure, is one of the more consequential mistakes new arrivals make, one that only becomes apparent, painfully, at the exact moment you actually need care.

Geography matters considerably too. Panama City's private healthcare infrastructure is genuinely world-class, but that standard doesn't extend evenly across the country — smaller towns and more rural provinces have considerably less robust medical infrastructure, and expats settling outside the capital, in places like Boquete or Bocas del Toro, need a realistic plan for accessing specialist or emergency care that may mean a genuine journey back to the city.

## Estonia: Healthcare as Quietly Perfected Infrastructure

Estonia has approached healthcare from a completely different philosophical starting point, one consistent with the country's broader, genuinely remarkable digital governance ambitions: rather than selling a premium experience, it's engineered away as much unnecessary friction as technically possible, building a system where the primary interface for most routine healthcare needs is a laptop or phone rather than a waiting room.

The e-health portal, tied to Estonia's digital ID system, functions as a genuinely comprehensive hub — medical records, prescriptions (which are issued and filled digitally, with no paper prescription changing hands at any point), appointment booking, and increasingly remote consultation options, all accessible through a single, well-designed government platform that other countries have spent years studying and failing to replicate at the same level of actual usability. Renewing a prescription, checking test results, or booking a specialist referral typically takes minutes, not the multi-step bureaucratic ordeal it represents elsewhere.

The system does, however, run on a structured gatekeeping model that surprises newcomers used to more open access — every resident registers with a perearst (family doctor), who functions as the required entry point for most non-emergency care, including referrals to specialists. Trying to skip this step and access specialist care directly generally doesn't work, and new residents who don't register with a family doctor promptly find themselves oddly locked out of a system that otherwise runs remarkably smoothly.

What Estonia's system doesn't offer, and doesn't pretend to, is Panama's premium hospitality-adjacent experience. Facilities are functional, competent, and efficient rather than luxurious, and the entire cultural framing around healthcare here treats it as well-run infrastructure rather than a marketed amenity. For anyone who's spent time in more transactional, service-oriented private healthcare systems, Estonia's approach can initially feel almost brusque — you're processed efficiently and correctly, but nobody's trying to make the waiting room feel like a spa.

## The Verdict

Panama wins decisively on experience and speed-to-specialist for anyone with private insurance and the budget to match — nowhere else in this comparison will you feel more like a valued customer rather than a patient. Estonia wins on systemic elegance and equitable, low-friction access — the e-health system represents genuinely one of the most quietly impressive pieces of government infrastructure I've encountered anywhere, treating every resident, not just the well-insured, to the same efficient digital access. If you want healthcare that feels like a premium service, Panama delivers, provided you've budgeted for it. If you want healthcare that simply, unglamorously works, every time, without needing to think about it, Estonia has quietly built the system most other countries are still trying to figure out how to copy.

## Conclusion

Panama and Estonia represent two genuinely different, genuinely successful answers to the same fundamental question of what good healthcare should feel like — one leaning into premium, personalised service, the other leaning into frictionless, equitable infrastructure. Neither approach is transferable to the other's population or budget realities, and pretending otherwise misses what each has actually accomplished. Get insured properly before you need care in Panama. Register with a family doctor immediately in Estonia. Do either backwards, and you'll learn exactly how each system actually works at the worst possible moment to be learning it.
