# One Country Shuts Down for the Heat, The Other Just Ties Everything to a Tree

🇹🇳 Tunisia × 🇯🇲 Jamaica — *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Tunisia's weather problem is dry, relentless, Saharan heat that arrives in July and simply refuses to leave, like an unwanted houseguest with excellent tanning technique. Jamaica's weather problem is wet, dramatic, occasionally apocalyptic — hurricanes that announce themselves for a week, then either fizzle into a good excuse for a party or take the roof clean off. Both nations have built entire personalities around coping with their respective sky-based hostility, and both would find the other's version of "extreme weather" faintly amusing.

I've watched a Tunis shopkeeper close for four hours in the middle of the day not from laziness but from sheer thermodynamic self-preservation, and I've watched a Jamaican neighbour board up his windows with the calm efficiency of a man doing his weekly shop, because he's done it eleven times before and mildly resents the twelfth. Neither reaction is panic. Both are just weather literacy that the rest of us, cowering indoors at the first hint of drizzle, simply don't have.

## Do's & Don'ts: 🇹🇳 Tunisia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Adjust your schedule to the siesta-adjacent midday shutdown — fighting it just means fighting alone | Plan strenuous outdoor activity for 1–5 p.m. in July or August; the heat is not decorative |
| Drink water constantly and carry more than you think you need, especially inland away from the coast | Assume coastal Tunisia and the desert interior share a climate — Tunis and Tozeur can differ by 15°C |
| Invest in proper shading — a good hat and light, covering fabric beat sunscreen alone | Underestimate how fast dehydration creeps up in dry heat; you sweat less visibly than in humidity, which is deceptive |

## Do's & Don'ts: 🇯🇲 Jamaica

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take hurricane season (June–November) seriously — stock water, batteries, and cash well before a named storm approaches | Wait until a hurricane warning to buy supplies; shelves empty fast and generators sell out faster |
| Learn the local calm-under-pressure attitude — panic doesn't stop a storm, preparation does | Assume a "tropical wave" forecast is nothing; storms can intensify with very little warning |
| Embrace the heat-and-humidity pace of life — mornings and evenings are for getting things done | Schedule anything demanding for peak afternoon humidity; even locals slow to a crawl then |

## Tunisia: the art of disappearing at exactly the right hour

Tunisia's summer heat is the dry, punishing kind that turns the desert interior into something close to an oven and leaves even the Mediterranean coast simmering by midday. Tunis regularly clears 35°C in July and August, and Tozeur, deep in the south near the Sahara's edge, can flirt with 45°C — numbers that would shut down most European cities entirely but here just mean the day gets rearranged rather than cancelled.

The rearrangement is the interesting part. Life compresses into the early morning and the evening, with the hours roughly between one and five in the afternoon becoming a kind of unofficial national pause. Shops shutter, streets empty, and anyone with any sense retreats indoors or into whatever shade can be found, emerging again as the sun loses its worst intensity. It isn't laziness dressed up as culture — it's a genuinely sound adaptation to a climate that punishes anyone who insists on Western nine-to-five stubbornness in 40-degree heat.

What catches visitors off guard is the dryness. Unlike humid heat, which announces itself through visible sweat and immediate discomfort, Tunisia's dry heat evaporates moisture off the skin so efficiently that dehydration sneaks up without warning. Newcomers who feel "fine" at 2 p.m. can be alarmingly unwell by 4 p.m., having drunk a fraction of what the climate actually demanded. Locals, by contrast, treat water consumption as a constant background task, not something you remember only once you're thirsty — by which point, in this heat, you're already behind.

## Jamaica: prepared, unbothered, occasionally boarding up the windows

Jamaica's weather threat operates on a completely different rhythm — long stretches of gorgeous, humid tropical warmth punctuated by an annual hurricane season that runs June through November and occasionally produces a storm serious enough to reorder the entire island's priorities for a week. What's striking to anyone arriving from a country where a storm warning means genuine dread is how unbothered Jamaicans generally are about the whole business, right up until the moment preparation actually needs to happen, at which point everyone moves with brisk, practised efficiency.

That efficiency is hard-won. Hurricane preparation here isn't performative — it's water storage, battery stockpiling, boarded windows, and a mental checklist refined over generations of storms that ranged from non-events to genuinely devastating. The famous Jamaican calm ("soon come," "everything irie") isn't complacency; it's a coping mechanism built by people who've learned that panic doesn't move a hurricane's path one degree, so you might as well prepare thoroughly and then get on with your day.

The humidity, meanwhile, is a year-round negotiation rather than a seasonal event. Kingston's afternoon heat, thick with moisture off the Caribbean, slows everything to a crawl between roughly noon and 3 p.m. — a softer, less absolute version of Tunisia's midday shutdown, observed more by instinct than by any formal closing time. Expats who ignore it and schedule demanding activity for early afternoon typically learn the lesson exactly once, usually while lying face-down under a ceiling fan regretting every decision that led them there.

## The Verdict

Tunisia's heat is more honest — it tells you exactly what it's going to do every single day, and you either adapt your schedule or you suffer, with no ambiguity involved. Jamaica's weather is more theatrical, mostly glorious with the occasional catastrophic exception, which demands a different kind of readiness: not daily discipline, but seasonal vigilance that can't ever fully relax.

Given the choice, I'd take Tunisia's predictable furnace over Jamaica's beautiful-until-it-isn't gamble — at least the Sahara doesn't take your roof. But Jamaica wins, hands down, on how to actually feel about weather: an entire culture that has decided dread is optional and calm preparation is a personality trait. Tunisia could learn something from that, right around minute three of its fourth consecutive 42-degree afternoon.

## Conclusion

Tunisia teaches you to respect the clock; Jamaica teaches you to respect the calendar. Get either one wrong and the consequences range from an unpleasant afternoon to genuinely being caught without water, batteries, or a functioning roof. Get them right, and you inherit a small piece of local wisdom that most tourists never bother to learn: that extreme weather isn't something to fight, it's something to plan around, ideally with better humour than the rest of us manage during a light drizzle.
