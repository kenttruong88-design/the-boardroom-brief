# France Weighs Its Own Tomatoes and Closes on Sunday. Brazil Delivers Fish by WhatsApp and Asks for Your Tax Number

🇫🇷 France vs 🇧🇷 Brazil

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The supermarket is the most honest room in any country. Nobody performs there. You see exactly how people plan, what they refuse to compromise on and how much faith they have in a stranger holding a till. France approaches groceries like a negotiated settlement between convenience and principle. Brazil approaches them like a negotiated settlement between three kinds of shop, two payment systems and your butcher's WhatsApp.

I have eaten in sixty countries and found that grocery shopping is the quickest way to feel like a toddler in a new one. You do not know where the eggs are, how to weigh the apples, or why the cashier is asking about a number you have never heard of.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**France**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Weigh and label your own produce before the till | Hand unweighed fruit to the cashier |
| Bring your own bags or buy one at the till | Expect free plastic |
| Buy fresh fish and produce at the market | Shop on a Saturday afternoon |
| Carry a small tote and some cash for market stalls | Assume every shop opens on Sunday |

**Brazil**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Try the cash-and-carry stores for big shops | Expect small packs and big-store prices to coincide |
| Visit the street feira in the morning | Turn up at one in the afternoon for the best produce |
| Learn your CPF number for the tax question at the till | Be baffled when the cashier asks for it |
| Get to know your butcher and your padaria | Assume imported goods will be cheap |

## France

France has turned grocery shopping into a ritual with a manual. According to Expatica, hypermarkets such as Auchan, Carrefour, Intermarché, Leclerc and Super U sit alongside Monoprix, Casino, Lidl and Aldi. Hours run roughly eight in the morning to eight or nine at night, and, in the guide's words, most supermarkets in France stay closed on Sundays, though some open in the morning. HousingAnywhere adds that families visit a hypermarket about once a month, buying the bulk of the cupboard goods there and turning to the market for fresh produce and fish.

The etiquette is where the foreigner trips. There are no free plastic bags. A bag at the till costs around ten cents, and a reusable tote is the grown-up option. In the produce section you weigh your own fruit and vegetables on the scale, press the button for the item, and stick the label to the bag before you reach the cashier. A Rick Steves forum thread captures the confusion nicely. One visitor who lived in Paris in 2014 said that at Monoprix the staff weighed produce while at Carrefour you did it yourself, and that bags cost about three cents. A Basel-based poster summarised the system in a line: weigh it, stick it, bag it. Another noted that Rue Mouffetard market takes cash only, and a Montreal visitor pointed out that corner shops open on Sundays for milk, cheese and wine, which is the French compromise in one sentence.

Avoid Saturday afternoons, HousingAnywhere warns, unless you enjoy queues. The meaning of the Sunday closure is cultural as much as legal: Sunday is for lunch, and a country that treats lunch as sacred will not restock a shelf in its honour. Plan accordingly, and keep a loaf of bread in reserve.

France at the till is a quiet, slightly severe experience. Nobody hurries you, nobody chats, and the cashier assumes you know what you are doing. If you do not, they will tell you by sighing.

## Brazil

Brazil makes you work for the best price and then hands you the prize in the form of a feira. The Rio Times, in a piece I would treat with caution since the publication labels it AI-generated and many of its numbers are uncited, describes the three-tier shop: the big supermarket, the cash-and-carry atacarejo with its bulk packs, and the street fair held in the morning, where produce is cheaper and vendors discount as the day wears on. Bags cost a few reais, bagging is often self-service, and the cashier asks the question that baffles every newcomer: "CPF na nota?", a request for your taxpayer number to attach to the receipt.

Expat shoppers on Expat.com offer the useful, less polished version. A Brazil-based American advised that Carrefour and a regional chain called Angeloni are the likely stores in Santa Catarina, and told another poster to bring loose tea from home. A third complained that breakfast sausage links, boxed macaroni cheese, canned beans, sharp cheddar and sour cream are hard to find, and mentioned a single can of split-pea soup at around forty reais. The practical consequence is a shopping life organised around substitutes, with an in-house butcher counter that will cut to order and dry-salted Norwegian cod sold almost everywhere.

The delivery culture is brilliant. On another Expat.com thread, one member said he orders fish by WhatsApp and it arrives within minutes, and buys his meat from a butcher in person. Another uses Mercado Livre for household goods but still picks his meat himself. An expat in Manaus relies on Amazon Brazil recurring orders for cereal, coffee and shaving cream because local supply is inconsistent.

On cost, careXpat's 2023 guide put the grocery bill for one person at roughly 180 euros a month, about 38 percent below France. That is the point: you can eat very well in Brazil, but you must learn three shops, a few apps and one number.

Money shapes the habit too. The careXpat guide notes that a three-course dinner in Brazil costs about thirteen euros, which explains why the padaria, open from around six in the morning to ten at night and serving as a cafe and lunch counter, is part of the grocery ecosystem. In France, a family doing the monthly hypermarket run and a weekly market trip is following a rhythm that has barely changed in a generation. In Brazil, the rhythm is whatever the delivery app suggests. Neither is wrong. They are simply different answers to the question of how much you want to plan.

## The Verdict

France wins on discipline and Brazil wins on flexibility. French grocery shopping is a system with a sign for everything and a mood for the rest. If you like a clear rulebook and fresh produce at a fair price, you will be happy. If you like improvisation, WhatsApp, and a butcher who knows your name, you will be happier in Brazil.

The honest comparison is about trust. France trusts you to weigh your own apples and punishes you with a sigh if you cannot. Brazil trusts you to know your tax number and punishes you with a puzzled look. Both are asking you to learn a language, but only one asks you to learn it at the till.

For a cook who likes the market, I pick France. For anyone who lives by delivery apps and gets irritated by closed shops, Brazil wins. As a resident I would choose the French market and the Brazilian butcher, and I would defy any government to separate me from either.

## Conclusion

Supermarkets are not glamorous, but they explain a country better than any monument. France shows you its respect for order, its love of the market and its devotion to lunch. Brazil shows you its fondness for bargains, its comfort with improvisation and its appetite for a good delivery app.

Learn the local rules and you will feel at home within a fortnight. Ignore them and you will spend your first month holding an unweighed tomato and apologising to a stranger.

Either way, bring a bag. They will charge you for one in both countries, and they will remember.
