# Rwanda Will Let You Coast in English. Ecuador Will Humble You By Lunch.

🇷🇼 Rwanda vs 🇪🇨 Ecuador — Language Barrier Experiences

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is a fact that will annoy every gap-year linguist who spent eighteen months "immersing" in Kigali: you did not need to. Rwanda switched its official government and school language from French to English in 2008, on a Tuesday, essentially by decree, and got on with it. Ecuador, by contrast, has never once considered switching to anything, will never consider it, and regards your enthusiasm for Duolingo streaks with the polite indifference of a country that was speaking perfectly good Spanish long before you turned up with a backpack and a savior complex.

I have lived in both. Rwanda coddles you. Ecuador does not care that you're uncomfortable. Only one of these builds actual character, and it isn't the one where the taxi driver switches to English the second you mangle a greeting.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇷🇼 Rwanda**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn ten Kinyarwanda greetings anyway — it buys enormous goodwill | Assume English fluency means cultural fluency; they are not the same transaction |
| Use English confidently in Kigali's offices, banks, and cafés | Expect the same ease the moment you leave the capital |
| Thank people in Kinyarwanda ("murakoze") even in an English conversation | Mock the accent — Rwandan English has its own legitimate cadence, not a mistake to correct |
| Pick up basic French too — older generations and rural officials may default to it | Assume the 2008 switch erased three decades of Francophone institutional memory overnight |

**🇪🇨 Ecuador**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Commit to real Spanish classes before you land, not after you're humiliated at the market | Rely on Cuenca's "Gringolandia" bubble and call that integration |
| Learn Ecuadorian-specific slang ("chévere," "ñaño") — it separates you from the tourists instantly | Expect hospital staff, landlords, or utility companies to accommodate English |
| Practice with intention — locals are patient with genuine effort, not with laziness dressed as charm | Assume Quito Spanish and coastal Guayaquil Spanish are interchangeable; the rhythm shifts hard |

## Rwanda: The Trap of Feeling Fluent When You Aren't

Kigali will lull you. English is genuinely everywhere in the capital — government ministries conduct business in it, private schools teach in it, the tech and startup scene run on it, and any expat with a laptop and a coworking membership can function for months without a single Kinyarwanda verb. This isn't an accident. Rwanda's 2008 decision to shift from French to English as the primary language of instruction was a deliberate pivot toward East African Community integration and global investment, and it worked with startling efficiency for a policy that essentially asked an entire generation of teachers to retrain mid-career.

What nobody explains before you arrive is that this fluency is a Kigali phenomenon, not a Rwanda phenomenon. Drive twenty minutes outside the city, and you are back in Kinyarwanda — spoken by 99% of the population as a first language, per every language survey Rwanda has ever run — with pockets of French clinging on among older civil servants and Catholic-educated elders who came up before the switch. English there is theoretical at best. I've watched newly arrived NGO workers, smug about their "no language barrier" posting, freeze completely the moment a market vendor in Musanze addressed them in rapid Kinyarwanda expecting the courtesy of a greeting back.

The deeper issue is that English fluency in Kigali creates an illusion of belonging that isn't earned. You can order coffee, negotiate a lease, and argue with your internet provider without ever learning that "murakoze" means thank you, and Rwandans — famously reserved, famously gracious under enormous historical weight — will simply let you. They will not correct you. They will not make you feel the gap. This is generosity, not proof you've integrated, and expats who mistake the two end up living in Rwanda for years as extremely comfortable outsiders, fluent in nothing but their own convenience. Rwandan English itself, incidentally, has developed its own legitimate features — rhythm, phrasing, borrowed structures from Kinyarwanda — and treating it as a lesser dialect to be corrected rather than a variety to be respected is the single fastest way to mark yourself as the worst kind of expat: certain, and wrong.

## Ecuador: No Bubble Is Big Enough to Hide In Forever

Ecuador offers you no such mercy, and it is better for you. Spanish is not negotiable. Cuenca has built an entire retiree economy — direct flights, property agents fluent in English, expat forums thick with Americans discussing residency visas — around the fantasy that you can live here indefinitely without the language, and thousands do, badly, ordering the same three dishes for a decade and calling it "island time" logic in a country nowhere near an island.

But step outside Gringolandia — a nickname the expats gave themselves, with zero irony — and Ecuador does not perform accommodation for you. Hospitals, pharmacies, landlords, the entire municipal bureaucracy: all Spanish, all the time, no exceptions made for your visa status or your Yelp reviews of the city. Expat forums are full of the same confession, restated a thousand different ways: the people who thrive are the ones who took real classes, ideally before arrival, and treated Spanish as the entry fee rather than a nice-to-have. Ecuadorian Spanish also isn't monolithic — the clipped, fast highland Spanish of Quito bears little resemblance to the looser, dropped-consonant coastal rhythm of Guayaquil, and assuming one prepares you for the other is its own small humiliation waiting to happen.

What I respect about Ecuador is precisely that it refuses to flatter you. Locals are warm and patient with genuine, visible effort — praise a mangled sentence generously, correct you kindly — but they extend zero grace to the expat who's decided fluency is optional because enough other foreigners agreed to make a parallel English-speaking economy for each other. That parallel economy exists. It is also, unmistakably, a cage.

## The Verdict

Rwanda's English fluency is a gift and a trap in equal measure: it makes daily survival painless in Kigali while quietly excusing you from ever actually arriving. Ecuador's total lack of an English safety net is exhausting for approximately six weeks and then, if you do the work, genuinely transformative — you come out the other side able to argue with a plumber in a second language, which is worth more than any amount of comfortable English-speaking small talk in a Kigali café.

If I'm ranking which country teaches you more about yourself: Ecuador wins, decisively, because it never lets you pretend the barrier isn't there. Rwanda's kindness is real, but kindness that lets you avoid growth isn't actually doing you any favors. Discomfort, it turns out, is the more honest host.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora, r/Kigali expat thread — paraphrased: an NGO worker of six years admitted they still couldn't hold a Kinyarwanda conversation past greetings, and no Rwandan colleague had ever once made them feel bad about it, which they now realize was the problem.*

> *Expat.com Ecuador forum — paraphrased: a longtime Cuenca resident warned newcomers that the "everyone speaks English here" reputation applies to maybe four blocks downtown, and the first hospital visit outside that radius is a rude and expensive awakening.*

> *Expat.com Ecuador forum, "Speaking Spanish in Ecuador" thread — paraphrased: a poster noted that Quito Spanish felt "clean and slow enough to follow" after two years, then took a bus to the coast and understood almost nothing for the first week, describing it as starting over from scratch.*

## Conclusion

Two countries, two completely different lessons in humility, and only one of them makes you actually learn something. Rwanda will let you live an entire expat life on autopilot, fluent in English, fluent in nothing else, and call it integration because nobody's rude enough to tell you otherwise. Ecuador will make you feel like an idiot in a pharmacy queue within the first fortnight and keep doing it, gently, relentlessly, until you either learn or leave. I know which one produces better dinner-party stories. I also know which one you'll actually thank later — and it's not the one that let you off easy.
