# Nairobi Commutes by Matatu. Lagos Commutes by Stubbornness.

🇰🇪 Kenya vs 🇳🇬 Nigeria

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Let me start with something that will upset the tourism boards: neither city is a fun place to be late in. If you are the sort of person who thinks a commute is a quiet moment with a podcast, Nairobi and Lagos will correct that view before your first coffee has cooled. Both are big, loud, ambitious and badly outpaced by their own roads.

What interests me is not that the traffic is bad, because every large city has traffic, but how differently each one has decided to live with it. Nairobi built an entire folk art around the minibus. Lagos built a system of buses, ferries, motorbikes and sheer willpower, and then let the clock lose. I have commuted in both, and the question is not which is easier. It is which one lets you keep your dignity and your afternoon.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇰🇪 Kenya ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn your matatu routes with a local before you try them alone | Hop on at rush hour expecting a short trip, because it will be anything but |
| Build a small circle of drivers you trust for taxis and boda bodas | Walk around after dark just because the map says it is close |
| Be driven to and from your destination at night | Assume a motorcycle taxi is a safe shortcut |
| Choose your departure time as carefully as your route | Treat cycling as a casual option, since many drivers ignore cyclists |

| 🇳🇬 Nigeria ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use ride-hailing apps or book a taxi ahead of time | Hail a cab on the street without agreeing the fare or checking the meter |
| Consider the BRT, which runs in dedicated lanes, and carry a Cowry card | Expect the BRT to cover every corridor, because some routes are simply missing |
| Allow extra time, even for a school drop-off | Plan a tight schedule, because a few kilometres can take hours |
| Check the okada and keke restrictions before relying on them | Walk alone at night on poorly maintained pavements |

## 🇰🇪 Kenya

Nairobi runs on the matatu, a privately operated minibus that is cheap, plentiful and loud. Forum posters on Expat.com agree it is the cheapest and most common way to get around, with some recommending it for daytime travel and one noting it is perfectly fine for a commute to a university across town. The catch is that you need help learning the routes, because there is no neat map. Knowledge is passed from person to person, like a recipe. The same posters warn that rush-hour trips can take very long, which in Nairobi means you are in a vehicle with a thumping sound system, a conductor leaning out of the door, and a driver treating the lane markings as a suggestion.

I like the matatu. I know that is a controversial thing to say, but it is a system with character, as anyone who has seen a minibus painted like a hip-hop album sleeve will tell you. It is also a system that frustrates the people who rely on it. A commuter quoted by The Standard, who drives from Thika Road to Mombasa Road rather than take a matatu, says he would happily use one if the network were organised and predictable. He accepts that driving may cost him more. What he is buying is certainty about his arrival time. That is the whole sad economics of Nairobi in one line: people will pay extra not for speed but for predictability.

For expats without a car, the usual solution is a mix of taxis and boda bodas, the motorcycle taxis that cut through everything. One poster advises building a small network of drivers you know and trust so you do not get overcharged or stranded. Another cautions that boda bodas have a high accident rate and recommends against them, while someone else says an experienced rider can save money by owning a cheap, sturdy motorbike. Walking is not recommended overall, particularly after dark, and cycling is considered risky because drivers rarely look for you, although the authorities are promoting it.

My verdict on Kenya is that it has a commuting culture with flair, personality and a high cost in patience. The advice from the forum is sensible and slightly grim: avoid the peak hours, get driven at night, and trust only the people you have vetted. It is a system that works if you know the rules, and bruises you if you do not.

## 🇳🇬 Nigeria

Lagos does not have a commuting culture so much as a commuting endurance sport. Pulse Nigeria, citing the state's transport adviser, says residents lose about four hours a day to congestion, with roughly ten million people using the roads daily. I will not repeat the cost in naira because it is too depressing, but the point is clear: this is not an irritation, it is a tax on your life.

The options are many and each has its own sort of danger. Ride-hailing apps, from Uber and Bolt to inDrive, are the most reliable and the most expensive, with waits of around five to twenty-five minutes and surge pricing at the worst moments. Shuttlers, the app-booked air-conditioned buses, are cheaper but limited in routes and timing. The BRT, with its red and blue buses, uses dedicated lanes to dodge the gridlock between mainland and island, though it skips the Lekki, Victoria Island and Ikoyi corridor and demands queues and a Cowry card. The yellow danfo and the smaller korope are cheap and ubiquitous, and often in poor condition and driven recklessly. Okadas and kekes slip through gaps in the traffic, although the state has restricted them in parts of the city and on major roads, and the drivers have protested.

Then there are the ferries, which bypass the roads entirely, and a rail network that is affordable but in need of improvement. Ferries peak at around half past six to nine in the morning and half three to five in the afternoon, and the waterways close at six in the evening. You cannot miss the logic: Lagos has found every way to move people except the one that is quick.

Expat Arrivals notes that many foreigners simply hire a driver, often provided by their employer, because the roads are chaotic, and warns you to plan journeys well ahead even for school drop-offs. That is the real Lagos lesson. In Nairobi you manage the route. In Lagos you manage the entire day around the route.

## The Verdict

I am going to give this to Nairobi, with reservations. The matatu may be loud and unpredictable, but the city is at least a place where you can reason with the commute. You learn the routes, you pick your hour, you find a driver, and you live. There is a rhythm to it, and a visual joy in the minibuses that I have never found in the Lagos danfo.

Lagos is more ambitious. It has a BRT with its own lanes, ferries, app-based shuttles and a flood of ride-hailing options, which makes it feel like a city taking the problem seriously. It is also the city where the sheer scale of the traffic defeats every one of those clever solutions. When four hours a day vanish, no amount of innovation feels like a victory.

So Nairobi wins on survivability and personality, while Lagos wins on effort and loses on results. If you are thinking about relocating, the question is whether you prefer a messy system you can learn or a brave system that cannot keep up. Personally I would rather be late in a matatu than early to nothing in a go-slow.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com forum poster, Nairobi — Matatus are cheap and plentiful in daytime, but get someone to show you the routes first, avoid rush hour because trips drag on, and be driven door to door after dark.*

> *A Nairobi commuter in The Standard — He drives rather than take a matatu, not because it is cheaper, since it is not, but because he wants predictability, and says he would switch if public transport were organised.*

> *Expat Arrivals on Lagos — Congestion is so heavy that a few kilometres can take hours, so many expats hire a driver and plan every journey well ahead, even the school run.*

## Conclusion

Here is what fourteen moves and a lot of delayed arrivals have taught me. A city's transport system is its honest autobiography. Nairobi tells you it is improvised, creative and a bit reckless, but that it will usually get you there if you speak the language. Lagos tells you it is huge, pressured and brimming with options, none of which can escape the same roads.

Neither one is for the faint-hearted. In Nairobi, learn the routes, find your driver and respect the night. In Lagos, plan the day around the commute and consider the ferry before you consider your sanity.

And whichever city you pick, never schedule anything important for the afternoon after a morning commute. The road will always get the last word.
