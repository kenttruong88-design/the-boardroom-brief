# Ghana Spends the Weekend Celebrating Other People's Funerals. Britain Spends It Queueing for a Garden Centre

🇬🇭 Ghana vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A Ghanaian weekend has structure and noise. Saturday belongs to weddings, funerals and naming ceremonies, often all three in the same fortnight, and Sunday belongs to church, which can take half the day and requires an outfit that has been planned since Wednesday. Food is communal, music is loud and nobody has ever described the weekend as a time to be alone.

A British weekend has structure too, but the structure is a supermarket car park, a walk in drizzle and a pub. It is a very different religion. The British hold that the weekend is for doing as little as possible with as many layers on as necessary, preferably with a tea, and any attempt to make it a social event is treated with suspicion until about four in the afternoon.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Ghana**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress properly for church, weddings and funerals | Turn up in casual clothes and expect nobody to notice |
| Greet the elders first and take your time doing it | Rush a social event |
| Bring something to share, in cash or in kind | Arrive with nothing |
| Say yes to the invitation and plan for a long day | Plan another event for the same afternoon |

**United Kingdom**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Layer up and carry an umbrella | Trust the morning forecast |
| Use free museums and parks for cheap days out | Assume a day out has to cost money |
| Join a group through church, football or a hobby | Wait to be invited |
| Take your turn when the round comes to you | Leave the pub before your round |

## Ghana

The Ghanaian side of this comparison rests partly on general knowledge, and partly on a small, useful thread. On Expat.com, a user asking about free and low-cost leisure in Ghana received a single reply from "hkann", who said embassies and High Commissions often host free films and performances, advertised on accraexpat.com/events, and that bush-walking and bird-watching groups are usually free. That is the quiet end of the Ghanaian weekend: a film night, a walk, a group that meets because somebody sent a message.

The loud end is more representative. The weekend is organised around people, and nothing is done alone if it can be done in a crowd. Church is the anchor, family obligations fill the gaps, and a free Saturday is something to fill, not to protect. That explains why Ghanaians abroad build the weekend back from scratch, and why so much of it still happens in a church hall.

Look at what the diaspora does with an actual day off. On 24 August 2025, a bank-holiday Sunday, the London Ghana Seventh-day Adventist Church held an event at Beckton Park in east London, according to the South England Conference. It opened with a health expo where NHS and other staff did checks, followed by football and athletics, and then a feast with fufu pounded by hand with a pestle and mortar alongside grilled meat and vegetarian food. The organiser, Esther Benefo Boateng, said the point was to show Ghanaian culture that is hard to recreate in Europe, and she complained that powdered fufu is over-processed. A recently baptised member said it made him feel included, a home away from home.

That is the Ghanaian weekend in miniature: a church, a park, an elaborate meal and a person with firm views on cassava.

The Beckton Park day deserves a few more details, because they show what an immigrant weekend is made of. The cassava, cocoyam and green plantain for the fufu were all bought in London, and the fufu itself was pounded by hand. The health expo that opened the day was staffed by NHS and other health workers doing checks, so the same people who spend their week in a hospital spend their holiday offering free blood pressure tests to the congregation. That is Ghanaian weekend culture exactly: a feast, a sports day and a public health service, held in a park, led by a church.

## The United Kingdom

The British weekend is a quieter, more private affair, shaped by weather and thrift. The ACE Money Transfer blog, which is marketing from a remittance company, lists the standard free repertoire for Ghanaians in the UK: Hyde Park, the Peak District and the South Downs, Brighton and Bournemouth for the seaside, the British Museum and Tate Modern for free culture, and Borough Market for people who like to look at expensive cheese. It also suggests Meetup groups for football and cycling, and cooking jollof or fufu at home. None of this is wrong. It just takes a weekend and shrinks it into a to-do list.

The harder lesson is about the season. A Ghanaian seminarian, Emmanuel Donkor, writing in The Tablet, describes arriving in deep winter on 21 December 2021 on his first flight ever, and calls the cold harsh and unforgiving. He says he slept in layers, and that the four seasons were new to him, each putting him in a different mood. He also noticed that most parishioners in his first parish in Kent and London were older, with few young people in the pews, which is a very British contrast with the full churches many Ghanaians know.

That is the real shock. In Britain, the weekend's default setting is solitude, plus weather. A person who has grown up with a house full of neighbours and a Sunday that lasts all day can find the quiet unsettling. The cure is to do what the East London church did: build the community yourself, pound the fufu, hire the park.

The British will help, in their way, as long as you do not expect them to say so.

Britain does have some things worth importing into any weekend. The ACE Money Transfer blog, for all its marketing, points to Kelvingrove Park in Glasgow, St Nicholas Market in Bristol and Africa on the Square, an annual free African festival in London. Emmanuel Donkor, who later moved to the Spiritan retirement home in Chester and helps with administration at the provincial office, says he learned a great deal from retired priests who had worked across Africa. It is a very British irony that a young Ghanaian could cross the world and find his best company in an old missionary's armchair. But it is also a lesson. The weekend you want is often in a room full of people who know where you have come from.

## The Verdict

Ghana wins the weekend, and I would not argue about it with a Ghanaian aunt. A weekend that revolves around people, food and an outfit is a better use of two days than one that revolves around a drizzle and a retail park. The British do the quiet bits well, from a Sunday roast to an empty hill, but they do the loud bits badly, and loud is where the fun lives.

What Britain gets right is access. A free museum, a decent park and a seaside within a train ride are real gifts, and they cost nothing. If you can combine British infrastructure with Ghanaian company, you have the ideal weekend.

The trick is not to choose one. It is to import the party and use the park.

## Conclusion

A weekend is the only part of life you get to design yourself. Ghana designs it as a gathering. Britain designs it as an escape.

Take the best of both: the park, the museum, the free train ride and the huge pot of something warm in the middle of a crowded room. Do it in the right coat.

And when someone invites you to a thing, say yes. You can always sulk about the drizzle later.
