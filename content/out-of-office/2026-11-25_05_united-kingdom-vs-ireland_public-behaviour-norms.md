# Britain Queues in Silence and Ireland Talks to the Queue

🇬🇧 United Kingdom vs 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two islands, one sea, a shared history that neither side tells the same way and a dispute over which of them invented the pub. They share a language, a taste for tea and a talent for complaining while pretending they are not. A foreigner would assume they behave the same way in public. A foreigner would be wrong, and would be corrected, politely, by someone who will not say so directly.

I am British, which means I apologise to doors and fear a confrontation like other people fear sharks. I have also lived in Ireland, where a stranger on a bus told me about his daughter's divorce between two stops and asked after my mother. This is the story of how two peoples who look identical on a map handle the single most important thing on the planet: other people in public.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇬🇧 United Kingdom ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the back of the queue and wait in silence | Push in, or worse, ask if you can |
| Say sorry when someone else bumps into you | Assume the apology means anything but a reflex |
| Keep voices low on trains and buses | Take a loud call on the quiet carriage |
| Say 'cheers' and 'thanks' constantly | Treat politeness as optional |

| 🇮🇪 Ireland ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Make small talk with the person beside you | Sit in stony silence and expect it to be respected |
| Take your turn buying a round when you are out with a group | Slip off before it is your shout |
| Accept tea and biscuits when invited to a home | Refuse hospitality and leave the kettle unused |
| Ask someone from Northern Ireland which identity they prefer | Call anyone 'British' or 'southern Irish' unprompted |

## 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

Britain's public behaviour is built on a single principle: do not make a scene, even if the scene is already happening. The queue is sacred. It is a national ritual, an unspoken contract that people honour without enforcement, and the penalty for breaking it is a cold stare so refined it could cut glass. Nobody will confront the person who pushed in. They will sigh, tut, check their watch and discuss it with a friend later, in great detail, for a decade.

The English sorry is not an apology. It is a lubricant, a hello, a request and a polite way of saying 'you are in my way' without having to say it. A Canadian who has lived in England for eleven years noted that shops are not open all night, that people are oddly formal about washing up and that the whole system runs on small understated rituals nobody explains. An Irish poster living in the UK observed something sharper: that they were shocked to be automatically assigned a class, and that the English are keen to signal a refined or privileged background through tiny cues of language.

Personal space matters, noise is a sin and the quiet carriage is a theological position. Strangers do not talk on trains. Eye contact is a risk. A cup of tea is offered to every visitor and the offer is a ritual, not a question. It is a society of enormous manners and enormous distance, and it works because everyone agrees to maintain the fiction that nobody is annoyed. The cost, of course, is that nobody ever says what they actually think, which is why the British have invented sarcasm, understatement and the word 'interesting'.

There is also the matter of dress and venue. An expat guide notes the legal drinking age in Ireland is 18, that after a certain hour many bars serve only drinks, not food, and that some places refuse entry to men in trainers, which the locals call runners. Formal shoes and a button-up shirt will get you through almost any door. It is a small thing and an excellent clue: in Britain nobody checks, because everybody has already judged you. In Ireland someone will check, and then offer you a drink. The British pub is an extension of the living room. The Irish pub is an extension of the street, with a bouncer and a story.

## 🇮🇪 Ireland

Ireland has a different operating system. The same Englishwoman who spent eleven years in the West of Ireland said that strangers smiled and asked how she was, which startled her at first, and that in London she now has to remind herself not to do it. That is the whole difference in a sentence. In Ireland the public space is not neutral ground to be crossed in silence. It is a stage, and everyone is invited.

Small talk is not optional. One expat guide says skipping it can come across as rude, since the Irish value a good chat, and the rule of the pub round is law: when you are out in a group, you take your turn buying for everyone. Skip your shout and your reputation does not recover. Hospitality is warm and automatic, with tea and biscuits whenever you visit a home, and the cutting-in-line habit is rare even though the queue is a looser concept than in England. The thing that slows everything is time. A North Tipperary resident described how Irish time makes lateness normal, repairs slow and paperwork a patience test, and another expat found that a simple bank account required proof of residency by post and took nineteen more days.

There is a delicate side to all this warmth, which is identity. A tourist guide advises asking someone from Northern Ireland how they define themselves, and that people from the Republic prefer 'Irish' and do not enjoy being called British or English. Road signs and station announcements carry Irish alongside English, and accents change by county. Nobody will make a scene about it. They will simply mention it, at length, over the next hour, with a story about an uncle.

Ireland's bureaucracy deserves a footnote, because public behaviour includes how you queue for paperwork. One expat who moved to Enniscorthy said a bank wanted proof of address as a letter posted by a government agency or a state-controlled business, discovered that a car insurance letter counted, and still waited about nineteen more days for the account. Another found that getting children into local schools was extremely hard. Nobody shouts. Everybody is delightful. And nothing happens on Tuesday. I find this more tolerable than the British alternative, which is a perfectly efficient call centre that apologises as it hangs up.

## The Verdict

I have to pick Ireland, and I say this as a British person with a very good queue. The Irish approach to strangers is more humane, more fun and a great deal more likely to get you out of a dull Tuesday. A country where a stranger will ask how you are and wait for an answer has something Britain, for all its good manners, has quietly misplaced.

Britain's strength is reliability. You know the rules, you know the queue and you know nobody will interrupt your thoughts on the 7.42 to Waterloo. If your idea of public life is a series of respectful distances, the UK is a masterpiece of social engineering. I admire it greatly and I would not live in it without a good book.

The honest score is Ireland by a short margin. The Irish are rude in the nicest way, chatty to the point of invasion, and capable of making you feel entirely welcome while also taking an hour to explain why your bank form is wrong. Britain is polite in the coldest way. Pick your poison. I pick the one with a story and a round of drinks.

## Conclusion

The great British cliche is that nothing is ever said and everything is meant. The great Irish cliche is that everything is said and nothing is meant, and neither is quite true. What is true is that each island has built a society around a different idea of the stranger. In Britain a stranger is a risk to be managed with courtesy. In Ireland a stranger is a story you have not heard yet.

Pack a good queue in one case and a good anecdote in the other. Carry small coins for your round, say sorry before you are pushed and ask how people are before you ask what they want. And if you are unsure which island you are on, check whether someone is talking to you on the bus. That is Ireland. That, or you are about to be told something inappropriate in Scotland.
