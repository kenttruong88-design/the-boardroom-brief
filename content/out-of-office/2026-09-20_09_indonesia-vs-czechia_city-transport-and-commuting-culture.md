# One City Runs on Two Million Motorbikes Weaving Through Gridlock. The Other Runs on Trams So Punctual You Could Set a Watch by Them, If Anyone Still Wore One

🇮🇩 Indonesia vs 🇨🇿 Czechia — City Transport and Commuting Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Jakarta and Prague represent two entirely opposed philosophies of getting a city's population to work on time. Jakarta has essentially surrendered to gridlock and built an entire commuting culture around the motorbike, weaving between stationary cars with an agility that borders on performance art. Prague, meanwhile, runs one of Europe's most reliable, elegant, and genuinely punctual tram and metro networks, a system so orderly it makes chaos look like a foreign concept.

I have been driven through Jakarta traffic on the back of a Gojek motorbike at a speed and proximity to other vehicles that redefined my personal relationship with fear, and I have stood on a Prague tram platform watching the digital countdown hit zero at the exact second the tram doors opened. Both are transport systems. Only one of them made my heart rate a relevant travel statistic.

## Do's & Don'ts: Indonesia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use Gojek or Grab for motorbike taxis — they're cheap, fast, and often the only realistic way through Jakarta traffic | Expect Jakarta's roads to move at any predictable speed during rush hour; delays of hours are routine |
| Wear the helmet provided and hold on properly — motorbike commuting carries real, non-trivial risk | Assume TransJakarta's busway lanes run everywhere; coverage is extensive but not universal |
| Build significant buffer time into any cross-city journey, especially during peak hours | Get frustrated at drivers weaving aggressively; it's the accepted, necessary rhythm of the road, not recklessness |

## Do's & Don'ts: Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Validate your ticket immediately upon boarding a tram or metro — inspectors do check, and fines are real | Assume a ticket bought once covers multiple separate journeys; most require validation per trip |
| Trust the posted schedules — Prague's public transit is genuinely, reliably punctual | Stand in the tram doorway or block boarding; efficient flow is a shared, seriously observed expectation |
| Get a multi-day transit pass if staying more than a couple of days — it covers trams, metro, and buses seamlessly | Expect late-night frequency to match daytime; night trams run but on a reduced schedule |

## Indonesia: The Motorbike as the Only Rational Response to Total Gridlock

Jakarta's traffic congestion is not a minor inconvenience — it's one of the most severe in the world, a product of explosive urban growth outpacing road and rail infrastructure for decades, and the city's commuting culture has organised itself almost entirely around coping with that reality rather than fixing it outright. The motorbike, and specifically the ride-hailing motorbike taxi through apps like Gojek and Grab, has become the dominant practical answer: nimble enough to weave between stalled cars, cheap enough for daily use, and fast enough to turn a potential two-hour car journey into a twenty-minute ride, provided you're willing to accept genuinely close proximity to other vehicles at speed.

TransJakarta's dedicated busway system and the expanding MRT and LRT lines have made real inroads into offering an alternative, and usage has grown substantially, but for the sheer flexibility of door-to-door movement through a city this congested, the motorbike remains culturally and practically dominant, ridden by everyone from office workers in business attire to entire families balanced improbably on a single seat. Commuting time itself is treated with a resigned pragmatism — asking a Jakarta resident how long their commute takes typically yields an answer with a wide range attached, because on any given day, it genuinely could be either extreme.

What's easy to miss amid the chaos is how functional the system actually is on its own terms — millions of people move through one of the world's most congested cities every single day via an informal, decentralised, motorbike-driven network that, however hair-raising it looks from the back seat, works.

## Czechia: Punctuality as a Point of Civic Pride

Prague's public transport system stands in almost comedic contrast — a dense, well-integrated network of trams, metro lines, and buses that runs with a level of punctuality and reliability that would be unremarkable by strict Central European standards but feels genuinely remarkable to anyone arriving from a more congestion-plagued city. Trams glide through the historic centre on tracks laid generations ago, digital displays count down arrivals to the minute with real accuracy, and the whole system operates on an unspoken social contract of order: validate your ticket, don't block the doors, let people off before boarding, and the system rewards you with genuine, dependable efficiency.

Ticket validation is taken seriously and enforced with real inspections, a detail that catches out more tourists than almost anything else in the city — a valid ticket that hasn't been stamped in the little yellow validator machine is, functionally, not a valid ticket at all, and fines for riding without proper validation are neither rare nor gentle. This bureaucratic precision, layered onto an otherwise elegant historic tram network, is very characteristically Central European: beautiful, old, and administered with total seriousness.

Beyond the trams, Prague's metro handles longer cross-city journeys with similar reliability, and the whole integrated network — covered by a single transit pass system — makes Prague one of the more genuinely stress-free major European cities to navigate without a car, a status the city clearly takes real pride in maintaining.

## The Verdict

Jakarta turned severe, structural congestion into a thriving, chaotic, genuinely functional motorbike culture that gets millions of people where they need to go despite the odds. Prague built quiet, disciplined, almost clockwork-precise public transit that makes efficient movement look effortless. Jakarta wins on sheer resourceful adaptability under pressure. Prague wins on the simple, underrated pleasure of a transit system that does exactly what it says it will, every single time.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/indonesia — paraphrased: a Jakarta commuter said they now measure commute times not in minutes but in "good traffic day" versus "bad traffic day," since predicting an exact duration is essentially pointless.*

> *Internations Prague — paraphrased: a new resident said the biggest early adjustment wasn't the trams themselves but training their own instincts to trust a schedule that was actually, reliably accurate.*

> *expat.com Czech Republic forum — paraphrased: a poster warned that forgetting to validate a tram ticket, even an otherwise valid one, led to a fine on the spot from an inspector who showed no interest in the explanation "I forgot."*

## Conclusion

Jakarta's transport culture is a masterclass in adaptive chaos, millions of motorbikes turning gridlock into something that, against all odds, actually moves. Prague's is a masterclass in disciplined precision, a tram network that has quietly decided punctuality is simply non-negotiable. Choose your commute based on your tolerance for either genuine risk or genuine rule-following — there is very little overlap between the two experiences, and even less room for indecision on either one.
