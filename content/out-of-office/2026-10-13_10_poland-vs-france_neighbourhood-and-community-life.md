# In Poland, Your Neighbour Knows Your Business and Occasionally Fixes Your Pipes. In France, Your Neighbour Knows Nothing About You and Would Like It to Stay That Way.

🇵🇱 Poland vs 🇫🇷 France

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Move into a Polish apartment block and within a week you will know which neighbour makes the best pierogi, which one is feuding with the building committee, and which one will absolutely help you carry a washing machine up three flights of stairs if asked nicely. Move into a French apartment building and within a year you may still only know your neighbours by the specific, clipped "bonjour" exchanged in the stairwell, delivered with just enough warmth to confirm you both live there and just enough reserve to confirm that's as far as this is going.

Poland treats the apartment block as an extended, occasionally nosy family. France treats it as a shared address with strict boundaries and an unspoken agreement that everyone's business remains, firmly, their own. Both produce functioning communities. Only one of them will show up uninvited with soup when you're sick.

## Poland: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet neighbours in the stairwell and courtyard — a friendly "dzień dobry" is expected, not optional small talk | Don't ignore the building committee (wspólnota) meetings; decisions about shared costs and repairs get made there whether you show up or not |
| Accept help, food, or borrowed tools graciously when offered; reciprocity is the social glue holding blocks together | Don't be surprised when a neighbour comments directly on your business, your renovations, or your visitors; privacy norms are looser here |
| Join in on informal courtyard gatherings, especially around holidays — they matter more than they look like they do | Don't skip contributing to shared building costs or communal cleaning rotas; free-riding gets noticed fast and remembered longer |
| Keep noise down during the customary quiet hours (often enforced more strictly than official building rules suggest) | Don't assume anonymity in a block of flats; everyone generally knows who you are within a month, whether you've introduced yourself or not |

## France: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say "bonjour" to neighbours in shared spaces — it's a genuine social obligation, brief as it is | Don't expect that greeting to lead naturally into deeper familiarity; many neighbours stay cordial strangers for years |
| Respect strict quiet hours, especially around lunchtime and late evening; French apartment living takes this seriously | Don't knock on a neighbour's door uninvited for anything beyond a genuine emergency; it reads as an intrusion, not neighbourliness |
| Participate properly in the "syndic" (building management) when required; decisions affecting the building are handled formally | Don't assume community happens inside the building; it more often happens at the café, market, or association down the street |
| Build relationships through neutral third spaces — a local market stall, a café, a shared hobby — rather than expecting it from proximity alone | Don't take the lack of spontaneous neighbourly contact personally; it's a cultural default around privacy, not a judgment of you |

### Poland: The Block as an Informal Village

Polish community life, particularly in the ubiquitous apartment blocks that house a huge share of the urban population, runs on a social density that can startle anyone arriving from a more privacy-guarded culture. The wspólnota — the building's resident association — isn't a bureaucratic afterthought; it's a genuinely active decision-making body that handles shared costs, repairs, and disputes, and skipping its meetings means decisions about your building, your money, and your shared stairwell get made without your input, by neighbours who absolutely will remember you weren't there.

What makes this intensity bearable, even pleasant once you adjust, is its reciprocity. Help flows in both directions with a consistency that genuinely surprises newcomers: a neighbour might show up with soup when you're visibly unwell, offer to watch a package, or lend a tool without being asked twice, and the expectation, implicit but clear, is that you'll do the same when the situation reverses. This isn't performative kindness, it's closer to a functioning informal insurance system, built on the assumption that everyone in the block is, to some degree, each other's backup plan.

The cost of this density is a privacy that's considerably thinner than many expats expect. Comments about your renovation noise, your visitors, your schedule — these arrive directly and often, not maliciously, but as a natural extension of a community that considers knowing its members' business a feature rather than an intrusion. Courtyard gatherings, especially around major holidays, function as genuine social glue, and skipping them repeatedly doesn't go unnoticed; it marks you, gently but persistently, as the resident who keeps to themselves, which in Polish building culture is considered a little sad rather than admirably private.

### France: The Building as a Boundary, Not a Community

French neighbourhood life draws its community lines in almost the opposite place: not inside the building, but outside it, in the cafés, markets, and neutral third spaces where relationships are built deliberately rather than assumed by proximity. The building itself remains governed by a formal, almost legalistic structure — the syndic handles maintenance and major decisions through official channels, meetings, and voting procedures, keeping communal life procedural rather than personal.

The stairwell "bonjour" is real, and skipping it is a genuine faux pas — French etiquette treats acknowledging a neighbour's existence as a basic social obligation, non-negotiable even between people who will never learn each other's names. But this greeting is also, often, the entire relationship, maintained indefinitely at exactly that level without either party feeling any particular need to deepen it. Quiet hours, meanwhile, are taken with a seriousness that borders on the sacred, especially around the traditional lunch period and late evening, and violating them draws a complaint through the syndic rather than a direct knock on the door — the formal channel is preferred precisely because it keeps the conflict impersonal.

Genuine community, when it exists, tends to form through chosen affiliations rather than inherited ones: a local market where the same vendors recognise you week after week, a café where the owner starts your usual order before you ask, an association built around a shared interest. These relationships can become genuinely warm, even intimate, but they're opt-in by design, built through repeated voluntary contact rather than the simple accident of sharing a building. Knocking on a French neighbour's door without a clear, significant reason reads not as friendly but as a minor violation of an unspoken boundary everyone else is quietly maintaining.

Renovation etiquette captures the gap neatly too. In a Polish block, telling neighbours in advance about noisy building work is a genuine courtesy, but it also opens the floodgates to opinions — about your contractor, your choice of tile, the exact hour drilling should stop — delivered with the easy confidence of people who consider your apartment partly their business because you share a water pipe. In a French building, the same renovation notice goes up as a formal printed notice in the entrance hall, dates and hours specified, syndic-approved, and that's the end of the conversation; nobody comments on your tile choice because nobody considers it any of their concern in the first place, however curious they privately might be.

## The Verdict

Poland offers a safety net you didn't ask for but will eventually be grateful exists; France offers a privacy you didn't ask for but will eventually appreciate maintaining. One culture assumes your neighbours are, by default, part of your support system. The other assumes your neighbours are, by default, people you happen to share a wall with, and nothing more unless you both decide otherwise.

I lean Polish on this one, mostly because I've been the recipient of unsolicited soup during a genuinely miserable flu, and no French café acquaintance, however charming, has ever offered me anything more than correct change and a nod. Community that shows up uninvited beats community you have to schedule.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com forums, Poland — paraphrased: several posters describe being startled, then genuinely touched, by neighbours offering unsolicited help during illness or moving days, framing it as one of the most pleasant adjustments of apartment life there.*

> *Internations Paris community notes — paraphrased: members frequently mention that building relationships in France took noticeably longer than expected, with real friendships usually traced back to a market, café, or shared class rather than the apartment building itself.*

> *Internations Warsaw community notes — paraphrased: newcomers commonly describe needing to attend their first wspólnota meeting to understand how much shared-building business actually gets decided there, after initially assuming it was optional.*

## Conclusion

The real divide between these two neighbourhood cultures comes down to where each one draws the line between "community" and "privacy," and neither answer is wrong so much as differently calibrated to what each culture has decided it needs more of. Poland's density produces warmth with a side of exposure. France's formality produces privacy with a side of distance.

I've lived happily inside both systems, but only one of them has ever left soup outside my door without being asked. That's the one I'd choose again, pierogi recommendations included.
