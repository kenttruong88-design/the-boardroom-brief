# Germany Built the Timetable, Poland Built the Tram That Actually Arrives

🇩🇪 Germany vs 🇵🇱 Poland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Fourteen relocations in, I can tell you that the quality of a nation's character is revealed at 8:07 on a wet Tuesday, on a platform, with a coffee going cold in your hand. Germany has a reputation here that it earned in 1985 and has been coasting on ever since. Poland has no reputation at all, which is precisely why it keeps surprising people who should know better.

Let me be plain about the brief. This is not about trains to the seaside or a romantic week on the Rhine. It is about the grim, daily, elbow-in-your-ribs business of getting to a desk in a city, on time, without losing your will to live. On that narrow and unglamorous question, the answer is not the one the guidebooks hand you. Those guidebooks, I should add, were mostly written by people who took a taxi from the airport and have never once been trapped on a replacement bus in sideways rain, nodding politely at a stranger's wet umbrella. I have, repeatedly. It builds character, in the sense that it removes any you had.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇩🇪 Germany ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy and validate your ticket before you board, because the honour system has teeth | Assume "no gates" means "no inspectors" |
| Build a backup route into every journey, then a backup for the backup | Book a tight connection and trust the board |
| Stand where the sign tells you and let people off first | Stand in the exact spot someone else has decided is theirs |
| Learn which line is yours, because the network is dense and genuinely useful | Judge the whole country by one late ICE |

| 🇵🇱 Poland ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the integrated city ticket across bus, tram and metro | Expect the metro to run all night, because it stops at midnight on weekdays |
| Learn the night bus map before you need it | Climb into an unmarked "grey" taxi when a Polish phrase would save you money |
| Take the tram, because that is where the city actually lives | Assume the signs will be in English once you leave the centre |
| Give up your seat without being asked | Treat the elderly rider with the shopping trolley as an obstacle |

## 🇩🇪 Germany

Germany does not have a public transport problem. It has a long-distance rail problem that the rest of the world has politely mistaken for a public transport problem, and the distinction matters. Inside a German city the system is genuinely excellent. There are tram networks in dozens of towns, S-Bahn and U-Bahn lines in Berlin, Munich and Hamburg, and a ticket system with no gates, which works because everyone has agreed, in the way only Germans can, to simply not cheat. The whole thing runs on a faintly sinister collective conscience, and I admire it enormously.

Then the national railway walks in and ruins the party. Reporting from IamExpat notes that in 2024 more than a third of Deutsche Bahn long-distance services ran late, and that in July 2025 only about 56 percent were on time. The same piece points out that around 85 percent were punctual in the early 1990s, so this is not an immutable national trait but a decline with a paper trail, caused by years of underfunding, asset sales and a network asked to carry far more passengers than it was built for. The Deutschlandticket, launched at 49 euros, made local travel absurdly easy and pushed demand up, which is lovely for you and catastrophic for the people in charge of the infrastructure.

What it does to the culture is fascinating. The German commuter has developed a stoicism that borders on the liturgical. Nobody screams. Nobody rolls their eyes. They simply look at the departure board, absorb the news, and make a second plan with the serenity of a monk. Regional trains are no better in places, with one commenter on a transit newsletter citing roughly a quarter of regional services in North Rhine-Westphalia running late.

My honest advice is to live where there is a tram, never rely on a single connection, and treat the word "punctual" as a historical term. Germany will give you the best city network you have ever used and the least dependable national one, often in the same morning, and you will thank it for both. The new management has promised that 70 percent of long-distance trains will run on time by 2029, a target so modest that it is basically an apology with a deadline attached.

## 🇵🇱 Poland

Poland does not do a great deal of boasting about its commuting, and this is the first thing you should find suspicious about how good it is. Take Warsaw. Buses, trams and the metro share one integrated ticketing system, with time-based tickets you can buy at kiosks and station machines. The metro is two lines, M1 running north to south and M2 east to west, which sounds modest until you notice it is clean, frequent and simple enough to be understood by a tired adult on the first attempt.

The trams are the real heroes. About twenty-five lines crisscross the city, and they run until around midnight, which means the average Varsovian expects a vehicle to arrive before they have finished being annoyed. There are night buses for the post-midnight crowd, and the metro keeps going until 3am at weekends. There is even a public bike scheme, Veturilo, which runs from March to November with the first twenty minutes free. I would not call any of this glamorous. I would call it competent, and competent is the rarest and most attractive thing a city can be.

There are catches, naturally. Expat guides warn that signs are mostly in Polish, that smaller cities lean on buses alone, and that unofficial taxis will happily overcharge anyone who sounds foreign, so use a licensed cab or an app. Many older vehicles still struggle with accessibility, though fleets are being renewed. The culture is quieter than you expect, too, because Polish commuters have a reserved dignity. They will give up a seat to someone elderly with a speed that makes you feel slightly ashamed of every time you pretended to be asleep in London.

If I am honest, Poland's greatest trick is not sophistication. It is the absence of drama. A tram arrives. You get on. Nobody gives you a lecture on the political economy of rail privatisation.

## The Verdict

Here is the thing nobody in Berlin wants to hear. For the specific, brutal, daily act of commuting in a city, Poland wins on the metric that decides whether you are happy, which is whether the thing turns up. Germany has the denser, deeper, more ambitious network, and if every line ran to schedule it would be the best on the continent without argument. But a network is only as good as its worst Tuesday, and Germany's worst Tuesday now arrives with a frequency that would embarrass a Victorian railway company.

Poland has the smaller system, the fewer lines, and the less impressive reputation, and yet a Warsaw commuter can plan a morning without a contingency spreadsheet. That is not a small thing. It is the whole thing.

I will give Germany full credit for the thing it still does supremely well, which is trust. The ticketless gate is a quiet masterpiece of civic faith, and no amount of delay has yet cured Germans of their belief that rules exist to be followed. It is the one national habit I would export tomorrow. But I live my life by what arrives, not by what is admired, and on that basis the crown goes east. Germany remains the better idea. Poland is simply the better commute.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *IamExpat — Reporting on Deutsche Bahn's slide notes that punctuality has fallen from roughly 85 percent in the early 1990s to just over half in some months, blaming underfunding, asset sales and passenger numbers the network was never built to handle.*

> *Next Metro commenter — A reader flags that around a quarter of regional and local trains in North Rhine-Westphalia were delayed in the latest data, up sharply from a few years earlier, and blames staff poaching between operators and a construction sector priced out of building anything.*

> *Expat.com guide to Poland — The guide advises newcomers to use official taxis with a company logo, since so-called grey taxis can overcharge people who do not speak Polish, and warns that most signage outside the big cities is only in Polish.*

## Conclusion

After fourteen relocations, I have learned that you do not fall in love with a transport system. You marry it, and then you discover its habits. Germany is the brilliant spouse who is always twenty minutes late and has a very good explanation. Poland is the quiet one who turns up on time, makes no fuss, and never mentions it.

If you are moving for a job in a city, pick the one where you can leave at a sensible hour and arrive at a sensible hour. Take the tram. Buy the ticket. Stand where you are told, and do not, under any circumstances, believe the departure board in Hamburg.

Germany taught the world how to run a railway. Poland, apparently, was the only one taking notes.
