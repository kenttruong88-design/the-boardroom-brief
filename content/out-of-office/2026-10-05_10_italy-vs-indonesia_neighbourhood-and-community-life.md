# One Country Judges You by Your Piazza Etiquette; the Other Will Simply Assign You Community Labour Within a Month

🇮🇹 Italy 🇮🇩 Indonesia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Italian neighbourhood life runs through the piazza — the town square as social nervous system, where the evening *passeggiata* lets an entire community watch, judge, and quietly catalogue everyone else's outfit choices, relationship status, and general life trajectory without a word needing to be said. Indonesian neighbourhood life runs through *gotong royong* — mutual communal labour, formalised through RT/RW neighbourhood associations, where residents are expected to actually show up and sweep the drainage ditch or help build a neighbour's fence, not merely observe each other attractively from a café table. I have been silently assessed by an entire Sicilian piazza's worth of elderly women within minutes of arriving. I have also been handed a broom in a Yogyakarta neighbourhood and informed, warmly but firmly, that Saturday morning cleanup was simply what one did.

Both cultures treat the neighbourhood as a genuine social unit rather than an administrative accident of geography. The difference is whether that unit expresses itself through aesthetic observation or actual, sweaty, shared labour.

## Do's & Don'ts: Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet shopkeepers and neighbours by name once you've learned them — it matters | Rush your evening passeggiata; strolling slowly through the piazza is the entire point |
| Dress with real intention even for a casual errand — the piazza is always, quietly, watching | Assume quick friendliness equals a real friendship; deeper integration takes genuine years |
| Support the same local bar or café consistently; regulars earn visible, real status | Skip the local festa or block party out of shyness; attendance is noticed, absence more so |
| Let elderly neighbours offer unsolicited advice gracefully — it's affection, not intrusion | Discuss a neighbour's business loudly within earshot of the piazza; discretion is expected |

## Do's & Don'ts: Indonesia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Participate in gotong royong (communal work days) if invited — it's a real integration test | Pay someone else to cover your community labour obligation; showing up yourself matters |
| Register with your local RT/RW (neighbourhood association) soon after moving in | Skip introducing yourself to your RT head; they're the actual information hub of the block |
| Attend neighbourhood selamatan (communal meals/ceremonies) when invited | Treat community meetings as optional small talk; real local decisions get made there |
| Greet neighbours actively and often, even briefly — passive politeness reads as distant | Assume privacy norms match Western suburban expectations; communal visibility is the default |

## Italy: The Piazza as Social Panopticon

Italian neighbourhood life has an aesthetic dimension that genuinely functions as social infrastructure, not mere decoration. The evening *passeggiata* — an unhurried stroll through the town's central piazza, dressed properly, greeting people properly, being seen properly — operates as a daily social census. Who's walking with whom. Who looks tired. Who's wearing something new. None of this gets said aloud, necessarily, but all of it gets registered, filed, and occasionally referenced months later with unsettling specificity by an elderly woman who has, apparently, missed nothing.

Becoming a genuine fixture in an Italian neighbourhood takes real time, measured in years rather than months, and the process can't be rushed through enthusiasm alone. Consistency matters more than charisma — the same bar every morning, the same greeting to the same shopkeeper, the same seat at the same café, repeated with a patience that eventually, gradually, earns you an actual nod of recognition rather than the polite but distant courtesy extended to obvious newcomers. I spent eight months being friendly at my local bar in a small Puglian town before the owner finally started making my coffee without asking how I wanted it. That moment felt more significant than most actual friendships I've built elsewhere in half the time.

The local *festa* calendar matters enormously here too — skipping a neighbourhood's patron saint celebration or summer block party isn't just missing an event, it's a visible absence that gets noted, gently but definitely, by the community's collective memory. Showing up, even briefly, signals investment. Showing up consistently, year after year, is eventually how you stop being "the foreigner on Via Roma" and start being, simply, a neighbour.

## Indonesia: Community as Shared Labour

Indonesian neighbourhood culture, formalised through the RT (Rukun Tetangga) and RW (Rukun Warga) neighbourhood association system, treats community membership as something earned through actual participation rather than passive proximity. *Gotong royong* — the tradition of mutual, voluntary communal work — remains a genuinely active practice in much of Indonesia, particularly outside the densest parts of major cities: residents gather to clean drainage ditches, repair shared pathways, or prepare for a neighbourhood celebration, and showing up is treated as a basic, expected civic contribution rather than an optional kindness.

New residents, including foreigners, are generally expected to register with their local RT relatively promptly, and the RT head functions as something between an information desk, a mediator, and the neighbourhood's actual institutional memory. Skip this registration, and you'll find yourself mysteriously excluded from information everyone else seems to have — a water shutoff, a community meeting, an upcoming selamatan (communal ceremonial meal) — not out of deliberate exclusion, but simply because you were never plugged into the actual information network to begin with.

Selamatan ceremonies — communal meals marking everything from a new baby to a house blessing to a significant anniversary — reveal how deeply communal Indonesian neighbourhood life runs beneath the RT/RW's practical structure. These aren't optional social events so much as genuine community maintenance rituals, and attending, even as an outsider still finding their footing, signals a willingness to actually belong rather than simply reside. The contrast with Western suburban privacy norms can be genuinely disorienting at first — visibility and mutual obligation are simply the default setting, not an imposition to politely resist.

## The Verdict

Italy wins on sheer aesthetic richness — there is something genuinely beautiful about a culture that turns a daily stroll into a slow, patient act of communal recognition. Indonesia wins on functional depth — gotong royong builds a kind of practical, sweat-earned solidarity that an evening stroll, however elegant, simply can't replicate. If I want to be beautifully, slowly noticed, Italy. If I want to actually build something alongside my neighbours with my own two hands, Indonesia, without hesitation. Grab a broom. It's a faster route to belonging than any amount of good posture in a piazza.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/italy — paraphrased: a newcomer to a small town said it took nearly two years before neighbours stopped referring to them by nationality instead of name, and treated this as entirely normal, not unkind.*

> *Internations Milan — paraphrased: an expat noted that genuine integration into an Italian neighbourhood happened almost entirely through consistent small gestures, not big ones — the same bar, the same greeting, repeated for months.*

> *expat.com Jakarta — paraphrased: a relocated teacher admitted missing their first gotong royong day due to a scheduling mix-up, and spent weeks afterward gently rebuilding trust with the RT head as a result.*

## Conclusion

Italy and Indonesia both prove that a neighbourhood is never just a place — it's a slow-built social contract, observed in one country through aesthetics and patience, and in the other through shared labour and mutual obligation. Both will eventually let you belong. Neither will let you skip the process. Learn your barista's name, or learn to use a broom properly. Preferably both.
