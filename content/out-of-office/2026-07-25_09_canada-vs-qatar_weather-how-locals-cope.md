# One Country Builds Tunnels to Avoid Its Weather, the Other Builds Malls the Size of Small Nations

🇨🇦 Canada vs 🇶🇦 Qatar

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Winnipeg, I once walked six city blocks entirely underground through a heated tunnel system without ever stepping outside into minus thirty-five weather, past a pharmacy, a food court, and a dry cleaner, all buried beneath the frozen city like a secret civilisation. In Doha, I once left an air-conditioned mall, walked eleven metres to a car, and felt my sunglasses genuinely warm against my face from ambient heat before I'd even reached the door. Both countries have built entire parallel infrastructures whose sole purpose is helping their citizens never actually touch the outdoors when it counts.

Canada's winters and Qatar's summers occupy opposite ends of the temperature spectrum, but the coping mechanism is oddly identical: retreat indoors, build elaborate climate-controlled alternatives, and treat several months of the year as something to be endured through infrastructure rather than willpower.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in genuinely warm, layered winter gear before your first winter | Underestimate windchill — it's more dangerous than the raw number |
| Learn your city's underground or skywalk network | Skip winter tires if you're driving anywhere outside a major city |
| Embrace outdoor winter activities — skating, skiing, snow walks | Assume a "mild" winter day still doesn't need a proper coat |
| Check road and transit conditions before winter travel | Leave your car without an emergency kit in deep winter months |

### 🇶🇦 Qatar

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan outdoor errands for early morning or after sunset | Exercise outdoors during peak midday summer heat |
| Use covered parking and shaded walkways whenever possible | Leave children or pets in a parked car, even briefly |
| Stay hydrated constantly — heat exhaustion creeps up fast | Assume summer heat rules are optional for newcomers |
| Take advantage of indoor malls, gyms, and entertainment in summer | Schedule outdoor events without checking the extreme heat index |

## Canada: An Entire Society Built Around Avoiding the Cold

Canadian winter culture isn't really about braving the cold so much as engineering elaborate ways around it. Cities across the prairies and central Canada have built underground pedestrian networks — Montreal's RÉSO, Toronto's PATH, Winnipeg's more modest tunnels — that let residents shop, eat, commute, and socialise for months without ever stepping into open air. It's a quietly impressive feat of urban planning built entirely around the premise that minus thirty is simply not weather anyone should have to tolerate for a ten-minute walk.

For those without underground infrastructure, the coping strategy shifts to gear. Canadians take winter clothing with a seriousness that borders on obsession — proper layering, genuinely insulated boots, parkas rated for temperatures most of the world will never experience, all treated as basic necessity rather than luxury. Newcomers who arrive underprepared, in a stylish but useless coat, are gently and repeatedly corrected by locals who've clearly seen this mistake made a thousand times before.

What's genuinely admirable is how much of Canadian life continues outdoors despite the cold, rather than solely retreating from it. Skating rinks, cross-country skiing, winter festivals — there's a cultural insistence on embracing the season rather than merely surviving it, captured in the popular local sentiment that there's no such thing as bad weather, only bad clothing.

Still, the toll is real. Seasonal affective patterns are widely acknowledged and openly discussed, and vitamin D supplementation is practically a national pastime through the darkest winter months. Canadians have made peace with several months a year that demand real physical and psychological management, and the infrastructure, gear, and culture around coping reflects decades of practice.

## Qatar: Retreat Indoors, Emerge After Dark

Qatar's summer heat, regularly exceeding 45 degrees Celsius with brutal humidity along the coast, produces a mirror-image coping strategy: build indoors as elaborately as Canada builds underground. Doha's malls aren't shopping destinations so much as climate refuges — vast, cavernous, aggressively air-conditioned spaces where entire days can be spent without ever confronting the outside temperature, complete with indoor ski slopes and amusement parks as if daring the desert outside to notice.

Daily life restructures itself entirely around the sun's position. Outdoor labour is legally restricted during the hottest midday hours in peak summer, a regulation taken seriously given the genuine danger extreme heat poses to outdoor workers. Errands, exercise, and socialising shift to early morning or after sunset, when temperatures become merely uncomfortable rather than actively hazardous, and the streets noticeably empty out during the punishing midday hours.

Cars become mobile climate pods, air conditioning running constantly, covered parking treated as a near-essential amenity rather than a bonus. Leaving a car exposed to direct sun for even twenty minutes turns the interior into something genuinely dangerous, a lesson every newcomer learns exactly once before adjusting permanently.

What surprises visitors most is how normalised this indoor retreat becomes. Qatari and expat life alike simply reorganises around the heat rather than fighting it — evening becomes the new daytime for six months of the year, and an entire social and commercial ecosystem, from late-night shopping to after-dark family outings, has grown up specifically to serve the hours when the outdoors becomes tolerable again.

## The Verdict

Canada and Qatar sit at opposite temperature extremes and arrive at strikingly similar solutions: build around the problem rather than through it. Canada tunnels underground and layers up. Qatar retreats indoors and shifts its clock. Neither country pretends its climate is anything other than genuinely hostile for a significant chunk of the year, and neither wastes energy pretending otherwise.

If forced to choose, I'd take Qatar's heat over Canada's cold — you can always take another layer off, but there's a limit to how many you can add before you resemble a duvet with legs. Suki's verdict: Canada wins for the sheer ingenuity of its underground cities, Qatar wins for having the good sense to simply move life to the hours when the weather isn't actively trying to kill you.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/canada — a newcomer described their first prairie windchill warning as "the weather app trying to scare me personally," and was told, seriously, to take it at face value.*

> *Reddit r/qatar — someone recounted touching a metal car door handle in July and getting an actual minor burn, calling it "a rite of passage nobody mentions in the onboarding pack."*

> *expat.com Doha — a longtime resident advised scheduling absolutely nothing outdoors between June and September, calling summer "an indoor season" with total sincerity.*

## Conclusion

Canada teaches you that cold this extreme demands infrastructure, gear, and a stubborn cultural refusal to simply stay inside forever. Qatar teaches you that heat this extreme demands the opposite — total surrender to air conditioning and a schedule built around the sun's mercy. Move to Winnipeg and invest in a coat that costs more than your rent. Move to Doha and make peace with the mall as a genuine second home. Both climates will try to kill you a little. Only one of them will let you skate through it.
