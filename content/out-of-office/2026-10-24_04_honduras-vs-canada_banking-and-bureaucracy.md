# Bureaucracy Is a Love Language in Exactly One of These Countries

🇭🇳 Honduras vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Everyone treats "banking culture" like a quaint little quirk, the sort of thing you mention over fondue to prove you've lived somewhere real. It isn't quirky. It is the single most accurate measure of how a country actually feels about you, the foreigner, the money, and the paperwork currently standing between the two. Forget the visa interview. Forget the landlord. The bank teller is the one who decides, with a shrug and a stapler, whether you get to participate in the economy you just moved your entire life into.

Honduras and Canada solve this problem in opposite directions and arrive, infuriatingly, at the same place: you, standing in a lobby, holding the wrong document. Honduras is at least candid about it — the bank wants a residency card, the residency office more or less assumes you already have money moving through a bank, and nobody in this closed loop seems remotely bothered that it is functionally circular. Canada, by contrast, will smile, call you by your first name, hand you a lollipop-coloured brochure about a "newcomer package," and then quietly make you wait several business days for a wire transfer while complimenting your coat. One system denies you with a shrug. The other delays you with a hug. Only one of them is being honest about which is kinder.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇭🇳 Honduras

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Hire a Honduran lawyer before you land | Expect your passport alone to open anything |
| Get your RTN tax number as early as possible | Wait for your residency card before starting the bank queue — run both at once |
| Keep a small recurring deposit moving through the account | Let it sit idle for a year; dormant accounts get frozen |
| Ask three different banks the same question | Trust the answer the first one gives you |

### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Walk in with a passport and proof of address | Assume you need a Social Insurance Number for a basic account |
| Ask specifically about newcomer banking packages | Forget the fee-free year has an expiry date nobody reminds you of |
| Tell your home bank you're relocating before you leave | Expect your old debit card to behave itself on arrival |
| Bring a little Canadian cash for the first week | Wait on an international wire to clear before buying groceries |

## Honduras: The Catch-22 as a Lifestyle

Honduras does not believe in beating around the bush, mostly because the bush itself would require three notarised copies and an apostille. To get residency, you need, among other things, a valid passport, an apostilled police record, and proof you can support yourself — all translated into Spanish by someone official enough that a judge would nod approvingly. You register with the Instituto Nacional de Migración within thirty days of arrival, apply for an RTN tax number from the tax authority, and then — this is the part nobody mentions at the welcome dinner — you discover that several banks will not open a personal account without a residency card already in hand. Expats on forums compare notes on this circular logic the way other people compare notes on root canals: with weary camaraderie and zero expectation of a fix.

Once you clear that hurdle, the banking itself has its own personality, by which I mean its own weather system. Lafise, Davivienda and Ficohsa all treat foreigners differently depending on the week, the branch, and possibly the moon. One longtime poster on expat.com recommends Davivienda for dollar and lempira accounts handled with relative ease; another warns that Lafise tightened up considerably after money-laundering charges hit some of its own managers, which is the sort of sentence that should alarm you more than it apparently alarms anyone who actually lives there. Cash withdrawals run on daily caps, often around two hundred dollars a visit, and currency exchange has its own ceiling too, because the country has, not unreasonably, decided that large unexplained dollar movements are a national pastime worth curbing.

Then there's dormancy, Honduran banking's favourite small cruelty. Leave an account untouched for a year and it quietly dies; one bank reportedly wants a monthly deposit of as little as seventeen US dollars just to keep the lights on. The sensible expat keeps a parallel US account running in the background, treats services like Wise as basic infrastructure, and hires a lawyer for the parts that actually matter, because — and this is the unanimous, hard-won advice from everyone who has actually done it — attempting it alone is how you spend four months discovering exactly what "pending" means in Spanish, and what your own patience is made of.

## Canada: Bureaucracy With Exceptional Customer Service

Canada's banking system has one genuine trick, and it's a good one: by law, a federally regulated bank must open a basic personal account for almost anyone with acceptable identification, regardless of employment status, credit history, or how long ago you landed. "You just got here" is not, officially, grounds for a teller to say no. You do not need a Social Insurance Number to open this kind of account — that's only required once it starts earning interest — and the fees on a qualifying basic account are capped at a few dollars a month, when they aren't waived outright. On paper, this is the more expat-friendly banking law of the two countries by a wide margin, and I will say so plainly, because it happens to be true.

What the brochure does not say is how much of that fairness is wrapped in small print and gentler language. Newcomer packages — Scotiabank's StartRight, and its cousins at TD, RBC, CIBC and BMO — waive monthly fees and bundle in a credit card, but only for a limited window, after which the ordinary pricing resumes and nobody sends a reminder. Two-factor authentication texts a code to a phone number you may not have reactivated yet. International transfers can take several business days to clear, a delightful discovery when you've just arrived with no furniture and a sudden, urgent desire for groceries. IBANs, a system you may have spent a decade internalising, simply do not exist here, and explaining that to your home bank over the phone is its own small, specific kind of hell. One newcomer forum puts it bluntly: if a branch asks for documents you can't yet provide, you're told to just try a different one.

The paperwork itself is almost suspiciously reasonable — a passport, a permit, proof of address — next to Honduras's demand for apostilled everything. But reasonable is not the same as fast, and polite is not the same as simple. Canada's bureaucracy wears noticeably better manners than Honduras's, hands you a lanyard and a smile, and still, somehow, makes you wait.

## The Verdict

If forced to choose — and I am, by the rules of this exercise, forced — Canada wins on paper, and it should. A legal right to a basic account beats a circular residency requirement every single time; fairness, when it's actually written into regulation, is not a small thing, and pretending otherwise purely to sound edgier would be its own kind of cowardice.

But here is the sting, because there is always a sting: Honduras's system is at least honest about how hard it is. Nobody tells you the bank catch-22 is charming. Nobody calls the dormancy fee a "newcomer package." The country simply says no, repeatedly, until you hire a lawyer, and you end up respecting it the way you respect a bouncer who doesn't pretend the club has capacity when it just doesn't like your shoes.

Canada's system, for all its legal decency, dresses its waiting periods in customer-service language so soothing you barely notice you've been delayed for the better part of a week with a smile pointed directly at you. One country makes you fight it openly. The other makes you grateful for the privilege of waiting. Pick your discomfort; you're getting one regardless of which flag is on the building.

## Conclusion

Fourteen relocations in, I have learned exactly one universal truth about banking abroad: the institution does not care about your feelings, your flight, or your five-star reviews of the local ceviche. It cares whether you have the right stamp on the right form, and it will let you discover that the hard way regardless of which hemisphere you're currently standing in.

Honduras will make you jump through a circular hoop and quietly charge you for the privilege. Canada will hand you a lanyard and a lollipop, then make you wait anyway. Neither country is doing you a favour. Both are simply doing their jobs, which is different, and worse. Bring a lawyer, bring patience, bring a spare passport photo you didn't think you'd need — and stop calling either country "exotic" while you're at it. Bureaucracy isn't exotic anywhere on earth. It's just bureaucracy, wearing a different lanyard.
