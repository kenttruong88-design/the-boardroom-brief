# India Has a Festival Every Week; Czechia Has a Carp in the Bathtub

🇮🇳 India vs 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

An Indian calendar is a rolling riot with occasional pauses for work. Diwali, Holi, Eid, Navratri, Onam, Pongal, Durga Puja, Ganesh Chaturthi and a long supporting cast of regional festivals mean that someone, somewhere, is lighting something, throwing something or eating something with a great deal of ghee. The sound is considerable. The colour is not optional. The sweets are, in my professional opinion, the most underrated argument for being alive.

The Czech calendar is quieter and odder. It has Christmas, which involves a carp bought live and sometimes kept in the family bath for a few days before the ritual conclusion, a golden pig that you may see if you fast all day, apple-slicing fortune-telling and walnut-shell boats set afloat to predict the year ahead. It also has Easter, which involves boys with braided whips and a long tradition I will not explain at this stage of the article. Two cultures with a deep love of ritual, then, that have arrived at it by roads that never crossed.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇮🇳 India ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept every sweet you are offered | Refuse the third plate on principle |
| Join in the colour on Holi and wear clothes you can lose | Wear white and expect it to survive |
| Learn which festivals are regional before assuming a national holiday | Assume everyone is celebrating the same thing |
| Expect fireworks, noise and late nights during Diwali | Plan a quiet week at home |

| 🇨🇿 Czechia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Try the carp soup and the potato salad | Judge the carp before you have eaten it |
| Plan your shopping around public holidays | Assume supermarkets will open on Easter Monday |
| Enjoy the Christmas markets, which even sceptics like | Skip them because you have seen a market before |
| Learn the Christmas Eve customs, including the apple and the walnut shell | Treat 24 December as the quiet night before 25 December |

## 🇮🇳 India

India does not have a festival culture so much as a festival climate. I cannot reproduce the calendar here, and I would not trust any article that tried, but the general shape is fairly clear. Festivals come with food, family, clothes, special sweets and a rolling sequence of public holidays that vary by state. Diwali is the one the world knows, with lamps and fireworks and visits to relatives, and Holi is the one that photographs best. Christmas is a minor affair for most of the country but a major one in Goa, Kerala and the Northeast, with midnight mass, carols and nativity scenes. Expat Exchange notes that in some places Santa appears as Christmas Baba, riding a horse or a bullock cart, and that the usual treats include kulkuls, rose cookies, marzipan and a plum cake soaked in rum or brandy.

What distinguishes the Indian approach is the density of participation. A festival is not something you watch, it is something that happens to your whole neighbourhood, whether you planned for it or not. The sound is part of it, the crowd is part of it and the demand to be present is part of it. You will be offered food. You will be invited. You will be asked, politely and repeatedly, whether you have eaten, and your reply will not matter.

The public-holiday structure, in turn, is a patchwork. Some holidays are national, some are regional, and employers may allow a menu of restricted holidays on top. A newcomer needs a local colleague and a calendar, because the date of a festival can move with the lunar calendar, and the person at the next desk may be celebrating something you have never heard of.

The result is that Indian festival life is exuberant, inclusive and a little exhausting. It is also deeply communal, and the expat who joins in tends to be adopted quickly. The thing to remember is that you cannot opt out of being a guest. You may only choose how gracefully you accept.

## 🇨🇿 Czechia

Czechia's festival life is smaller and weirder, and I mean that as a compliment. The Expats.cz piece I read draws on a poll of staff at ExxonMobil's Prague headquarters, drawn from more than eighty countries, about Czech Christmas customs. Forty percent called the carp disgusting and 60 percent disliked the carp soup, with schnitzel the preferred main, and everybody liked the potato salad. A third had not experienced any Czech holiday custom at all, and most had heard of the golden pig. Most liked the apple-slicing fortune-telling and the walnut-shell boats and said they would adopt them.

One of the voices in that piece is Satya Prakash Gupta, an Indian employee, who says he enjoyed the carp soup and compared it to Rohu fish from his home region. He also observes that Indians tend to picnic with family and go to church at Christmas regardless of religion, which is a lovely detail and also a quiet reminder that the festival is a family occasion in both countries. Another Expats.cz article, from 2016, collects the Christmas habits of mixed families: Polish twelve-dish suppers, a Slovak fried cod instead of carp, and a Finnish-Czech household that rotates the holiday between three homes.

The real practical point for an expat is the public-holiday law. A Prague guide from View from Prague explains that supermarkets over 200 square metres must close on seven national holidays, including New Year's Day, Easter Monday, 8 May, 28 September, 28 October and 25 and 26 December. If you forget, you will stand outside a closed shop on Christmas Day wondering where all the food went. This is the other side of Czech holiday culture: it is quiet, domestic and enforced by the opening hours.

The Indian community in Prague is small but visible. An older Expats.cz article, from 2012, put the number at about 500, mostly professionals, and listed Diwali events, a first public Holi show and a Bollywood film festival. The figures are old and I would not quote them as current, but the pattern is persistent: Prague has Diwali and Holi, and the Indian restaurants have multiplied from three in the nineties to over thirty at the time of writing. If you miss the lights, you can find them.

## The Verdict

India wins on sheer exuberance and Czechia wins on charm, and I know which I would rather be invited to on a cold night. The Indian calendar is a festival of excess, and the pleasure is in being swept up in it, whatever your religion, by neighbours who will not take no for an answer. For pure joy and noise, nothing in Europe comes near.

Czechia's customs are smaller, and to my mind more intimate. A walnut shell, a candle and a family that cares about the outcome is a decent summary of what a holiday should be. The closed supermarket is annoying and, on reflection, correct: one day a year, nobody is selling you anything. If I were forced to choose, I would spend Diwali in Delhi and Christmas Eve in Prague, and I would not let anyone mention the carp until after the soup.

## Conclusion

India will light the street, feed the street and invite the street. Czechia will buy a fish, close the shops and ask you to tell the year's fortune with an apple. Neither will let you be a spectator. Take a local colleague, a calendar and a very large appetite, and remember that in both countries the real festival is the people around the table. The carp, I am told, is optional.
