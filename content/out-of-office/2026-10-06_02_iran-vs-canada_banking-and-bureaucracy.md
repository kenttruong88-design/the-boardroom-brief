# One Country Will Make You Wait Six Weeks for a Credit Card. The Other Made an Art Form Out of a Stamp.

🇮🇷 Iran · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Bureaucracy is the one universal language, and I say that as someone who has queued for a stamp in a provincial Iranian registry office and queued for a SIN number in a Service Canada branch with the same low, ambient dread on my face both times. The difference is that Canada's bureaucracy is slow because it is thorough, risk-averse, and terrified of a headline. Iran's bureaucracy is slow because it is slow, has always been slow, and nobody currently employed remembers a version of the system that wasn't.

Both will test the limits of your patience. Only one of them will also quietly test the limits of your international transfers, which is the sort of plot twist nobody includes in the relocation pack.

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your SIN number in your first week — everything else is downstream of it | Expect a Canadian bank to care that you had excellent credit back home |
| Ask specifically for a "newcomer" banking package — most big banks have one | Assume a secured credit card is beneath you; it's the fast lane, not the slow one |
| Keep every utility bill — Canadian bureaucracy worships a proof-of-address | Get frustrated when "it's in the mail" turns into three actual weeks |
| Build credit deliberately with small, boring, repaid purchases | Skip renters insurance — landlords and banks both quietly expect it |

## Do's & Don'ts: Iran

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Befriend someone who already understands the sarafi exchange network | Rely on a foreign debit or credit card working anywhere, ever |
| Keep physical, stamped paperwork for absolutely everything, permanently | Expect a single government office to resolve anything in one visit |
| Learn which documents need a notarized Persian translation before you're asked | Assume international wire transfers will arrive on anything resembling a schedule |
| Carry cash as your actual primary currency, not your backup plan | Get impatient with the queue — impatience has never once sped up a queue here |

## Canada: A Masterclass in Polite, Glacial Thoroughness

Canadian bureaucracy has a personality, and that personality is a slightly anxious middle manager who has read every possible liability scenario and will not move forward until each one has been addressed in triplicate. You arrive, you apply for your Social Insurance Number, and this part is genuinely fast and almost charming in its efficiency — a rare early win that lulls you into thinking the rest will follow suit. It will not.

Banking is where the real comedy begins. You walk into a major bank — RBC, TD, Scotiabank, pick your crest — with a perfectly respectable credit history from wherever you came from, and Canada regards that history the way a sommelier regards a box of wine: pleasant that it exists, irrelevant to this establishment. You start from zero. Actual, literal zero, regardless of whether you managed a six-figure mortgage flawlessly for a decade elsewhere. The system does not import your financial reputation; it makes you rebuild it, brick by brick, usually via a secured credit card where your own money acts as collateral against your own spending, which is either the most Canadian sentence ever written or a very polite form of institutional distrust.

What makes it bearable is that the thoroughness is at least consistent and rarely personal. Nobody at Service Ontario is trying to make your life difficult; the system is simply built by committee, for worst-case scenarios, and you are not special enough to warrant an exception. Expect a six-week wait for your first real credit card, expect your landlord to want both a credit check and first-and-last month's rent, and expect every single step to be documented, dated, and theoretically appealable. It's bureaucracy as insurance policy — tedious, impersonal, and, crucially, something that eventually, predictably, works.

## Iran: Bureaucracy as Inherited Craft

Iranian bureaucracy did not evolve to be efficient; it evolved to survive sanctions, revolutions, currency collapses, and forty years of institutional improvisation, and it shows. The system runs on paper, stamps, and a very particular kind of patient fatalism that locals have long since made peace with and newcomers find genuinely disorienting. You will be sent between offices for a single signature. You will be told to come back "tomorrow," and tomorrow will mean something closer to "eventually." This is not malice. It is simply how the machine has always run, long before you arrived and long after you leave.

Banking is where expats hit the real wall, and it has very little to do with Iranian institutions being incompetent and everything to do with international sanctions making standard cross-border banking close to impossible. Foreign cards don't work in Iranian ATMs — not occasionally, not sometimes, essentially never — which means you are operating almost entirely in cash from the moment you land, sourced through currency exchangers called sarafis who function as a genuinely vital, semi-informal financial infrastructure that no official brochure will ever mention to you. Wiring money in or out of the country is its own odyssey, often routed through intermediary countries, intermediary currencies, and intermediary trust in whichever friend-of-a-friend is handling the transfer this month.

The upside, and there is a real one, is that once you accept cash is your currency and stamped paper is your proof of existence, Iranian bureaucracy becomes almost quaint in its predictability: everyone struggles with it equally, nobody pretends otherwise, and the camaraderie of the queue is a genuine social good. You will never, not once, be confused about whether the system is hard. Iran tells you immediately. Canada waits until week six to reveal it was hard the whole time, just politely.

## The Verdict

Canada wins on eventual function — the SIN arrives, the credit builds, the system resolves, slowly but reliably, and you can plan around its slowness because it is at least consistent. Iran wins, bizarre as it sounds, on honesty: nobody there is under any illusion that the system is smooth, so you're never blindsided by a six-week wait you weren't warned about.

Given the choice between a bureaucracy that lies to you gently about its speed and one that tells you bluntly it has no speed at all, I'll take the blunt one. At least in Tehran, the queue and I are both in on the joke.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/PersonalFinanceCanada — "Had a spotless twelve-year credit history back home. Canada made me get a secured credit card with my own $500 as collateral. Nobody tells you that 'starting fresh' means actually, literally fresh."*

> *Internations Toronto — "The SIN number took one visit and twenty minutes. My first 'real' credit card took six weeks, two branch visits, and a very patient explanation from a teller about why my old bank statements were essentially decorative."*

> *expat.com Tehran forum — "I budgeted for inflation. I did not budget for the fact that my Visa card would simply not exist as far as any Iranian ATM was concerned. Learn the sarafi system before you land, not after."*

## Conclusion

Neither country will let you skip the queue, metaphorical or literal, and both will humble anyone who arrives assuming their old financial life travels with them. Canada's bureaucracy is a long, polite negotiation with a system that eventually says yes. Iran's is a long, cash-based negotiation with a system that was never going to pretend otherwise. Pack patience for both, pack cash for one of them, and leave your assumptions about "how banking works" at whichever airport you took off from — neither destination is interested in honouring them.
