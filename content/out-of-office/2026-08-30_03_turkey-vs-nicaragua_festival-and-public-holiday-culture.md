# One Country Shuts Down Entirely to Celebrate, the Other Explodes Fireworks at Strangers' Doors

🇹🇷 Turkey vs 🇳🇮 Nicaragua — Festival and Public Holiday Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Turkey treats its two major religious holidays — Ramadan Bayram and Kurban Bayram — with a solemnity and logistical thoroughness that shuts down entire cities for days at a time, banks included, in a way that will genuinely ruin your week if you didn't plan around it. Nicaragua, meanwhile, has La Purísima, a nine-day December tradition in which neighbours knock on each other's doors, shout devotional call-and-response chants at full volume, and are rewarded with sweets, fruit, and small toys, essentially trick-or-treating with the theological stakes turned up to eleven.

I have been stranded in an Istanbul suburb during Kurban Bayram with every single shop, bank, and pharmacy shuttered for four consecutive days, genuinely unprepared and mildly panicked. I have also stood on a Managua street at 8pm being handed a mandarin orange by a stranger's grandmother after correctly shouting back "¡Que viva!" to her devotional chant, which remains one of the more unexpectedly moving five seconds of my professional travelling life.

## Do's & Don'ts: Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stock up on cash and groceries before Kurban Bayram — most banks and shops close for days | Book urgent errands or bank visits during either Bayram — everything genuinely stops |
| Greet elders with "Bayramınız kutlu olsun" and expect a small hand-kiss-to-forehead ritual from children | Eat, drink, or smoke visibly in public during Ramadan daylight hours, even if you're not fasting |
| Accept sweets and coffee if visiting a Turkish home during Bayram — it's a core ritual | Assume Bayram is only religious — it's also the country's biggest domestic travel period |
| Expect intercity transport to be fully booked well in advance around both Bayrams | Plan any business meetings in the days immediately before or after either holiday |

## Do's & Don'ts: Nicaragua

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join in La Purísima's call-and-response if neighbours knock — shout "¡Que viva!" back | Ignore knocks at your door during Purísima season — it's a genuine community ritual, not a nuisance |
| Expect loud fireworks nightly throughout December — it's tradition, not an emergency | Assume Semana Santa is a beach holiday only — most of the country genuinely shuts down for it |
| Accept small gifts (fruit, sweets, toys) offered during the season — declining is considered odd | Plan quiet evenings in December — the noise is constant and communal, not optional |
| Visit León or Granada for the most elaborate Purísima altars and processions | Expect much government or bank service during the week before Christmas |

## Turkey: A Nation That Actually, Fully Stops

Turkish public holidays operate with a completeness that catches most newcomers off guard — Ramadan Bayram (Eid al-Fitr) and Kurban Bayram (Eid al-Adha) aren't single-day observances but multi-day national closures, during which banks, government offices, and a large share of private businesses shut entirely, sometimes for four or five consecutive days once weekends are folded in. This isn't a soft, optional slowdown; it's closer to the entire country's infrastructure taking scheduled leave at once, and visitors who assume they can run errands "sometime during Bayram" are reliably, comically wrong.

The social rituals underneath the closures run deep and are taken seriously across the country regardless of individual religious observance. Kurban Bayram, the Feast of Sacrifice, involves ritual animal sacrifice and the distribution of meat to family, neighbours, and those in need — a genuine communal obligation, not just a holiday backdrop. Ramadan Bayram follows the month of fasting and centres on family visits, hand-kissing rituals where children touch an elder's hand to their forehead as a sign of respect, and the giving of sweets and small gifts to children, which has earned it the popular nickname "Sugar Feast." Visitors, including non-Muslim ones, are expected to observe basic Ramadan etiquette in public — no eating, drinking, or smoking visibly during fasting hours in more traditional areas — even though enforcement is social rather than legal.

Beyond the two Bayrams, Bayram periods double as the country's single largest domestic travel event, with intercity buses, flights, and coastal resort towns booking out weeks in advance as urban Turks return to ancestral hometowns or head to the coast, a migration pattern that turns Istanbul briefly, eerily quiet while everywhere else fills up.

## Nicaragua: Nine Nights of Noise, Devotion, and Free Fruit

Nicaragua's most distinctive festival tradition, La Purísima, runs for nine nights each December leading up to December 7th's grand finale, La Gritería, and operates on a logic that takes real getting used to: groups go door to door singing devotional call-and-response chants honouring the Immaculate Conception, and whoever answers the door — resident or startled foreign visitor — is expected to shout back "¡Que viva!" before receiving small gifts of fruit, sweets, or toys from the household's own stockpile, purchased and prepared specifically for this purpose over the preceding weeks. It's a tradition that blends genuine religious devotion with a neighbourhood-wide gift economy, and the fireworks accompanying it — loud, constant, largely unregulated — turn December nights into a soundscape that newcomers initially mistake for civil unrest before realising it's just Tuesday.

Semana Santa (Holy Week) carries a different but equally total weight — much of the country genuinely shuts down for the week, with processions in colonial cities like León and Granada drawing crowds that rival anything in Spain or the Philippines, and the coastal towns simultaneously filling with domestic tourists treating the same week as a beach holiday, two entirely different observances of the same closure happening in parallel without much friction.

What distinguishes Nicaraguan festival culture is its sheer participatory intimacy — these aren't spectator events watched from a safe distance, they're door-to-door, neighbour-to-neighbour rituals that assume your active involvement the moment you're within earshot, whether that's answering a chant or simply tolerating three straight weeks of fireworks at hours that would get you arrested almost anywhere else.

## The Verdict

Turkey's festival culture is vast, institutional, and genuinely disruptive to anyone unprepared for it — a whole country observing at once, with rituals of real depth underneath the closed shutters. Nicaragua's is smaller in scale but deeper in immediate, door-to-door participation, turning an entire December into a nightly, noisy act of communal devotion that no bystander gets to opt out of. Turkey wins on scale and solemnity. Nicaragua wins on the sheer, chaotic joy of being handed a mandarin orange by someone who's never met you.

## Conclusion

Turkey teaches you that a public holiday can be a genuine act of national pause, complete and total, with rituals worth planning your entire month around rather than resenting. Nicaragua teaches you that devotion doesn't need to be quiet, solemn, or private — it can be loud, communal, and delivered door to door with a piece of fruit attached. Go to Turkey and stock up on groceries in advance. Go to Nicaragua in December and just answer the door.
