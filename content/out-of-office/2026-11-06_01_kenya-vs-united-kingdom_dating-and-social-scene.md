🇰🇪 Kenya vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every corporate relocation package comes with a tacit, unstated promise: somewhere between the tax briefing and the school-fees spreadsheet, you will also be expected to have a social life, possibly even a romantic one, in a country where you know nobody and understand none of the unwritten rules. Nairobi and London are both, on paper, warm and welcoming cities. One of those adjectives is doing a great deal more lying than the other.

Kenya ranks 7th in the world for "Building Connections & Friendships" in InterNations' latest Expat Insider survey, with 78% of expats rating Kenyan friendliness positively against a 61% global average. The United Kingdom, a country that invented the stiff upper lip and then exported it as a lifestyle brand, ranks 45th of 53 for social life, with Social Life specifically singled out as one of its weakest categories. One of these countries will invite you to a wedding within a month of meeting you. The other will let you read a bus timetable in silence for six years and call that a friendship.

## Do's & Don'ts: Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept that dowry conversations with the extended family are near-inevitable once things get serious — treat them as due diligence, not an ambush | Assume a stable income and a valid work permit are optional extras; Kenyan families will ask, politely but firmly |
| Join a social institution with a built-in calendar — a running club like Urban Swaras, a cycling group, a church — rather than waiting to be discovered | Expect Nairobi's nightlife to mean nightclubs by default; a large share of Kenyans socialise at home, not at Skydome |
| Keep public affection restrained, whatever your relationship looks like | Mistake restraint for disapproval; it's a social default, not a verdict on you specifically |
| Let your Kenyan friends curate your first few outings | Wander into Mombasa's older-foreign-men scene expecting it to read as charming rather than exactly what it looks like |

## Do's & Don'ts: United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat a "we should get a drink sometime" as a conditional clause, not a commitment | Take silence on a group chat as rejection — it is simply the national resting state |
| Have your banter ready; dry, self-deprecating humour is the actual entry fee to British friendship | Smile warmly at strangers on public transport expecting warmth back — you will get a flinch, not a friend |
| Accept that colleagues keeping work and personal life in separate airtight containers is not coldness, it's infrastructure | Show up to a first date and perform enthusiasm; British dating rewards understatement, not energy |
| Pick an app — Tinder still leads with 1.9 million monthly UK users — and expect it, not the pub, to do the introductions | Assume declining dating-app usage (down year-on-year per Ofcom) means people have gone back to meeting in real life; mostly they've just logged off entirely |

## Kenya: a culture that actually wants to know your mother's name

Nairobi does not do arm's-length. The InterNations numbers aren't a fluke of survey design — 67% of expats here report being happy with their social life, against a 52% global average, and anyone who has spent a weekend in the city can tell you why: Kenyans socialise the way the British apologise, constantly and as a matter of reflex. You will be invited to things. You will be fed. You will, with alarming speed, be asked when you're getting married, and to whom, and whether her people have been informed.

That last part is not a joke. One longtime expat on Expat.com's forums, a Briton with roughly fifteen years and one Kenyan marriage behind him, describes intercultural dating in Kenya as "totally accepted" — except where religion draws a hard line — while warning that a foreign partner without a visible income or a proper work permit will be viewed with real suspicion by the family, and that dowry negotiations are close to unavoidable once a relationship gets serious. This is not Kenya being difficult. This is Kenya being honest about what marriage actually involves, which is families, which the UK insists on pretending isn't the case until the in-laws show up at Christmas anyway.

Where it gets more complicated: public affection, gay or straight, is broadly discouraged, and same-sex sexual activity remains criminalised, a real legal exposure that simply does not exist on the UK side of this comparison and shouldn't be glossed over for the sake of a tidy column. Nairobi's queer expat community does exist and does socialise — often through the same sports-and-hobby networks everyone else uses — but privately, and advisedly.

And do not, under any circumstances, assume Nairobi nightlife means a club. "A lot of Kenyans don't drink or do clubs so we hang out at home," one forum poster notes, flatly. Westlands has its bars (The Alchemist, Brew Bistro, Yunion), but just as much of Nairobi's social life happens over a home-cooked meal, at a church hall, or on a Saturday hike organised by someone you met twice. Bride price itself is being actively re-litigated in Kenyan media right now — "is dowry payment outdated" pieces run constantly — which tells you this is a living culture arguing with itself, not a museum exhibit for visiting expats to gawp at.

## United Kingdom: friendliness as a paperwork category

The United Kingdom's 2025 Expat Insider ranking fell to 41st of 46 countries, its worst showing yet — and when you dig into why, it is not the weather, or the trains, or even the cost of living exclusively. It's the people. 21% of expats here report not feeling welcome, against 17% globally; "Friendliness toward foreign residents" ranks 38th. The single category where Britain genuinely excels is bureaucratic efficiency — this is a nation that will process your visa with Swiss precision and then decline to make eye contact with you at the bus stop for the rest of your natural life.

A South African respondent in InterNations' own survey put it with admirable economy: "The people, like the weather, can be very cold." A Nigerian man writing for Zikoko's Abroad Life series recounts a date that is, genuinely, a small horror story — ninety minutes of travel, three hours waiting outside in the cold, a vague non-apology, and a dismissal, after which he concludes that Nigerians who stay in Britain long enough eventually catch the coldness themselves, like a slow-acting virus. On Mumsnet, a thread asking exactly this question — why are English people so reserved — gets pushback from the British posters themselves, several of whom insist it's "social awkwardness, not coldness," which is the most British possible defence of a stereotype: not "we're warm, actually," but "technically, this isn't coldness, it's a different, adjacent malfunction."

Dating here has outsourced itself almost entirely to apps — nearly five million UK adults used one in 2024, Tinder alone drawing 1.9 million monthly users — while usage declines year over year, suggesting the apps aren't even working particularly well, just working better than the alternative, which is standing in a pub hoping someone looks up from their pint. Work and personal life are separated with the rigour of a border crossing; expect your British colleagues to be unfailingly pleasant at your desk and essentially unreachable outside it. This isn't cruelty. It's a boundary. It's just a boundary erected around something Kenya never bothered to wall off in the first place: other people.

## The Verdict

If you want to be folded into a community, fed, interrogated about your marriage prospects and quietly vetted by somebody's uncle within a fortnight, Kenya wins this comparison by a landslide, and the InterNations data backs me up rather than the other way around. If you want a highly efficient visa process and a lifetime supply of weather-based small talk that never escalates into anything, London is right there waiting, forever, politely, from a safe distance.

Britain's own expats will tell you the bureaucracy is lovely. Nobody — not one single respondent, not in any survey I've read — has ever called the small talk the highlight of their week. Kenya wins. Decisively. Bring a gift for the mother-in-law.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com forum, Kenya — a long-term British expat notes that dowry and bride-price conversations with a Kenyan partner's family are close to unavoidable once a relationship is serious, however casually the relationship began.*

> *Zikoko, Abroad Life — a Nigerian man in Britain describes waiting three hours in the cold outside a date's home for a vague, unexplained cancellation, and concludes the British dating scene runs colder than advertised.*

> *Mumsnet — a poster who recently returned from Italy asks why English colleagues seem "emotionally stunted," only for several British commenters to insist, not entirely convincingly, that it's social awkwardness rather than genuine coldness.*

## Conclusion

Here is the uncomfortable truth buried in the survey data: the United Kingdom is extremely good at being left alone in, and extremely bad at being lived in with other people. Kenya is the reverse, arguably to a fault — nobody's business is entirely their own, your relationship status is public knowledge by Tuesday, and your partner's uncle will have opinions. But I know which failure mode I'd rather have. A society that asks too many questions about your love life is, at the very least, interested in you. A society that asks none at all has simply decided you're not worth the paperwork. Pack accordingly, and bring your own conversation starters to London — you'll need them, because nobody there is going to offer you one first.
