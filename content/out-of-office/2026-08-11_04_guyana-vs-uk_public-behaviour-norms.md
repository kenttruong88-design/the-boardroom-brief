# Guyana Will Talk To You On The Bus. Britain Would Rather Die

🇬🇾 Guyana vs 🇬🇧 UK — *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Guyana is the only English-speaking country in South America, a fact that confuses geography teachers and delights absolutely nobody more than the British colonial administrators who left it that shared language as a parting gift. What they didn't leave behind, mercifully, was the emotional repression. Georgetown's minibuses are loud, communal, and full of strangers who will happily tell you their entire life story before your stop arrives. The London Underground is loud only in the sense that the trains are loud; the humans inside are engaged in a silent, near-religious commitment to acting as though nobody else exists.

Both places have "rules" about queuing, noise, and personal space. Guyana's rules bend generously the moment a conversation gets interesting. Britain's rules are load-bearing infrastructure, and violating them — cutting a queue, making prolonged eye contact, speaking too loudly on a train — will get you a very specific, very silent form of national disapproval that somehow communicates more contempt than shouting ever could.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Guyana**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect and enjoy unsolicited conversation on minibuses and in markets | Assume quiet reserve means you've done something wrong — it's just not the local style |
| Bargain cheerfully at markets; it's expected, even welcomed | Take loud haggling personally — it's theatre, not hostility |
| Greet shopkeepers and neighbours verbally, every time | Walk past someone without a greeting and expect it to go unnoticed |
| Embrace the volume — conversations, music, minibus horns are all part of daily texture | Complain about noise levels; you'll get politely baffled looks |

**UK**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Queue immediately, instinctively, the moment more than one person is present | Cut a queue, even accidentally — it is treated as a moral failing |
| Keep voice low on public transport and avoid eye contact | Strike up conversation with a stranger on the Tube unless you enjoy visible discomfort |
| Apologise reflexively, even when someone bumps into you | Take up more space than strictly necessary — arms in, bags off seats |
| Respect the "two-metre bubble" of unspoken personal space | Stand too close in a queue — it registers as an act of aggression |

## Guyana: Volume as a Love Language

Public life in Guyana runs warm and loud, and the minibus system — the privately operated, reggae-blasting backbone of Georgetown's transport — is the best possible classroom for understanding it. Conductors shout destinations out the window at volume. Passengers negotiate fares, discuss politics, and occasionally sing along to whatever's playing, all within the same fifteen-minute ride. Personal space, in the Western sense, simply isn't the organising principle here; proximity is normal, physical contact while squeezed onto a bench seat is expected, and treating a stranger's conversation as an imposition would be the actual social error.

Market culture reinforces this. Stabroek Market in Georgetown is a full-volume sensory event where haggling isn't just tolerated but performed — vendors expect a counter-offer, expect some theatrical reluctance, and genuinely enjoy the back-and-forth more than a silent transaction. Walking through without engaging verbally, treating the whole exchange like a self-checkout machine, reads as standoffish in a culture that treats commerce as inherently social.

This extends to everyday courtesies that visitors often miss: greeting shopkeepers, neighbours, and even strangers on the street with a verbal "good morning" or "how you dey" isn't optional politeness, it's baseline function. Guyana's ethnic and cultural mix — Indo-Guyanese, Afro-Guyanese, Indigenous, and more, layered from a complicated colonial and indentured-labour history — means public behaviour norms have absorbed influences from all directions, but the throughline is consistent: quiet, unengaged public conduct is the anomaly here, not the baseline.

## The UK: The Queue Is Load-Bearing Infrastructure

The stereotype about British queuing is, if anything, an understatement. It is not merely a preference; it is a deeply internalised social contract that activates the instant two or more people find themselves near the same counter, bus stop, or till. Jump a queue and you won't be shouted at — that would require someone breaking the far more sacred rule against public confrontation. Instead, you'll receive a symphony of pointed sighs, raised eyebrows, and possibly a single devastating "excuse me" that somehow contains an entire lecture on civic decay.

Public transport etiquette runs on a similarly precise, unwritten code. Eye contact is minimised. Conversation with strangers is treated as a mild emergency, something to be handled with maximum brevity and a hasty return to staring at a phone. The famous British reserve on the Tube or a train isn't rudeness; it's a shared agreement that everyone's commute is improved by universal, mutual invisibility. Break it — chat too enthusiastically, sit too close when other seats are free — and you'll be met with polite words and a body language that says, unmistakably, please stop.

Personal space norms extend the same logic outward. There's an invisible, widely respected buffer in queues, on pavements, at bars, and violating it — standing too close, leaning in during conversation — registers as genuinely intrusive rather than merely unusual. Combine this with the national reflex to apologise for things that are objectively not your fault (someone else's bag hits your leg, and you apologise to them), and you get a public behaviour code built almost entirely around minimising friction and maximising personal bubble, at the cost of anything resembling spontaneous warmth.

## The Verdict

Guyana's public life is generous, loud, and occasionally exhausting if you're an introvert who just wanted a quiet minibus ride. Britain's public life is orderly, private, and occasionally soul-crushing if you're an extrovert who just wanted someone, anyone, to make eye contact on the bus.

I'll take Guyana. A culture that talks to you is infinitely more interesting than one that's perfected seventeen ways to avoid it, even if the volume occasionally requires earplugs. Britain's queue discipline is genuinely admirable as civic infrastructure. It is not, however, a personality.

## Conclusion

Both countries will teach you something about your own tolerance for noise and silence, respectively, and both will do it without a shred of apology, because why would they — this is simply how it's done. Guyana wants you talking, haggling, and squeezed onto a minibus bench with a stranger who now knows your business. Britain wants you queuing in respectful silence, three feet from the next person, apologising for existing. Pick your discomfort. Just don't bring British queue etiquette to a Georgetown market, or Guyanese conversational enthusiasm to the London Underground. Either mistake will be noticed, immediately, and never forgotten.
