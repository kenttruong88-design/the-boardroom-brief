# One Country Runs on Paper and Patience. The Other Runs on an App You Can't Open Without an ID You Don't Have Yet.

🇵🇪 Peru · 🇰🇷 South Korea
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Peru's bureaucracy operates on a principle familiar to anyone who has ever queued for a government stamp anywhere in Latin America: nothing is real until it exists on paper, stamped, signed, and ideally notarised twice, by two different offices that do not speak to each other. South Korea's bureaucracy has the opposite problem entirely — it's staggeringly, almost aggressively digital, so advanced that opening a bank account without a Korean phone number, a resident registration number, and functional Korean feels less like paperwork and more like being locked out of a video game you didn't know you were playing.

One country will make you wait in a room. The other will make you wait for an app to load, in a language you don't read, on a phone plan you can't get without the very account you're trying to open.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your carné de extranjería (foreign resident card) sorted early — most banks won't open an account without it | Expect a single visit to finish anything — bring a folder, bring patience, expect to return at least once |
| Use a Peruvian gestor (fixer/facilitator) for complex paperwork if available — genuinely worth the fee | Assume online banking replaces in-person needs — many processes still require a physical branch visit |
| Keep physical copies of every document, stamped and signed — digital copies are often not accepted as valid | Lose your temper at a bank teller — bureaucratic delays are rarely personal, and patience moves things faster than frustration |

### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your Alien Registration Card (ARC) as the very first priority — nearly everything else is gated behind it | Try to open a bank account before getting a Korean phone number — most require one for SMS verification |
| Use a bank with English-language app support (KEB Hana, Woori) if your Korean is limited — it saves real pain | Assume Western banking apps' logic applies — Korean banking security (public certificates, ActiveX legacy systems) is a different world entirely |
| Ask your employer or university for help navigating the ARC-to-phone-to-bank chain — most have done this before for foreign staff | Expect weekend or evening flexibility — Korean bank branches keep strict, limited hours despite the digital-first image |

## Peru: The Bureaucracy of Paper, Patience, and the Second Stamp

Peruvian bureaucracy runs on a logic that can feel, to visitors from more digitised economies, like time travel — not because it's unsophisticated, but because it operates on a fundamentally different relationship with proof. A document isn't valid because it exists; it's valid because it has been stamped, signed, and often notarised, sometimes at two separate offices, sometimes requiring a third to confirm the first two did their job correctly.

Opening a bank account as a foreigner starts, unavoidably, with the carné de extranjería — the foreign resident ID card — which itself requires its own bureaucratic gauntlet through Peru's immigration authority. Without it, most banks won't even begin the conversation. Once that's sorted, the actual account-opening process typically requires an in-person branch visit, physical copies of residency documents, proof of address, and often a level of teller discretion that varies noticeably from branch to branch, and even person to person within the same branch.

This is where the informal economy becomes relevant: a significant share of everyday Peruvian commerce, especially outside Lima's wealthier districts, still runs substantially on cash. Formal banking penetration, while growing steadily, hasn't fully displaced a deeply embedded cash culture, particularly among older generations and in smaller cities and rural areas. Many expats find that a local gestor — an informal fixer who specialises in navigating exactly this kind of paperwork maze — is worth every sol of their fee, cutting weeks of confusion down to days.

What ultimately makes Peruvian bureaucracy manageable, rather than maddening, is understanding that it isn't personal. Delays reflect institutional friction, not individual obstruction, and the visitors who do best are the ones who bring a folder of stamped documents, a book to read in the queue, and the emotional discipline not to take a three-hour wait as a personal insult. It rewards patience over efficiency, and once you accept that trade, it becomes almost predictable.

## South Korea: The Bureaucracy of Being Digitally Locked Out

South Korea presents almost the inverse problem: not too little digital infrastructure, but a digital-first system so deeply optimised for its own residents that newcomers can find themselves excluded not by paper mazes but by a chain of interlocking digital dependencies, each one requiring the other to already exist.

The sequence is almost comically circular: to get a Korean phone number, many providers want proof of residency or an Alien Registration Card (ARC). To open a bank account, most banks require SMS verification via a Korean phone number. To get the ARC in the first place, you need to have already navigated immigration procedures that assume you have local support. Each step gates the next, and newcomers without an employer or university handling the process on their behalf can lose real weeks untangling it.

Once inside the system, Korean banking is undeniably advanced — mobile-first, heavily automated, deeply integrated with the country's broader digital infrastructure. But "advanced" doesn't mean "intuitive" for outsiders. Korean banking security has historically relied on public certificate systems and, until relatively recently, legacy software requirements that made even domestic users groan; while this has modernised significantly, the apps still assume a level of Korean fluency and local context that leaves plenty of foreign residents defaulting to whichever bank happens to offer a workable English interface — KEB Hana and Woori are frequently recommended in expat circles for exactly this reason.

Physical branches, despite the country's digital reputation, still keep notably strict and limited hours, often closing by 4pm on weekdays with no weekend access — a detail that surprises newcomers expecting a hyper-modern economy to also mean hyper-flexible service hours. The lesson many expats learn the hard way is that Korea's digital sophistication is real, but it was built for people who already have the credentials to use it. Getting those credentials in the first place is where the actual bureaucratic friction lives.

## The Verdict

Peru's bureaucracy is slow, physical, and transparent about being slow — you know exactly what you're waiting for, even if you're waiting a while. South Korea's bureaucracy is fast, digital, and quietly punishing to anyone who hasn't already cleared its invisible prerequisite chain — a system so advanced it forgets, occasionally, that newcomers exist.

If I have to choose which frustration I'd rather sit inside, I'll take Peru's paper trail. At least I can see the whole maze laid out in front of me, stamp by stamp. Korea's maze is hidden inside an app I can't open yet, gated by a phone number I can't get, guarded by a card I'm still waiting on. Efficient, in theory. Exclusionary, in practice, until you're finally, fully in.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/peru — an expat asking why their bank rejected a digital copy of a document that had already been physically stamped twice, and the top comment: "if it's not on paper in your hand, it doesn't exist here."*

> *Reddit r/korea — a foreign worker describing the ARC-phone-bank loop as "Korea's actual final boss," with dozens of replies confirming it's a universal rite of passage for every new arrival.*

> *expat.com Seoul — a thread on discovering, three weeks in, that their bank's app simply didn't support English past the login screen, forcing them to bring a Korean-speaking colleague to every transaction.*

## Conclusion

Peru and South Korea sit at opposite ends of the bureaucratic spectrum, yet somehow produce a similar amount of newcomer frustration by entirely different mechanisms. Peru slows you down with paper, stamps, and patient in-person waiting; Korea locks you out with a digital system so self-referential it assumes you already hold the keys before it will hand them over. Neither is objectively worse — both simply reward a different kind of preparation. Go to Peru with a folder and low blood pressure. Go to Korea with a local contact and an employer willing to make three phone calls on your behalf. You will need both, and neither system will apologise for requiring them.
