# Ireland's Weekend Is a Performance. Moldova's Weekend Is a Secret.

🇲🇩 Moldova vs 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every expat who's spent a weekend in both these countries arrives at the same uncomfortable realisation: Ireland has turned leisure into a national theatre production, complete with chanting and a four-pint intermission, while Moldova has quietly decided the best weekend is one nobody outside the family hears about. I have relocated fourteen times. I have sat through enough GAA matches to lose feeling in both buttocks, and enough Moldovan grandmothers' Sunday tables to lose feeling in my waistband. Neither country is "exotic," so let's retire that word before someone uses it unironically about a place with a wine cellar you could drive a car through for an hour.

What both nations share is a flat refusal to waste a weekend on nothing. The Irish weaponise free time into pub sessions, GAA pilgrimages, and Sunday roasts booked three weeks out. The Moldovans disappear into forests with charcoal grills, emerge smelling of shashlyk, and reappear Monday looking suspiciously well-rested. One is loud about it. One isn't. Guess which is easier to actually enjoy once the novelty wears off.

## Do's & Don'ts: 🇲🇩 Moldova

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the family's shashlyk grill invitation without a counter-offer | Assume Chișinău's "nightlife" stretches past three decent bars and a trivia night |
| Book a wine-cellar tour at Mileștii Mici or Cricova days ahead | Try to see either cellar on foot — one runs 150 miles underground |
| Treat Sunday lunch as sacred, immovable, non-negotiable | Schedule anything that competes with a grandmother's table |
| Make the day trip to Orheiul Vechi or Soroca properly, by car | Rely on public transport to get you there on anything resembling a schedule |

## Do's & Don'ts: 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to make one pint last ninety minutes of actual conversation | Buy a round and expect anyone to let you pay for the whole thing |
| Pick a GAA county to loudly, permanently support | Call hurling or Gaelic football "soccer" near anyone holding a hurl |
| Book match tickets and the Sunday roast table weeks in advance | Assume "just a quiet pint" ends before midnight |
| Say yes to the session even on the nights you're not drinking | Expect an invite for "just the two of us" — it is never just the two of us |

## 🇲🇩 Moldova

Moldova's reputation precedes it, and the reputation is wrong. Europe's so-called poorest country, by the GDP figures Brussels loves to cite, somehow sits on top of the largest wine collection on the planet. Mileștii Mici, a converted limestone mine outside Chișinău, holds roughly two million bottles across about 150 miles of tunnel kept at a constant mid-50s°F — a Guinness World Record for sheer volume, with streets inside literally named after grape varieties, because even underground, Moldova wanted things organised. Cricova, nearby, adds another 75-odd miles of tunnel and a Soviet-era history of hiding Hermann Göring's personal wine stash (you cannot script this). The official tourism board, moldova.travel, has built an entire "Wine Road" itinerary around the fact that weekend leisure here means driving between cellars with a designated driver and an empty trunk.

But the real weekend — the one tourists never see — happens in the forest. Moldovans grill shashlyk with a devotion the Irish reserve for hurling. Families decamp to parks or riverside clearings, charcoal goes on, and the afternoon dissolves into the unhurried togetherness expats on forum threads keep describing, almost apologetically, as "much more laid back than the states," where "family is of utmost importance." One expat on Expat Exchange's Chișinău thread put it plainly: people "make sure to take time out for social gatherings," and a forest cookout in summer is routine, not an occasion.

What nobody tells you before you arrive is how little city nightlife there is to fall back on when the family thing isn't happening. A diplomatic spouse's report on Tales from the World lists the going options as a spa day at the banya, water slides, "endless outdoor concerts and festivals" in summer, and — with a bluntness I respect — "some bars and a trivia night." That's the list. If you need more than that to feel entertained on a Saturday, Moldova will test you.

## 🇮🇪 Ireland

Ireland does not have a weekend; it has a liturgy, and the pub is the altar. The expat.com guide to Irish sports and pub culture isn't exaggerating when it calls the pub "without a doubt what makes Dublin, and Ireland as a whole, so charming and socially appealing" — it's where you watch the match, debate the match, and get gently absorbed into a group of strangers who will, by pint three, be discussing your love life with more confidence than you have. Women drink at the bar as readily as men, children are permitted in with adults until 9pm (10pm in summer), and this entire ecosystem runs on the unspoken rule that declining a round is a diplomatic incident.

The real backbone is the GAA — Gaelic football and hurling, run by an amateur association that outperforms most professional leagues for sheer national buy-in. The 2025 Senior Football Championship drew over one million spectators, up from 858,459 the year before, a roughly 20% jump the GAA itself credits to new football rules. This is the actual shape of the Irish weekend: county jersey worn to the shop, Sunday afternoons built around throw-in times. The Government of Ireland's own tourism messaging treats the GAA as cultural infrastructure, which, decades after rugby and soccer tried to compete for the same Saturday, tells you who won.

Here's what the brochure skips: making actual Irish friends, as opposed to drinking companions, is its own sport. One Dublin expat on Expat Exchange's forum admitted flatly, "it's very difficult to become friends with natives, so the expats are all that are left" — a line that should be stitched onto a cushion in every relocation packet. The craic is real. It's also, frustratingly, mostly happening between people who've known each other since primary school.

## The Verdict

I'll say the unfashionable thing: Moldova wins, and it's not close. Ireland's weekend is magnificent theatre — I will defend the GAA and the session to my grave — but it demands you perform belonging for four hours before you're allowed to feel it. Moldova doesn't perform anything. It hands you a skewer, points you at a fire, and lets the afternoon do the work. Nobody there is auditioning for your friendship over a pint they half-resent buying.

Ireland gives you better stories afterward. Moldova gives you a better Sunday while you're having it. Pick your poison, but don't pretend the pub is relaxing — it's a contact sport with a license.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat Exchange Dublin forum — one expat admitted "it's very difficult to become friends with natives," which meant their entire weekend social circle ended up being other expats rather than actual Dubliners.*

> *expat.com Moldova forum — a longtime poster searching for English-speaking company noted that organised meetups in Chișinău are "organized very rare," and that the real system is just messaging a stranger to see if they fancy a beer.*

> *r/AskIreland — paraphrased from a recurring thread theme: a "quiet night out" of a few pints and a match ticket adds up fast, and newcomers are routinely blindsided by how much an ordinary Saturday in Dublin actually costs.*

## Conclusion

Here's the sting nobody wants: both countries have built entire identities around how they spend the two days nobody's paying them for, and both will quietly judge you for doing it wrong. Skip the match in Ireland and you're a tourist who missed the point. Skip the family grill in Moldova and you're the foreigner who'd rather be alone — sometimes true, and nobody will say so to your face. Pack a liver for one, patience for the other. Neither is exotic. Both are simply very good at convincing you their way is the only correct way to waste a Saturday, which might be the single most universal trait expat life ever teaches you to spot in a culture.
