# One Country Wraps a Single Biscuit Like a Wedding Gift. The Other Sells You Cheese From a Bicycle.

🇯🇵🇳🇱 By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office

Japan has turned the act of purchasing anything — a rice ball, a pair of socks, a single individually foil-wrapped strawberry — into a small ceremony involving tissue paper, a bow, and enough packaging to survive re-entry from orbit. The Netherlands, by contrast, will sell you a wheel of aged Gouda directly from a cargo bike parked at a jaunty angle, hand you your change in coins that have clearly seen things, and move on with its day.

I have shopped in department stores where the escalator operator wears white gloves and bows at every landing, and I have shopped at a Dutch market where the vendor was mid-argument about football while weighing my vegetables. Both experiences left me oddly moved. Only one left me with seven layers of packaging to dispose of.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇯🇵 Japan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the little tray for cash instead of handing it directly to the cashier | Expect every small shop to take foreign cards, even now |
| Say a polite "arigatou gozaimasu" on the way out | Rip open your purchase in front of the person who wrapped it |
| Browse depachika (department store basement food halls) for the full spectacle | Assume convenience stores are a downgrade — they are a religion here |
| Carry cash for smaller, older shops and shrines gift counters | Haggle. It will simply confuse and mildly wound everyone involved |

**🇳🇱 Netherlands**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your own bag — plastic ones cost extra and judgment is free | Expect shops open on Sunday morning in smaller towns |
| Use pin (debit card) — plenty of places still don't take Amex or cash | Block the bike lane while browsing a market stall, ever |
| Visit a Saturday street market for the full cheese-and-herring experience | Be surprised when a shopkeeper tells you an item "isn't worth" your money |
| Learn the koopzondag (shopping Sunday) schedule for your city | Assume Dutch directness at checkout is rudeness — it's efficiency |

## Japan: Retail as Devotional Practice

Japan does not sell you a product. It sells you an experience of receiving a product, and the two are treated as entirely separate, equally important disciplines. Walk into a department store — a depato — and you will be greeted, guided, and eventually handed your purchase wrapped with the kind of precision usually reserved for surgical instruments. Buy a single box of sembei crackers and it emerges cocooned in tissue paper, sealed with a small sticker, placed in a bag, that bag taped shut, occasionally with a second bag for the rain that isn't falling. JETRO's own retail data points to Japan's obsession with omotenashi — a hospitality ethic baked so deeply into commerce that unwrapping your shopping can feel faintly like undoing someone's hard work out of spite.

The depachika — those cavernous basement food halls beneath major department stores — are where Japanese retail reaches its final form. Rows of impeccably presented wagashi, bento boxes arranged like still-life paintings, staff calling out greetings in unison. It is retail as theatre, and you will spend money you did not intend to spend simply because the presentation made refusal feel rude.

Then there is the convenience store, or konbini, which in most countries is a fluorescent-lit apology for real shopping and in Japan is a genuinely trusted institution. You can pay bills, buy surprisingly excellent fried chicken, print documents, and buy a full outfit of emergency socks and underwear at 3am, all while the staff maintain a level of courtesy that would embarrass most five-star hotels elsewhere. What catches newcomers off guard is the lingering cash culture — despite the country's technological reputation, plenty of smaller establishments still want yen in hand, placed politely in a small tray rather than pressed into anyone's palm, because apparently even the transfer of coins deserves ceremony.

## Netherlands: Retail as a Practical Transaction Between Adults

The Dutch have precisely zero interest in ceremony, and their shopping culture reflects a nation that considers unnecessary packaging, small talk, and sentimentality about commerce to be, frankly, a waste of everyone's time. Saturday markets — the weekly street markets found in nearly every Dutch town — are where this directness is on best display: a vendor will weigh your stroopwafels, tell you the price once, and move on to the next customer with the brisk efficiency of someone who has genuinely got somewhere to be.

CBS retail data shows the Netherlands has one of Europe's highest rates of card-based payment, and cash is increasingly treated as a mild inconvenience rather than a right — plenty of shops, especially in Amsterdam, will visibly wince if you produce a banknote. What they will not accept, more often than tourists expect, is a foreign credit card; the Dutch pin system runs on its own rails, and Amex in particular gets a look somewhere between confusion and pity.

The genuinely charming Dutch quirk is the cargo bike as shopping cart. Locals do their weekly shop by bakfiets, stacking crates of vegetables and the odd child into a wooden box on wheels, and if you're on foot, learn fast that blocking a bike-accessible market aisle is a transgression roughly equivalent to queue-jumping in Britain. Sunday closures remain a genuine trip hazard for newcomers — smaller towns still shut retail entirely, a koopzondag hangover from more religious decades, and turning up hungry to a shuttered high street on a Sunday morning is a rite of passage for every expat.

Dutch directness extends to the till. Ask if something can be discounted and you'll get a flat, unbothered "nee" with zero apology attached — not rudeness, simply an economy that doesn't see the point in softening a fact.

## The Verdict

Japan wins on sheer craft — no country on Earth makes the act of buying a snack feel this considered, this respectful, this quietly beautiful. But it is, undeniably, retail as performance, and performance takes time and generates an alarming quantity of tissue paper. The Netherlands wins on honesty — what you see is what you get, what you're charged is what it costs, and nobody is going to bow at you for buying cheese.

If you want shopping to feel like being cared for, choose Japan. If you want shopping to feel like being treated as a competent adult who can carry their own bag, choose the Netherlands. I have never needed a system for recycling as much wrapping paper as I have in Tokyo, and I have never been told "no" this many times, this cheerfully, as I have in Amsterdam.

## Conclusion

Japan will wrap your single banana like it's precious cargo and thank you twice for the privilege of selling it to you. The Netherlands will sell you the same banana from a bike, tell you the price once, and expect you to already have your card ready. Both systems work brilliantly for the culture they serve — one built on care, the other on candour — and both will, in their own way, make you feel slightly inadequate as a customer. Bring cash for Japan, a card for the Netherlands, and patience with tissue paper for one of them specifically.
