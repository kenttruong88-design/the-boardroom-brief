# Czechia Treats a Forest Like a Pub and Switzerland Treats It Like a Library

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The Czechs go to the woods the way the British go to the pub: automatically, in groups, wearing impractical footwear, and with a deep conviction that it improves the soul. Every weekend, the entire country decants itself into forests, onto riverbanks and up hills, carrying sausages and a quiet certainty that anyone indoors is wasting their life.

The Swiss go to the same places, but with an altogether different attitude. Their nature is immaculate, rule-governed, perfectly signposted and apparently supervised by someone with a clipboard. A Swiss park is a masterpiece of order. A Czech park is a masterpiece of improvisation. Both countries will tell you theirs is the superior way to be outdoors, and both are slightly right.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Czechia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a weekend walk to a hill or a forest | Treat the outdoors as an occasional hobby |
| Bring food and something to drink | Expect a kiosk on every trail |
| Take the train to a trailhead | Assume you need a car to find the woods |
| Dress for mud | Wear white trainers |

**Switzerland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Follow the signs and stay on marked paths | Wander off and improvise a route |
| Sort and take away your rubbish | Leave a single wrapper behind |
| Respect quiet hours, especially on Sundays | Blast music at the lakeside |
| Check lake and river swimming rules before you jump in | Assume every water is safe |

## Czechia

Czechs are a nation of walkers, and they do not consider this a hobby. It is a civic duty. The famous Czech tourist trail network, with its colour-coded markings painted on trees and posts, is one of the best in Europe, and it exists because for generations ordinary people considered a long Sunday walk the only acceptable form of leisure. If you move to Prague, a colleague will invite you on a hike within a fortnight, and you will be the only person in a coat that is not waterproof.

Prague itself is generous with green. A feature published by Expats.cz in May 2024 collected readers' favourite routes and shows the city's range. One reader recommends Vítkov, with its long car-free trails. Another points to the Náplavka riverside, especially the bridges early in the morning. Another describes Klanovice, a forest with a train station in the middle of the woods, which is the most Czech sentence ever written. The article also notes that Prague was ranked seventh among cities to explore on foot.

That is the whole appeal: the green is woven into daily life and usually reachable by public transport. A park in Prague is not a distant destination. It is a hill you can see from the office, a riverbank where people meet for a cheap beer, a forest that starts where the tram line stops. Letná, with its view over the river, is practically an extension of the living room in summer.

The catch is informality. Facilities can be patchy, bins can be sparse, and rules are enforced with a Czech shrug. This delights some expats and baffles others. If you want your nature neat, you may find the improvised approach a little rough around the edges. If you want your nature social, a Czech park will make friends for you within a pint.

## Switzerland

Switzerland has a reputation for pristine landscapes, and the reputation is entirely earned and slightly intimidating. Parks and public green spaces in Swiss cities are beautifully maintained, trails are clearly marked, and the country's public transport takes you from a city platform to the trailhead with a punctuality that borders on rude. A Swiss walk is a precision instrument.

The social contract is the thing to understand. The Swiss treat shared space as a shared responsibility, and the rules, written and unwritten, reflect it. Rubbish is sorted and carried out. Noise is considered, particularly on Sundays and in the evening. Barbecues happen in designated spots. A Czech visitor who treats a lakeside like a festival field will learn quickly, via a polite and devastating glance from a stranger, that it is not one.

That sounds restrictive, and sometimes it is. But the payoff is real: a park that is genuinely clean, safe and calm, in which nobody is playing music from a phone at you. The lakes in particular are a treasure, with swimming spots in many Swiss cities that feel like a municipal miracle. You pay for a pass to a lakeside bathing area, or you use a free one, and you swim in water so clean it would embarrass most European rivers.

The cost is spontaneity. Switzerland rewards planning, and the countryside is not a place to improvise your way into a mess. The outdoors is a service, delivered excellently, and you are a customer who has been told the rules in advance. Many expats find this soothing. Others, particularly those from looser cultures, feel supervised even in a forest.

## The Verdict

If you want a park that feels like a friend, Czechia. If you want a park that feels like an institution, Switzerland. I know exactly which one I would take for a long summer evening with a beer, and it has a beer garden in the middle of it and no clipboard in sight.

Czechia wins for sheer accessibility and warmth. The culture of walking is deeply ingrained, the countryside is a train ride away, and the cost of an afternoon in nature is roughly the price of a sandwich. The Czech approach treats the outdoors as a birthright, not a product.

Switzerland wins on polish, and I say it through my teeth. But polish is not the same as joy. A perfect lake with a rulebook is still a lake with a rulebook. I would rather be a bit muddy and a bit loud and rather happier. Winner: Czechia, with a soggy sausage and no regrets.

## Conclusion

Both countries love the outdoors, but they love it in different languages. Czechia speaks in sausages, trail markers and impromptu company. Switzerland speaks in timetables, clean lakes and regulated peace. One is a bear hug. The other is a firm, perfectly timed handshake.

If you move between them, bring humility and good boots. In Prague, accept every invitation to a hill. In Zurich, read the rules before the picnic. And in both, remember that the best green space is the one you actually use. A park you admire from the window is only a very expensive painting.
