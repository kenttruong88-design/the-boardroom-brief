# Czechs Escape to the Cottage and Canadians Escape to the Wilderness, and Both Bring Beer

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every nation has a theory of nature. The Czech theory is that nature is a place you go on Friday evening, in a slightly overpacked car, to a small wooden building where your family will grill sausages and argue pleasantly about the garden. The Canadian theory is that nature is enormous, dangerous and should be approached with a permit, bear spray and a reservation made five months in advance.

Both are correct. Both are also, in their own way, a form of escapism from offices. One is a cosy retreat with a beer tap and a hammock. The other is a heroic expedition with a canoe. The question for the expat is which kind of escape you want, and how much paperwork you are prepared to do to achieve it.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Czechia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pick mushrooms in a basket, not a plastic bag | Eat anything you cannot identify with confidence |
| Greet the neighbours at the chata | Show up and ignore everyone |
| Try Letná or Riegrovy sady on a warm evening | Expect a quiet picnic with no beer garden nearby |
| Bring a book and earplugs to a family cottage weekend | Say anything critical about the garden |

**Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book campsites months ahead | Turn up on a summer Saturday and hope |
| Keep a safe distance from bears and wildlife | Feed anything, ever |
| Buy firewood locally | Carry it across provincial borders |
| Check fire bans before lighting anything | Assume a campfire is a right |

## Czechia

Prague is a green city that does not boast about it. A 2008 Expats.cz guide, now dated, says the city has 88 protected areas covering more than two thousand hectares, with about a fifth of the city being parkland. The parks have personalities. Letenské sady is known mostly for its beer garden, and Riegrovy sady has a beer garden, a concert stage and a big screen for football. Stromovka is wilder, a former royal game preserve with open space, while Divoká Šárka is large "wilderness" within the city with a public pool and food stands. Průhonice, further out, has about 23 kilometres of paths.

Then there is the thing the Czechs actually do with greenery: they pick mushrooms. A September 2025 Expats.cz piece names spots across Prague, including Petřín, Kinský Garden, Grébovka, Hvězda, Divoká and Tichá Šárka, Kunratický háj, Klánovický les and Chuchelský háj, and advises a basket, not plastic, and a photograph of the mushroom in its habitat. Identification apps exist, and the Czech Mycological Society publishes spots and group walks. A separate Expats.cz piece collecting expat voices notes that many respondents mention mushrooming, and one says doing it with his wife and father-in-law is "always fun… and still a challenge". I could not verify any legal limit per person, so check before you fill the basket.

The great Czech institution, though, is the chata. Radio Prague reports that cottages and cabins used as second homes number more than 500,000 and account for around a fifth of domestic buildings, with Czechia ranking fifth for cottages per person, behind Scandinavia. A *chata* was built for recreation, while a *chalupa* began as a residence. The culture took off from the late 1950s and 1960s, and the tradition goes back to the 1920s tramping movement along the Vltava, Berounka and Sázava rivers. Prices, reported by Expats.cz, average around CZK 3.2 million, with cheap older cottages starting near CZK 850,000 and rentals at CZK 1,500 to 3,000 a night.

An expat writer describes surviving weekends at her husband's family cottage on the Sázava with a set of tongue-in-cheek rules: praise the air, stay visibly busy, bring earplugs, greet every neighbour and expect grilled sausages and beer. There is a quiet class element to all this that Canadians will find unfamiliar. The chata is not a luxury; it is the ordinary person's escape, and it grew in an era when travel abroad was restricted and a patch of land in the woods was the closest thing to freedom on offer. Radio Prague notes the cottage-going boom of the late 1950s and 1960s, when people bought former German houses for a symbolic price, and a weekly television programme for cottage owners still airs on Sunday at noon. A country that televises gardening advice for its second homes has made a decision about what matters. The cottage is the national hobby, and the weekend departure from Prague on a Friday afternoon is its liturgy.

That is the Czech version of national park etiquette, and it is more honest than any sign.

## Canada

Canadian green space is not a place you visit; it is an operating system. Stanley Park in Vancouver covers roughly 405 hectares, opened in 1888 and draws around eight million visitors a year, with a seawall section of about 8.8 kilometres and more than 27 kilometres of forest trails. It is a park on the scale of a district, and it sits in the middle of a major city.

Beyond the cities is the national and provincial park machine. A third-party guide to the 2026 Parks Canada Discovery Pass lists an adult pass at about CAD 72.25 and a family pass at CAD 145.25, covering 37 national parks and over 170 historic sites. National park campsites run roughly CAD 18 to 45 a night plus a reservation fee, with park entry charged separately. Ontario Parks opens booking five months ahead and BC Parks four months ahead. These figures come from a commercial guide, so check Parks Canada before you pay.

The difference with Czechia is who the green space is for. Prague's parks are places to drink, picnic and watch football on an outdoor screen, a social extension of the pub. Canada's parks are places to be alone, and the culture of solitude is so strong that people travel for hours to find a lake nobody else has found. One country builds beer gardens into its parks; the other builds bear lockers into its campsites. Neither is wrong, but you should know which one you are signing up for before you pack.

The rules are the real culture. Bringing firewood across provincial borders is prohibited, fire-ban fines in BC begin at CAD 1,150, you are expected to stay a hundred metres from bears and feeding wildlife can attract fines of up to CAD 25,000. Crown land camping is free for residents for up to 21 days in one spot. Canada treats wilderness as a shared asset managed by permit, and the result is clean, spectacular and slightly bureaucratic.

The gap in my research is that I could not verify specific facts about Toronto's High Park or Montreal's Mont-Royal, so I will not pretend to describe them. What I can say is that a Canadian-raised blogger who spent time in Prague says she missed Riegrovy sady, with its view of the Castle and its off-leash dog area, and that Prague being landlocked was her main contrast with Vancouver. It is a small voice, but a revealing one: Canadians want space and water, and Prague offers beer and a view.

## The Verdict

Canada wins on scale, and Czechia wins on habit. A Canadian park will give you a mountain, a lake and a story about a bear. A Czech park will give you a bench, a beer and a neighbour who knows the best place to find boletes.

The expat who thrives in Prague is the one who adopts the chata. The expat who thrives in Canada is the one who adopts the reservation system. Both require a commitment, and both repay it with a weekend that feels like a country's best secret.

If I had to choose, I would take the Czech approach for a normal Friday and the Canadian approach for a big birthday. Nature as a ritual beats nature as a project every week of the year, but nobody is going to write a poem about a sausage.

## Conclusion

A park is a country's argument about what free time is for. Czechia argues that it is for the family, the garden and a cold lager at the end of a wooden table. Canada argues that it is for solitude, scale and a sense that you are smaller than the landscape.

Take both. Learn to pick a mushroom without poisoning anyone, learn to hang your food away from a bear and, most importantly, learn not to ask a Czech how long they have owned their cottage unless you have an hour spare. The answer is always longer than you expect, and the story always ends with beer.
