# Nobody Is Coming to Save Your Saturday, So Make It Loud

🇭🇹 Haiti and 🇭🇳 Honduras
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every Western guidebook I've ever thumbed through describes both of these countries with the same tired adjective — "exotic" — as though a Saturday afternoon in Port-au-Prince or Tegucigalpa is a theme park ride built for your benefit. It isn't. It's just Saturday, and it happens to be considerably better organised for enjoying itself than the one you're used to having in a city where "leisure" means scrolling in bed until 2pm.

Haiti and Honduras get lumped together by outsiders who can't be bothered to learn the difference between the Caribbean and Central America, which is itself a small crime. But both nations share something worth studying properly: the weekend isn't scheduled around rest. It's scheduled around noise, proximity to family, and the firm belief that silence is for the dead. If you've arrived expecting a quiet expat retreat, you have made an error, and I'd like to walk you through exactly how large an error it is.

## Do's & Don'ts: Haiti

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to a "kompa" street party expecting it to run four hours past whenever you thought it would end | Expect an invitation to specify a start time that means anything |
| Bring your own chair to a beach outing — Haitians pack for comfort, not minimalism | Assume the beach is for swimming only; it's a full-day social institution |
| Let a Sunday church service run its course before proposing anything else that day | Schedule brunch reservations like this is Brooklyn |
| Learn to appreciate dominoes as a spectator sport with real emotional stakes | Underestimate how seriously a dominoes match is being contested at 11am |

## Do's & Don'ts: Honduras

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the "ronda" — the Sunday walk around the central park that entire towns still do | Expect the mall food court to be a lesser social venue than a restaurant; it isn't |
| Accept that a "quick" family lunch on Sunday is a four-hour minimum commitment | Try to leave a Honduran Sunday lunch after dessert; there is no after dessert |
| Bring cash to a "feria" weekend market — cards are treated as a personal inconvenience to the vendor | Assume weekend football is casual; regional derbies have ended actual friendships |
| Get comfortable with reggaetón at a volume that would get a noise complaint anywhere in Western Europe | Bring noise-cancelling headphones to a pool party and expect anyone to respect them |

## Haiti: The Weekend as an Act of Defiance

Here is what nobody tells you before you land in Port-au-Prince or Cap-Haïtien: leisure in Haiti is not a luxury bolted onto a hard week. It is a deliberate, almost political insistence on joy, delivered at a volume that dares the rest of the world to say something about it. Given the country's press coverage — which treats Haiti as a synonym for catastrophe and little else — this insistence reads less like escapism and more like a national personality trait forged specifically to spite the narrative.

Weekends in Haiti begin properly on Friday evening, when "lakou" culture kicks in — the shared courtyard life where neighbours who've been in and out of each other's homes all week finally sit down together with a bottle of Barbancourt rum and no plans to move for several hours. Music isn't background noise; it's the organising principle of the entire event, usually kompa or rasin, played loud enough that "ambient" isn't a concept anyone recognises.

Saturday belongs to the market, and I mean genuinely belongs to it — the Marché en Fer in Port-au-Prince or smaller regional markets turn into all-day social circuits where buying a mango takes forty minutes because you're also catching up on three families' worth of gossip. Foreigners who try to "get shopping done efficiently" on a Haitian Saturday have fundamentally misunderstood the assignment. Efficiency was never the point.

Sunday is church, full stop, for a significant majority of the population, whether Catholic, Protestant, or Vodou-adjacent syncretism that Western commentators love to sensationalise and rarely understand. Mass can run for hours, singing included, and afterwards, families spill onto the street in their best clothes — genuinely their best, pressed and considered — to be seen, to visit, to eat. If you're an expat who thought you'd use Sunday to catch up on emails, you will instead be fed, several times, by people who consider your solitude on a Sunday a personal failing on their part to correct.

Beach days, when they happen — Jacmel, Cormier, Kokoye — are not the sun-loungers-and-silence experience marketed to tourists elsewhere in the Caribbean. They are extended family productions involving speakers, coolers the size of steamer trunks, and food that takes longer to prepare than the swimming takes to enjoy. You do not go to a Haitian beach to be alone with your thoughts. You go to be absorbed into someone else's family for the day, whether you were invited specifically or not.

## Honduras: The Ronda, the Feria, and the Sacred Sunday Table

Honduras runs on a gentler frequency than Haiti but is no less committed to the principle that a weekend unaccompanied by extended family is a weekend wasted. The clearest evidence is the "ronda" — a tradition, especially strong in smaller towns like Copán or Gracias, where the entire community walks laps around the central park on Sunday evenings. Teenagers flirt in one direction, grandparents gossip in the other, and everyone accepts this as a perfectly rational use of two hours that a European would spend refreshing a group chat instead.

Saturdays belong to the "feria" — the open-air market that in towns like La Ceiba or Santa Rosa de Copán turns the town centre into a single enormous transaction that's really a social event wearing a market's clothes. Cash is not optional; card payments at a feria stall get you a look that says you've personally offended someone's grandmother. You haggle, you chat, you're offered a taste of something you didn't ask for, and you leave two hours later than you intended with produce you didn't plan to buy.

Then there's Sunday lunch, the true institution. Baleadas for breakfast if you're lucky enough to be invited early, then a slow build toward the main event: an entire extended family — cousins, in-laws, the neighbour who's basically family at this point — gathered around a table for a meal that treats "quick" as a foreign concept. Plates of tajadas, pollo con tajadas, the inevitable horchata, and conversation that loops back on itself for hours. Trying to leave before dessert is treated as a diplomatic incident.

Honduran weekends also have an unshakeable football obsession — Olimpia versus Motagua is not a casual rivalry, it is closer to a religious schism, and showing up at a bar wearing the wrong colours during a clásico is a risk I'd only recommend to people who enjoy being loudly, publicly wrong. Beach weekends on the Bay Islands or the north coast run at a marginally lower volume than Haiti's, but "lower" here is relative — reggaetón from a portable speaker is still doing structural damage to the concept of a quiet afternoon.

## The Verdict

If you want your weekend to recharge you quietly, neither of these countries is interested in your plan, and frankly, good. Haiti wins on sheer defiant intensity — the volume, the colour, the refusal to let anyone's misery narrative dictate how loudly Haitians are allowed to enjoy themselves. It's exhausting in the best possible way, like being handed a drink you didn't order and finding out it's exactly what you needed.

Honduras wins on structure and inclusion — the ronda, the feria, the Sunday table that simply assumes you'll be there whether or not anyone formally asked you. It's less combustible than Haiti's weekend, more of a slow, communal simmer than a full boil.

My verdict: Haiti for pure adrenaline, Honduras for the version of "family" that quietly absorbs strangers into it without paperwork. Either way, bring earplugs you have no intention of using, because using them would be missing the entire point.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/expats — a Port-au-Prince-based aid worker paraphrased: nobody mentions that "just popping to the market" on Saturday is a four-hour commitment involving at least two marriage proposals from strangers and one adopted auntie.*

> *Quora — a respondent living in Tegucigalpa noted that the Sunday "ronda" walk feels like a Victorian courtship ritual crossed with a town hall meeting, and after six months you'll find yourself doing it out of genuine enjoyment, not obligation.*

> *expat.com forum — a Honduras-based retiree paraphrased: the Olimpia-Motagua rivalry is not a football match, it's closer to declaring your extended in-laws' political allegiance, choose your bar shirt with real caution.*

## Conclusion

Neither Haiti nor Honduras will let you rest on your own terms, and that's the whole lesson. The Western idea of a weekend — quiet, private, recuperative — simply doesn't translate, and trying to force it onto either country will only leave you isolated in a place that was actively trying to include you the entire time. Say yes to the feria. Say yes to the lakou. Let the reggaetón win. You didn't move somewhere "exotic" for the scenery; you moved somewhere with a functioning, un-embarrassed relationship to joy, and the least you can do is show up for it at full volume.
