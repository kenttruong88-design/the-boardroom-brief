# One Country's Buses Have No Signs and Everyone Just Knows, the Other's Commute Basically Requires a Car

🇪🇹 Ethiopia vs 🇳🇿 New Zealand — City Transport and Commuting Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Addis Ababa runs on a minibus system so informal it has almost no signage, no fixed timetable, and no official route map that anyone actually consults — instead, a young conductor hangs out the sliding door shouting the destination and a hand signal, and the entire system somehow, reliably works because everyone simply knows. New Zealand, meanwhile, has built a transport culture almost entirely around private car ownership, particularly outside Auckland and Wellington, to the point that "commute" in most of the country quietly means "drive," full stop, with public transport functioning more as a backup plan than a real option.

I have flagged down an Addis Ababa minibus using only a vague hand gesture I'd learned an hour earlier, been correctly delivered to a destination I never verbally stated, for the equivalent of about fifteen cents. I have also lived in a small New Zealand city for six months without once seeing a bus I could realistically use, and bought a car within the first month purely out of logistical necessity, which felt like admitting defeat before I'd even really tried.

## Do's & Don'ts: Ethiopia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the hand signals and shouted destinations used to flag minibuses — it's the actual system | Expect fixed schedules or posted routes — minibuses run on informal, learned local knowledge |
| Try the Addis Ababa Light Rail Transit for a more predictable, fixed-route alternative | Assume the cheapest minibus fare reflects poor quality — it's simply the standard pricing |
| Ask locals or a conductor directly if unsure of a route — everyone expects and helps with this | Get in the front seat uninvited — seating norms in minibuses have their own local etiquette |
| Carry small denomination cash — minibus conductors rarely have change for large notes | Expect punctuality in the Western sense — minibuses leave once full, not on a clock |

## Do's & Don'ts: New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget for a car if living outside central Auckland or Wellington — public transport coverage drops fast | Assume intercity or regional public transport will be frequent — services can be sparse |
| Use Auckland's ferries — a genuinely pleasant, underused commuting option across the harbour | Expect late-night public transport outside the biggest cities — services often stop early |
| Get a Hop Card (Auckland) or Snapper (Wellington) for smoother public transport payment | Underestimate driving distances — New Zealand's geography makes "nearby" deceptively far by road |
| Cycle where infrastructure allows — several cities are investing seriously in bike lanes | Assume New Zealand's small population means short commutes — sprawl makes many commutes lengthy |

## Ethiopia: An Informal System That Somehow Never Fails

Addis Ababa's minibus network — blue-and-white Higer vans that form the backbone of the city's transport system — operates almost entirely without the infrastructure a visitor would expect from a functioning mass transit system: no printed route maps in common circulation, no digital app most riders actually use, and no fixed schedule beyond "leaves once full." Instead, the system runs on an oral, gestural code passed down through pure lived experience — a conductor leans out the sliding door calling the destination, sometimes just a neighbourhood name shouted rapidly, sometimes a specific hand signal indicating a route, and passengers along the road recognise it instantly and flag the vehicle down accordingly.

For newcomers, this initially reads as chaos, and then, with surprising speed, reveals itself as a remarkably efficient, high-frequency, and extraordinarily cheap system that moves an enormous number of people daily with minimal formal infrastructure. The city's more recent Light Rail Transit line, Sub-Saharan Africa's first modern light rail system, offers a more legible, fixed-route alternative along its two lines, popular for its predictability, though it covers only a fraction of the routes the minibus network handles organically.

Etiquette within this system carries its own local logic — seating arrangements in a minibus follow unspoken norms around who sits where, fares are collected by the conductor mid-journey rather than paid on boarding, and the whole apparatus depends on a level of shared, unwritten cultural knowledge that makes the system simultaneously intimidating to outsiders and almost impossible to actually break for anyone who's grown up using it.

## New Zealand: A Country Built Around the Car

New Zealand's transport culture, outside its two largest cities, is built on an assumption so foundational it barely registers as a choice: you will own and drive a car, because the alternative — public transport coverage across a famously sprawling, low-density, geographically stretched-out country — simply doesn't reach most places with any real frequency. Auckland and Wellington have made genuine, sustained investment in their public transport networks over the past decade, including improved bus networks, rail lines, and in Auckland's case a well-regarded ferry system connecting the harbour's northern suburbs to the CBD, but even in these cities, car ownership remains the practical default for a large share of residents, particularly those living beyond the inner suburbs.

Outside the two major centres, the picture shifts more starkly — smaller cities and towns often have minimal, infrequent bus services that function more as a social safety net for those without cars than a genuine commuting alternative for the broader population, and intercity travel beyond the main highways relies heavily on private vehicles or a limited long-distance coach network. New Zealand's dramatic and often mountainous geography compounds this, turning distances that look short on a map into genuinely long drives, a reality locals absorb as simply how the country works rather than something worth resenting.

The upside of this car-centric culture is a genuinely excellent, well-maintained road network and a national driving culture that, outside a few notorious black-spot routes, prioritises courtesy and patience — indicating, giving way, and general road manners tend to be taken seriously, a small compensation for a transport system that otherwise assumes, correctly, that you'll mostly be doing this alone.

## The Verdict

Ethiopia's transport culture is a masterclass in informal, high-density efficiency — a system with almost no visible infrastructure that nonetheless moves millions of people daily through pure shared cultural knowledge. New Zealand's is the opposite case entirely: excellent infrastructure for the car it assumes you'll own, and a public transport system that only really commits in two cities. Ethiopia wins for sheer improvisational ingenuity. New Zealand wins if your definition of good transport involves never having to shout out a moving vehicle's window to be understood.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/ethiopia — paraphrased: spent my first two weeks in Addis genuinely baffled by the minibus system before realising there was never a map to find in the first place — you just learn it by riding it, badly, for a while.*

> *Internations Auckland — paraphrased: moved to a smaller New Zealand city assuming I could get by without a car like I did back home, and bought one within three weeks once I realised the bus came once an hour, if that.*

> *Quora — paraphrased: someone asked how tourists are supposed to know an Addis minibus's destination with no signs, and the top answer was simply "ask literally anyone standing nearby, they will happily tell you."*

## Conclusion

Ethiopia proves that a transport system doesn't need signage, schedules, or an app to function beautifully — it just needs a critical mass of people who've collectively agreed on the rules and pass them on by doing. New Zealand proves the opposite lesson works too, that a genuinely small population and dramatic geography can make car dependency the rational, unremarkable choice rather than a failure of planning. Go to Ethiopia and learn to shout for a minibus. Go to New Zealand and just buy the car — you were going to need it anyway.
