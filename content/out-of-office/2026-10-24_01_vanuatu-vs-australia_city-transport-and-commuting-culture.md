# Vanuatu's Buses Have No Route, Sydney's Trains Have No Soul

🇻🇺 Vanuatu vs 🇦🇺 Australia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is a sentence I never thought I'd write with a straight face: the most honest public transport system I have ever used had no timetable, no map, and no idea where it was going until I told it. That system is Port Vila's bus fleet, and it is, infuriatingly, better at its one job than most Western cities' multi-billion-dollar transit authorities are at theirs. Meanwhile, three hours south, Australia has built a transport network so comprehensively planned, so relentlessly tapped-on-and-off, that it has somehow engineered all the warmth out of getting from A to B and replaced it with a $280-a-month parking invoice.

This is the part where lesser writers would call Vanuatu "charmingly chaotic" and Australia "impressively efficient," pat themselves on the back, and move on. I am not going to do that, because both of those sentences are doing a lot of unpaid labour to avoid the actual point: one of these countries has decided that commuting should be a conversation, and the other has decided it should be a system you swipe your way through and resent. Neither is wrong. Both will test you. Let's get into it.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇻🇺 Vanuatu

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wave with conviction — a half-hearted flap gets ignored | Don't expect a fixed route; the bus goes where the passengers go |
| Carry small vatu notes and coins for the fare | Don't hand a driver a large note and expect change on a short hop |
| State your destination clearly the moment you board | Don't assume the bus that got you there will get you home the same way |
| Book a taxi or arrange a ride after dark | Don't count on bus service once the sun drops — it thins out fast |

### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get an Opal card (or your city's equivalent) on day one | Don't drive into the CBD at peak hour expecting mercy or parking |
| Choose where you live based on train lines, not vibes | Don't assume public transport reaches every outer suburb equally |
| Sort your licence exchange rules in your first three months | Don't try to out-manoeuvre a kangaroo on a country road at dusk |
| Budget for parking like it's a second rent payment | Don't expect "reliable" to also mean "cheap" |

## Vanuatu: The Bus That Finds You

In Port Vila, a bus is not a vehicle that has been designated a bus by some transport authority with a logo and a mission statement. A bus is any minivan whose number plate happens to start with the letter B, which is the entirety of the regulatory framework, and which is somehow sufficient. There are no scheduled stops in any meaningful sense. You stand at the edge of the road, you raise a hand or a thumb, and a B-plate van materialises, already carrying however many other people it has collected on its way to wherever it was going before you flagged it down. You tell the driver where you want to go. He recalculates. Everyone else on board recalculates with him, wordlessly, because this is simply how it works and asking "but what's the route?" marks you out as someone who has not yet let go.

You pay in cash, usually on the way out, usually somewhere between 150 and 500 vatu depending on distance, and you keep small notes because nobody on this van is running a float. There is no air conditioning to speak of, the roads have opinions of their own in the form of potholes, and after dark the whole informal choreography simply stops, at which point you call a taxi like a sensible adult who has accepted the sun has set on improvisation for the day.

What's extraordinary is that this works at all, let alone that it works well, and local authorities know it. The Vanuatu Daily Post reported growing public pressure for Port Vila to adopt zoned, fixed bus routes to ease the congestion caused by roughly 1,400 individually owned vans all converging on the same stretch of town — but the PLTA's own CEO has conceded that reorganising an industry built on 1,400 separate livelihoods is a far harder problem than adjusting fares. Which tells you everything: this isn't a system anyone designed. It's a system that grew, the way market stalls grow, the way friendships grow, which is precisely why it has more humanity in it than anything with a logo ever will.

## Australia: The Grid That Judges You

Australia, by extravagant contrast, has engineered the humanity out of its commute and charged you for the privilege. In Sydney, you get an Opal card, you tap on, you tap off, and a fare is silently deducted by a machine that has never once asked how your day is going. The metro runs every four to five minutes at peak. Trains are, by multiple accounts, "regular, air-conditioned and not too expensive" — right up until you actually tally the weekly spend, at which point one Sydney expat family clocked their transport costs at roughly $45 USD a week and called it "all great and convenient. Not cheap." That is Australia's commuting culture in four words: great, convenient, not cheap, repeat until retirement.

The driving experience fractures entirely depending on where you've landed, which is its own small cruelty. Set up in Perth or the Lockyer Valley and you'll find freeways so unclogged that locals on expat forums describe peak hour as "five or six cars in a queue" and genuinely relish getting back behind the wheel. Land in Sydney or Melbourne and you inherit bridge traffic, tunnel bottlenecks, and urban sprawl that has pushed Melbourne's newer suburbs further and further from anything resembling a train line. Parking in the inner city is, in the words of one forum poster, simply "outrageous" — you pay anywhere you stop, full stop. Add a three-month grace period on your foreign driving licence before the state insists you sit its tests, a genuine need to watch for kangaroos on unlit country roads at night, and hills steep enough that Sydney's cyclists earn every cent of the city's free tune-up subsidies, and you have a transport culture that is comprehensively planned, admirably punctual, and completely indifferent to whether you're enjoying yourself.

## The Verdict

If you want reliability, Australia wins, decisively, on paper, in a laminated timetable nobody needed because the app already told you. If you want to arrive anywhere with a story, Vanuatu wins, every single time, because the story is the commute.

Here is the uncomfortable truth neither tourism board will put on a brochure: Australia's system is built to remove you from other people, and it succeeds magnificently — tap on, plug in headphones, tap off, never once make eye contact with a human being for the entire journey. Vanuatu's system is built, by complete accident, to force you into other people's business, and the strange result is that you remember your bus drivers' faces years later in a way you will never remember an Opal gate.

I'm calling it for Vanuatu. Not because it's more efficient — it categorically is not — but because efficiency was never actually the point of getting somewhere. Australia will get you to the office on time. Vanuatu will get you there having had an actual conversation. Only one of those is a life.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com "Traffic in Australia" forum — a Queensland commuter describes peak hour as "five or six cars in a queue," which tells you everything about how relative the word "traffic" really is.*

> *TalesMag Sydney Post Report — a posted expat family calls Sydney's buses "great and convenient," then immediately flags the catch: "Not cheap," at roughly US$45 a week just to get everyone to school and work.*

> *ispyplumpie.com travel blog, "Vanuatu Adventures: Port Vila" — the writer admits the "B"-plate minivan system is "one of the strangest things about Port Vila," right before conceding that once you've flagged your first one down, you simply stop questioning it.*

## Conclusion

Nobody relocates for the commute, and yet the commute is the thing that quietly rewires how you feel about the place you've moved to. Australia will hand you an Opal card, a punctual metro, and a parking bill that reads like a second mortgage, and call that progress. Vanuatu will hand you a stranger's minivan, no fixed route, and a driver who already knows three of your neighbours by name, and call that Tuesday.

Both of these are, in their own way, entirely functional transport cultures. One simply happens to be functional in the way a spreadsheet is functional, and the other is functional in the way a small, slightly chaotic family dinner is functional — everyone gets fed eventually, nobody quite agrees on how, and somehow that's the charm. Choose your relocation accordingly, and stop describing either one as "exotic" in your group chat. It's not exotic. It's just somebody else's Tuesday, and you're the one who has to learn to wave properly.
