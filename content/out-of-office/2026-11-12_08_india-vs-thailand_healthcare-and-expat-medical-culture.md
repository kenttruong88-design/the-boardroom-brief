# Thailand Sells You a Hospital Like a Five-Star Hotel. India Sells You One Like a Lifelong Argument

🇮🇳 India vs 🇹🇭 Thailand

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two schools of expat medicine. The first treats a hospital as a service business: friendly staff, English-speaking doctors, a gleaming lobby and an invoice that arrives before you have recovered. The second treats it as a public institution you must negotiate with: patient, vigilant, armed with a second opinion and a relative who knows someone senior.

Thailand is the first school's headmaster. India contains both, depending on whether you are visiting an elite private hospital in a metro or a public clinic in a district town. The question for the corporate expat is not which country has better doctors. It is which country knows how to be ill politely.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Choose a major private hospital for anything serious | Assume every clinic meets the same standard |
| Get a second opinion on any major recommendation | Accept the first quote for surgery |
| Keep a local contact who knows the system | Navigate admissions alone |
| Carry your records and insurance details | Arrive without documentation |

**Thailand**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use a major Bangkok private hospital with English-speaking staff | Rely on a small clinic far from the city for complex care |
| Compare prices for the same procedure across hospitals | Assume the nearest private hospital is the cheapest |
| Check the doctor's biography on the hospital website | Take the first suggestion without question |
| Confirm third-party billing before inpatient care | Discover the insurance gap at discharge |

## India

I will start with a confession of absence. I could not fetch a reliable India healthcare source or an expat voice for this piece, so what follows on India is observation and opinion, not citation. Treat it accordingly.

India's medical system is a great paradox. At the top end, the metro private hospitals in Delhi, Mumbai, Chennai and Bangalore are among the best in Asia, staffed by doctors with global training and equipped for complex procedures. Costs are a fraction of Western prices, which is why medical tourists arrive in numbers. At the bottom end, public facilities are overstretched, queues are long and the experience can be chaotic.

For the corporate expat, the practical answer is simple: stay in the top tier. Insure privately, choose a major hospital with international accreditation and let your employer's HR department tell you which one it trusts. Do not expect a government clinic to provide the bedside manner of a hotel. Expect, instead, a system in which the family is part of the treatment team, often camped in the corridor with tiffins and opinions.

The cultural point is that Indian medicine is relational. Doctors are consulted, challenged and compared among relatives, and a second opinion is not rude; it is routine. Medication is available over the counter in a way that horrifies Western regulators and delights everyone with a headache. The risk is that you can acquire a prescription too easily and an explanation too slowly.

If you want a single sentence of advice, it is this: ask for the plan in writing, and ask why. A good Indian doctor will respect the question, and a bad one will reveal himself in the answer.

The social side of Indian healthcare is just as important as the clinical one. Hospitals are busy, family members stay, and decisions are often made collectively, with elders weighing in on the choice of doctor or procedure. For a foreigner used to private consultations, this can feel intrusive. It is better read as support. When something serious is at stake, the Indian family will camp in the waiting room, bring food, ask the questions you forgot and remember the names of the nurses. That network is a form of care that no insurance policy can buy, and one reason many Indians prefer to be treated at home.

## Thailand

Thailand is better documented. The country's universal coverage scheme is open to foreigners as well as citizens, according to Expat Exchange, but expats usually rely on private insurance and the private hospitals of Bangkok. Bumrungrad, Bangkok Hospital and Samitivej are the names that recur, joined by BNH, Phyathai 2 and Vibhavadi in the Expat Arrivals directory. Several of them also serve the three million or so international patients Thailand receives each year, notably for cosmetic and dental work.

Prices are the draw, with caveats. A simple procedure at a premium hospital may cost a few hundred dollars, the Bangkok guide says, but major surgery or emergency admission can reach five or six figures. An American columnist who had his over-forty checkup at Bumrungrad paid about 575 dollars for the comprehensive package and noted that the hospital had no separate pricing for the insured and uninsured. That transparency is rare and valuable.

The voices are practical. A Bangkok member on Expat Exchange advised comparing prices for the same procedure across hospitals, checking doctors' biographies on hospital websites, using private hospitals for shorter waits and confirming third-party billing before inpatient care. A Krabi resident said local clinics have poor reputations, while the nearest international hospital is half an hour away. A French expat quoted by Expat Arrivals said that with good insurance the quality is excellent, but price and quality vary by hospital and doctor, so seek second opinions. A Canadian expat described a doctor who suggested an MRI and asked if he was free that evening, which is either impressive efficiency or a warning. He chose to come back the next day.

And some people skip insurance altogether. An expat in Chiang Mai said walk-in care is so cheap that he doesn't bother, while another reported that three X-rays, a doctor visit and a prescription cost about 50 dollars, which is less than a typical American deductible. That works until it does not.

The Thai system has its own blind spots. Several of the guides and posters stress that quality varies by hospital and doctor, and that the glossy private hospitals in Bangkok are not representative of the whole country. Outside the capital, expats describe clinics with poor reputations and long drives to a proper hospital. The sensible response is to treat the Bangkok names as the standard and to plan accordingly if you live in a smaller town: know where the nearest international hospital is, carry your insurance details and do not wait for the emergency to find out.

## The Verdict

Thailand wins on service. India wins on depth. If you need a smooth, English-speaking hospital experience with transparent pricing and little drama, Bangkok's private hospitals are among the easiest in the world to use.

India, though, has something Thailand cannot match: scale and specialist depth at a price that makes Western systems blush. For complex cardiac, orthopaedic or oncological work, the top Indian hospitals deliver results that compare with the best, and often faster than a waiting list at home.

I will give the expat prize to Thailand, because most expats are not having a heart transplant. They are having a sinus infection, a sprained ankle and a vague worry that something is wrong. For those, Thailand is unbeatable: you walk in, you are seen, you pay a reasonable bill, and you walk out with your dignity. India demands more of you. In return it offers more medicine than almost anywhere on earth.

The truth, as ever, lies in your insurance policy. Read it before you need it.

## Conclusion

Healthcare abroad is a test of how well a country treats strangers. Thailand has turned it into an industry and so treats you like a customer. India treats you like family, which means you get both extra care and extra opinions.

Buy proper insurance. Learn which hospitals your policy accepts before the emergency, not during it. Ask for written quotes, and do not be shy about a second opinion.

And remember that the best doctor on earth cannot fix a billing error. Keep your receipts, keep your records and keep your sense of humour. You will need all three.
