# Portugal Will Jog You Along the Atlantic; Cambodia Will Just Hand You a Kettlebell and a Fan

🇵🇹 Portugal 🇰🇭 Cambodia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Fitness culture reveals more about a country's climate, income structure, and general relationship to sweating in public than almost any other lifestyle category. Portugal has spent the last decade building a genuinely serious, EU-standard fitness infrastructure along its coastline. Cambodia has built something scrappier, cheaper, and — I'll be honest — more fun, out of open-air equipment, a handful of serious CrossFit boxes, and a climate that makes "cardio" redundant just by existing in it.

## Do's & Don'ts: Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take advantage of the coastal running and cycling infrastructure | Assume every gym is air-conditioned — check before you sign, especially inland |
| Use a low-cost chain (Fitness Hut, Holmes Place, VivaGym) for value | Expect American-style 24-hour gym access everywhere outside Lisbon/Porto |
| Try group fitness classes — genuinely popular and social | Skip reading the contract fine print — cancellation terms vary wildly |
| Combine gym membership with outdoor sport (surfing, hiking) — it's the local norm | Assume small-town Portugal has the same gym density as Lisbon or Porto |

## Do's & Don'ts: Cambodia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use Phnom Penh's public outdoor gym equipment — it's free and everywhere | Expect climate control at most budget gyms — bring a towel, expect sweat |
| Try a CrossFit-style box for a genuine, well-run expat fitness community | Assume rural gym access matches Phnom Penh's relative density |
| Negotiate monthly rates directly — many gyms are flexible on price | Skip checking equipment quality before committing to a membership |
| Exercise early morning or evening to avoid the worst heat | Underestimate the heat — hydration is not optional, it's survival |

## Portugal: Coastal, Structured, Increasingly Serious

Portugal's fitness culture has professionalized rapidly, and Expat Focus's own guide to staying fit in the country captures the shift well: this is no longer a country where exercise means an occasional beach walk — low-cost gym chains, well-run studios, and a genuinely strong running and cycling culture along the Atlantic coast have made structured fitness a mainstream part of Portuguese daily life, particularly in Lisbon and Porto.

myselfstudio.pt's 2026 fitness data for Portugal reflects a country whose gym membership rates and participation numbers have climbed steadily, driven partly by an influx of remote workers and digital nomads who arrived expecting (and now finding) EU-standard facilities at prices well below what they left behind in Northern Europe or the US. Budget chains like Fitness Hut, Holmes Place, and VivaGym have expanded aggressively, offering month-to-month flexibility that suits transient expat populations far better than the annual-contract gyms that once dominated.

What makes Portugal's fitness culture genuinely distinct, though, isn't the gyms themselves — it's the outdoor infrastructure surrounding them. The country has built out its coastal paths, cycling routes, and surf culture into something that functions as a de facto national gym extension: plenty of Portuguese residents treat a gym membership as a wet-weather backup to a lifestyle that's actually organized around the ocean, the hills, and a genuinely enviable amount of usable outdoor space for exercise most of the year. Porto Expats' own gym guide notes this explicitly — ask a Porto local about their "workout routine" and there's a decent chance surfing or trail running comes up before anything happening indoors.

## Cambodia: Scrappy, Hot, and Surprisingly Serious About It

Cambodia's fitness culture runs on an entirely different infrastructure model, and Move to Cambodia's own Phnom Penh guide describes it well: free public outdoor gym equipment — the kind installed along riverside parks and public squares — has become a genuinely central part of daily fitness for both locals and expats, requiring no membership, no contract, just a willingness to work out in visible, communal public space, humidity and all.

Beyond the free public equipment, Phnom Penh has developed a surprisingly serious dedicated fitness scene: several well-regarded CrossFit-style boxes have built strong, tight-knit expat and local communities around them, and expat.com's own guide to staying fit in Phnom Penh notes that these boxes function as much as social hubs as workout spaces — often the fastest way for a new arrival to build a genuine friend group, not just a training routine. Budget gyms exist too, considerably cheaper than their Portuguese equivalents, though air conditioning and modern equipment are far from guaranteed and worth checking in person before committing.

The overwhelming, unavoidable variable in Cambodian fitness culture is heat. A Tales Mag "real post report" from a Phnom Penh-based expat put it about as plainly as possible: gym costs are refreshingly low and access is genuinely widespread, but nothing prepares newcomers for training in tropical humidity, and most serious exercisers quickly shift to early-morning or evening sessions purely out of self-preservation. It's a fitness culture that demands you work with the climate rather than against it, which, once accepted, becomes oddly liberating — there's no illusion of controlling the environment, only adapting your schedule to it.

## The Verdict

Portugal offers structure, EU-standard facilities, and a genuinely enviable outdoor-fitness lifestyle for a fraction of what similar infrastructure costs further north — hard to beat if consistency and comfort matter to you. Cambodia offers something scrappier and, honestly, more community-driven: free public equipment, a tight CrossFit scene punching well above its size, and a climate that will teach you humility about your own cardio fitness within about four minutes of your first outdoor session. If you want your workout to feel like a well-run business, Portugal wins. If you want it to feel like an actual community sweating through the same heat together, book Phnom Penh — just bring three towels, not one.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Tales Mag "Real Post Report" — a Phnom Penh-based expat said gym membership cost "less than my monthly coffee budget back home," but warned that "the heat will humble you faster than any personal trainer ever could."*

> *Porto Expats — a community post described a new arrival joining a surf club instead of a gym entirely, noting "half the city treats the ocean as their actual membership, the gym is just the rainy-day plan."*

> *Quora — a respondent answering about staying fit as an expat in Southeast Asia recommended Phnom Penh's CrossFit boxes specifically "not for the workout, but because it's the fastest way to make real friends in a new city."*

## Conclusion

Fitness culture, it turns out, is downstream of everything else — climate, income, urban design, social norms. Portugal has built a polished, professionalized version that mirrors its broader EU integration; Cambodia has built something rawer and more communal that mirrors its own scrappy, fast-growing urban energy. Both got me sweating for entirely different reasons and in entirely different amounts of humidity. I'd take Portugal for consistency and Cambodia for the stories — and I'd take neither seriously without checking, in advance, whether the gym has air conditioning.
