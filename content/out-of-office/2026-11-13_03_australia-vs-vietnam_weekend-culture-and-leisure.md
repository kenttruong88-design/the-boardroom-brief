# Australia Shuts the Shops on Sunday and Calls It Lifestyle. Vietnam Never Shuts Anything and Calls It Tuesday

🇦🇺 Australia vs 🇻🇳 Vietnam

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Weekends are where a country tells you what it thinks work is for. In Australia the answer is "so we can stop", and the entire nation executes the stopping with ritual precision. In Vietnam the answer appears to be "so we can do the same things at a different volume", and the weekend is less a pause than a change of venue.

An expat posted from Sydney to Saigon will go through a very particular kind of grief. The grief is not about missing home. It is about discovering that the thing you thought was universal, a long, closed, vaguely sacred Sunday, is a local custom with its own opening hours.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Australia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy your groceries before the weekend ends | Assume every shop opens on Sunday |
| Treat the weekend as time with friends and family | Schedule a work call on Saturday morning |
| Join a club or sports team to meet people | Wait for neighbours to knock first |
| Check regional trading hours before a road trip | Expect city hours in a small town |

**Vietnam**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Walk the lakes in the evening, when the heat breaks | Plan outdoor sightseeing at midday |
| Use the weekend for road trips and street-food tours | Stay in on a Saturday night |
| Ask in local Facebook groups for flea markets and events | Rely on a single guidebook |
| Take a taxi until you understand the traffic | Hire a motorbike on day three |

## Australia

Australia treats the weekend as a civic institution. A Property Guides article on what expats soon learn in Australia notes that even in Sydney, shops commonly close early on weekends, and that in Perth its author found no shops open on Sundays at all. The writer frames this as part of a wider value: relaxing with friends and family is the point of the week, not a reward for surviving it. That is a lovely idea. It is also a slightly inconvenient one when you have run out of milk.

The forum evidence is a delightful snapshot of a national argument. On a Fodor's travel thread titled with a plaintive "I live here", a Perth resident said most shops close at five and all day Sunday, a Melbourne poster living fifteen kilometres from the city said that on weekends only supermarkets and big shopping centres are reliably open and the strip shops mostly are not, and a Queensland poster said they rarely shop on Sundays at all, preferring family time. A Sydney poster remembered when shops in New South Wales closed at noon on Saturday. That thread is old and trading hours have loosened in places, so treat it as a portrait rather than a statute. But the portrait is consistent: the weekend is protected.

What fills it? Sport, mostly, and the outdoors. An Expat Exchange guide to living in Australia advises newcomers to join clubs, and a Brisbane expat there says sport is huge. The same guide mentions that Newcastle rugby clubs welcome men, women and volunteers. Sunday markets with live music turn up in the Expat.com activities thread, along with New Year fireworks at around nine in the evening watched from a picnic rug and the great state shows, the Ekka in Brisbane and the Royal Easter Show in Sydney.

It is a gentle, social, deliberately unambitious kind of leisure. If you need to be entertained you will find the weekend thin. If you want to be left alone with a barbecue and a few friends, it is paradise with a bin collection.

## Vietnam

Vietnam does not do weekends so much as extend the evening. A New Daily series called Trading Places Global, which sent an Australian to live in Ho Chi Minh City, describes streets and parks that fill up once the heat breaks, quick weekend trips to the Mekong Delta or the coast, and a cost of living that makes the whole thing absurdly cheap: local restaurant meals around six or seven dollars, a cafe lunch three or four, street food one or two, and rent roughly a third of Sydney or Melbourne. Expats, the writer notes, compete to see who can eat the most for ten dollars.

The Expat.com leisure guide to Ho Chi Minh City adds the new-expat circuit: Saigon Outcast in District 2 with its climbing wall, Thursday movie nights and a farmers' market on weekends, a backyard live-music venue open seven nights a week with a bottomless-drinks Friday, and vegan workshops and monthly weekend hikes that book out. Hanoi runs on a different clock. A member of the Expat.com forum recommends a seventeen-kilometre cycle loop around West Lake, a street-food tour, a half-day bike trip to a ceramic village for paper rice cakes, and weekend flea markets advertised through Facebook. Another suggests heading to Hoan Kiem Lake between two and five in the morning for the late-night crowd, and some bars in the capital are said to stay open until three or four.

There is also the question of getting there. On an Expat.com thread about adapting to the pace of life in Vietnam, an American with around three years in the country said a church group helped him settle, he eventually bought a motorbike, and Ho Chi Minh City traffic ignores right of way, so newcomers should take taxis at first. That advice should be framed and hung in every arrivals hall.

Vietnam's weekend is louder, later and more spontaneous. It rewards the curious and the nocturnal. It does not reward anyone who wants quiet before noon.

Back in Australia, the calendar builds in rest more formally than any employer could. An Expat Exchange guide explains that the school year has three two-week breaks and one six-week break, with Term 4 running October to December and about six weeks over December and January. The country plans its leisure in blocks, and the weekend is the smallest block of all. Compare that with Hanoi, where a local member suggests quiet outings at Hoan Kiem Lake, Tay Lake and the botanical garden, malls such as Royal City for the air conditioning, and bars like Eden, Madake and Light House that stay open until three or four in the morning. One country schedules its rest. The other lets it happen to you.

## The Verdict

Australia wins on rest and Vietnam wins on range. If your idea of a perfect Saturday is a long breakfast, a swim and an afternoon you cannot account for, Australia delivers it to your door. If your idea is a street-food crawl followed by a midnight lakeside walk and a motorbike ride to a ceramic village, Vietnam is the only answer.

I also have to be honest about cost. Vietnam makes leisure cheap, and cheap leisure is addictive. Australia makes it affordable but organised, and you will plan more than you expect.

For a single twenty-something I pick Vietnam without hesitation. For a family with small children I pick Australia, where the weekend is a protected space and the biggest hazard is sunburn. For anyone in between, take the Australian weekend home and the Vietnamese evening, and refuse to explain yourself to either.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Fodor's community, a Perth resident — most shops shut at five and all day Sunday, so you plan your life around the clock rather than the other way round.*

> *Expat.com, Anyse1, a Hanoi regular — go to Hoan Kiem Lake between two and five in the morning if you want to see the late-night crowd properly.*

> *Expat.com, ronlafave, an American in Vietnam — Ho Chi Minh City traffic ignores right of way, so take taxis at first and buy a motorbike only once you understand it.*

## Conclusion

A weekend is a mirror. Australia holds it up and shows you someone resting. Vietnam holds it up and shows you someone who has just thought of something else to do.

Pick the one that fits the person you are, not the one you plan to become. If you need quiet, take the barbecue. If you need noise, take the lake.

And whichever you choose, do your shopping before Sunday. Australia does not negotiate and Vietnam does not need to.
