# One Country Negotiates for Everything, the Other Refuses to Buy Bread and Cheese in the Same Building

🇮🇳 India 🇮🇹 Italy
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

India and Italy have both, independently, decided that a supermarket is a slightly suspicious compromise and that real food shopping is a relationship, not a transaction. India expresses this through the mandi, the local market, where price is a starting position rather than a fact and bargaining is basically a form of socialising. Italy expresses this through sheer, principled specialisation: the butcher does meat, the fishmonger does fish, the bakery does bread, and asking any of them to also sell you tinned tomatoes would be interpreted as a mild insult to their craft.

Having shopped my way through both systems with the enthusiasm of someone who has eaten in sixty countries and judged all of them, I can tell you that the supermarket, in either country, is where you go when you've given up on doing it properly.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇮🇳 India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate at the mandi, it's expected and even appreciated as good manners | Pay the first price quoted anywhere that isn't a fixed-price chain store |
| Build a relationship with one or two regular vendors, the prices will drop | Squeeze fruit aggressively to test ripeness, ask the vendor instead |
| Bring your own bags, plastic is often charged for or simply unavailable | Expect weekend mandi hours to be convenient, go early or go hungry |

**🇮🇹 Italy**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit the specific shop for the specific item, that's the actual system | Ask the butcher for cheese, or the baker for wine, it just isn't done |
| Greet the shopkeeper properly before asking for anything | Touch the produce before asking, many stalls prefer to select it for you |
| Shop daily or every few days for freshness, big weekly hauls are rare | Expect Sunday opening hours, much of Italy still properly closes |

## India

Shopping for groceries in India, outside the growing chain-supermarket sector, is a genuinely social, improvisational experience built around the mandi — the local wholesale and retail produce market — where price is never fixed and the real skill is knowing roughly what something should cost before you start the conversation. Negotiating isn't rude, it's the actual mechanism, a small daily performance of mutual respect where both sides know the first number isn't the real number and nobody takes offence at the back-and-forth. I found this terrifying for my first fortnight and now genuinely miss it every time I'm forced to accept a fixed supermarket price without so much as a token haggle.

The sensory experience alone makes the supermarket feel like a sad, beige compromise by comparison — stacks of produce in colour-coded pyramids, vendors calling out prices, the specific choreography of being handed fruit to smell rather than touch it yourself, because a good vendor selects for you once they know what you actually want. Regulars get better prices and better produce set aside quietly under the counter, and becoming a regular is simply a matter of showing up consistently and being polite about the haggling.

Supermarket chains have expanded significantly in Indian cities and they're genuinely useful for packaged goods, imported items, and convenience, but using one exclusively, for everything, is treated by most Indians I know less as a lifestyle choice and more as a minor personal failure, something you do when you're too tired to do it properly.

The mandi's rhythm follows the agricultural calendar far more closely than any supermarket ever could, and a good vendor will tell you, unprompted, exactly which fruit is at its peak that particular week and which you should skip entirely until next month — a level of seasonal honesty that a shrink-wrapped supermarket display, flown in from wherever, simply cannot offer. There's also a quiet generosity built into the system that surprises newcomers: a little extra thrown in "for luck," a free handful of coriander with a vegetable order, small gestures that have no equivalent line item in a fixed-price till. Learning to shop this way properly takes real time, and more than a few overpaid transactions in your first weeks, but the eventual payoff — better produce, better prices, and an actual relationship with the people who feed you — makes the whole enterprise feel less like a chore and more like a daily appointment worth keeping.

## Italy

Italian grocery shopping runs on a principle that borders on religious: specialisation is not inefficiency, it is respect for the craft, and the idea of one building selling bread, meat, fish, cheese, and wine under one roof strikes many Italians as a fundamentally American idea that misunderstands what food is for. The macelleria does meat. The panetteria does bread. The alimentari does the small general bits in between. Each shopkeeper is, in their own domain, something close to an artisan, and asking them to stock something outside their specialty isn't just impractical, it mildly offends the whole logic of the system.

What this produces, day to day, is a shopping routine built on multiple small stops rather than one big one — a quick trip most days rather than a weekly haul, which keeps things fresh and keeps the relationship between shopper and shopkeeper alive in a way a single supermarket run never quite manages. Greeting the shopkeeper properly, by the way, is not a nicety, it is the actual opening move of the transaction; walk in and ask for your order cold, with no "buongiorno," and you will be served, technically, but with a noticeable chill that no amount of good Italian can thaw.

Supermarkets exist and plenty of Italians use them for the basics, but the specialty shops survive and thrive precisely because quality and relationship still outcompete convenience for most people most of the time — a genuinely rare thing to witness holding firm against the global tide toward one-stop shopping.

The Saturday or mid-week open-air market, where it exists, adds another layer entirely on top of the specialty shops — stalls from the same handful of regional producers appearing week after week, with regulars greeting vendors by name and the vendors remembering exactly how each customer likes their peaches. Seasonality is treated with similar near-religious respect; asking for tomatoes in January marks you out instantly as someone who hasn't yet absorbed the system, since a good Italian grocer would rather tell you plainly that it isn't the season than sell you a flavourless substitute. The entire structure rewards patience and repetition over convenience, and once you've built that repetition, shopping stops being a task and starts being, almost accidentally, one of the better parts of your week.

## The Verdict

India wins on sheer theatre and value — the negotiation is half the fun, and a good relationship with your mandi vendor will save you real money while giving you something closer to a daily social ritual than a chore. Italy wins on principle and quality — a system built entirely around specialists doing one thing extremely well, every day, for people who actually care. Both make the supermarket look slightly embarrassing by comparison. I will not be taking questions on this.

## Conclusion

Both countries have quietly rejected the convenience of the one-stop shop in favour of something slower, more specific, and far more human, just via completely different mechanisms — negotiation in one, specialisation in the other. Shop either way for long enough and the supermarket starts to feel less like a time-saver and more like a slightly joyless last resort. You will, inevitably, become the person who refuses to buy tomatoes from the wrong shop, and you will be right to.
