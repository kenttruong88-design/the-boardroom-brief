# Germany Sorts Your Rubbish and Poland Sells You Dinner

🇩🇪 Germany vs 🇵🇱 Poland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The supermarket is where a nation reveals its character without a press office. Walk into one in Germany and you will find an exercise in logistics: narrow aisles, plain packaging, a cashier who treats the till as an Olympic event, and a bottle-return machine in the corner that judges your recycling habits. Walk into one in Poland and you will find something warmer, noisier and surprisingly sophisticated, with a deli counter, a bakery and a queue of grandmothers who know exactly what a kielbasa should cost.

These are neighbours who share a border and a great deal of history, and they have developed entirely different attitudes to the weekly shop. One treats it as a task. The other treats it as a mild social occasion.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇩🇪 Germany ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your own bags and pack at the checkout at speed | Dawdle at the till while the line stares at you |
| Return bottles to the Pfand machine and keep the voucher | Throw deposit bottles away like a tourist |
| Do your shopping before Saturday evening | Expect anything to open on a Sunday |
| Carry some cash, because some shops still prefer it | Assume every small shop takes your card |

| 🇵🇱 Poland ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the discount chains for everyday groceries | Assume the cheapest shop is a poor one |
| Check the Sunday trading calendar before you plan | Assume Sunday shopping works as it does elsewhere |
| Use contactless or BLIK for payment | Hunt for a cash machine to buy a loaf |
| Try the local bakery and deli counter | Skip the pastry because you have a plan |

## 🇩🇪 Germany

The German supermarket is a place where efficiency becomes a moral code. Aldi, Lidl, Rewe, Edeka and the rest compete fiercely on price, and the discounters are so good that middle-class Germans shop there without embarrassment. The aisles are functional, the range is considered rather than exhaustive, and own-label products are good enough that brand loyalty is a minor pastime.

At the checkout the rules begin. The cashier scans at a speed that makes you feel like you are in a race, and you are expected to bag your own items on the other side at a matching pace. Nobody will pack for you. Nobody will wait for you. If you are slow, a gentle sigh will be audible from the third person in the queue. Learn to load the bags fast, or step aside to the packing shelf and do it there.

Then there is the deposit system. Plastic and glass bottles carry a Pfand, a small deposit that you recover by feeding them into a machine in the shop entrance. It works superbly and generates a small ritual of its own, with a clanking queue at the machine and a faint moral superiority among those who have done it properly.

Sundays are closed. Shops shut almost entirely, which means Saturday is the shopping day and Sunday is the day you discover you have no milk. Expats from countries with twenty-four-hour retail never quite get over it. Locals just plan ahead, because planning is a national hobby.

One more detail that Germans abroad miss is the quiet. German supermarkets are not noisy places. There is no music to speak of, staff do not chat, and the till beep is the only sound track. Customers rarely speak to each other, and nobody makes eye contact over the tomatoes. It is a space designed for getting the job done, and the sense of calm it creates is almost meditative. Many expats say that after a few months, they begin to appreciate the absence of small talk, particularly after a long day.

## 🇵🇱 Poland

Poland's grocery landscape looks different the moment you step into it. Biedronka is the dominant discounter and a fixture of nearly every neighbourhood, with Lidl and others close behind. The small convenience chain Żabka sits on corners across the country, sells everything from bread to coffee, and has turned the idea of a corner shop into a national brand.

The surprise for many newcomers is quality. Polish fresh produce, bread, cured meats, dairy and pickles are often excellent, and the fruit and vegetable market remains a living institution, especially in summer. A Polish supermarket often has a bakery that smells like a proper bakery and a deli counter run by staff with strong opinions. Buying a cake on a Saturday morning is a perfectly normal act, and nobody blinks.

Payments are modern. Contactless cards and phone payments are universal, and BLIK, the instant mobile payment system, is used for everything. In some ways the country is more digitally advanced at the till than its western neighbour, which still drags a purse of coins to the baker.

Sunday trading is restricted, with shops closed on most Sundays and open on a small number of designated ones each year. The rule leads to a predictable pattern of Saturday queues and a lively trade at petrol-station shops and in the Żabka corner shops, which have found ways to stay open. The concept of the planned shop is not alien here either, but the response is warmer: you buy more, you chat more, and you leave with something you did not plan to eat.

A final observation concerns the cashier. In Poland, a good cashier remembers regulars, asks whether you need a bag, and comments on the weather. Customers say goodbye on the way out, and strangers in the queue often help with packing. The pace is human rather than mechanical. Prices are lower than you might expect, and the discount chains are seriously good, so the notion that cheaper means poorer is something to unlearn as soon as you arrive.

## The Verdict

Poland wins on atmosphere and Germany wins on process. If I want the weekly shop done in twenty minutes with no surprises, I will go to Aldi and nod at the bottle machine. If I want to enjoy the experience of buying food, I will take Poland, where the bread is better, the corner shop is open late and nobody treats a slow packer as a criminal.

The German system deserves respect for its bottle-return scheme alone, which every country should copy. The Polish system deserves respect for its payment technology and for treating food as pleasure. If I had to pick one for a long posting, I would take Poland for the lifestyle and keep a German list on the fridge for the discipline.

The shopping habits also reveal something about each country's attitude to time. Germans buy for the week and plan the meals. Poles are more likely to pop out for bread, a handful of apples or a pack of ham on the way home, which explains the thousands of small shops. Neither is more sensible, but one produces a tidy fridge and the other produces a good lunch.

If you want a single test, try buying a hot meal on a Sunday evening. In Germany you will be directed to a station kiosk or a restaurant, and you will pay for the convenience. In Poland you will find a Żabka with a heated snack and a smiling cashier. That small scene captures the whole difference between the two countries' attitudes to convenience.

## Conclusion

You can learn a lot about a country by following its shopping trolley. Germany shows you rules, discipline and a faint pride in not being wasteful. Poland shows you hospitality, modernity and a love of bread that cannot be faked.

Take the best of both. Bag fast like a German, pay by phone like a Pole, and always keep something in the cupboard for Sunday. If you do, you will never be the person holding up the queue, and you will never be hungry on a day when everything is shut.

Take the German habit of making a list and the Polish habit of making friends with the baker, and you will do well in either country.
