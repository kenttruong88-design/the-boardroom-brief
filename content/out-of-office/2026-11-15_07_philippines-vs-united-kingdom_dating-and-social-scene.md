# In Manila They Meet Your Family Before the Second Date. In Britain You Apologise to Your Date for Existing, Then Split the Round

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Filipino dating is a community project with candlelight. The courting is often slow, gracious and public. Friends are consulted, cousins are informed, families are quietly assessed, and by the time anyone says "boyfriend" half the street has an opinion on his shoes. It is romantic, theatrical and thoroughly supervised.

British dating is a different species of anxiety. Nobody says what they mean, everybody says sorry, and the whole thing is conducted over drinks in a pub where one party hopes the other will go first. Whether the evening was a date, a drink or a misunderstanding is a question that may take three weeks to resolve. Two cultures, one planet, and absolutely no compatibility in the first fifteen minutes.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Philippines**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat family approval as part of the relationship | Assume it is only between the two of you |
| Show courtesy and effort in courtship | Rush straight to the point |
| Expect group hangouts before one-on-one time | Read it as lack of interest |
| Be generous, especially on a first meal | Split the bill to the penny |

**United Kingdom**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say what you want, once, clearly | Expect your date to guess |
| Take your turn buying the round | Forget it is your turn |
| Understand that understatement is a love language | Mistake "not bad" for indifference |
| Join clubs and groups to meet people | Wait for introductions |

## The Philippines

Let me state up front that the Philippine side of this rests on general knowledge. The country's dating culture has a long courtship tradition, a strong emphasis on family and church, and an enthusiasm for group outings that can baffle outsiders. A man who wants to be taken seriously is expected to show respect to the parents, bring something to the table and make it clear that his intentions are honourable. A woman is expected to be warm, funny and a good judge of character, which in practice means she will have been consulted on three other people's romances before you arrive.

The pace can be slow, but the signals are clear. There is a vocabulary for it, and everyone understands it. The British equivalent of "I think we should see each other again" is a long, sideways glance at the bar. A Filipino equivalent is being invited to a family lunch.

The surprise for many Filipinos abroad is how romance is bound up with migration. A Rappler feature on married life in the UK cites Commission on Filipinos Overseas data showing about 6,660 Filipinos moved to the UK for marriage between 1988 and 2018. It profiles Cat Wigley, who met her husband, a 23-year-old British missionary, in Iloilo in 2010; he proposed after three months, they married three months after that, and they arrived in England in March 2011. Another, Viva Andrada, met her husband in 2014 at London cooking classes, after which he flew to Manila three times in eight months before proposing at Manila Ocean Park. They married in January 2016 and she moved within a week.

Those are not casual love stories. They are decisions with luggage.

## The United Kingdom

British dating, by contrast, is a social science problem. A commercial guide from Kabayan Remit, a remittance company, repeats the BBC claim that the average Briton apologises eight times a day and describes pub rounds as a reciprocal exchange of drinks. It also lists the stoicism, class awareness, humour and false modesty that run through the culture. That is useful context for a Filipino arriving single, because it explains why nobody will tell you how they feel. The British are not unfriendly. They are simply allergic to saying anything directly, especially in a pub.

The social scene itself is organised around alcohol, which can feel limiting. A Nigerian migrant interviewed by Zikoko, a master's student who arrived during lockdown in January 2021, says he stayed indoors with a relative for about six months and only began making friends once classes resumed. He calls the UK dating scene bad, describes taking a 90-minute train to see an old friend, waiting about three hours outside her door in the cold and being refused an outing, and plans to date someone fresh from Nigeria. That is a Nigerian voice, not a Filipino one, and I include it because the loneliness he describes is a migrant's, not a nationality's.

For Filipinos the pattern is similar, with a different texture. Cat Wigley, who started the Facebook group "Filipino Mothers UK" in March 2021, now has a community of thousands. The Rappler piece says it had about 4,800 members at the time of writing. Viva Andrada says she cried when alone at home because she missed her family, and calls homesickness an immigrant problem. What both are describing is a social world rebuilt from nothing, one group chat at a time.

If you are single and Filipino in Britain, the advice is annoyingly practical. Join something with a purpose, whether a church, a football team or a cooking class. The British will talk to you eventually, as long as there is an activity to hide behind.

The calendar adds its own strangeness. The Kabayan Remit guide lists eighteen dates worth knowing, including Burns Night on 25 January and Bonfire Night on 5 November, which suggests that a Filipino arriving in Britain will be invited, at some point, to stand outside in the dark to watch a fire commemorate a failed plot. It also says British culture is not the same as English culture, an observation that anybody who has tried to explain the difference to a Scot will appreciate. For a social scene built on half-understood references, that matters. You cannot date a country until you know its jokes.

Of the two women profiled, the details point to a hidden infrastructure. Cat Wigley, who has four children and is a qualified primary school teacher in the UK, started her Facebook group in March 2021, and the Rappler piece mentions an NHS home-start volunteer scheme as one of the places new mothers can turn. Viva Andrada runs a cakes and crafts business in Gloucestershire. These are women who arrived for love and built a life from the pieces, and the real romance in the story is not the proposal at Manila Ocean Park. It is the group chat at midnight. Dating abroad ends, for many, as a community project, and the Filipino instinct for building one is the most useful thing in the suitcase.

## The Verdict

The Philippines wins romance, hands down. A culture that treats courtship as an occasion, that expects effort and that makes you feel like the main event is simply better at it than one that asks you to split a bill and apologise for the weather. I am not sentimental, but I can recognise when a society has done the work.

Britain wins on one thing: independence. You can date who you like, when you like, without a committee. For people escaping family expectations, that is a freedom worth having.

The honest verdict is a split. For falling in love, go Filipino. For being left alone afterwards, go British. For both, emigrate carefully and pick your pub.

## Conclusion

Dating abroad is not just about finding a partner. It is about finding a place where you can be a person with a past.

The Philippines gives you a crowd that cares. Britain gives you a pub that does not. The best you can do is keep both: the warmth of the one and the breathing room of the other.

And if you are ever unsure whether it was a date, ask. Just apologise first.
