# One Country Will Correct Your Deadlift Without Being Asked. The Other Will Hand You a Family-Sized Plate of Mansaf Instead.

🇯🇴🇷🇴 Jordan & Romania
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Fitness culture tells you more about a country's relationship with discipline than almost anything else, and Jordan and Romania sit at genuinely opposite poles of that spectrum. Romania has, somewhat improbably, become a serious weightlifting nation, producing gym-goers who treat form correction as a civic responsibility they owe total strangers. Jordan's fitness culture is quieter, more segregated by gender, and routinely interrupted by someone's aunt insisting you eat something before you "waste away" doing all that unnecessary exercise.

I've been unsolicited-coached in a Bucharest gym by a man who simply walked over mid-set to fix my hip hinge, no introduction offered or required. I've also been gently but firmly discouraged from a sunset run in Amman by a shopkeeper who seemed personally affronted that I would choose cardio over his baklava. Both experiences taught me something real about how each country understands the body.

## Do's & Don'ts: Jordan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Seek out women-only gym hours or dedicated women's gyms if that matters to you — they exist and are popular | Don't assume mixed-gender gyms operate the way they do at home; many are genuinely segregated by design |
| Dress modestly even in gym settings, especially outside the big international hotel chains | Don't be surprised if your trainer insists on a long rest between sets to "talk properly" — it's hospitality, not laziness |
| Try a public park workout at dawn or dusk when the heat breaks — it's a genuinely social scene | Don't skip the post-workout snack offered by a new acquaintance; declining food here reads as a small rejection |
| Ask locals which gym chains are actually serious vs. purely social clubs — the difference is real | Don't expect 24-hour gyms outside Amman's wealthier neighbourhoods — opening hours are still fairly traditional |

## Do's & Don'ts: Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect unsolicited form corrections from strangers — it's meant as genuine kindness, not condescension | Don't wear headphones so loud you can't hear someone offering a spotter — it will be read as rudeness |
| Learn basic lifting terminology in Romanian — the gym crowd takes technique seriously and respects effort to engage properly | Don't hog equipment during peak hours (6-9pm) — supersetting on three machines at once will get pointed looks |
| Try a sala de forță (strength gym) rather than a glossy chain if you want the real, serious culture | Don't assume cardio-only is the norm — Romania's gym culture leans heavily toward strength and powerlifting |
| Re-rack your weights — this is non-negotiable and will be enforced, politely but firmly | Don't take the direct, blunt feedback personally — it's a compliment that you're worth correcting |

## Jordan: Fitness as a Social Negotiation

Amman's fitness scene has grown substantially over the past decade, but it still operates within a framework shaped heavily by gender norms and hospitality culture, both of which take precedence over whatever workout plan you arrived with. Women-only gyms and dedicated hours are widespread and genuinely well-used — far from a niche accommodation, they're often the default option, staffed and designed specifically for a clientele that wants to train without a mixed-gender audience. For men, the mixed gym scene is more developed, particularly in international hotel chains and newer commercial gyms in West Amman, but even there, the atmosphere tends toward sociable rather than silently grinding through a programme.

Public space exercise has its own rhythm, dictated almost entirely by climate. Serious outdoor activity happens at dawn or after sunset, when the heat has broken; the hours in between are simply not compatible with vigorous movement, and anyone insisting otherwise is usually a tourist who hasn't yet learned better. Parks like Al Hussein in Amman fill with walkers, joggers, and informal exercise groups in those cooler windows, and the atmosphere is markedly communal — people greet each other, check in, occasionally fall into step together for a lap.

What trips up newcomers most is the hospitality override: any sign of physical effort near someone's home, shop, or family gathering tends to trigger an immediate, well-meaning intervention involving food. Refusing it outright is considered mildly insulting. The workaround most expats eventually learn is to accept a small amount graciously and finish the workout later — a compromise that took me embarrassingly long to figure out.

## Romania: The Gym as a Meritocracy of Iron

Romania's fitness culture, particularly around strength training and powerlifting, punches dramatically above what its size would suggest, and the atmosphere inside a serious sala de forță reflects that seriousness completely. Form is taken as a matter of near-moral importance; a stranger correcting your squat depth mid-set isn't an intrusion, it's considered basic gym citizenship, the same way you'd point out spinach in someone's teeth. Expect bluntness. Expect it to be useful anyway.

Unlike the glossier international chains that have arrived in Bucharest and Cluj in recent years, the older-school gyms retain a stripped-down, function-over-form atmosphere: free weights dominate, machines are secondary, and the social hierarchy runs almost entirely on visible competence rather than friendliness. Re-racking weights isn't merely etiquette here; neglecting it will earn you a direct correction delivered with zero diplomatic softening, because efficiency and respect for shared equipment outrank your feelings about being told off by a stranger.

Peak hours, roughly six to nine in the evening after the workday ends, turn every serious gym into a genuinely competitive environment — not aggressive exactly, but intensely focused, with an unspoken scoreboard running at all times. Cardio exists but occupies a distinctly secondary cultural position; the real currency is strength numbers, and people will absolutely ask what you're lifting, expecting a real answer.

## The Verdict

Jordan treats fitness as something that must coexist peacefully with hospitality, modesty, and climate — a holistic, socially embedded practice rather than an isolated pursuit. Romania treats fitness as a discipline with its own internal logic, where strangers correct your form because competence is a shared communal resource worth protecting. One asks you to slow down and accept a plate of food; the other asks you to add weight to the bar and stop making excuses.

I'm giving this one to Romania, and it's not close. A culture that has organically produced this much genuine expertise in barbell technique, sustained entirely bottom-up rather than imported from a fitness influencer, deserves real respect — even if it means getting unsolicited coaching from a man built like a filing cabinet while you're just trying to get through leg day in peace.

## Conclusion

What strikes me most, looking back at both, is how little either country's fitness culture is actually about fitness in isolation. Jordan folds exercise into a web of social obligation, modesty, and hospitality that a solo gym rat would find constraining and a genuinely social person would find wonderful. Romania folds it into a meritocratic, almost monastic devotion to getting stronger, where feelings are optional but competence is mandatory. Show up in either country expecting to simply put headphones in and disappear into your own workout, and you'll quickly discover that nobody asked for your isolation — they asked for your deadlift numbers, or your acceptance of a second helping. Choose accordingly.
