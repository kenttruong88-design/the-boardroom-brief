# Nairobi Guards Its Green Space Like a Vault. Kathmandu Just Points You at a Mountain.

🇰🇪 Kenya vs 🇳🇵 Nepal — Parks and Green Space Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nairobi is, technically, one of the only capital cities on earth with a national park inside its municipal boundaries — lions, giraffes, the works, a twenty-minute drive from a Zoom call — and yet getting genuine, unpoliced access to green space in the city itself has become an actual civic battle, with community groups fighting land grabs on public parks in real time. Kathmandu, meanwhile, has almost no equivalent urban park culture to speak of — the city itself is dense, chaotic, poorly served by planned green space — but hands you the entire Himalayan foothill system within a bus ride, no application required.

Kenya makes you fight for green space inside the city and gifts you an actual safari on the edge of it. Nepal gives you almost nothing manicured and hands you, instead, the single greatest hiking access of any country I've lived in. Different bargains entirely.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇰🇪 Kenya**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit Karura Forest and Nairobi National Park regularly — they're genuinely world-class assets | Assume every green space marked on a map is still protected; land-grab disputes are real and ongoing |
| Support community conservation groups if you're able — public pressure has repeatedly saved parkland | Wander unmarked forest trails alone at dusk; go with a group or guide, especially in Karura |
| Time park visits for early morning — heat, crowds, and wildlife activity all favor it | Expect the same manicured, benches-and-playgrounds park experience common in Europe or the US |
| Budget properly for Nairobi National Park entry fees — pricing tiers differ for residents vs tourists | Treat the park boundary casually; this is a functioning wildlife reserve, not a themed backdrop |

**🇳🇵 Nepal**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a proper trekking permit (TIMS/conservation area permits) before heading into the hills | Assume Kathmandu itself offers much reliable urban green space — it largely doesn't |
| Check air quality before planning an outdoor day in the valley — it varies sharply by season | Underestimate altitude effects even on "easy" day hikes near the valley rim |
| Hire local guides for serious treks — it supports the local economy and genuinely improves safety | Assume infrastructure (marked trails, signage) matches what's typical in Western national parks |
| Escape to the valley rim (Nagarkot, Shivapuri) for a genuine green-space fix close to the city | Expect Kathmandu's urban parks to compensate for the city's density; they largely can't |

## Kenya: A Wildlife Reserve at Your Doorstep, A Fight for the Rest

Nairobi's defining green-space paradox is genuinely unlike anywhere else I've lived: Nairobi National Park, an actual functioning wildlife reserve with lions, rhinos, and giraffes roaming against a backdrop of the city's skyline, sits essentially inside the municipal boundary — one of the only capitals on the planet that can make that claim. It's spectacular, genuinely, and locals treat weekend visits there with a casualness that still stuns newcomers: "we're doing the park this weekend" carries the same energy elsewhere as "we're doing brunch."

But the city's other green spaces tell a rougher story. Karura Forest, an urban forest reserve minutes from downtown, has an actual activist history — it was the site of real physical confrontation in the 1990s when conservationists, led by Nobel laureate Wangari Maathai, fought off attempts to carve it up for private development, and that fight over public green space hasn't fully ended; local conservation groups continue monitoring encroachment on Nairobi's remaining parkland today. What this means practically for an expat is that "going to the park" in Nairobi carries a faint undercurrent most Western park-goers never have to think about: this space exists, in part, because people fought for it, and it could still shrink if nobody's watching.

The upside of all this vigilance is that the spaces that have survived — Karura especially, with its waterfalls, caves, and genuinely well-maintained trail network run partly through community trust management — are excellent, safe (in daylight, in groups), and free of the over-manicured sterility that makes some Western urban parks feel more like municipal furniture than actual nature. You just have to accept that green space here is a maintained privilege, actively defended, rather than a background assumption.

## Nepal: Skip the Park, Take the Mountain

Kathmandu, by contrast, offers almost nothing in the way of conventional urban park culture. The valley is dense, the traffic is relentless, and if you're looking for the tidy, bench-lined municipal park experience common across much of the rest of Asia, you will mostly come up empty — Kathmandu simply wasn't built with that kind of planned green infrastructure in mind, and rapid, largely unplanned urbanization over recent decades has only compressed what little existed.

What Nepal offers instead is something no manicured park could ever compete with: near-immediate access to genuine Himalayan wilderness. Within a single day trip from the city center, you can be on the valley rim at Nagarkot or Shivapuri National Park, staring at a horizon of actual Himalayan peaks. Within a matter of hours further, you're in trekking territory that draws hikers from across the planet specifically because the access is this unglamorous and this easy simultaneously — no elaborate logistics chain required, just a permit, a guide if you're sensible, and a bus ticket.

The tradeoff, and it's a real one, is air quality and infrastructure. The valley's pollution — a genuine, well-documented seasonal problem driven by geography (the bowl-shaped valley trapping emissions) and traffic — means outdoor days in the city itself require actual planning around conditions, something Nairobi's expats rarely have to think about. And the trekking infrastructure, while extensive, doesn't come with the marked-trail, signposted safety net common in Western national parks; altitude, weather, and route-finding are genuinely your own responsibility, mitigated mostly by hiring people who know the terrain.

## The Verdict

Kenya gives you defended, curated green space inside the city and an actual safari on its edge — a package that rewards you the moment you arrive, no planning required. Nepal gives you almost no usable urban green space at all, but compensates with the single most extraordinary hiking access of any country on this list, provided you're willing to plan around air quality and altitude rather than city infrastructure.

If I'm ranking which country's relationship with green space I find more admirable, it's Kenya — there's something genuinely moving about a city that fought, and keeps fighting, to keep its parks intact rather than paving over them for one more development. But if I'm ranking which one gives me the better weekend, Nepal wins outright. A functioning municipal park is nice. A Himalayan ridge line by lunchtime is something else entirely.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com Kenya forum — paraphrased: a new Nairobi resident said they assumed Karura Forest was simply "the local park" until a Kenyan colleague explained its activist history, after which their weekend runs there felt entirely different.*

> *Quora, thread on Nairobi's green reputation — paraphrased: a longtime resident argued Nairobi deserved more credit as a genuinely green African capital, but admitted most of that green space required active community vigilance to stay protected rather than existing as a guaranteed civic amenity.*

> *Expat.com Nepal forum — paraphrased: a newcomer described being blindsided by a week of thick valley smog that made even a short outdoor walk unpleasant, having assumed "Himalayan country" automatically meant clean air everywhere, all the time.*

## Conclusion

Kenya and Nepal both puncture the same lazy assumption — that abundant nature nearby means effortless access to it. Nairobi's green spaces are real, spectacular, and actively defended by people who understand exactly what's at stake if they stop paying attention. Kathmandu's urban core offers you almost nothing manicured, then quietly hands you the Himalayas as compensation. I've had better weekend hikes in Nepal than anywhere else on earth. I've had more meaningful mornings in a Nairobi forest that people once bled to protect. Only one of those experiences comes with a Sunday brunch equivalent, though, and it's not the one at altitude.
