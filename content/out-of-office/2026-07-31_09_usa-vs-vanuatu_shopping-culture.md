# America Will Sell You A Return Policy As Generous As Its Portion Sizes. Vanuatu Will Sell You Whatever Mama Grew This Week.

🇺🇸 USA + 🇻🇺 Vanuatu

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Shopping culture is a surprisingly precise window into a country's relationship with consumption itself, and few pairs illustrate the extremes better than the United States and Vanuatu. America has turned retail into an entertainment category — vast malls, generous return windows, and a national retail infrastructure so aggressively convenient it borders on excess. Vanuatu, a Pacific island nation of roughly 80 islands, runs its commerce through Mama's Markets — open-air stalls run predominantly by women selling whatever was harvested, caught, or made that week.

I've returned a barely-worn coat to an American department store eleven months after buying it, no questions asked, and I've haggled over a single papaya at a Port Vila market with a woman who eventually threw in a free bunch of island cabbage purely because she liked my attempt at Bislama. One system runs on convenience and abundance. The other runs on relationship and what's actually, physically available that day. I know which one made me a better person, however briefly.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇺🇸 USA**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep receipts — return policies are generous but require proof | Expect prices shown to include tax; it's added at checkout |
| Sign up for loyalty apps; discounts are often app-exclusive | Assume "sale" signs mean urgency; they're basically permanent |
| Browse big-box stores for genuinely competitive bulk pricing | Be surprised by the sheer scale — American malls can take a full day |
| Use self-checkout if you're short on time; it's now standard | Expect small, independent shops to match big-box return policies |

**🇻🇺 Vanuatu**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring small cash (vatu); cards are rarely usable at markets | Expect the same produce to be available two days running |
| Haggle respectfully — a little negotiation is expected, not rude | Overbargain aggressively; small margins matter enormously to sellers |
| Arrive early for the best selection — markets sell out fast | Expect refunds or exchanges; market sales are final, always |
| Learn a few words of Bislama; it goes a long way with sellers | Assume city-style retail exists outside Port Vila or Luganville |

## USA: Consumption As A National Pastime

American retail culture has been engineered, quite deliberately, around volume, convenience, and the psychological comfort of always having an exit ramp. The return policy is the clearest expression of this: many major retailers will accept returns weeks or even months after purchase, no real interrogation required, a level of consumer generosity that visitors from almost anywhere else find borderline suspicious. It's not altruism — it's a calculated bet that generous returns drive more overall spending than they cost in refunds — but the effect is a shopping culture with remarkably low purchase anxiety. Buy it now, decide later, is basically the national retail philosophy.

The scale is the other defining feature. American malls and big-box stores are built for a kind of shopping-as-destination experience: food courts, entertainment, parking structures the size of small countries. A "quick errand" to a Target or a Costco routinely turns into a ninety-minute expedition, not because anything is inefficient, but because the sheer breadth of choice invites browsing that wasn't on the original agenda. This abundance is genuinely impressive and, after enough exposure, genuinely exhausting — decision fatigue as a lifestyle.

What trips up visitors most reliably is the tax display quirk: American price tags almost never include sales tax, which is calculated and added at checkout, varying by state and even by city. That $9.99 item becomes something else entirely at the register, and the resulting confusion is one of the most consistent complaints from first-time visitors, right alongside the mild culture shock of a cashier asking "did you find everything okay?" with an intensity usually reserved for medical checkups.

## Vanuatu: Commerce Built On What's Actually There

Vanuatu's shopping culture couldn't be more different, and it starts with the Mama's Market system — open-air markets, run overwhelmingly by women (the "mamas"), selling fruit, vegetables, seafood, and handicrafts sourced directly from their own gardens, boats, or workshops. There's no supply chain smoothing out seasonal gaps here: what's for sale is what grew, what was caught, or what was made this week, full stop. Visit twice in the same fortnight and the entire selection might have completely changed, which is either charming or mildly inconvenient depending on how badly you wanted that specific fruit again.

Haggling exists but operates on a completely different ethical register than, say, a Middle Eastern souk or a Southeast Asian night market. Margins here are genuinely thin, and sellers are often supporting entire extended families off modest daily takings, so aggressive bargaining down to the last vatu is viewed less as savvy and more as faintly graceless. A little good-natured negotiation is fine and expected; grinding someone down over pocket change most tourists won't even notice is not a good look, and locals notice the difference.

Beyond Port Vila and Luganville, retail infrastructure thins out dramatically — many outer islands have no real shops at all, just informal trade and subsistence exchange. This isn't underdevelopment to be pitied, it's a genuinely different, resilient economic model that's operated successfully for generations, and expats who arrive expecting Western-style retail convenience outside the two main towns are consistently, and unnecessarily, disappointed.

## The Verdict

America has optimised shopping into a frictionless, almost limitless experience, at the cost of a certain soullessness — you can buy anything, anytime, from anyone, and connect with none of it. Vanuatu offers the opposite: limited, unpredictable, deeply personal commerce where you genuinely know whose garden your dinner came from. I'll take the papaya and the free cabbage over the eleven-month return window every time, but I'll also concede that when you actually need a specific thing, right now, in a hurry, nowhere on Earth beats American retail's sheer bloody-minded efficiency.

## Conclusion

Two countries, two completely different theories of commerce. America has built an entire culture around eliminating friction from buying things, generous returns and endless choice smoothing over any possible regret. Vanuatu has built its markets around what's genuinely available and who's actually selling it, with no safety net and no pretence of infinite abundance. One will happily take your unwanted purchase back eleven months later. The other will sell you exactly what Mama grew this week, no more, no less, and somehow that feels like the more honest transaction of the two.
