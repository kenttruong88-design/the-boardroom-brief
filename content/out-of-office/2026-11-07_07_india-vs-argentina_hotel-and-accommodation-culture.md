# India's Star Ratings Are a Suggestion. Argentina's Rental Deposits Are a Hostage Negotiation.

🇮🇳 India 🤝 🇦🇷 Argentina
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A five-star hotel in India might genuinely be five-star — or it might be a well-connected property that successfully navigated the classification process years ago and never been reassessed since. Outlook Traveller's own investigation into "who decides a hotel is 5-star" in India lays out a system that's government-administered, infrequently updated, and prone to the kind of gap between official rating and actual, current experience that Fodor's own long-running travel forum threads treat as a running joke among seasoned visitors: check reviews, not stars, is close to universal advice at this point.

Argentina has a completely different accommodation headache, and it has nothing to do with stars. Long-term rentals in Buenos Aires traditionally required a garantía — a local guarantor, often a property-owning Argentine citizen willing to vouch for you financially — a requirement that functionally locked most newly-arrived foreigners out of the regular rental market entirely, pushing them toward a parallel, pricier "temporary" rental sector built specifically around people who can't produce a guarantor. One country's hotel rating system is aspirational. The other country's rental system is a trust exercise you're disqualified from by definition of having just arrived.

## India, Deep Dive

India's hotel classification system is run by the government (through the Ministry of Tourism's Hotel and Restaurant Approval and Classification Committee process, broadly), which is structurally different from the brand-consistency-driven rating systems many international travellers are used to. Outlook Traveller's own deep dive into "who decides a hotel is 5-star" makes clear that classification happens at a point in time, based on a specific inspection, and isn't necessarily revisited on any predictable cycle afterward — meaning a property's star rating can reflect conditions years out of date, for better or worse.

Fodor's Travel Talk Forums have run this exact conversation for years, under thread titles as direct as "Hotel Classifications in India" and "General Question About Hotels in India" — the accumulated community wisdom, built from genuinely extensive first-hand traveller experience, consistently lands on the same advice: treat the star rating as a loose starting point, not a guarantee, and weight recent, specific guest reviews far more heavily. CEIC Data's own industry statistics on guest-arrival distribution by hotel star rating show a market that doesn't cluster tightly around the top tiers the way you might expect if the rating were the primary driver of traveller choice — further evidence that experienced travellers in India have already, collectively, discounted the official system in favour of other signals.

Practically, this means doing a bit more homework than you might elsewhere: confirming specific amenities directly rather than assuming they're implied by rating, checking recency of reviews and photos, and being prepared for real variation in what a given star-tier "means" between a major metro and a smaller city, where the same nominal rating can represent quite different actual standards. Boutique and heritage properties — restored havelis, smaller family-run operations — often sit outside the formal classification system altogether, or carry a lower nominal rating than their actual standard would suggest, precisely because the owners haven't bothered re-applying for a reassessment that costs time and money without obviously improving bookings.

## Argentina, Deep Dive

The garantía system is the single biggest structural obstacle in Argentina's long-term rental market, and it's one most newcomers don't hear about until they're already trying to sign a lease. Traditionally, renting an apartment the standard way required a local guarantor — typically an Argentine citizen who owns property — willing to personally vouch, financially, for the tenant. For an expat with no local network yet, this is close to an impossible bar to clear on arrival, which is precisely why a parallel "temporary rental" market exists: furnished short-to-medium-term apartments, usually listed and priced specifically for people who can't produce a garantía, at a real premium over the standard market.

Expat.com's own Argentina accommodation guide and ExpatArrivals' Buenos Aires-specific guide both treat this as simply the expected first step for most incoming foreigners, not an edge case — you rent in the temporary, guarantor-free market initially, often for months longer than you'd like, while building the local relationships and financial footprint that eventually make the standard market accessible. ExpatsBA's own long-running forum thread on "Advice for Moving to Buenos Aires" reflects this consensus clearly in its accumulated replies: budget for the premium, expect the paperwork and negotiation to be more involved than a typical Western lease, and don't assume you'll crack the standard market in your first few months regardless of your income or creditworthiness back home.

The deeper logic, once you understand it, isn't arbitrary gatekeeping — it reflects a rental market where landlord recourse against a defaulting or damaging tenant is otherwise genuinely limited, so the guarantor exists to backstop risk that a credit-check-and-deposit system might handle differently elsewhere. Some newcomers eventually work around the requirement entirely by paying a specialised garantía-provider company a fee to act as guarantor on their behalf — a now-established, if still relatively costly, formal workaround that several Expat.com threads mention as a faster route into the standard market than waiting to build local credit history from scratch.

## The Verdict

India's hotel system will occasionally oversell you on stars while underselling you on substance — annoying, but recoverable with five minutes of review-reading. Argentina's rental system will simply shut the door on you entirely until you've built enough local standing to open it, which is a bigger, slower problem to solve than checking a review site. If I had to rank the inconvenience, Argentina's is structurally worse — it isn't a trust issue you can research your way around in an afternoon, it's a trust issue you have to spend months earning your way out of.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Fodor's Travel Talk Forums, "Hotel Classifications in India" — seasoned travellers across many years of posts converge on the same core advice: never book purely on the star number, always check what recent guests actually say.*

> *ExpatsBA's "Advice for Moving to Buenos Aires" thread — long-time posters describe the garantía requirement as the first real wall newcomers hit, often before they've even found a specific apartment they like.*

> *Expat.com's Argentina "Renting in Buenos Aires" thread — repeatedly notes that the temporary-rental premium, while real and sometimes resented, is well understood locally as the standard cost of entry for anyone arriving without an existing local network.*

## Conclusion

Both systems are, in their own way, built around a kind of institutional trust that doesn't automatically extend to a newcomer. India's hands you a number that might be outdated and asks you to verify it yourself. Argentina's hands you a closed door and asks you to spend months building the standing to open it. Neither is a conspiracy against foreigners specifically — they're simply the visible seams of two different systems that were never designed with your arrival, this particular week, as the priority case. Plan around both, and neither will ruin your move; ignore either, and you'll learn the hard way, probably in a lobby, possibly at 2am.
