# Seoul Will Organise Your Community Whether You Like It Or Not. Norway Will Let You Rot Indoors For A Decade, And Call It Respect.

🇰🇷 🇳🇴 *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have lived in fourteen cities and can tell you, with the confidence of someone who has been forced to sort her own recycling into eleven categories at 7 a.m. on a Saturday, that "community" means wildly different things depending on which hemisphere your landlord operates in. In Seoul, community is a mandatory participation sport, run by a resident's association with the organisational rigour of a small government and the enforcement powers of your grandmother. In Oslo, community is a rumour. You've heard it exists. You've seen the noticeboard. You have never once been invited to anything, and you never will be, and if you ask why, the silence you receive will itself be the answer.

Both systems produce people who are, in their own way, deeply attached to where they live. One does it through a thousand tiny obligations. The other does it through the sacred, unbothered right to never speak to the man in 4B, ever, even during a fire.

## Do's & Don'ts: South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn your building's recycling schedule immediately — food waste, non-food waste, and general are different days and different bins with different chips | Assume "close enough" sorting is fine; the ajumma monitoring the bins will find you |
| Bow or nod to your building's security guard daily — he is the actual mayor of your apartment complex | Ignore the resident association group chat; missed dues notices escalate fast |
| Bring a small gift when you move in — fabric softener and instant noodles are the classic housewarming combo | Play music or run a washing machine after 10 p.m.; Korean apartment soundproofing is famously bad |
| Join the building's KakaoTalk group even if you don't post — it's where every actual decision gets made | Expect anonymity; in a Seoul apartment block, someone always knows |

## Do's & Don'ts: Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to dugnad (the communal building clean-up day) at least once a year — skipping it is remembered for years | Expect anyone to knock on your door uninvited, ever, for any reason short of a fire |
| Say a clipped "hei" in the stairwell and keep walking — that's the whole interaction, don't extend it | Organise a spontaneous building gathering; Norwegians plan socialising weeks in advance, if at all |
| Respect the borettslag (housing cooperative) rules to the letter — they govern everything from pets to renovations | Take the quiet personally; it's a cultural default, not a verdict on you specifically |

## South Korea: Community As Infrastructure

Seoul's apartment complexes — and most Koreans live in apartment complexes, sprawling numbered towers with names like "Raemian" and "Xi" that sound like luxury sedans — function less like buildings and more like small city-states. There is a resident's association. There are dues. There is a security office that logs every delivery, and a loudspeaker system that will announce, unprompted, that the water will be shut off between 2 and 4 p.m. on Thursday for maintenance, a message that arrives in every unit whether you wanted it or not.

This is not incidental. It's the mechanism through which an enormous, dense, vertically stacked population maintains order without anyone needing to be friends. You don't need to like your neighbours in Seoul. You need to comply with the same system they comply with, and compliance functions as a kind of intimacy substitute. The recycling sorting, done wrong, will get you a sticky note. The parking spot, taken without a permit, will get you towed by consensus. There's a rhythm to it that outsiders find suffocating at first and then, embarrassingly, start to miss when they leave.

Where it gets genuinely warm is the informal layer sitting on top of the bureaucratic one: the corner convenience store owner who starts bagging your usual order before you ask, the neighbourhood market ajumma who slips an extra garlic bulb into your bag, the building's unofficial elder who organises a kimchi-making day every November whether the association approved it or not. Korean community isn't built on small talk. It's built on showing up, repeatedly, in the same fifty square metres of the city, until you are simply accounted for.

## Norway: Community As Boundary Maintenance

Norway's neighbourhood culture runs on the opposite premise: that the highest form of respect you can show another person is to leave them completely, gloriously alone. This is not coldness, Norwegians will tell you, slightly annoyed that you'd even suggest it — it's *courtesy*. Nobody wants to be bothered, so nobody bothers anybody, and everyone gets to enjoy their evening undisturbed by small talk they didn't consent to.

The exception, the one crack in the wall, is dugnad — a peculiarly Norwegian institution where an entire building or street turns out on a Saturday to rake leaves, paint the fence, or fix the shared shed, followed by waffles and weak coffee in the courtyard. It is, for exactly one day a season, mandatory conviviality, and everyone treats it as such: pleasant, obligatory, over by 2 p.m. Skip it without a good excuse and you'll be quietly filed under "that person," a reputation that follows you for the duration of your lease.

Outside of dugnad, the borettslag — Norway's ubiquitous housing cooperative model — handles everything through paperwork, not conversation. Renovation requests, noise complaints, and shared costs are processed via formal notices, not doorstep chats. Expats consistently report the same disorientation: months, sometimes years, of living beside someone without exchanging more than a nod. It's not hostility. It's a culture that has decided solitude is a public good, and your neighbour's silence is a gift they're giving you, not a slight.

## The Verdict

If you want a place that organises itself around you whether you ask for it or not, Korea wins by a landslide — you will never wonder where the community is, because it will be knocking, sorting your recycling for you, and possibly judging your parking. If you want to be left entirely, magnificently alone with the occasional forced waffle, Norway is unmatched. Neither is "friendlier" in the sense a Californian means it. Korea mistakes proximity for intimacy and mostly gets away with it. Norway mistakes distance for respect and mostly gets away with that too. I know which one gets my Christmas card, and it's neither, because I don't send Christmas cards. I send opinions. You're welcome.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/korea — a poster reported living in the same building for two years before learning their downstairs neighbour's name, despite exchanging bows almost daily — the association group chat, they said, felt more intimate than any face-to-face conversation.*

> *Reddit r/Norway — an expat described dugnad as "the one day a year Norwegians remember they have neighbours," and said the rest of the year the stairwell was silent enough to hear a pin drop.*

> *expat.com Oslo — a longtime resident warned newcomers not to mistake the quiet for unfriendliness, noting it took three years and one dramatic pipe burst before a neighbour finally initiated a real conversation.*

## Conclusion

Somewhere between Seoul's loudspeaker announcements and Oslo's stairwell silence is a version of community most people would actually choose, and neither country has built it, because neither country was trying to. Korea built a machine for managing density. Norway built a shrine to personal space. Both work, spectacularly, on their own terms, and both will make you insane if you arrive expecting the other one. My advice, for what it's worth: learn the recycling chart in Seoul, learn the dugnad date in Oslo, and stop expecting either city to hug you. It won't. It'll just make sure you're accounted for, or make sure it never has to find out your name. Pick your poison.
