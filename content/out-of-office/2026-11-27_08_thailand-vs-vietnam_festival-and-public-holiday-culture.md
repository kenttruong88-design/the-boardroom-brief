# Thailand Soaks the New Year and Vietnam Sweeps It Home

🇹🇭 Thailand vs 🇻🇳 Vietnam

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every culture has a moment when the year turns, and every culture has an opinion on how loudly to do it. Thailand's opinion is that you should be drenched. Vietnam's opinion is that you should be at your mother's house, in your best clothes, being asked about your career and your marital status.

These are two of the great festival cultures of Southeast Asia, and an expat in either will discover that public holidays are not a day off but a total rewrite of how the country functions. Plan accordingly, or find yourself standing in a closed city holding a very wet phone.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇹🇭 Thailand ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Put your phone and documents in a waterproof pouch during Songkran | Walk to the office in a good suit in mid-April |
| Accept a dab of white powder or a splash with a smile | Throw water at monks, the elderly or people on motorbikes |
| Join a temple visit or a merit-making ceremony | Treat the holiday as only a street party |
| Book transport and accommodation well in advance | Assume you can travel upcountry on the day |

| 🇻🇳 Vietnam ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stock up on groceries and cash before Tet begins | Expect restaurants and shops to be open in the first days |
| Bring a gift when visiting someone's home, such as fruit or sweets | Arrive empty-handed |
| Give lucky money in red envelopes to children | Give money in unlucky amounts or colours |
| Dress well and greet elders first | Wear black or white on the first days of the year |

## 🇹🇭 Thailand

Songkran, the Thai New Year in mid-April, is the world's most cheerful water fight. What began as a gentle ritual of pouring scented water over the hands of elders has become a nationwide splashing festival. Streets close, pickup trucks carry barrels, and children, tourists and grandparents alike march around with water guns and buckets. In Bangkok, Chiang Mai and Phuket it is a three-day riot of good humour, ice water and talcum powder.

The surprise for outsiders is the depth beneath the splash. Many Thais return to their home province to see their families, visit temples, make merit, and pour water over the hands of elders as a sign of respect. The cities empty out, and the long weekend becomes one of the great annual migrations. The party is real, but so is the tradition.

Other festivals sit in the calendar too. Loy Krathong, in the autumn, sends little floating baskets onto rivers by moonlight and is one of the loveliest sights in the country. The King's birthday and various Buddhist holy days shape the year, with alcohol bans on some, and everyone is expected to behave.

For an expat, the lesson is to join, within reason. You will get wet. Your phone will suffer if you are careless. But Songkran is also the easiest way to be absorbed into Thai life, because nobody can stay aloof when a six-year-old hits them with a water pistol.

Practical advice for Songkran is simple: pick your location with care. Chiang Mai and Khao San Road in Bangkok are famous for large crowds and intense water battles, while smaller towns and temple celebrations are gentler. Wear clothes that dry fast, protect your valuables, and remember that the holiday is also a time of respect for elders. You can enjoy the splashing and still attend a temple, which is exactly what many Thais do.

## 🇻🇳 Vietnam

Tet, the Lunar New Year, is the centrepiece of the Vietnamese calendar, and it behaves less like a holiday and more like a national reset. For about a week, the country slows down. Factories close, cities empty out, and tens of millions of people head home. The streets of Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, normally a roaring river of scooters, go suddenly and unnervingly quiet.

The preparation begins weeks in advance. Houses are cleaned, debts are paid, and families buy peach blossom or kumquat trees to decorate the home. Special foods appear, including sticky rice cakes called banh chung, and people visit ancestors' altars with offerings. Children receive lucky money in red envelopes, and everyone dresses in their best.

The first days of the year are about family and superstition. The first visitor of the year is thought to determine the family's luck, so hosts are careful about who walks through the door first. Quarrels are avoided, sweeping is frowned upon, and the mood is one of hope. Many shops and restaurants close for days, and the ones that stay open charge a premium.

For expats, Tet is both a gift and a logistical test. It is a chance to see a deeply personal side of Vietnam, if you are invited. It is also a very good reason to buy food in advance and to book your own holiday elsewhere. Trying to find a taxi or a decent lunch in the first days is a lesson in humility.

Another feature of Tet is the way it shapes the working calendar. Many workers return to their home provinces, and cities that normally never sleep go quiet for a week or more. Businesses reopen gradually, and expats should expect a slow return to normal in the following days. Prices rise for services and travel, and the final days before the holiday are a frenzy of shopping, cooking and getting home. It is a beautiful and exhausting season.

## The Verdict

Thailand wins as a party and Vietnam wins as a ritual. If you want the most joyful public celebration on Earth, with a chance of laughing until you cry, Songkran is the one. If you want a window into the heart of a culture, with family, memory and superstition running through every detail, Tet is deeper and harder to access.

I would send a first-time visitor to Thailand and an experienced traveller to Vietnam. One teaches you how to enjoy a country. The other teaches you why its people love it. And if you are an expat with the choice, do both, and carry a dry bag for the first and a full fridge for the second.

Both festivals ask something of you. Songkran asks you to be open, playful and slightly vulnerable. Tet asks you to be patient, respectful and a good guest. The expat who can do both will have a richer experience than the one who retreats to a hotel with the curtains closed.

A practical word on money. Prices for rooms and transport climb around both holidays, and booking early is not a luxury but a necessity. Banks, offices and government services close, so complete your paperwork before the break. Bring cash for small vendors, since card machines and apps may be unreliable in the busiest places. These details sound dull until you are standing at a closed counter on the first day of the new year.

There is a small lesson in how each country treats its guests. Thai hosts will often pull you into a celebration with a laugh and a bowl of food, assuming you want to join. Vietnamese hosts are more likely to offer a chair, a tea and a plate of sweets, and expect you to wait to be invited further. Both are forms of hospitality, and both reward an expat who is gracious about it.

## Conclusion

Festivals are where a country shows you what it cares about. Thailand cares about joy, cleansing and communal silliness. Vietnam cares about family, ancestry and the quiet hope of a better year.

If you are in either country when the new year comes, take part. Get soaked in April. Stay in with the family in February. And in both cases, ask the locals what you are meant to do next, because they will tell you gladly, and then hand you something to eat.

Whatever you do, say the greeting. A good new year wish in the local language is worth a dozen guidebooks.
