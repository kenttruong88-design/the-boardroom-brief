# The Philippines Speaks English With a Smile and Singapore Speaks It With a Lah

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is a language problem that barely counts as one: two Asian countries where English is everywhere, and where the arriving expat is still lost within an hour. The Philippines hands you English with a warm, rounded accent and a ready supply of Tagalog on the side. Singapore hands you English that has been through a blender with Hokkien, Malay and a little Tamil, and reassembled itself as something quicker, shorter and far more efficient.

Neither country is hard in the way that Japan is hard. You will not need an interpreter. What you will need is an ear, because the barrier here is not vocabulary but music. Words you know arrive in shapes you do not, and the person across the table is not confused by you at all. They are amused.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Philippines**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn a few Tagalog courtesies and use them warmly | Assume English gets you everything in the provinces |
| Listen for the mix of English and Tagalog in a sentence | Panic when a conversation switches language mid-flow |
| Smile and ask politely when you do not understand | Answer confusion with a louder voice |
| Expect "po" and "opo" as markers of respect | Treat formality as stiffness |

**Singapore**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Listen first and learn the common particles | Imitate Singlish at the table in your first week |
| Keep your sentences short and clear in a meeting | Assume Singlish at lunch is how the office writes email |
| Ask for clarification without embarrassment | Nod politely and then miss the instruction |
| Notice who switches register and when | Think the code switch is a sign of disrespect |

## Philippines

The Philippines is one of the largest English-speaking countries in the world, and the Filipino relationship with the language is warm, fluent and more complicated than it looks. English is an official language, used in law, business, education and media, and the country's call centre industry is built on the premise that Filipino English is clear, friendly and well understood. A professional expat will find that meetings, contracts, signs and menus are in English almost everywhere in the major cities.

What surprises newcomers is the mix. Filipinos switch between English and Tagalog in the middle of a sentence, a habit often called Taglish, and the switch carries meaning: shifts of tone, familiarity, humour. A forum on Expat.com devoted to language barriers in the Philippines makes the practical point that English helps in the cities and with educated speakers, but outside them, or with some older people and service staff, a little Tagalog or the regional language makes a real difference. The country has more than a hundred languages in total, so the regional picture is complex.

Courtesy is a language of its own. The little words "po" and "opo" signal respect to elders and superiors, and a foreigner who uses them earns a smile far out of proportion to the effort. Indirectness is also common: a Filipino will often avoid saying a flat no, preferring a soft hedge that means no to anyone who knows the code. The expat who hears "maybe" and books a celebration is making an understandable mistake.

The pleasure of the Philippines is that the language barrier feels more like a handshake than a wall. People are curious, patient and happy to help, and a mistake in a Tagalog phrase becomes a shared joke instead of a humiliation. The frustration is in the details: accents, speed, borrowed phrases and the occasional word that looks English but behaves differently. A little humility goes a very long way.

## Singapore

Singapore sounds easier on paper. English is the working language of government, business and schools, and the four official languages are English, Mandarin, Malay and Tamil. A foreigner can live, work and shop in English without ever opening a phrasebook. And then you get on the train and someone says, "Can, lah," and the whole structure of your confidence quietly collapses.

That is Singlish, more formally Singapore Colloquial English, a creole-like variety that borrows grammar and words from Hokkien, Malay, Cantonese and Tamil and strips the sentence down to its working parts. A guide such as the Culture Trip article on expat struggles in Singapore points out that newcomers find the local speech fast, clipped and full of particles that carry tone and attitude instead of dictionary meaning. A "lah", a "leh", a "lor" or a "meh" at the end of a sentence can turn a statement into a suggestion, a doubt or a gentle rebuke.

The official line is that the government encourages standard English in schools and the workplace, and Singaporeans can switch smoothly between a formal register and a casual one. That switch is the thing a newcomer has to learn. In a meeting with the regional director, the English is crisp and standard. At the hawker centre at lunchtime, it dissolves into something quicker and more local. Neither is wrong; the skill is knowing which room you are in.

For a Filipino expat, Singapore is an interesting case. Many Filipinos arrive with strong English and find the transition easier than most, and the TripZilla piece on the similarities between Singaporeans and Filipinos plays on the shared warmth, humour and love of food. But the speed and the shorthand can still catch you off guard, and a Filipino accent is occasionally the subject of an unthinking joke. Nobody is immune from small awkward moments at the bus stop.

There is also a quieter truth that expats rarely say aloud. Being fluent in the official language does not make you fluent in the room. In both countries, I have watched perfectly competent English speakers lose the thread of a conversation because they were listening to the words instead of the tune. The meaning lives in the tone, the pause, the particle at the end of a sentence, the softness of a refusal. That is the real language barrier, and no phrasebook covers it. The cure is boring and effective: spend time with locals, laugh at your own mistakes, ask what a phrase actually means and then use it badly until someone corrects you. It is humbling and it works faster than any course, and it makes you a better listener in your own language too.

## The Verdict

The Philippines wins on warmth, and Singapore wins on efficiency. If you want to be forgiven for your mistakes, take the Philippines. People will meet your bad Tagalog with delight and your confusion with patience. If you want to understand how a modern, multilingual, brisk city actually talks, Singapore will teach you faster, with less forgiveness.

For an English-speaking expat, the Philippines is the gentler landing. The language is closer to the one you studied, the culture of politeness helps you recover from a slip, and the pace gives you time to think. Singapore asks you to keep up. It is not unfriendly, but it is quick, and the person behind you in the queue is already waiting.

My advice is the same for both. Listen before you speak. Learn the particles, the respectful words and the rhythm. The language barrier here is not a wall. It is a door with a very complicated handle.

## Conclusion

A language barrier is a country's way of telling you that vocabulary was never the point. The Philippines teaches you that courtesy is a grammar. Singapore teaches you that brevity is a form of respect. Between them, you will learn more about English than any grammar book ever offered.

If you are moving from the Philippines to Singapore, speed up, trim your sentences and enjoy the shorthand. If you are going the other way, slow down, add a "po" and let the conversation wander. In both places, the real fluency is not in the words but in knowing when to smile and when to say lah.
