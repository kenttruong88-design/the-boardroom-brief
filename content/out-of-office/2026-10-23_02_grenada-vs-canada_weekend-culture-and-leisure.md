# Two Ways to Waste a Weekend, Both Fully Endorsed by Local Governments

🇬🇩 Grenada 🇨🇦 Canada
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Grenada and Canada have, between them, produced two of the most sincerely committed weekend cultures I have encountered in sixty countries of eating other people's regional specialties and judging their public transport. One nation has built its entire social contract around something called "liming," which is the art of doing absolutely nothing with maximum enthusiasm. The other has built its weekend around driving four hours to a lake, in a car packed to the gunwales with gear, specifically so it can do the same amount of nothing, but colder, and after far more admin.

What unites them, bizarrely, is total sincerity. Nobody in Grenada apologises for spending six hours under a mango tree with a rum punch and zero agenda. Nobody in Canada apologises for owning more outdoor equipment than most national armies, purely to sit at a cottage and stare at the same lake they stared at last weekend. Both consider this a complete and satisfying life. I have opinions about both, and as always, they are correct.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇬🇩 Grenada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept any invitation to "lime," even if you don't know what's involved | Schedule anything specific for a Sunday, nothing starts on time and nobody minds |
| Bring something to share, rum or otherwise, it's the price of entry | Rush a beach day, there is no version of this island that rewards urgency |
| Learn the difference between a lime, a limin', and a full-blown jump-up | Call it "chilling," locals will correct you, it is a far more serious pursuit than that |

**🇨🇦 Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept a cottage invitation the moment it is offered, they are rationed socially | Show up without bug spray in summer, you will be eaten alive and mocked for it |
| Learn to love a Timbit run as a legitimate weekend activity | Assume winter cancels the weekend, it just relocates it to an ice rink or a trail |
| Bring your own firewood or beer, cottage economics are strict and unspoken | Underestimate how seriously a "quick portage" can ruin your Saturday |

## Grenada

The entire island appears to operate on the principle that the weekend begins the moment you decide it has, which in practice means sometime around Thursday afternoon. Liming, for the uninitiated, is a scheduled, cherished, almost liturgical form of hanging out — under a tree, on a wall, by the water, wherever — that requires no destination and no itinerary, only company and the correct ratio of rum to conversation. I was baffled by this for approximately four days before surrendering entirely and becoming, within a month, insufferably evangelical about it to anyone back in London who still thought "going out" meant booking a table.

Grenadian weekends have a rhythm built around the beach, the church, and the family, usually in that order on a Sunday, and the pace is non-negotiable. Trying to hurry a Grenadian weekend is like trying to hurry the tide — it will simply ignore you and continue at the speed it has always moved. The famous Grand Anse Beach on a Saturday is less a tourist attraction than a national living room, packed with extended families who have clearly been coming to the exact same spot for three generations and have the folding chairs to prove it.

What struck me most, after fourteen relocations, is how little Grenadian leisure depends on spending money. A fish fry in Gouyave on a Friday night costs almost nothing and delivers more genuine joy per dollar than any brunch I've paid forty pounds for in a city that thinks avocado is a personality. The island does not need you to buy an experience. It assumes, correctly, that sitting still with good people and good food already is one.

There's also a seasonal rhythm to all this that outsiders rarely clock on arrival: Carnival season turns the whole liming instinct up to full volume, with entire communities rehearsing soca routines and mas costumes for months beforehand, treating the weekend run-up to Spicemas with the same seriousness other cultures reserve for a major sporting final. Even outside Carnival, a Sunday lime after church has its own unhurried choreography — roti, a game of dominoes played loudly enough to be heard from the street, and conversation that drifts for hours without anyone checking a watch. Visitors who try to compress this into a single tidy afternoon activity miss the point entirely; the lime isn't a block of time you schedule around other things, it's the thing itself, and treating it as a side activity is the fastest way to be gently, kindly corrected by everyone present.

## Canada

Canadian weekend culture is, by contrast, a logistics operation disguised as relaxation. The cottage is not a holiday home, it is an ideology, often passed down through generations with the solemnity of a land title and the maintenance schedule of a small aircraft. Friday afternoon traffic leaving any major Canadian city toward "cottage country" is a phenomenon that should be studied by behavioural economists, because it involves millions of people voluntarily sitting in gridlock for hours to reach a destination whose entire appeal is the absence of traffic.

Winter does not cancel any of this, it simply redirects the same energy indoors or onto ice. Canadians will drive an hour to a specific hill for specific snow conditions with the same gravity Grenadians reserve for a specific beach. The weekend hockey rink, the cross-country ski trail, the toboggan hill packed with children and at least one adult taking it far too seriously — all of it operates on an unspoken rule that leisure must involve at least mild physical discomfort to properly count. A Canadian weekend without numb fingers at some point feels, to many Canadians, slightly unearned.

And yet underneath all the gear and the planning is something genuinely lovely: an entire culture that has decided unstructured time with family and nature is worth real effort and real money to protect, even when the nature in question is actively trying to freeze you. I respect the commitment. I simply think it would benefit enormously from a mango tree.

The provincial variation adds its own layer of chaos to all this earnest effort — a long weekend that triggers a mass cottage exodus in Ontario might pass almost unmarked in Alberta, and visitors are routinely baffled by how differently "the lake" is treated depending on which province they've landed in. Then there's the specific Canadian ritual of the potluck, which turns up at nearly every weekend gathering with an unspoken, fiercely competitive etiquette: bring something homemade, never store-bought, and never, under any circumstances, show up with "just drinks" unless you enjoy being remembered for it unfavourably for years. Combine the cottage, the potluck, and the seasonal gear rotation, and you start to see a weekend culture that isn't really about relaxation at all — it's about doing a very specific, very communal kind of work, cheerfully, because the alternative is admitting the long winter got to you.

## The Verdict

Grenada wins on pure, unbothered quality of rest — nobody does nothing as well, or as proudly, as a Grenadian on a Sunday. Canada wins on sheer range, managing to make winter, summer, lake, and ice rink all serve the same core goal of getting people outside and off their phones. If forced to choose, I'll take the lime over the cottage, because one requires a folding chair and the other requires a mortgage-adjacent conversation about dock repairs. But credit where it's due: both nations have correctly identified that doing nothing, properly, is a skill, and both have built an entire culture around protecting the right to practise it.

## Conclusion

Both places have quietly perfected the art of the deliberate weekend, just via opposite climates and opposite philosophies — one built on stillness, the other on strenuous, cheerful effort. Spend enough time in either and you'll stop seeing leisure as something to fit around work and start seeing it, correctly, as the entire point. Grenada will teach you to slow down. Canada will teach you to suffer slightly for your fun and call it a good time. Honestly, between the two, I'm not sure which lesson has done more damage to my old idea of a "quiet weekend in," and I regret nothing.
