# Finns Won't Speak To You On The Tram. Kenyans Will Greet You Like Family Within Minutes.

🇫🇮 Finland + 🇰🇪 Kenya

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Stand at a Helsinki tram stop and you will witness one of the most disciplined displays of personal space management on the planet — Finns spacing themselves along the platform with the precision of a military drill, silent, eyes forward, and visibly relieved when nobody attempts conversation. Stand at a Nairobi matatu stage for the same amount of time and you will likely be greeted, asked where you're from, offered directions you didn't request, and possibly invited to someone's cousin's wedding before the vehicle even arrives.

I've spent long enough in both countries to say with total confidence that these are not accidents of temperament — they are deeply held, culturally coherent philosophies about what public space is for. Finland believes it's for quiet coexistence. Kenya believes it's for connection. Both are right, and both will initially feel completely alien to anyone raised on a diet of polite, forgettable small talk.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇫🇮 Finland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Maintain a generous arm's length of personal space at all times, always | Sit next to a stranger on an empty bus if other seats are free |
| Respect silence as the default; let others initiate conversation | Make prolonged eye contact with strangers — it reads as confrontational |
| Queue with exact, evenly-spaced precision, even with just two people | Comment on someone's day, weather, or appearance unprompted — it's intrusive |
| Wait for a natural pause before speaking in any group setting | Assume quietness means unfriendliness; it's simply the cultural default |

**🇰🇪 Kenya**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet everyone — shopkeepers, matatu conductors, strangers on the street | Skip the greeting and go straight into a request; it reads as rude |
| Expect and return warm, extended handshakes, often with the left hand supporting | Rush a conversation; a bit of small talk before business is expected |
| Use respectful terms for elders — "mzee" or "mama" as appropriate | Take offence at direct personal questions about age, marriage, or income |
| Join in when a matatu's music or conversation includes you — it's a compliment | Stay silent and isolated in a group setting; it can read as standoffish |

## Finland: The World's Most Committed Introverts

Finnish public behaviour operates on an unwritten but universally honoured rule: your personal space is sacred, silence is comfortable rather than awkward, and initiating unsolicited conversation with a stranger is viewed roughly the way most cultures view an unsolicited hug. The famous bus-stop photos of Finns standing an implausible distance apart aren't a stereotype exaggerated for comic effect — I've watched it happen in real time, an entire platform of people arranging themselves with the evenly-spaced precision of a Mondrian painting, nobody making eye contact, nobody remotely bothered by the silence.

What took me the longest to understand is that this isn't coldness — Finns will talk your ear off once a real connection is established, usually somewhere involving a sauna and considerable trust-building. Public space, though, is treated as a shared resource to be used with minimal imposition on others, which means no loud phone calls, no unsolicited chat, and a level of queuing discipline that borders on the spiritual — I've seen two people form an orderly, evenly spaced line at an empty bus stop, which is either deeply reassuring or a little bit funny depending on your mood that day. Visitors who mistake this reserve for unfriendliness miss the point entirely; it's a form of respect, just one expressed through absence rather than warmth.

## Kenya: Every Interaction Starts With A Greeting

Kenyan public life runs on the opposite assumption entirely: that space is shared, connection is the default, and skipping a proper greeting before getting to business is a genuine social misstep, not a minor one. Walk into a shop, board a matatu, or stop a stranger for directions, and the exchange will almost always open with "Habari?" or "Sasa?" — a greeting ritual that isn't optional small talk but the actual social glue holding the interaction together. Get straight to your request without it, as I did more than once in my first week, and you'll be met with a polite but unmistakable pause that tells you exactly what you did wrong.

Matatus — the privately run minibuses that form the backbone of Kenyan urban transport — are their own entire social universe, decorated with elaborate art, blasting music, and often doubling as an impromptu community space where conversation between strangers is completely normal and frequently entertaining. Personal questions that would raise eyebrows elsewhere — your age, your marital status, your salary — are asked with genuine curiosity rather than rudeness, and the expected response is equally direct rather than evasive. Elders are addressed with real deference, using terms like "mzee" for an older man or "mama" for an older woman, a linguistic habit that signals respect instantly and is noticed immediately when it's missing. The overall effect, after Finland, felt like emerging from a library into a wedding reception, and it took me a genuinely disorienting few days to recalibrate.

## The Verdict

Finland offers the rare, quietly luxurious experience of being left completely alone in public, which after a long trip is its own kind of relief. Kenya offers near-constant human connection, which after a lonely stretch of travel is exactly the antidote you didn't know you needed. Neither is better — they're simply solving for different things, one for peace, one for belonging. If I had to choose which one made me a better traveller, though, it's Kenya, without question — it forced me to actually talk to people again, a skill Finland had let me happily forget.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Kenya — paraphrased: forgot to greet a shopkeeper properly before asking for directions, got a noticeably cooler response than my friend who greeted first. Lesson learned fast.*

> *expat.com Nairobi — paraphrased: got asked my salary by a stranger on a matatu within the first five minutes of meeting them, still not used to how normal that question is here.*

## Conclusion

Finland will teach you to treasure silence and respect the invisible bubble around every stranger you pass. Kenya will teach you that a greeting isn't a formality, it's the actual foundation of every functioning interaction, and skipping it costs you more than you'd think. Go to Finland if you want to disappear into a crowd. Go to Kenya if you want the crowd to notice you, greet you properly, and possibly ask about your marital status before the matatu's even left the stage. Both will recalibrate your sense of what public space is actually for, and neither will apologise for it.
