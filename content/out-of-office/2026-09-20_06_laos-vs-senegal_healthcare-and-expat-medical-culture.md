# One Country Wants You to Have a Medevac Plan Before You've Even Landed. The Other Has a Genuinely Good Private Hospital, Provided You Can Actually Get to Dakar in Time

🇱🇦 Laos vs 🇸🇳 Senegal — Healthcare and Expat Medical Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nothing sharpens a traveller's relationship with a country quite like discovering, mid-trip, exactly how far the nearest competent hospital actually is. Laos and Senegal both ask foreigners to think seriously about healthcare logistics before arrival, but for different reasons: Laos because its public system is genuinely under-resourced and serious cases routinely get evacuated to Thailand, and Senegal because Dakar itself has solid private options, but the moment you're anywhere outside the capital, the safety net thins dramatically.

I have watched a fellow traveller in Luang Prabang quietly, calmly arrange a medevac flight to Bangkok for what turned out to be a manageable but not locally treatable issue, and I have been genuinely impressed by a private clinic in Dakar that handled a minor emergency with more efficiency than several European hospitals I could name. Preparation, in both places, is not optional.

## Do's & Don'ts: Laos

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy travel insurance that explicitly includes medevac coverage to Thailand — this is a genuine, expected necessity | Assume Lao public hospitals can handle anything beyond basic care; serious cases are routinely referred abroad |
| Identify the nearest private clinic in Vientiane or Luang Prabang before you need one | Wait to research medical options until you're already unwell — connectivity and access can be limited |
| Carry a basic first-aid kit and any regular medications with you — pharmacy stock outside major towns is inconsistent | Rely on rural or provincial facilities for anything beyond minor injuries or common illness |

## Do's & Don'ts: Senegal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use Dakar's reputable private clinics and hospitals — several offer genuinely solid, internationally comparable care | Assume the same standard of care exists outside Dakar; regional facilities vary enormously |
| Get comprehensive international health insurance, ideally with evacuation coverage for serious cases | Rely solely on public hospitals, which are often under-resourced and overcrowded |
| Keep a stocked personal medical kit for travel outside the capital, especially to rural regions | Delay treatment hoping symptoms resolve — private care in Dakar is accessible and worth using promptly |

## Laos: A Country That's Honest About Its Own Limits

Laos's public healthcare system remains genuinely under-resourced relative to its neighbours, a reality the government and most informed expats are refreshingly upfront about rather than trying to paper over. Public hospitals, even in the capital Vientiane, are generally reserved for basic care, and anything more serious — major surgery, complex diagnostics, significant trauma — typically gets referred onward, most commonly to Thailand, which has become the de facto regional medical hub for exactly this reason. This isn't a hidden inconvenience; it's baked into how both locals with means and virtually every long-term expat plan their healthcare from day one.

Private clinics exist in Vientiane and, to a lesser degree, Luang Prabang, and they handle a meaningful range of everyday issues competently, but they too tend to have a working relationship with the medevac system for anything beyond their scope, rather than pretending otherwise. This makes travel insurance with explicit medevac coverage to Thailand not a nice-to-have but close to a baseline requirement for any foreigner spending real time in the country, a point every informed expat forum thread on the subject makes within the first few replies.

Outside the two main population centres, the picture thins further still — provincial and rural healthcare access can be minimal, pharmacy stock unreliable, and the practical reality is that serious travel or long-term stays in more remote parts of Laos require carrying a genuinely well-stocked personal medical kit and accepting a higher baseline of self-reliance than most Western travellers are used to.

## Senegal: Genuinely Good Care, If You Can Reach It

Senegal, and Dakar specifically, occupies an interesting middle position in West African healthcare — the capital has developed a solid, in places genuinely impressive, private hospital and clinic sector, drawing not just resident expats but medical travellers from across the region for certain specialities. Facilities like the major private clinics in Dakar offer care that holds up reasonably well against international standards for a meaningful range of conditions, staffed by well-trained doctors, many with international training or experience.

The catch, and it's a significant one, is geography: this quality of care is concentrated almost entirely in Dakar. Step outside the capital into Senegal's regional towns and rural areas, and the healthcare infrastructure drops off sharply, with public facilities often under-resourced, understaffed, and unevenly equipped. For anyone living or travelling meaningfully outside Dakar, the practical calculus becomes similar to Laos's: comprehensive insurance, evacuation coverage where feasible, and realistic expectations about what local facilities can actually handle.

What distinguishes Senegal's situation from many of its regional neighbours is that Dakar's private sector genuinely earns its reputation rather than merely claiming it — expats who've needed serious care in the capital consistently report being pleasantly, sometimes emphatically, surprised by the standard on offer, a rarer thing to hear repeated so consistently across independent sources.

## The Verdict

Laos is candid about its limitations and has built a functioning, if slightly nerve-wracking, medevac-to-Thailand pipeline around them. Senegal offers a genuine bright spot in Dakar that punches well above regional expectations, provided you're not too far from the capital when something goes wrong. Laos wins on honest, well-understood systemic clarity. Senegal wins on the sheer pleasant surprise of actually good private care existing at all. Either way: buy the insurance, and know exactly where the nearest real hospital is before you need to ask.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/expats — paraphrased: a longtime resident of Laos said medevac insurance isn't a precaution so much as an assumed baseline requirement, describing it as the first thing any serious expat guide to the country mentions.*

> *Internations Dakar — paraphrased: a resident said it took real trial and error to identify which private clinics genuinely matched their reputation versus which just had good marketing, and recommended asking other expats directly rather than trusting online reviews alone.*

> *Quora — "Is healthcare in Laos safe for foreigners?" — paraphrased answer: a respondent with regional experience said Vientiane's private clinics handle routine matters well, but anyone facing a serious diagnosis should expect to fly to Bangkok rather than wait for local options to improve.*

## Conclusion

Laos teaches you to plan for the healthcare system you actually have, not the one you'd prefer, and to make peace with Thailand as the real safety net. Senegal teaches you that genuinely excellent care can exist in unexpected places, provided you understand exactly how far that excellence travels beyond the capital. Neither country will apologise for the gap between city and countryside. Your insurance policy shouldn't either.
