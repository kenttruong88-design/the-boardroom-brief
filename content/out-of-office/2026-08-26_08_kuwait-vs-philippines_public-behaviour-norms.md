# One Country Polices Your Volume, The Other Never Had The Room To Bother

🇰🇼 Kuwait vs 🇵🇭 Philippines — Public Behaviour Norms

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Public behaviour is really a negotiation between a culture's values and its available space, and Kuwait and the Philippines land in two entirely different places on that spectrum. Kuwait enforces a quiet, modest, carefully composed public register rooted in conservative social norms, where visible restraint is the default and loudness reads as a genuine transgression. The Philippines, packed into some of the densest cities on earth, has made peace with a public sphere that is loud, tightly packed, and physically close by simple necessity — there was never enough room to be precious about personal space.

I have been quietly, firmly corrected in a Kuwait City mall for laughing too loudly near a family section, and I have been wedged into a Manila jeepney so tightly that a stranger's elbow and my ribs negotiated a truce entirely without either of us speaking, both of us simply accepting the physics of the situation with a shrug.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Kuwait**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress modestly in public regardless of gender — shoulders and knees covered is the safe default | Show public displays of affection, even mild ones; they're genuinely frowned upon and can cause real trouble |
| Lower your voice in public spaces, especially malls, mosques, and family-designated areas | Assume alcohol is available or acceptable in public settings; Kuwait is fully dry |
| Respect gender-segregated seating sections in many restaurants and public venues | Photograph strangers, especially women, without clear permission — this is taken very seriously |

**Philippines**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept close physical proximity on public transport as completely normal, not personal | Show visible frustration or anger in public disputes; maintaining "hiya" (social composure) matters deeply |
| Smile and greet people warmly; Filipino public culture runs on friendliness as default | Expect quiet public transport; jeepneys and tricycles are loud, social, chaotic by design |
| Use "po" and "opo" (respectful terms) with elders in public interactions | Rush interactions with vendors or strangers; a bit of warmth first goes a long way |

## Kuwait: Composure As Public Virtue

Kuwait's public behaviour norms are shaped heavily by conservative Gulf social values, and the resulting culture prizes restraint, modesty, and a kind of visible dignity that governs nearly every public interaction. Loud conversation, physical displays of affection, and casual dress are all quietly but firmly discouraged, and the enforcement mechanism isn't primarily legal — it's social, delivered through disapproving looks, gentle correction, or simply being asked directly to adjust your behaviour by someone entirely unrelated to you who has decided it's their business, and in Kuwait, it genuinely is considered everyone's business.

Gender segregation still shapes public space meaningfully — many restaurants maintain separate "family" and "singles" sections, malls have designated family days, and mixed-gender socializing in public carries different expectations than most Western visitors are used to navigating. This isn't presented as oppressive by most Kuwaitis; it's simply the accepted architecture of public respectability, and foreigners who arrive assuming Western norms transfer directly tend to have an uncomfortable adjustment period involving several well-meaning but pointed corrections from strangers.

What's easy to miss, especially for first-time visitors, is how much genuine warmth exists underneath this formality. Kuwaitis are famously hospitable in private and semi-private settings — invitations to tea, elaborate generosity toward guests — but that warmth operates within a public register that stays composed, quiet, and modest at all times. The lesson for outsiders isn't that Kuwait is cold; it's that Kuwait separates public composure from private warmth far more sharply than many cultures do, and confusing the two, or assuming public restraint signals unfriendliness, is a genuine misread that takes most expats a few months to unlearn.

## Philippines: Warmth And Density Solved The Same Problem

The Philippines, especially in Manila and other major cities, has built its public behaviour norms around an entirely different constraint: extraordinary population density combined with a cultural instinct toward warmth that refuses to curdle into resentment even under serious physical pressure. Jeepneys — the iconic, brightly decorated shared transport vehicles — pack passengers in with a closeness that would trigger genuine outrage in more space-abundant cultures, and yet the prevailing public mood remains good-natured, chatty, tolerant.

Central to this is the concept of "hiya" — a deep-rooted social value around maintaining composure, avoiding public shame, and prioritizing smooth interpersonal harmony even in objectively uncomfortable circumstances. Visible anger, raised voices in conflict, or aggressive confrontation in public are all things Filipino culture actively works to avoid, not out of passivity but because losing composure publicly is genuinely embarrassing in a way that outweighs whatever the immediate grievance might be. This produces a public sphere that's loud in volume — vendors calling out, jeepney barkers shouting destinations, constant ambient chatter — but rarely hostile in tone.

Respect language is baked directly into daily interaction: "po" and "opo," added to sentences when addressing elders or authority figures, aren't optional politeness, they're structurally expected, and their absence is noticeable even to foreigners who don't yet understand why. Physical closeness on transport, in markets, in queues is simply accepted as the cost of living in cities this dense, and the collective agreement not to make a production out of it is, in its own way, as much a social achievement as Kuwait's careful public modesty — just built to solve a completely different problem.

## The Verdict

Kuwait wins if you value formality, modesty, and a public sphere carefully insulated from noise and physical contact — provided you're prepared to adjust your entire wardrobe and volume accordingly. The Philippines wins if you'd rather be crammed into a jeepney with six strangers, all of them smiling, than isolated in composed silence anywhere else, because that density comes wrapped in a warmth that's genuinely hard to manufacture elsewhere.

Both cultures have simply optimized public behaviour for what actually works given their circumstances — Kuwait's spaciousness allows for careful, deliberate composure; the Philippines' density demands a different kind of resilience, buffered by relentless good humour. Neither is more "civilized" than the other, whatever a smug tourist might privately assume. They're just answering different questions.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Kuwait forum — a newcomer described being politely asked by mall security to move to a family section after laughing loudly with a mixed-gender group of friends, and said it fundamentally changed how they behaved in public afterward.*

> *expat.com Philippines forum — someone recounted their first jeepney ride being so physically close to other passengers that they apologized reflexively for twenty minutes, only to be told by a fellow rider, laughing, "why are you sorry, this is just Tuesday."*

> *Quora — a foreigner asked what mistake Western expats make most in Kuwait, and the top answer said simply: "Confusing quiet for cold. It's not. It's just not loud."*

## Conclusion

Kuwait and the Philippines have both built genuinely functional public cultures, they just solved for opposite scarcities — one for space it has plenty of and guards carefully, the other for space it has none of and shares generously, loudly, and with remarkably good humour. Show up assuming your home country's version of "normal" applies in either place, and you'll be the one standing out, badly, while everyone else quietly, or loudly, gets on with their day exactly as they always have.
