# Pakistan Haggles With the Man Who Knows Your Mother. Hong Kong Hands You a Red Sticker and Dares You to Have Standards

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Pakistan, buying groceries is a relationship, usually an unfinished one. The vegetable seller knows your family, the butcher knows your preferences and the shop around the corner will extend credit on your good name and your cousin's. Prices are discussed, not read. Freshness is judged by squeezing, sniffing and mild accusation.

In Hong Kong, groceries are a logistics exercise performed at speed in a basement. Two giant chains share the territory, the aisles are narrow, the labels are in two scripts and the end-of-day discount sticker is the closest the city comes to sentiment. Yet if you know where to look, a very different Hong Kong appears, one with halal counters, imported snacks and shopkeepers who are very glad to see you.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Pakistan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bargain politely at markets and small shops | Accept the first price with a straight face |
| Build a relationship with your regular vendors | Rotate sellers every week and expect favours |
| Buy meat from a butcher you trust | Assume every label means the same thing |
| Carry cash and your own bags | Expect a self-checkout |

**Hong Kong**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the red and yellow discount labels late in the day | Shop for dairy and bread at peak price |
| Learn which chains cater to which brands | Expect one shop to stock everything |
| Look for halal certification stickers | Treat "no pork" as the same as halal |
| Explore the wet markets for fresh produce | Haggle as if it were Karachi |

## Pakistan

I will keep the Pakistani half honest: this part rests on general knowledge and not on any single source I fetched. In most Pakistani cities, grocery shopping is a mix of the corner kiryana store, the neighbourhood sabzi wala and the weekend bazaar, with the modern supermarket sitting on top for those who want air conditioning and fixed prices. The relationship is the point. A shopkeeper who has known your family for years will give you the better tomatoes and tell you the truth about the chicken.

That matters when you leave. What expats from South Asia usually miss is not just the ingredients but the entire transaction: the greetings, the banter, the sense that the person selling you onions has a stake in your dinner. A Hong Kong supermarket, however well stocked, does not ask how your mother is. It scans your item and moves on.

The other thing that travels badly is certainty. At home you know where the meat comes from, who cut it and whether it is halal, because you know the butcher. Abroad you have to learn a new set of labels, and the stakes are higher than a wrong packet of crisps.

The Pakistani grocery calendar has its own weather. Ramadan sends the whole country shopping at once, with dates, flour, gram and fruit disappearing from shelves in the days before the first fast, and Eid brings a second rush for sweets and new clothes. Bread is bought hot and eaten the same day, milk often arrives from a delivery man you have known for years, and the weekend bazaar is where the family stocks up and argues about prices. Again, that is general background and not a fetched source, but anyone who has lived there will recognise it. The point is that groceries are tied to the calendar, the faith and the family in a way the Hong Kong chains simply are not.

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong's grocery scene is dominated by a famous duopoly. The Honeycombers guide says ParknShop has more than 300 branches and treats Wellcome as its counterpart, with sub-brands Fusion and Taste in the same family. Expat Living says Wellcome has close to 300 stores, and that ParknShop opened its first one in Stanley in 1973. Its upmarket sub-brands cater more to expats. Wellcome runs end-of-day discounts on dairy, tofu and bread, flagged with red and yellow labels, which is the single most useful piece of local knowledge in this article.

Beyond the duopoly, the city specialises. City'super has branches in Causeway Bay, Harbour City, Sha Tin and IFC, A&M and Gateway serve American brands, M&S and Bargain Mart serve British ones, and Levant Foods sells Greek and Middle Eastern goods. Wet markets, such as the one on Bowrington Road between Causeway Bay and Wan Chai, remain where residents buy fresh produce, and Central Market was reopened in 2021. None of these fetched guides mention halal at all, which tells you what the mainstream shopping press considers its audience.

For halal, you need a different map. The Nomado Travel guide says the area around Haiphong Road in Tsim Sha Tsui is known locally as "Little Pakistan", near Kowloon Masjid on Nathan Road, and that halal certification from the Incorporated Trustees of the Islamic Community Fund of Hong Kong is shown by a green-and-white sticker. It also notes tiers from fully certified to "no pork, no lard". Treat its figures with care, since some of its claims are unsourced, but the practical lesson holds: read the sticker, and do not assume.

The more charming discovery is smaller. A 2020 Hong Kong Baptist University piece describes a neighbourhood walk in Kwai Chung that ends at a South Asian-run grocery, Pak store (HK), where visitors can try Pakistani food. The best sellers there, according to the story, are popcorn and mango juice. The guide, Minhas Rashad, who has lived in Hong Kong for over thirty years and speaks fluent Cantonese, says the city has become so busy and self-conscious that neighbours barely talk. The shop owner says he is glad locals come to understand his culture. That is a grocery store functioning exactly as a neighbourhood should.

For completeness, the specialist map is better than the duopoly suggests. The Expat Living guide lists Regency Spices, in business since the mid-twentieth century, Levant Foods for Greek and Middle Eastern goods, and the wet market on Bowrington Road, while pointing out that the Canton Bazaar on Queen's Road Central is described as the first wet market, dating from the 1840s. Sogo's basement supermarket in Causeway Bay, Don Don Donki and Aeon each draw shoppers from outside their neighbourhoods, and Oliver's The Delicatessen in Central stocks British, Australian and New Zealand brands. The Nomado guide adds that Indonesian domestic workers, whose day off is Sunday, fill Chungking Mansions and Kowloon Park. And the Kwai Chung walk exists precisely because, as Fikiyo Yiu of the Lady MacLehose Centre says, it gives locals a safe group setting in which to walk into a halal restaurant for the first time. Hong Kong's groceries are a map of who lives there, and it takes a guide to read it.

## The Verdict

Pakistan wins on relationships, Hong Kong wins on systems, and neither wins outright. If I am buying a chicken, I want the butcher who knows my name. If I am buying yoghurt at nine at night, I want the shop with the discount sticker.

But for an expat from Pakistan, the decisive factor is not the supermarket. It is the special shop. Find the place where the owner speaks your language, the shelf is stocked with the things your mother used to buy and the halal question has a real answer. That one shop will do more for your sanity than six hundred branches of anything.

Hong Kong is not hostile to your groceries. It is just indifferent until you go looking. Go looking.

## Conclusion

A grocery store is a small act of trust. In Pakistan you trust a person. In Hong Kong you trust a label. The trick for anyone crossing from one to the other is to keep both habits: ask questions like a Karachi shopper and read stickers like a Hong Kong one.

And if a stranger on a Sunday offers you mango juice and a plate of something spicy, say yes. It is the best supermarket in town.

You will not find that on the discount shelf.
