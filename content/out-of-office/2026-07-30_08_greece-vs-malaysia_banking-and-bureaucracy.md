# One Country's Bureaucracy Requires a Folder, the Other's Requires an Appointment You Actually Get

🇬🇷 Greece vs 🇲🇾 Malaysia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Greece's bureaucracy operates on a principle I've come to respect the way you respect a force of nature: nothing gets done in one visit, ever, and the AFM tax number you need for literally everything — bank accounts, phone contracts, rental agreements — requires a physical trip to a tax office that seems designed by someone who has personally never needed anything from a tax office. Malaysia, particularly for anyone using the MM2H residency pathway or simply opening a local account, runs a comparatively organised system: documented requirements, actual appointment slots, and staff who, astonishingly, show up expecting to help you.

I've made four separate trips to a Greek tax office for a single AFM number, each visit revealing a new document I apparently should have known to bring, delivered by a clerk who seemed personally aggrieved I hadn't guessed. I've also opened a Malaysian bank account in a single, almost suspiciously smooth appointment, walking out with a functioning debit card before lunch. Both systems eventually work. One of them tests your will to live first.

## Do's & Don'ts: Greece

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your AFM (tax registration number) sorted first — nothing else in Greek bureaucracy works without it | Show up to any government office without photocopies of everything; originals alone are rarely enough |
| Bring a Greek-speaking friend or a certified translator for anything beyond the basics | Expect a single visit to resolve anything; budget for at least two to three trips as the default |
| Arrive early — queues form well before opening and the office may stop taking new numbers by mid-morning | Assume email or phone follow-up works; in-person remains the only reliable channel for most processes |

## Do's & Don'ts: Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research your bank's specific foreign-resident requirements before your appointment — they vary by institution | Assume every bank branch handles foreign accounts; some require designated branches for non-residents |
| Use the MM2H programme's structured checklist if pursuing long-term residency — it's genuinely well documented | Skip travel or medical insurance proof; it's a hard requirement, not a suggestion, for most account and visa processes |
| Book appointments where possible — walk-ins work but appointments move noticeably faster | Expect identical processes across banks — Maybank, CIMB, and others each have their own quirks worth researching first |

## Greece: Bureaucracy as an Endurance Sport

The Greek AFM (Arithmós Forologikoú Mitróou) is the single most important nine digits a foreign resident will acquire, and obtaining it is a rite of passage that seems specifically engineered to test patience rather than verify identity. It's required for opening a bank account, signing a rental contract, getting a phone plan, registering a car — genuinely everything — which means it's simultaneously the most important errand on your list and the hardest one to actually complete, because you often need supporting documents that themselves require an AFM to obtain, a bureaucratic ouroboros that has driven more than one newcomer to genuine despair.

The process rewards a specific kind of person: someone with unlimited patience, a folder of photocopies nobody explicitly asked for but everyone silently expected, and the emotional resilience to be told, calmly, that today's visit accomplished nothing because of a missing stamp from a different office entirely. Offices open early, close for extended lunch breaks, and stop processing new numbers well before the posted closing time if the queue looks long enough to inconvenience the staff's schedule. None of this is explained clearly in advance. All of it is common knowledge among anyone who's survived the process, delivered afterward with the weary solidarity of war veterans.

And yet — once you have the AFM, everything genuinely does get easier. It's not that Greek bureaucracy doesn't work; it's that it front-loads all its difficulty into a single, formidable gatekeeping step, and expects you to earn your way through the rest of Greek life via that one relentless morning, or several.

## Malaysia: Bureaucracy as a Structured, If Imperfect, System

Malaysia's approach to foreign residency and banking is, by comparison, almost disappointingly straightforward — which, after Greece, feels like a genuine luxury. The Malaysia My Second Home programme lays out its requirements in clear, published detail: minimum deposits, income thresholds, insurance requirements, all documented well enough that a diligent applicant can prepare everything before setting foot in an office. Bank account opening for foreign residents follows a similarly structured path, though it's not without its own local texture: not every branch handles non-resident accounts, requirements shift slightly bank to bank, and the paperwork burden, while real, is knowable in advance rather than discovered mid-process.

What impresses most is the appointment culture. Where Greek bureaucracy often treats scheduling as an aspirational concept, Malaysian institutions — banks especially — genuinely honour appointment slots, staff show up prepared, and a well-documented applicant can walk out the same day with a functioning account. This isn't universal perfection; queues exist, some processes take longer than advertised, and navigating between competing bank policies takes real research. But the system is legible. You can, with effort, know what's coming.

The trap for newcomers is assuming Malaysian efficiency means casualness — showing up without insurance proof, or expecting a branch that doesn't handle foreign accounts to make an exception. The system rewards preparation; it just rewards it faster and more predictably than Greece does.

## The Verdict

Greece wins, if you can call it winning, on sheer narrative value — everyone who's survived the AFM process has a genuinely great story to tell at a dinner party, years later, once the trauma has properly composted into anecdote. Malaysia wins on actually respecting your time, which, when you're the one standing in the queue, matters considerably more than a good story. Give me the appointment I actually get.

## Conclusion

Greek bureaucracy demands you prove your worthiness through repetition and patience; Malaysian bureaucracy demands you prove your preparedness through research, and rewards it promptly. Show up in Athens without a folder of photocopies and you'll be sent home to make more. Show up in Kuala Lumpur without checking your bank's specific foreign-resident requirements and you'll simply be redirected to the correct branch. Both systems eventually get you a bank account. Only one of them will still be a funny story a decade from now — and that's not necessarily a compliment to Greece.
