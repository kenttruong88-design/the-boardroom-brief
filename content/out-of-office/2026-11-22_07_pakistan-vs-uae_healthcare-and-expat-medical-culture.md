# Pakistan Charges You in Waiting; the UAE Charges You in Dirhams

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇦🇪 UAE

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Pakistan, you pay for healthcare with your time, your family and your nerves. A public hospital may be free or nearly so, but it will also be crowded, and half the work of getting treated is done by the relatives who stand in queues, carry the files and argue with the pharmacy. The private hospitals are better and cost what the market will bear, which is often less than anywhere else you could name.

In the UAE, you pay with a card, and the card is usually your employer's. The hospitals are gleaming, the doctors come from forty countries, and the waiting room has better furniture than most hotels. The catch is that the system is built on insurance, and the person without it discovers, at the worst possible moment, that a consultation can cost as much as a weekend away. I have been unwell in both places. One was an ordeal and the other was an invoice.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇰 Pakistan ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a relative to hospital visits | Go alone to a crowded public clinic |
| Ask private hospitals for a price up front | Assume low cost means low quality |
| Keep every report and prescription | Rely on a clinic to hold your records |
| Know your local emergency number | Wait for a crisis to find it |

| 🇦🇪 UAE ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your employer's insurance and its limits | Assume the policy covers every clinic |
| Ask for a cost estimate before tests | Treat a first visit as a small expense |
| Keep your insurance card with you | Go uninsured and hope for the best |
| Compare clinics for routine care | Use a premium hospital for a cough |

## 🇵🇰 Pakistan

Pakistan's healthcare is a story of two systems running in parallel. According to the Expat Exchange page, public healthcare is available to all citizens, and foreign residents may need to pay for some services. The same page quotes expats saying the public system is often overcrowded and understaffed, while one says private hospitals provide really good quality of service at very low prices compared with international rates. None of that is official data, and the quotes are anonymous, so read it as a flavour rather than a ruling. But it matches what I have seen: the private sector is where those who can afford it go.

The experience is shaped by family. In a Pakistani hospital, the patient is rarely alone. A relative handles the paperwork, another buys the medicine, a third argues with the receptionist, and an aunt provides an opinion on the diagnosis. It is a system that relies on social infrastructure, and it works because that infrastructure is dense. The page gives 112 as the national emergency number, and it is worth saving before you need it.

For an expat, the most important difference is how care is negotiated. A private consultation is usually quick to arrange and relatively cheap. You can often see a consultant within a day, and diagnostic tests are available in the same building. What you will not find is the polished, standardised, English-speaking efficiency of a Gulf hospital. Records are often kept by the patient, appointments are fluid and the doctor may be late for a good reason.

I should add that I found no hard data on Pakistani insurance rules or costs in rupees on the pages I read, so I will not invent any. If you are moving to Pakistan, ask your employer what is covered, ask a local colleague which hospital they would send their mother to, and keep a copy of everything. That last piece of advice, I have found, works in every country on earth.

## 🇦🇪 UAE

The Expat Exchange Dubai page says health insurance is mandatory for all residents in Dubai, and that employers are required to provide basic health insurance for their employees. It also contradicts itself about public access, saying in one place that public healthcare is primarily for UAE nationals and expatriates must have private insurance, and in another that public services are available to nationals and expatriates with valid insurance. I would not rely on it for the rules. I would rely on it for the general shape: in the UAE, your care is organised around a policy.

The cost of being uninsured is vivid in a March 2024 Khaleej Times report. Ahmed Ateeq, a businessman in Deira, said he had not applied for medical insurance for a long time, paid about Dh600 for consultation, tests and medicines the first time he fell ill, and Dh800 for his medical expenses the second time, before buying insurance for Dh900 from a broker. Abu Aiza, a Sharjah resident who had recently moved his family to the UAE, said even a routine doctor's visit could set him back financially, and described paying about Dh650 for one visit for his uninsured daughter: Dh350 for the consultation, Dh200 for tests and Dh90 for medicines. Shahbaz Khan, also from Sharjah, told the paper he uses community clinics at low rates, citing an Ajman doctor who charges just Dh20 for treatment.

The headline of that article says Dh800 for consultation, but the body says Dh800 was his medical expenses, so I will not call it a consultation fee. These are individual accounts, not a price list. They do, however, convey the point: the difference between an insured and an uninsured patient is enormous, and the system assumes you are the former.

When you are covered, the experience is excellent. One expat quoted on Expat Exchange said they paid about 30 dirhams, roughly ten dollars, for a doctor's visit with employer-provided insurance. The facilities are modern, the doctors are international, and the system is built for speed. It is the polar opposite of the crowded public ward, and it is a luxury that depends entirely on a piece of plastic in your wallet.

## The Verdict

The UAE wins for comfort, and Pakistan wins for affordability, provided you know where to go. If you are a corporate expat with proper cover, the UAE is a dream: modern, fast and English-speaking, with no need to recruit your relatives as project managers. For anyone with a salary and a policy, it is the more pleasant place to be ill.

But the UAE is a poor place to be uninsured, and the Khaleej Times reporting shows how quickly small illnesses become real money. Pakistan, for all its overcrowding, has a private sector that can be remarkably good value, and a social fabric that supports the patient. If I were choosing for an employed professional, I would pick the UAE. If I were choosing for a family on a thin budget, I would think harder than I want to admit.

## Conclusion

Pakistan will ask for patience, and the UAE will ask for a policy number. Both will treat you, in the end, and both will remind you that health is the thing you take for granted until the receptionist asks a question. Get covered, keep your paperwork, learn the emergency number and find a clinic you trust before you need one. It is the least glamorous advice in this series, and the most likely to save your week.
