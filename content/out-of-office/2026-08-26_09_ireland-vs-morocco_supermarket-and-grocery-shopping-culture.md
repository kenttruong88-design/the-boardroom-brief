# One Country Does The Big Shop On Autopilot, The Other Treats Every Vegetable Like A Negotiation

🇮🇪 Ireland vs 🇲🇦 Morocco — Supermarket and Grocery Shopping Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Grocery shopping is the most honest cultural indicator there is, precisely because nobody thinks of it as performance — it's just what people actually do, repeatedly, without an audience. Ireland has streamlined the whole affair into a brisk, loyalty-card-driven weekly ritual at a handful of familiar chains. Morocco has kept it alive as daily theatre: souks full of produce piled with architectural precision, vendors who remember your face, and a pricing system that rewards relationship over convenience.

I have done a thoroughly unremarkable weekly shop at a Dublin Tesco, trolley squeaking, playlist of nothing in particular playing overhead, and I have spent forty minutes in a Marrakech souk buying six lemons because the vendor insisted on telling me about his cousin in Lyon first, and somehow I have stronger memories of the lemons.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Ireland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Sign up for a loyalty card at whichever chain you frequent — Irish shoppers are fiercely loyal to one | Expect much variation between major chains; Tesco, Dunnes, and SuperValu cover most needs similarly |
| Do a proper weekly "big shop" — it's the cultural default over frequent small trips | Assume Sunday hours match weekday hours; many close earlier on Sundays still |
| Bring your own bags; plastic bag charges have been standard here for years | Expect much haggling flexibility anywhere; prices are as fixed as anywhere in Northern Europe |

**Morocco**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a relationship with a regular souk vendor — loyalty gets you better prices over time | Handle produce excessively before buying; ask the vendor to select instead, it's expected etiquette |
| Bargain respectfully and with good humor; it's a social ritual, not a hostile exchange | Assume every seller is trying to overcharge you; many simply price for negotiation as standard practice |
| Shop early morning for the best produce selection before the midday heat and crowds | Rush a purchase; a few minutes of conversation is part of the transaction, not a delay to it |

## Ireland: The Big Shop, Brand Loyalty, and Blessed Predictability

Irish grocery shopping runs on a rhythm so consistent it barely registers as a cultural practice at all to the people doing it — which is, in its own way, exactly what makes it worth examining. The dominant pattern is the "big shop": one substantial weekly trip to a large supermarket, trolley filled methodically, rather than frequent small visits to multiple specialty shops. Tesco, Dunnes Stores, and SuperValu dominate the landscape, each with a loyalty card program that Irish shoppers treat with a devotion bordering on tribal — ask someone which supermarket they use and you'll often get an answer delivered with the same certainty as a football allegiance.

What's notable here isn't dramatic difference between the chains — they're broadly comparable in price, range, and quality — but the sheer consistency of the ritual itself. Shopping happens at predictable times, follows predictable routes through predictable aisles, and involves minimal social friction: a polite "how's it going" at checkout, bagging your own groceries (plastic bag charges have been standard for long enough that reusable bags are simply assumed), and moving on with your day. There's comfort in this, a kind of low-grade civic reliability that Irish consumers clearly value, evidenced by how little the shopping habits have shifted even as online grocery delivery has become more available.

Sunday trading hours remain slightly more restricted than weekdays in many locations, a quiet holdover of older norms, and catches the occasional visitor off guard mid-week planning. But broadly, Irish grocery culture optimizes for exactly what it delivers: efficiency, familiarity, and the complete absence of any expectation that a trip to buy milk should involve conversation, negotiation, or memorable interpersonal drama of any kind. It's grocery shopping as background noise, which, depending on your temperament, is either a relief or faintly sad.

## Morocco: The Souk As Daily Social Infrastructure

Morocco's grocery culture, particularly outside the handful of Western-style supermarkets now present in larger cities, operates on an entirely different premise: the souk isn't just where you buy food, it's a genuine social infrastructure, visited daily by many households rather than weekly, built on personal relationships that compound in value over months and years. A regular customer who's built rapport with a specific produce vendor over time doesn't just get friendlier service — they get better prices, first pick of the best stock, and often small unrequested extras tossed in as a gesture of goodwill.

The visual theatre of a Moroccan souk is genuinely remarkable: produce stacked with an almost architectural precision, spice pyramids in impossible gradients of color, vendors calling out prices and greetings with practiced charisma. Bargaining is central to the experience, but understanding it as a social ritual rather than a hostile transaction is essential — it's expected, enjoyed by most participants, and conducted with humor rather than aggression. Refusing to engage, or treating the vendor's opening price as insulting rather than as the opening move it's meant to be, misreads the entire interaction.

There's also an etiquette around handling produce that catches many foreigners off guard: rather than picking through fruit and vegetables yourself, the expected practice is often to tell the vendor what you need and let them select it for you — a system built on trust that, once you lean into it, tends to produce better results than self-selection would anyway, since the vendor knows their stock far better than you do. Timing matters too; the best selection and calmest atmosphere is typically early morning, before the midday heat sets in and crowds thicken. What Morocco offers that Ireland's efficient chains simply can't replicate is a grocery run that doubles as genuine daily social contact, repeated, valued, and remembered.

## The Verdict

Ireland wins on convenience, predictability, and the simple relief of a grocery run that asks absolutely nothing of you emotionally or socially. Morocco wins on texture, relationship, and the genuine pleasure of a transaction that treats you as a person rather than a barcode scan, provided you're willing to invest the time and humor the ritual requires.

If you want your groceries handled with zero friction and zero memorable content whatsoever, Ireland's big-shop system will serve you flawlessly and forgettably. If you want to remember the person who sold you your vegetables, know their name within a month, and occasionally get a free herb bunch thrown in because you asked about their cousin, Morocco's souk culture delivers something Ireland's supermarkets were never designed to offer, and honestly never tried to.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Ireland forum — a newcomer noted that switching supermarket loyalty was treated by Irish colleagues almost like a personal betrayal, half-joking, half not.*

> *Internations Marrakech — a long-term resident described visiting the same vegetable stall daily for over a year before the vendor started setting aside the best produce specifically for them without being asked.*

> *Quora — someone asked if it was rude to browse a souk stall without buying, and the top answer said: "Not rude, just expect a bit of gentle persuasion — it's part of the fun, don't take it personally."*

## Conclusion

Ireland and Morocco have both figured out grocery shopping perfectly well, they've simply optimized for opposite things — one for frictionless efficiency, the other for daily human connection dressed up as commerce. Show up in a Marrakech souk expecting Tesco's brisk anonymity and you'll seem cold and confusing. Show up in a Dublin SuperValu expecting a warm negotiation over the price of onions and you'll simply be asked, politely, if you're alright there. Learn the local rhythm, or spend your entire trip mildly out of step with everyone quietly, or loudly, doing it correctly around you.
