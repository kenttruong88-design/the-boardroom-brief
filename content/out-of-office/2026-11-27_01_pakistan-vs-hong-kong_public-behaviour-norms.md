# Pakistan Invented the Crowd and Hong Kong Learned to Ignore It

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Let us begin with the great myth of the corporate relocation pack, which is that personal space is a universal human right. It is not. It is a local product, like tea, and the two countries on today's menu manufacture entirely different blends. Pakistan runs on proximity. Rickshaws, wedding halls, bazaars and bus stops all operate on the principle that if there is a gap, someone with a plan will occupy it, and nobody takes it personally.

Hong Kong runs on proximity too, with one crucial difference: it has agreed to pretend it is not happening. Seven million people share a few hundred square miles, so everyone has perfected the art of being physically adjacent and spiritually absent. Earphones in, eyes down, elbows tucked. A Karachi native stepping onto an MTR platform at rush hour will experience something unsettling, which is a crowd that behaves.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇰 Pakistan ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet people properly, with a handshake or a salaam, before getting to business | Launch straight into the request without the small talk |
| Accept the tea when it is offered | Refuse hospitality twice and expect the relationship to survive |
| Expect noise, horns and conversation at close range | Treat a raised voice as an argument |
| Use the right hand when giving or receiving | Hand over anything important with the left |

| 🇭🇰 Hong Kong ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stand on the right of the escalator and let walkers pass | Block the left lane because you are admiring the architecture |
| Queue properly for taxis, buses and restaurants | Drift to the front because the line looks informal |
| Keep phone calls short and quiet on the MTR | Hold a speakerphone meeting on the Tsuen Wan line |
| Take your rubbish with you | Eat on the platform and litter the evidence |

## 🇵🇰 Pakistan

Pakistani public life is loud, generous and gloriously improvised. Ask a Lahori for directions and you will receive three opinions, a cup of tea and possibly a lift. The queue exists as a concept, but it is applied with flexibility. At the bank, the ticket counter, the cricket stadium gate, the line is a living organism, expanding and contracting depending on who is standing in it and who their uncle knows. Women's queues and men's queues are separate in many places, which is a sensible idea that works in a fair number of them.

Noise is the background texture of daily life. Horns are a language, not an insult, and a driver leaning on his horn is usually saying "I am here" rather than "get out of my way". Conversations happen close, with hands, and nobody steps back. If you pull away from someone mid-sentence they will assume you are unwell.

What the outsider misreads is that this is not rudeness. It is a different contract. Personal space in Pakistan is replaced by personal obligation: you will be fed, helped, argued with and corrected by strangers who feel responsible for you. That is a better deal than most countries offer, even if the queue at the passport office makes you want to lie down on the floor. Anyone arriving in Hong Kong from Islamabad should know that the warmth does not travel with you, and the reserve of the new city is not coldness either. It is simply a different arrangement.

Consider how this plays out in a single working day. A Pakistani professional in Karachi leaves home to the sound of a dozen horns, negotiates a roundabout by eye contact, arrives at an office where the door is open and three people want a word, and eats lunch with colleagues who will absolutely comment on what is in the box. Nothing in that day is private, and nothing in it is lonely. The system is crowded, but it is also a net.

## 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

Hong Kong is the world's most efficient exercise in strangers sharing a very small box. The MTR is spotless, punctual and almost eerily quiet. Passengers queue in marked lines at the platform doors, let people exit before boarding, and stand on the right of the escalators so that walkers can pass on the left. Blocking the left side is a social crime, and the glare you receive is the sentence.

Queue etiquette runs deep. Restaurants will hand you a numbered ticket, shops will form orderly lines for a sale, and a queue jumper at a bus stop will be met with the most devastating weapon in the Cantonese arsenal, which is a sigh. Personal space is something you create inside your head. On a packed carriage you will be pressed against strangers with no apology and no eye contact, and neither party considers it intimate.

Noise is a more complicated matter. The streets are loud, the markets are louder, and restaurants run at a volume that would make an Italian wedding blush. But the transport network, the lifts and the office corridors are expected to be quiet. Taking a phone call on a packed train is poor form, and playing video without earphones is the sort of thing that gets you filmed and posted online.

There is also a rhythm to the pace. People walk fast, board fast and decide fast. Stopping dead in the middle of a pavement to read your phone is treated as a minor assault. The rule for newcomers is simple: move with purpose or move out of the way.

Now translate that same professional to Central. The lift is silent. The colleague who might once have shared a samosa nods at you and returns to his screen. The pavement is a conveyor belt and the only conversation is the ping of an Octopus card. The shock is not hostility, it is absence. Hong Kong gives you room and takes away the audience, and it takes most people a few months to decide whether that feels like freedom or like fog. My advice is to treat it as freedom and then go and find your own noise on a Sunday, in Kowloon, over dim sum.

## The Verdict

Hong Kong wins on logistics and Pakistan wins on humanity, and I refuse to pretend that is a draw. If I need to get from Central to Kowloon in eleven minutes without anyone touching my shoulder or my opinions, I want Hong Kong. If I need someone to notice that I am unwell, lost or about to make a terrible financial decision, I want Pakistan, where a stranger will intervene before you finish the sentence.

Where Hong Kong errs is mistaking silence for respect. Where Pakistan errs is mistaking volume for connection. The expat who can use both gets the best of two very different crowd cultures. Learn the escalator rule, keep your voice down on the train, and save the loud, warm, over-fed version of yourself for the dinner table. Hong Kong will forgive a lot, but it will never forgive the person who stops at the top of the stairs.

Personal space deserves a footnote, because the definition differs even within the same carriage. In Pakistan, standing close is warmth. In Hong Kong, standing close is a regrettable fact of geography, and the unwritten rule is that you do not acknowledge it. If you accidentally brush a stranger's arm in Karachi, you may end up with a lift home. If you do the same in Mong Kok, you will get a flicker of the eyebrows at best.

## Conclusion

Public behaviour is the most honest portrait of a society, because nobody is performing for it. In Pakistan, strangers invade your space and then adopt you. In Hong Kong, strangers respect your space and then forget you exist. Both are forms of manners, and neither is superior, though only one will lend you a charger.

So when you land, watch before you act. Find the invisible line, the lane, the volume. Then adjust, because your old habits are baggage and baggage costs extra. And if somebody sighs at you on the escalator, do not take it personally. It is the Hong Kong equivalent of a welcome speech.

And if you are really lost, Hong Kong will help. Ask directly and politely and most people will assist you in efficient, accurate English. They just will not volunteer it first.
