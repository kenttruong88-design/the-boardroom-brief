# One City Grew Its Jungle Back On Purpose, The Other Is Still Planting Its Future

🇲🇾 Malaysia 🇪🇹 Ethiopia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Cities reveal their priorities in the green space they bother to protect, and Kuala Lumpur and Addis Ababa have arrived at radically different answers, on radically different timelines. Kuala Lumpur sits on decades of accumulated, increasingly deliberate urban greening — a genuine rainforest reserve within walking distance of its most famous skyscrapers, botanical gardens with real colonial-era roots now serving a thoroughly modern population, and a skyline that has learned, unlike most of its Southeast Asian peers, to grow vertically without entirely sacrificing its canopy. Addis Ababa is mid-transformation, its most talked-about green spaces the product of a large, state-driven beautification push over the past several years — the "Beautifying Sheger" initiative and its flagship Sheger Park, alongside the older, higher-altitude Entoto Natural Park — projects still recent enough that longtime residents remember exactly what the land looked like before.

Kuala Lumpur's green credentials aren't a recent marketing exercise. The city genuinely preserved patches of primary and secondary rainforest as it grew, most famously the forest reserve now anchored by Taman Tugu, a restored green corridor near the city centre, alongside the older, more manicured Perdana Botanical Gardens and the internationally recognisable KLCC Park, whose lake and fountain sit in the literal shadow of the Petronas Towers — arguably the most photographed juxtaposition of rainforest canopy and glass skyscraper anywhere in the world. Addis Ababa's green transformation tells a different, more compressed story: Sheger Park, opened as the centrepiece of a beautification campaign along the city's rivers, converted what had been, by most accounts, a neglected and at times unsafe riverside corridor into landscaped walkways, cafes, and public gathering space, while Entoto Natural Park, higher on the hills that ring the capital, has worked to restore forest that had been heavily degraded by decades of eucalyptus harvesting and unregulated use.

## Do's & Don'ts: Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit Taman Tugu's forest trails early morning — the canopy cover makes it genuinely cooler and quieter before the heat and crowds build | Assume KLCC Park is the only green space worth seeing — it's the famous one, but Perdana Botanical Gardens and Taman Tugu offer a different, less touristy experience |
| Bring water and expect humidity even under forest cover — this is still equatorial rainforest, not a temperate park | Feed the monkeys or wildlife you'll encounter on forest trails — it's discouraged and can provoke aggressive behaviour |
| Use parks the way locals do — early morning exercise, evening family strolls, avoiding the flat midday heat | Expect total silence or total solitude — KL's best-known parks are genuinely popular, especially on weekends and public holidays |

## Do's & Don'ts: Ethiopia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit Sheger Park to see the riverside transformation firsthand — it's become a genuine point of civic pride worth experiencing | Assume the altitude in Entoto Park won't affect you — it sits considerably higher than the city centre, and the climb and thinner air catch people off guard |
| Ask locals about the area's history before the beautification project — many have vivid before-and-after memories worth hearing | Litter or treat the newer green spaces casually — they're widely seen as hard-won civic achievements, and locals notice disrespect |
| Dress in layers for Entoto — the temperature drop from the city centre to the hills is genuinely significant | Expect Addis Ababa's older neighbourhoods to have the same density of green space as the new flagship projects — the transformation is real but still uneven across the city |

## Malaysia: A Rainforest That Refused To Leave

Kuala Lumpur's relationship with green space has a genuinely unusual throughline: even as the skyline filled with glass towers, pockets of the original rainforest that once covered the entire region were deliberately, repeatedly protected rather than cleared. Taman Tugu is the clearest recent example — a forest reserve restored and opened to the public in the heart of the city, connected by elevated canopy walkways that let visitors move through genuine secondary rainforest without ever leaving the urban core, home to real, wild macaques and birdlife that have simply continued living there regardless of what got built around them. The older Perdana Botanical Gardens, dating back over a century, carries a different, more formal character — manicured lawns, an orchid garden, a deer park — while KLCC Park, the most internationally famous of the three, does the specific work of framing the Petronas Towers against landscaped greenery and a reflecting lake, a combination that's become genuine shorthand for modern Kuala Lumpur itself. What unites all three is usage: these aren't ornamental spaces locals ignore. Early mornings bring joggers and tai chi groups; evenings bring families, strollers, and couples; weekends bring the kind of crowds that make clear these parks are load-bearing social infrastructure, not backdrop.

## Ethiopia: Building The Green Legacy In Real Time

Addis Ababa's green space story is one of visible, recent, state-driven transformation, and residents talk about it that way — not as ancient civic heritage, but as something they watched happen. The Beautifying Sheger initiative, launched with explicit backing at the highest levels of government, converted stretches of the city's rivers — previously degraded, often associated with pollution and neglect — into landscaped parkland, walking paths, and public gathering spaces, with Sheger Park itself becoming the flagship, widely covered result. It's the kind of project that generates genuine, if occasionally debated, local pride: a visible, walkable answer to decades of underinvestment in public green space within the city centre. Higher up, ringing the capital, Entoto Natural Park represents a longer, slower restoration effort — reforesting hills that had been stripped by generations of eucalyptus harvesting and informal use, bringing back native vegetation and offering panoramic views over the whole capital from an altitude significant enough that visitors genuinely feel the air thin and the temperature drop. Together, the two projects represent Addis Ababa mid-transformation: a capital actively, visibly building the kind of accessible green infrastructure that took Kuala Lumpur generations of quieter, more incremental preservation to accumulate.

## The Verdict

Kuala Lumpur wins on sheer depth and maturity — genuine rainforest, decades of protection, a skyline that grew up around its green spaces rather than over them. Ethiopia's Addis Ababa wins on momentum and visible civic will — a capital actively, deliberately reclaiming and building green space at real speed, with locals who remember, vividly, what came before and clearly value what's replaced it. If you want ancient, established, thoroughly lived-in urban rainforest, Kuala Lumpur delivers it with monkeys included. If you want to watch a capital city consciously build its green legacy in real time, Addis Ababa's rivers and hills are doing exactly that, right now, in front of you.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/malaysia — paraphrased: a local recommended Taman Tugu over KLCC Park for anyone wanting an actual quiet forest experience, noting the famous park is "beautiful but basically always full of tourists taking the same photo."*

> *Expat.com Kuala Lumpur forum — paraphrased: a longtime resident described the canopy walkway at Taman Tugu as a genuine daily-exercise habit, crediting it with making the city's heat and humidity far more bearable than expected.*

> *Reddit r/Ethiopia — paraphrased: a commenter recalled the Sheger riverside area before the beautification project as an area most people actively avoided, calling the transformation "honestly hard to believe if you remember what it used to look like."*

## Conclusion

Kuala Lumpur proves that a city can grow upward without losing its canopy, protecting genuine wild rainforest as deliberately as it protected its skyline ambitions. Addis Ababa proves the opposite is also possible: that a capital can consciously, visibly rebuild green space it had lost, fast enough for its own residents to watch the change happen within a few short years. Bring insect repellent to Kuala Lumpur's forest trails and a jacket to Entoto's altitude. Both cities, in their own very different timelines, are betting that green space isn't decoration. It's infrastructure.
