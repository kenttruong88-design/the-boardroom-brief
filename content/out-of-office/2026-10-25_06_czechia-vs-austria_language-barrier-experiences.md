🇨🇿 **Czechia** vs 🇦🇹 **Austria**
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Czechia and Austria share a border, several centuries of intertwined history, and almost no patience for the same language mistakes, which tells you everything about how differently they handle a stammering foreigner. Czech is a language most expats quietly give up learning past "hello" and "beer," and the culture has largely made peace with that — younger urban Czechs code-switch to English fast and painlessly. Austria, meanwhile, speaks German, which plenty of newcomers arrive having studied for years, only to discover that textbook Hochdeutsch and actual Viennese German share perhaps seventy percent of their vocabulary and roughly none of their patience for your accent.

I've stumbled through both. Prague shrugged and switched to English within one sentence. Vienna let me finish my sentence, winced, and switched to English anyway — but made sure I knew exactly why.

## Do's & Don'ts: Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic phrases anyway — effort is noticed and genuinely appreciated, even if English takes over | Expect older civil servants or small-town shopkeepers to speak English fluently |
| Use English confidently in Prague's startup and tech scenes — it's often the actual working language | Assume Czech is easy just because it looks similar to other Slavic languages you might know |
| Accept that bureaucratic forms will likely still be Czech-only, even if the clerk speaks English | Get frustrated if a government office simply can't serve you in English — bring a Czech-speaking friend |
| Appreciate dry, deadpan humour — it travels surprisingly well across the language gap | Over-apologise for not speaking Czech; a quick acknowledgement and moving on works better |

## Do's & Don'ts: Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect formal, grammatically correct German to be genuinely expected in offices and banks | Assume your textbook German prepares you for Viennese dialect in daily speech |
| Use titles and formal "Sie" address until explicitly invited to switch to "du" | Jump straight to casual German with strangers, shopkeepers, or officials |
| Accept correction gracefully — Austrians will often fix your grammar mid-sentence, meaning well | Take language correction as rudeness; it's usually a genuine, if blunt, form of helpfulness |
| Try German first, even badly, before defaulting to English | Open every interaction in English — it can read as assuming others will accommodate you |

## Czechia: The Great English Workaround

Czech is, by near-universal expat consensus, one of the harder European languages to pick up casually — seven grammatical cases will do that to a learner's morale. The culture has adapted pragmatically rather than demanding fluency: in Prague especially, a critical mass of younger professionals, service staff, and virtually the entire tech and startup scene operate comfortably in English, to the point where some expats live years in the capital without ever needing more than survival Czech. This isn't universal — older generations, smaller towns, and anything involving a government office tend to stay firmly Czech-only — but the overall temperature is forgiveness rather than expectation.

Where it gets genuinely difficult is bureaucracy, which operates almost entirely in Czech regardless of a clerk's personal English ability, partly by policy and partly by institutional habit. Expats quickly learn that the real language barrier isn't conversational — it's administrative, encountered in visa renewals, tax forms, and health insurance paperwork that assumes a level of reading fluency casual learners never reach. The workaround almost everyone adopts is the same: find a bilingual friend, a paid translator, or a relocation agency for anything official, and reserve your own Czech-learning effort for daily life, where even badly mangled attempts get rewarded with genuine warmth rather than correction. Czechs, as a rule, would rather you try and fail charmingly than not try at all — the failure is forgiven; the absence of effort is what actually gets noticed.

Regional variation matters here too. Outside Prague and Brno, English coverage drops sharply, and older residents in smaller towns may have grown up with Russian as their mandatory second language rather than English, courtesy of the pre-1989 curriculum — a generational quirk that occasionally produces the odd, slightly surreal situation of two non-native speakers defaulting to a third, mutually awkward language just to complete a simple transaction. Younger Czechs, by contrast, have grown up steeped in English-language media and generally test as comfortably fluent, which is part of why the generational gap in language accommodation can feel so stark within the same family.

## Austria: Correct German or Bust

Vienna presents a specific trap for anyone who studied German seriously before arriving: you can be grammatically solid and still barely understand a shop assistant, a tram announcement, or your downstairs neighbour, because Austrian German and especially the Viennese dialect diverge from standard Hochdeutsch in vocabulary, rhythm, and a thick layer of regional slang no classroom covers. This is a genuinely documented phenomenon, not just a personal failing — research on Czech-Austrian contact has specifically noted how even fluent German speakers from Czechia are caught off guard by just how different lived Austrian German sounds from what they'd studied.

What compounds this is Austria's formality reflex. Offices, shops, and public institutions tend to default to correct, properly conjugated German, with titles and the formal "Sie" doing real social work — getting this wrong doesn't just mark you as foreign, it can read as mildly disrespectful, since formality signals acknowledgment of someone's position. Austrians will frequently correct a learner's grammar directly, mid-conversation, which newcomers from more conflict-averse cultures sometimes experience as cold or combative. It usually isn't; it's a cultural assumption that clear correction is a form of respect, treating you as someone capable of actually improving rather than someone to be humoured. The upshot is a language environment that rewards genuine effort and precision far more than charm — you won't get credit for trying if the trying is sloppy, in a way Czechia's more forgiving culture simply doesn't demand.

Dialect variation within Austria itself adds another wrinkle that catches out even confident German speakers: Viennese German differs meaningfully from the dialects spoken in Tyrol or Styria, meaning a learner who's adapted to the capital's speech patterns can still find themselves lost visiting the countryside. Austrians are generally aware of this internal variation and will often slow down or shift toward more standard German when speaking with a known non-native speaker — but only once they've registered you as a genuine learner making an effort, rather than someone expecting the accommodation by default.

## The Verdict

Czechia makes the language barrier emotionally easier and bureaucratically harder: nobody will judge your Czech, but you'll need a translator for anything official regardless. Austria makes the language barrier emotionally harder and bureaucratically more consistent: German competence is expected everywhere, uniformly, with no English safety net to fall back on in most non-tourist settings, but once you clear that bar, the system treats you the same as anyone else.

If I'm picking which country to be bad at the local language in, Czechia wins for sheer day-to-day survivability. But if I actually want to end up fluent rather than permanently English-dependent, Austria's unforgiving correction culture, much as it stings, gets you there faster.

> *Reddit r/Austria — a common complaint among expats was being corrected mid-sentence by shopkeepers, with several posters eventually admitting the correction actually helped their German faster than any class.*

> *expat.com Vienna forum — multiple posters described genuine shock on realising their years of German classes hadn't prepared them for the Viennese dialect spoken on the street.*

## Conclusion

Neither country is being unreasonable; they're just running different theories of hospitality. Czechia's theory is: don't make the foreigner suffer for a language this hard, switch to English, save the real demands for paperwork. Austria's theory is: hold the foreigner to the same standard as everyone else, correct them bluntly, and trust that respect is better shown through honesty than accommodation. Both get you to competence eventually. Only one of them will make you cry in a Sparkasse over an umlaut.
