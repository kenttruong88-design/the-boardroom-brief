# Italy Will Discuss Your Business at Full Volume Across an Entire Piazza. Prague Will Judge You Silently on the Tram and Never Once Say a Word About It.

🇮🇹 Italy 🇨🇿 Czech Republic
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Italian public life is conducted, essentially, out loud — arguments, affection, negotiations over the price of tomatoes, all performed at a volume and with a gestural vocabulary so expressive it borders on interpretive dance, and all of it entirely normal, unremarkable background texture to anyone who actually lives there. Czech public life, particularly on a Prague tram at 8am, operates on the opposite principle entirely: eye contact is minimal, conversation is hushed or absent, and the correct public posture is a kind of composed, watchful reserve that a newcomer can easily and wrongly read as coldness, when it's actually just an entirely different, equally coherent theory of what public space is for.

I have had an entire, passionate disagreement about parking conducted in Italian at a volume that drew a genuinely enthusiastic audience of uninvolved bystanders, several of whom offered opinions. I have also spent four months in Prague slowly learning that the silence on public transport wasn't hostility, it was simply the default setting, and that the same Czech people who seemed unreachable on the 22 tram were warm, funny, and generous the moment you were somewhere private with them.

## Italy: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the evening passeggiata — the ritual public stroll is genuinely social, not just exercise | Mistake volume or gesturing for actual anger; it's usually just normal conversational register |
| Dress with real intention even for a short errand — bella figura applies to grocery runs too | Rush a coffee at the counter standing up; savour it briefly, but don't treat the bar as a lounge |
| Greet shopkeepers on entry and exit — buongiorno and arrivederci are near-mandatory small courtesies | Cut in front of a queue assuming Italian queuing is as informal as it looks; locals notice and mind |

## Czech Republic: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep your voice down on public transport — quiet, composed conduct is the strong social default | Take initial reserve personally; it dissolves quickly once you're somewhere private with people |
| Give up your seat for elderly passengers without being asked — this norm is taken seriously | Smile broadly at strangers on the street expecting it back; it can read as slightly odd, not friendly |
| Bring a small gift if invited to someone's home — hospitality here is warm but happens in private | Assume a reserved first meeting means disinterest; Czech warmth just takes longer to surface |

## Italy: Expressiveness as the Actual Social Contract, Not a Breach of It

Italian public behaviour operates on a principle that's genuinely disorienting to anyone from a more reserved culture: expressiveness isn't a loss of composure, it is the composure, a fully developed and mutually understood language of gesture, tone, and volume that Italians deploy with total fluency and total social acceptability. A heated exchange over a parking spot, conducted loudly with both parties gesturing expansively, is not, to the people involved or to the bystanders watching with mild interest, evidence of a breakdown in civility — it's simply how disagreement is voiced here, resolved, often within minutes, with no lasting grudge and sometimes an espresso together immediately after.

Underneath the volume sits a genuinely codified set of expectations that newcomers routinely miss because the loudness distracts from the formality. Bella figura — the concept of maintaining a good public image — governs far more than it might appear: how you dress for even mundane errands, how you greet a shopkeeper (skipping buongiorno on entry is a real, if minor, faux pas), the unhurried ritual of the evening passeggiata through the piazza, dressed properly, seeing and being seen. Italians are, paradoxically, both far louder and far more socially formal in public than most visitors expect simultaneously, and missing either half of that equation — assuming the volume means informality, or assuming the formality means restraint — leads to the same category of foreigner mistake, just from opposite directions.

## Czech Republic: A Quiet Public Face Concealing a Genuinely Warm Private One

Czech public reserve has real historical texture to it — decades under a surveillance state where public expressiveness carried real risk left a lasting cultural instinct toward composed, guarded public conduct that persists, softened but recognisable, in how Czechs navigate shared space today. A crowded Prague tram in silence isn't coldness or disengagement, it's simply the accepted default: minimal eye contact, quiet voices, a kind of mutual, practised non-intrusion that Czechs themselves would likely describe as basic courtesy rather than anything worth commenting on.

What consistently catches newcomers off guard is how sharply this public reserve contrasts with Czech private warmth. The same commuter who gave you nothing on the tram will, once you're actually introduced properly — through a friend, a colleague, an invitation into their home — reveal a completely different register: genuinely funny, generous with time and hospitality, and often surprisingly direct once the initial formality clears, a directness some visitors find refreshing after the more performative warmth of other cultures. Czech social norms do carry real, consistently observed public courtesies — giving up a seat for an elderly or pregnant passenger is close to non-negotiable, queue order is respected without needing enforcement — they're just delivered without the expressive flourish that would make them legible to someone expecting warmth to look loud. The warmth is there. It's simply not on public display, and expecting it to be is the actual foreigner error.

## The Verdict

Italy makes its social contract loud, visible, and immediately legible — you know exactly where you stand within minutes, because everyone's actually saying it, often at volume. The Czech Republic keeps its social contract quiet and private, which means you'll spend longer feeling like an outsider before discovering the warmth was there the whole time, just gated behind an initial reserve. I find Italy easier and the Czech Republic more rewarding — the wait for Czech warmth to surface makes it feel earned in a way Italian friendliness, generous as it is, doesn't quite match. Italy wins on immediate accessibility. The Czech Republic wins on what's waiting once you're actually let in.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/italy — paraphrased: witnessed a loud, gesture-heavy argument between two elderly neighbours over a shared courtyard, was convinced the police would need to be called, watched them share coffee together twenty minutes later as if nothing had happened.*

> *Internations Prague — paraphrased: spent the first three months certain colleagues actively disliked them based on tram and hallway interactions, was invited to a colleague's family dinner and discovered an entirely different, warm, funny person had apparently been there the whole time.*

> *Quora — paraphrased response on Czech "coldness": it's not coldness, it's an unwillingness to perform warmth for people who haven't earned it yet, which feels harsh until you realize the warmth you do eventually get is completely genuine, not obligatory politeness.*

## Conclusion

Somewhere between an Italian argument that resolves itself over espresso and a Czech tram ride conducted in total, comfortable silence, you'll work out that loudness and warmth were never actually the same variable. Italy performs its social contract in public, generously, for anyone watching. The Czech Republic keeps its social contract private, and makes you earn the invitation in. Match the register you're given rather than importing your own, and don't mistake a quiet tram for a closed door — it's just a door that opens somewhere else, later, once you've actually been let inside.
