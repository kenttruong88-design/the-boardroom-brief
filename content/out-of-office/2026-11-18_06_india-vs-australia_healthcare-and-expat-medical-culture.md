# India Lets You Book a Specialist by Lunchtime and Australia Lets You Wait Politely

🇮🇳 India vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Indian healthcare is a market. You choose a hospital, a specialist and a time, often on the same day, and you pay or your employer's insurer does. Australian healthcare is a system. You see a GP, who decides whether you deserve a specialist, and the answer arrives in the fullness of time, in a letter, with a form.

Neither is wrong. Both are marvellous in the right circumstances and infuriating in the wrong ones. But an Indian professional arriving in Australia is, medically speaking, being moved from a shopping centre to a library.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check which hospitals your insurer covers for cashless treatment | Assume every hospital honours your card |
| Ask what your company policy covers, including parents | Discover your gaps in an emergency |
| Keep records handy for reimbursement | Rely on memory for claims |
| Compare private hospitals on cost before you commit | Choose on glossy lobbies alone |

**Australia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Hold the right visa-appropriate health cover | Assume your Indian policy counts |
| See a GP first for referrals | Expect to walk into a specialist |
| Learn what Medicare covers and what it does not | Assume dental and glasses are included |
| Mind the waiting periods on new private cover | Buy cover the day before surgery |

## India

The Indian system's most striking fact is how much of it you pay for yourself. Figures cited in an Australian migration guide say private insurance covers about nine per cent of total health spending in 2022-23, and out-of-pocket spending has fallen from about 64 per cent a decade earlier to roughly 39 to 43 per cent. About thirty per cent of Indians are uninsured by any scheme. The source does not name its primary data, so I treat the numbers as indicative.

For the corporate expat the picture is more comfortable. Multinational subsidiaries pay group premiums in rupees, and cashless treatment depends on which hospitals your insurer has tie-ups with. Parental coverage is often included, employer-funded or as an add-on, and it is a quiet differentiator when employees compare jobs. A vendor blog on how multinationals manage cover in India is the source here, and it gives no premium figures, so I will not invent any.

The great pleasure of this system is access. A Reddit post covered by Business Today in March 2025 praised same-day, affordable doctor access in India compared with long waits abroad. A physician who had worked in both India and Australia replied that Australia's universal system struggles with extra GP charges, slow specialists and poor emergency care, and told of a relative who waited six hours in pain. Others in that discussion said the UK, Canada and the EU had access problems of their own. It is anecdote, not evidence, but it is a very common anecdote.

The other thing the corporate expat will notice is how much of Indian healthcare is relational. You often know your doctor, you can message your doctor and your family doctor may be the same person who treated your parents. That is a form of continuity that a system built on rotating GPs and referral letters finds hard to match, even if its outcomes are excellent. The market sells you attention, and for many people attention is half of medicine.

The weakness is the flip side of the market: quality and price vary enormously, and a person without insurance is exposed. The best private hospitals are excellent, and the worst fall dangerously short. There is no gatekeeper to guide you, which feels like freedom until you are choosing between three cardiologists on a Wednesday.

## Australia

Australia's system is built around Medicare, which provides low or no-cost GP visits, public hospital care and some tests and medicines, but not dental, glasses or private hospital stays. Westpac's guide for migrants says some visa holders are eligible, some countries have reciprocal agreements, and visitors from certain countries, India included, may need private cover. Private policies come in hospital and extras types, and waiting periods apply.

For Indian students the rules are strict. A Migratio guide explains that student visa holders must hold Overseas Student Health Cover for the whole course, with no gaps, from approved insurers including Allianz Care, ahm, Bupa, Medibank and nib, and that an Indian private policy does not satisfy the condition. OSHC covers GP visits, admitted public hospital care, ambulance and prescription medicines on the PBS, but not dental, optical or physio, which need extras. There are twelve-month waiting periods for pregnancy and pre-existing conditions. Holders of 485 or 482 visas generally need a different type of cover. I could not verify a price, so I will say only: read the policy.

The expat forum experience is mixed. One poster on Expat.com says private cover is essential and that his mother's heart surgery still involved about a three-month wait even privately, and that, as a non-citizen, he paid the full cost; he has gone to Thailand for surgery and dental work. Another describes the GP-first pathway, mixed diagnostic experiences and complicated, expensive private insurance, but better eye care than the UK. A British citizen on a work visa was happy and said her husband's bypass was covered through the reciprocal agreement, plus bulk-billing GPs. These voices are not Indian, and I note it plainly.

There is also the matter of what Australians expect of you as a patient. The GP is a gatekeeper, and the polite way to use the system is to arrive with a problem, accept a referral and wait. Demanding a specific scan or a named specialist, as you might at a private hospital in Mumbai, is likely to be received as eccentric. The system is designed to protect public money, and it assumes that most coughs are just coughs. It is an acquired taste, but one that grows on you when the alternative is a bill.

Indian Link's coverage of University of Adelaide research is the sharpest point. Indian migrants, the researcher says, understand the system but expect direct access to specialists, often deprioritise preventive care and rely on family and community for advice. Mental-health stigma delays care. The cultural gap is not about knowledge. It is about expectation.

## The Verdict

Australia wins on safety net, and India wins on convenience. If you are seriously ill with no money, Australia's public system will treat you. If you are mildly ill with a corporate policy and a Tuesday free, India will have you in front of a consultant before the tea goes cold.

For an Indian expat in Australia, the lesson is to stop shopping and start queueing. Find a GP you like, understand your cover and use the system as designed. It will feel slow and paternal, and then, the first time you need it, quietly magnificent.

For an Australian expat in India, the lesson is to get proper insurance, ask which hospitals are covered and enjoy the freedom to choose. I prefer a gatekeeper in a crisis and a market in a sniffle, which is why I am glad I do not have to pick only one.

## Conclusion

Health systems reveal what a country thinks you owe each other. India says you owe yourself a good policy. Australia says you owe the system a visit to the GP first.

Whichever way you are moving, read your cover before you need it, find a doctor before you are ill and never assume that what worked in the old country counts in the new one. And if a stranger tells you the other system is the better one, ask them how recently they were actually sick.
