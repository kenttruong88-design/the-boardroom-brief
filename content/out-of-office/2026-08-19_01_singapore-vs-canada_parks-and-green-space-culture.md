# A Rainforest You Can Book Like a Restaurant Table Versus a Forest That Might Eat You

🇸🇬🇨🇦 By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office

Singapore has spent sixty years turning a swamp into a garden with the discipline of a Swiss watchmaker, and the result is a city where nature has been so thoroughly optimised that you half expect the butterflies to be running on a schedule. Canada, meanwhile, has more green space than it knows what to do with, so much that entire provinces are essentially unsupervised wilderness with a Tim Hortons every four hundred kilometres for morale.

Both countries will tell you they love the outdoors. One means a curated, climate-controlled biodome with a gift shop. The other means a place where you might legitimately be stalked by an apex predator on your lunchtime jog. I have sweated through both and been mildly terrified by only one.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇸🇬 Singapore**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book your Gardens by the Bay Cloud Forest slot online in advance | Turn up expecting to just wander in on a Saturday afternoon |
| Use the Park Connector Network to cycle between reservoirs | Assume "park" means anything less than immaculately manicured |
| Bring an umbrella for the 4pm thunderstorm that WILL happen | Complain about humidity — it is not going anywhere, ever |
| Respect the "no durian" signs near green spaces | Feed the otters, however photogenic they are |

**🇨🇦 Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry bear spray and know how to use it before you need it | Hike alone in grizzly country with headphones in |
| Check trail conditions before a day hike — weather turns fast | Assume a "short walk" on the map means short in practice |
| Learn the difference between a black bear and a grizzly, properly | Leave food in your tent, ever, under any circumstances |
| Tip your toque to the sheer scale of a national park system this size | Underestimate how quickly a Canadian afternoon becomes a Canadian blizzard |

## Singapore: Nature, But Make It Air-Conditioned

Singapore's approach to green space is the same as its approach to everything else: total control, immaculate execution, zero tolerance for chaos. This is a country that built an artificial rainforest inside two gigantic biodomes and then air-conditioned it, because apparently the actual tropics weren't tropical enough on Singapore's own terms. Gardens by the Bay's Cloud Forest is nine storeys of vertical garden with a 35-metre indoor waterfall, and it is, infuriatingly, one of the most beautiful things I have seen in any country — a fact I resent slightly because it was clearly achieved through sheer force of committee.

NParks manages over 400 parks and four nature reserves connected by a 300-kilometre Park Connector Network, which lets you cycle from one end of the island to the other without ever touching a road, which is either a triumph of urban planning or a subtle admission that Singapore doesn't trust its own citizens near traffic. Either way, it works. The Southern Ridges walk, an elevated boardwalk stitching together several parks, is the single best free activity in the country and I will die on this hill, ideally under some very well-maintained shade.

What nobody tells you is that "park" in Singapore rarely means "unstructured wild space." Everything is landscaped, signposted, and closed at specific hours with the efficiency of a Japanese train timetable. MacRitchie Reservoir has a genuine rainforest trail with actual monitor lizards and long-tailed macaques, which is as close as you get to danger here — and even that comes with laminated signage advising you not to make eye contact with the monkeys, advice I wish more human interactions came with. The humidity, by the way, is a permanent condition, not weather. You will sweat through your shirt walking to get more water to combat the sweating. This is simply Tuesday.

The real skill in Singapore is booking. Popular spots like the Cloud Forest or certain reservoir slots now require online reservations, turning a walk in the park into something resembling securing a table at an overhyped restaurant. Plan ahead or resign yourself to standing outside a rainforest, denied entry to nature by an app.

## Canada: Nature, Unsupervised and Occasionally Hostile

Canada has roughly 9.4 million square kilometres of land and appears to have decided that fencing any meaningful portion of it off would be, frankly, more effort than it's worth. The result is a country where "going for a walk" can mean a stroll through a manicured city park or can mean entering an ecosystem that has not been meaningfully altered by humans and contains creatures that could kill you before lunch. Parks Canada oversees over 48 national parks and reserves, and Banff alone draws around four million visitors a year, most of whom leave with a slightly haunted look and a new appreciation for bear spray retailers.

What genuinely startled me moving from a smaller, tamer country was the sheer scale-indifference of Canadian green space. Stanley Park in Vancouver is a 400-hectare urban forest bigger than New York's Central Park, sitting right next to a downtown of glass towers, and you can go from a business meeting to genuinely getting lost among old-growth trees in under fifteen minutes. Toronto's ravine system threads through the entire city like green veins, and locals treat wandering into these forested gullies with the same casualness Londoners treat the Tube — except the Tube does not, generally, contain coyotes.

Then there's the wildlife briefing every newcomer eventually receives, usually from a bemused local at a barbecue: bears are not a metaphor here, they are a Tuesday afternoon problem. You learn to make noise on trails so you don't startle a grizzly, you learn the difference between "play dead" (grizzly) and "fight back" (black bear) advice, and you learn that a sign reading "cougar sighting reported" is not decorative. Canadians discuss this with the same shrugging nonchalance the British reserve for discussing rain, which took me an embarrassingly long time to stop finding alarming.

The reward for this low-level, ambient danger is access to genuinely wild, unbothered nature on a scale most countries simply cannot offer — no booking app required, mostly because no algorithm has yet worked out how to charge you for a mountain.

## The Verdict

If you want nature that has been sanded down, curated, and delivered to you with the reliability of a five-star hotel turndown service, Singapore wins decisively — it is simply the most beautiful controlled environment on Earth, and I say that with genuine, if slightly grudging, admiration. If you want nature that does not care about your itinerary, your safety, or your feelings, Canada has that in cinematic abundance, along with the actual bears to prove it.

For pure loveliness-per-square-metre, Singapore takes it. For scale, wonder, and the faint thrill of being reminded you are, biologically, still prey — Canada wins, and wins big. Choose based on whether you'd rather book your sunset or be chased by something that also wants it.

## Conclusion

Singapore proves that with enough money, humidity, and committee meetings, you can build something that looks suspiciously like Eden and still charge you nothing to enter, provided you booked ahead. Canada proves that if you simply leave several million square kilometres alone, nature will happily remind you who was here first, teeth and all. Neither approach is wrong. One just requires a reservation app, and the other requires a will to live. Pack accordingly, and for heaven's sake, stop calling either of them "exotic" — they're both just doing what their landscapes were always going to do, with or without your Instagram caption.
