# France Joins a Gym to Say Hello and Switzerland Joins a Mountain

🇫🇷 France vs 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular type of expat who arrives in Europe carrying a protein shaker and a firm belief that the gym is a religion. They are wrong, of course. The gym is a religion in California. In France and Switzerland it is a lightly tolerated hobby, performed with the same enthusiasm one brings to dental appointments.

These two neighbours share a border, a language in places and a deep suspicion of anyone who sweats in public for fun. And yet they have arrived at completely different answers to the question of how to stay in shape. France does it with a shrug, a walk and a bakery. Switzerland does it with a mountain, a lake and a plan so organised that you will feel guilty for taking the lift.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇫🇷 France ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say bonjour and au revoir to the people in the gym | Walk in and out like a ghost |
| Read the contract carefully and ask for a draft to take home | Sign at the desk on the first visit |
| Check the opening hours, which may be short and closed on Sunday | Assume it opens at 5.30am |
| Walk or take the stairs as part of daily life | Treat the commute as dead exercise time |

| 🇨🇭 Switzerland ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the outdoors: lakes, trails and the mountains | Pay for a gym and ignore the free view outside the window |
| Book classes in advance and turn up on time | Arrive ten minutes late and expect a space |
| Respect quiet hours and keep the noise down | Drop your weights on the floor like it is a show |
| Budget for it, because prices tend to run high | Assume a Swiss gym costs what a French one does |

## 🇫🇷 France

The first thing to understand about a French gym is that it is largely a social space with weights in it. A British-born writer in Brittany described how members at his local gym greet each other as they arrive and say goodbye as they leave, and I have never felt so aware of my own bad manners as the first time I walked in and said nothing. Most people dress in black, as if the machines were a funeral for lunch. There is no performance, no leaderboard, no bro in a tank top shouting at the dumbbell rack.

The practical side is less romantic. A consumer investigation in 2022 and 2023 covered 571 gyms and found more than 70 per cent had at least one irregularity, with 46 per cent carrying unfair or illegal contract clauses. The Connexion advises you to ask for an unsigned contract to read at home, notes you can cancel within 14 days in many cases, and says an online contract must be cancellable online. France has around 5,000 gyms against about 7,000 in the UK, which tells you something about priorities. A 2014 post from a small town found a gym costing 46 euros a month, closed on Sundays and by one o'clock on Saturday, and with no air conditioning at all, so summer workouts were hot enough to count as a sauna session.

And the culture is different. Personal training is not a huge industry, women are less likely to lift heavy weights, and boutique studios are rare outside the big cities. An expat in Rennes joined a budget chain and noticed that everybody else was French and nobody was in a hurry. Another in Nice said the city is flat and walkable, so most of their exercise happened without them noticing. That is the secret: France does not exercise as an activity. It exercises as a side effect of living in a place where you walk to the market.

The French also approach exercise as a civic matter rather than a vanity project. A gym membership can be tied to a voucher scheme for children, with a 50 euro PassSport covering part of a young person's sports fees, and if a contract goes wrong there is a defined complaint route: a registered letter, a mediator, a consumer complaints website and then the courts. It is a bureaucratic way to deal with biceps, and it fits the national character. The fitness is done on the way to somewhere else: stairs instead of the lift, a metro stop earlier, the flat walk to the market with a shopping bag. Nobody calls it a workout. They call it Tuesday.

## 🇨🇭 Switzerland

Switzerland takes a different view, which is that exercise is something you do in the open air, in sensible clothes, with the correct equipment, before anyone else has woken up. I should be frank that I do not have a hard number to hand for you here, and I will not invent one. What I can tell you is what the country feels like: the lake swimmers who go in at seven in the morning, the walkers with poles who look as though they have been issued by the state, the cyclists in pristine kit, and the mountain, always the mountain, sitting there like a very large personal trainer who is also available at weekends.

Gyms exist, naturally, and they are clean, quiet and expensive. They are also organised in a way that would make a French person faint. Classes start on time. The equipment is spotless. Nobody leaves their sweat on the bench, because the culture of order does not bend for a squat rack. If you want to be loud, you will not enjoy it. If you want a perfectly maintained space with a peaceful sauna where nobody speaks, you will think you have died and gone to a very well-run spa.

But the truth is that the real Swiss gym is the country itself. You do not need a membership to hike, swim in a lake or ski. You need decent shoes and a train ticket, and the train will leave exactly on time. It is exercise as a civic habit, free, beautiful and faintly judgemental. Nobody will say anything if you skip it. They will simply be fit while you decide.

What would a Swiss gym make of the French habit of closing on Sunday and skipping the air conditioning? I suspect it would file a formal complaint and then arrive at 6am to prove a point. The truth is that both countries are self-disciplined in their own way. France disciplines through pleasure and habit, Switzerland through scheduling and landscape. I should add, in the interest of fairness, that I did not retrieve a Swiss source for this piece, and I have therefore kept my claims about it impressionistic. Anyone who lives there is welcome to correct my sweeping gestures with a very precise spreadsheet.

## The Verdict

Switzerland wins on fitness and France wins on life, and I know which one I would choose for a fortnight and which for a decade. If your goal is to become physically excellent, to run up hills and swim in clear water and eat well with a reasonable conscience, the Swiss way is superior. The environment is a gym with a view, and the culture takes your health seriously.

France, however, understands something the Swiss do not, which is that nobody wants to be fit at the cost of being a bore. The French gym is a pleasant place to nod at neighbours, a lazy bit of motion between meals and a guilt-free zone where nobody expects you to be a machine. It is a poor place to build a six-pack and a very good place to build a life.

My ruling is Switzerland for the body and France for the soul. If you can only pick one, pick the one that serves cheese after the workout. I recognise this makes me an unreliable authority on fitness, and I stand by it. The mountain does not care about my opinion, and the baker does.

## Conclusion

The French do not go to the gym to improve. They go to continue. The Swiss do not go to the gym at all, because the entire nation is already one. This is the basic truth of fitness in Europe and nobody in a boutique studio wants to hear it: it is the environment that does the work, and the gym is just a room.

Pack trainers for Switzerland, pack manners for France, and in both countries pack your own towel and your own opinions. Say hello when you walk in, read the contract before you sign and be prepared for the mountain to be unimpressed. It has seen better.
