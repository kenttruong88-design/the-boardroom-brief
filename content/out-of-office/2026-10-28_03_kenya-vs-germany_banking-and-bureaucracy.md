# Show Me Your Papers, Then I'll Show You Your Money

🇰🇪 Kenya vs 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country has a founding myth about money. Kenya's is that a teenager with a cheap phone and a Safaricom SIM can out-innovate every central bank on earth, and largely it has. Germany's is that money is far too serious a matter to be handed to anyone who hasn't first proven, in triplicate, that they definitely live somewhere. Both countries are right. Both are also, in their own special way, going to make you want to lie face-down on a government office floor.

I have opened bank accounts on four continents, and nothing tests the soul quite like the bureaucratic choreography these two have invented. Kenya skipped the bank entirely and built a parallel financial universe on a phone network. Germany kept the bank, kept the paper, kept the queue, and invented a credit score so opaque even Germans don't understand it. One nation solved banking by ignoring banks. The other solved it by worshipping them. Neither was designed with your sanity in mind.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Kenya**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for M-Pesa on day one — it is the actual banking system | Assume a "real" bank account is the priority; for most daily life, it isn't |
| Get your KRA PIN sorted before you need it for anything logistical, like shipping a container | Wait until a shipping agent or landlord demands it on a deadline |
| Bring your employer reference letter, passport, and residency documents to every bank visit, every time | Expect consistent rules between branches, let alone between banks |
| Ask other expats which specific branch manager is known to actually open accounts for foreigners | Trust a bank's official website requirements over what the teller in front of you decides today |

**Germany**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book your Anmeldung (address registration) appointment the day you sign a lease, before you've even unpacked | Assume you can "get to it later" — half of German adult life is gated behind this one stamp |
| Consider a branchless digital bank (N26, Comdirect) if you need an account before you have a fixed address | Expect English-language service outside a handful of big-city branches |
| Treat your Schufa score as a precious, fragile object from the moment your first account opens it | Miss a phone contract or rent payment, assuming it won't follow you — it absolutely will |
| Keep physical, paper copies of everything; German bureaucracy still worships the printed page | Try to resolve anything administrative by email and expect a fast, or even eventual, reply |

## Kenya

Kenya's great trick is that it never asked permission to leapfrog the twentieth century. While Germany perfected the art of the stamped form, Kenya built M-Pesa, and within a few years more Kenyans were moving money through their phones than through any branch network the old banks had bothered to build outside Nairobi and Mombasa. The Central Bank of Kenya's own FinAccess Household Survey, run jointly with the national statistics office, keeps confirming what anyone standing in a matatu queue already knows: mobile money, not a teller window, is Kenya's real financial infrastructure. The nation decided bureaucracy was too slow, so it built an entirely new economy to go around it.

Which makes the *actual* banks, when you do need one, feel like visiting a parallel, more suspicious civilisation. Expat forums are soaked in stories of inconsistency: one bank happily hands a tourist an account with nothing but a passport and a local referee, another flatly tells you a work permit is "mandatory," a third will do it, but only as a joint account with your Kenyan spouse, thank you very much. On the Kenya expat.com forum, one exasperated foreigner was told by Chase that citizenship-adjacent paperwork was non-negotiable, while a Standard Chartered staffer cited anti-terrorism rules for a residence permit nobody had mentioned a week earlier. The requirements aren't wrong, exactly. They're just entirely dependent on which human is behind the glass that day, and whether they've had their tea.

Then there's the KRA PIN — Kenya's tax identification number, which you will not need until the moment you desperately do, usually when a shipping company refuses to release your container. One forum thread captures it perfectly: a man stuck in a genuine Kafka loop, his shipping company refusing to move his belongings without a PIN, his PIN application stuck waiting on an alien ID card that hadn't been issued yet. Nobody in the thread could agree which document came first. The system works beautifully, right up until it needs you to prove something, at which point it becomes a philosophical exercise in which bureaucratic chicken caused which egg.

## Germany

Germany, by contrast, has turned banking bureaucracy into a religious initiation, complete with sacred texts (the Schufa report), a priesthood (Sachbearbeiter, the civil servants who process your forms), and an opening ritual so famous it has its own noun: the Anmeldung. This is the address-registration certificate every resident must obtain from the Bürgeramt, required, depending on the bank, for almost everything that follows — the bank account, the phone contract, the health insurance, occasionally your own sense of existing. BaFin, Germany's federal financial regulator, will tell you cheerfully that everyone has a legal right to a "Basiskonto," a basic account, regardless of address or status. What it won't tell you is how many Bürgeramt slots in Berlin vanish within ninety seconds of opening online.

The Reddit horror stories write themselves, because Germans write them constantly, with the weary precision of people filing an insurance claim against their own government. One especially pure specimen, posted to r/Ingolstadt, described a newcomer who couldn't register their address until they moved into their apartment, couldn't open a bank account without the registration, and needed the account to pay the deposit and rent that would let them move in at all. This is not an edge case. This is Tuesday. Germany invented a circular bureaucratic dependency so elegant it should be hanging in a museum, ideally one requiring three forms of ID to enter.

And once you get an account, the real game begins: Schufa, the private credit bureau that silently scores you from your first contract, punishing a single missed phone bill with reputational damage usually reserved for royal scandals. Nobody explains Schufa to you. You simply learn, slowly, painfully, that it governs everything from your next apartment to your mobile plan, and the only way to fix a bad score is to behave, for years, like an extremely boring adult.

There is a certain dark comedy in how both countries, near-opposites on every other metric, land on the same emotional truth: the newcomer is presumed guilty until enough paper proves otherwise. Kenya just does it with more charm and considerably faster Wi-Fi.

## The Verdict

If I'm grading on raw innovation, Kenya wins in a landslide. M-Pesa didn't just fix banking for Kenyans, it became the template the rest of the developing world has spent fifteen years trying to copy, badly. That's a country solving a problem the rest of the planet is still arguing about in committee.

But if I'm grading on *predictability* — whether I can walk in on a Tuesday and know what will happen — Germany wins, barely, bitterly, through gritted teeth. The Anmeldung-Schufa doom loop is infuriating, but it's infuriating in the same documented, BaFin-governed way every single time. Kenya's banks will surprise you, and not always pleasantly.

So here's the sting: Kenya built the better system and forgot to tell its own banks about it. Germany built a worse system and wrote it down so thoroughly you can at least complain in writing and wait eight weeks for a rejection stamped in triplicate. Neither deserves your gratitude. Both deserve your paperwork, filed early, filed often, with the quiet rage of someone who has done this fourteen times and still isn't over it.

## Conclusion

Here is the thing nobody tells you before relocating anywhere: banking bureaucracy is never really about banking. It's a stress test for how a country feels about outsiders, dressed up as "requirements." Kenya's chaos is at least an honest chaos — human, inconsistent, occasionally charming, powered by a financial revolution it built almost by accident. Germany's chaos is a bureaucratic Möbius strip engineered, apparently on purpose, by people who never once need to use it themselves.

Pack patience for both. Pack a portable charger for Kenya, so your M-Pesa never dies at the worst moment. Pack an actual, physical, tabbed folder for Germany, because somewhere a Sachbearbeiter is waiting to reject your application for being three millimetres out of spec. And pack, above all, the understanding that no amount of prior relocating prepares you for either. I've done this fourteen times. I still flinch at the word "Anmeldung." Some reflexes never leave you.
