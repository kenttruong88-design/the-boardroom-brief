# China Buys Dinner Alive at Seven in the Morning and Australia Buys It Shrink-Wrapped on a Tuesday

🇨🇳 China vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In a Chinese city, dinner begins as a conversation. You walk to the wet market or the neighbourhood fruit stall, you squeeze a pear, you question a fishmonger about the exact age of the fish and he answers with the weary pride of a man who has been asked this question every day since 1994. Or you tap an app, and a rider with a thermal bag brings you a bag of tomatoes within thirty minutes. Either way, it is quick, local and shamelessly fresh.

In an Australian city, dinner begins in a very large, very clean building owned by one of two companies. You push a trolley the size of a small car down aisles lit like an operating theatre, past forty kinds of milk and a wall of sauces named after a celebrity chef, and you leave with enough food to survive a siege. It is orderly, comfortable and strangely joyless, and it has no idea how beloved it is by people who have just arrived and cannot read the labels.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**China**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shop early at wet markets when produce is at its best | Arrive at closing time and expect a bargain on quality |
| Use the major apps for delivery and mobile payment | Carry only cash and wonder why nobody takes it |
| Ask the seller how to cook an unfamiliar ingredient | Be shy about asking questions |
| Check the date on packaged goods | Assume that everything on the shelf is fresh |

**Australia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring reusable bags if your state charges for them | Expect free plastic bags at the checkout |
| Compare prices with the Asian grocers and warehouse stores | Buy everything at the big two and complain about the bill |
| Use the self-checkout and scan carefully | Walk out without paying for something that did not scan |
| Learn the weekly specials cycle | Buy at full price when half price starts on Wednesday |

## China

A Chinese grocery run is a choice between three or four systems that all work. The traditional wet market remains a social institution in many neighbourhoods, a noisy theatre of live fish, whole chickens and stalls of leafy greens arranged with the care of an art installation. Prices are flexible, quality is visible and the vendor knows your family by name. Supermarket chains offer cleanliness and consistency, and the newer fresh-food chains and delivery apps have made it possible to get produce at your door faster than you can find your keys.

Mobile payment runs through everything. You scan, you pay, you leave. Cash is accepted in principle and viewed with polite suspicion in practice. For a visitor, setting up payment can be the real obstacle, and expat guides spend more time on this than on the food. Once you are set up, though, a grocery run is almost frictionless.

The food culture underneath is what makes the system special. Seasonality matters. Freshness is not a marketing claim but a basic expectation, and a cook can tell whether a fish was alive that morning. Many households shop daily, not weekly, because food is meant to be bought close to the moment it is eaten. The idea of a fortnightly shop would strike many Chinese families as bordering on neglect.

What can frustrate an expat is the sheer variety of the unfamiliar. Cuts of meat that do not exist in Western butchers. Vegetables with no English name. Labels in a script you cannot read and an ingredient you cannot identify. It is an education, and the best response is to ask. Vendors are usually delighted to explain.

Fresh produce has a cultural meaning in China that is hard to translate. A shopper judges a vegetable by smell, colour and weight, and the habit of choosing, not merely picking, is part of the ritual. In Australia, many items arrive packed, graded and priced by the kilo, with the choosing largely done for you. It is convenient and a little bit like being served a meal by a committee.

## Australia

The Australian supermarket is dominated by two large chains, and a competition regulator has spent considerable effort examining just how that arrangement affects prices. For a newcomer it is the first thing to understand: the convenience is real, the choice on the shelf is large, and the price of a basket of groceries has a way of surprising people who expected the country to be cheap because it is rich in farmland.

The shopping culture is weekly, car-based and planned. People drive to a large store, fill a trolley and drive home. Specials rotate on a schedule that regulars memorise, and half-price offers drive behaviour like a quiet religion. Self-checkout is widespread, and it will, at some point, tell you that there is an unexpected item in the bagging area, which there is not.

For Chinese newcomers, the response has been to build a parallel system. Asian grocery stores, warehouse-style retailers and specialist suppliers have grown precisely because the mainstream aisles do not stock what many households want: the right soy sauce, the right greens, the right cut of pork. SBS and other outlets regularly cover where to find the best value, and the answer is rarely the large chains. A Chinese-owned fresh-food business that studied the Australian giants closely has been a case study in what they do well and what they leave on the table.

The result is a quietly divided market. If you want a pavlova base, a fistful of snags and a tin of beetroot, the big supermarkets will supply them. If you want fresh bok choy and live fish, you will be taking a different route, and you will be happier for it.

There is a plus side to the Australian way that newcomers soon appreciate: strict food safety, clear labelling and a reliable cold chain. Allergens are listed, origins are stated and the price on the shelf is the price at the till. After a few months, even the most devoted market-lover will admit to a quiet affection for the sheer predictability of it all.

## The Verdict

China wins on freshness, variety, speed and the sheer joy of food shopping as an activity rather than a chore. Australia wins on consistency, cleanliness and the comfort of knowing that no two trolleys will ever collide at a market stall.

I will give the verdict to China, with reservations. A Chinese food market gives you the sense that dinner has a story. An Australian supermarket gives you the sense that dinner has a barcode. One of those is a more interesting way to live.

That said, Australia has an advantage worth admitting: nobody will ever try to sell you a fish you did not ask about, and the checkout will not hurry you. For a certain kind of tired expat, that is paradise.

## Conclusion

If you move from China to Australia, you will mourn the market, accept the trolley and spend three months hunting for the right chilli oil. If you move the other way, you will wonder why anyone puts up with a supermarket that does not smell of anything.

Food is where a country keeps its priorities. China keeps freshness at the front. Australia keeps convenience. You will end up shopping in both ways, in whichever country you land in, and complaining about both. That, I am afraid, is what it means to eat abroad.
