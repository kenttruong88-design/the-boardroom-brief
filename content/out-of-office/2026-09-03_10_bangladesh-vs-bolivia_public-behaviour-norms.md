# One Country's Streets Never Stop Moving, the Other's Never Stop Bowing

🇧🇩 Bangladesh 🇧🇴 Bolivia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Dhaka and La Paz sit at genuinely opposite ends of the public behaviour spectrum, and the contrast hits you within about ten minutes of arriving in either. Dhaka is one of the most densely packed cities on earth, and its public behaviour reflects that reality with total honesty: constant motion, minimal personal space, horns as a form of ongoing conversation rather than an emergency signal, and a collective acceptance that crowding is simply the price of urban life. La Paz, by contrast, particularly in its indigenous Aymara-influenced neighbourhoods, runs on a formal, almost ceremonial layer of public courtesy, greetings exchanged properly, respect shown to elders visibly and specifically, personal interactions given the time they're due even in a busy market.

I've been swept along Dhaka's Gulistan crossing in a human current that had its own gravitational pull, and I've stood in a La Paz market watching a vendor pause an entire transaction to greet an elderly customer with genuine, unhurried formality. Both experiences taught me that "crowded" and "polite" aren't actually opposites, they're just different solutions to the same basic question of how strangers should treat each other in public.

## Do's & Don'ts: Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept close physical proximity on streets and public transport as completely normal | Expect quiet, personal-space-respecting queues, crowd movement here is fluid, not linear |
| Use your right hand for eating, giving, and receiving, it's a genuinely important norm | Be alarmed by constant horn use, it's communication, not aggression, in Dhaka traffic |
| Dress modestly in public regardless of the heat, it's expected and widely appreciated | Take photos of people, especially women, without asking first, it's considered intrusive |

## Do's & Don'ts: Bolivia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet shopkeepers and market vendors properly before starting any transaction, it matters | Rush a greeting or skip it entirely, this reads as genuinely rude, not just brisk |
| Show visible respect to elders in public spaces, offering seats, using formal address | Touch or photograph indigenous women's traditional dress without clearly asking permission first |
| Use formal "usted" rather than casual "tú" with strangers and older people | Assume the relaxed Andean pace means informality, courtesy here is precise, not casual |

## Bangladesh: Crowding as a Way of Life, Not a Failure of Planning

Dhaka's population density is genuinely difficult to grasp until you're standing inside it, and public behaviour here has adapted with a kind of pragmatic acceptance that outsiders often mistake, at first, for chaos. It isn't chaos, it's a functioning, if intense, choreography. Personal space, in the Western sense of an invisible buffer zone around your body, simply isn't part of the operating system, on a rickshaw, on a bus, in a market, physical closeness with strangers is constant and unremarkable, and Bangladeshis navigate it with an ease that took me genuine time to learn.

The soundscape tells its own story. Horns in Dhaka traffic aren't primarily expressions of frustration, they're an ongoing informational broadcast, "I'm here," "I'm passing," "watch this gap," a communication system layered over the visual chaos that locals read fluently and visitors initially find overwhelming. Learning to stop flinching at every horn blast is, genuinely, a rite of passage.

Underneath the density, though, runs a layer of specific, non-negotiable etiquette that matters enormously despite the apparent free-for-all. The right-hand rule for eating, giving, and receiving objects is taken seriously, a holdover from the same broader South Asian cultural logic that governs so much regional etiquette. Modesty in dress remains expected and appreciated regardless of the heat. And photographing strangers, particularly women, without asking is a real, meaningful boundary violation, not a minor faux pas, something visitors with cameras would do well to remember before they start snapping away at the "colourful chaos."

## Bolivia: Formality as the Backbone of Public Respect

La Paz operates on almost the inverse principle. Density isn't the defining feature of daily public life here the way it is in Dhaka, what defines it instead is a deep, specific formality rooted in Aymara and Quechua cultural traditions layered onto Spanish colonial etiquette. Greetings aren't a throwaway pleasantry, they're a genuine social obligation, and skipping one, rushing into a transaction or a conversation without the proper opening exchange, registers as a real, if quiet, breach of manners.

This formality extends visibly to how age and status are treated in public. Elders are greeted specifically and respectfully, offered seats without hesitation, addressed with the formal "usted" rather than casual "tú" even by people who'd default to informality almost anywhere else in Latin America. Market interactions, even brief ones, tend to include a proper opening greeting and a bit of unhurried human exchange before business gets underway, a pace that can genuinely frustrate visitors used to transactional efficiency, right up until they realise the slower pace is the actual point.

There's also real cultural sensitivity around indigenous dress and customs that visitors need to navigate carefully. The traditional pollera skirts and bowler hats worn by many Aymara and Quechua women aren't costume or spectacle, they're everyday cultural dress, and treating them as a photo opportunity without asking, a habit some tourists bring from elsewhere, is a fast way to cause genuine, justified offence. Respect here isn't abstract, it's specific, learnable, and, once you get it right, warmly reciprocated.

## The Verdict

Bangladesh wins on sheer resilient adaptability, a society that's turned extreme density into a workable, even oddly graceful, daily rhythm without losing its core etiquette rules along the way. Bolivia wins on the depth and consistency of its formal courtesy, a public culture where respect isn't optional politeness, it's structural, expected, and specific. If you want to learn to move fluidly through genuine crowding without losing your patience or your manners, Dhaka will train you fast. If you want to relearn the actual weight a proper greeting can carry, La Paz will quietly, patiently, correct you until you get it right.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — "What surprises visitors most about Dhaka street life?" paraphrased: a longtime resident said the constant horns stopped registering as noise within a month, and that visiting a quiet city afterward felt oddly unsettling, like something was missing.*

> *expat.com Dhaka forum, paraphrased: a newcomer admitted they'd unconsciously started eating and passing items with only their right hand even back home, months after leaving, because the habit had become that deeply ingrained.*

> *InterNations La Paz, paraphrased: an expat described being gently but firmly corrected by a market vendor for skipping the greeting and going straight to asking a price, and said they never made that mistake again.*

## Conclusion

Public behaviour norms aren't really about crowding or space at all, they're about what a society has decided strangers owe each other in the moment they cross paths. Dhaka's answer is flow: keep moving, stay close, communicate constantly, and trust the system to sort itself out. Bolivia's answer is form: slow down, greet properly, show your respect visibly, and let courtesy set the pace. Both work. Both will, in their own way, quietly reprogram how you move through a crowd for the rest of your life.
