# Bangladesh Floods On Schedule. Australia Just Catches Fire Instead.

🇧🇩 Bangladesh · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country thinks its weather is the dramatic one. The British think a light drizzle is a national emergency. The French think forty degrees in Paris, a city built entirely of stone that absorbs and radiates heat like a pizza oven, is somehow a personality trait rather than a planning failure. Bangladesh and Australia, to their enormous credit, have actual weather — the kind that rearranges a calendar year around itself rather than the other way round. One drowns on a schedule. The other sets itself alight on a schedule. Both have turned catastrophe into choreography, and I find that more impressive than charming.

I have sat out a monsoon in Dhaka with water lapping at a rickshaw's axle and I have sat out a 46°C heatwave in Melbourne with the sky the colour of a bruise from bushfire smoke two states away. Neither experience made me want to complain about British drizzle again. Both made me deeply respect the sheer administrative competence it takes to live somewhere the weather tries to kill you annually and simply... continue going to work.

## Do's & Don'ts: Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build your June–September calendar around the monsoon, not against it | Schedule anything time-critical during peak flooding without a buffer day |
| Keep valuables and documents in waterproof bags as a permanent habit, not a panic measure | Assume a dry commute home means a dry commute back |
| Watch how locals route around low-lying streets — they know the flood map by muscle memory | Wade through standing floodwater if you can avoid it — sanitation runoff is the real hazard, not the water itself |
| Respect the Bangladesh Meteorological Department's warnings — they're conservative for good reason | Treat monsoon season as an inconvenience rather than the agricultural lifeline it actually is |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the Bureau of Meteorology's fire danger rating before any summer bushwalk, every single time | Assume a clear sky means clean air — bushfire smoke travels for days across states |
| Have a heatwave plan — hydration, shade hours, and a cool public space mapped nearby | Leave pets or anyone vulnerable in a car, even "for a minute," during extreme heat |
| Take total fire ban days literally — a single spark has ended towns | Underestimate how fast conditions shift from "pleasant barbecue" to "evacuate the district" |
| Sign up for state emergency service alerts the week you arrive | Assume the beach is a safe refuge from heat without checking surf and UV conditions too |

## Bangladesh: Weather as the Nation's Operating System

In Bangladesh, the monsoon isn't weather in the way a Londoner means weather — small talk, umbrella-related. It's closer to a second, temporary government. From roughly June to September, entire cities quietly reorganise themselves around the water: rickshaw drivers raise their seats, shopkeepers build little brick thresholds to keep floodwater out of doorways that have clearly flooded before, and everyone develops an uncanny internal map of exactly which streets will be ankle-deep by 4pm and which will be waist-deep by dusk.

What gets lost in the international flood-footage coverage is how functional this all is. Bangladesh is, per capita, one of the most climate-exposed nations on earth, and it has also become one of the most sophisticated at short-notice disaster response — early warning systems, cyclone shelters, community evacuation drills that put wealthier nations to shame. The monsoon isn't purely disaster, either: it's the reason the delta is one of the most fertile growing regions on the planet, the water that makes the rice that feeds the country. Locals don't fight the monsoon so much as negotiate a seasonal truce with it, and the negotiation is a genuinely impressive piece of civic choreography that outsiders mistake for chaos because it doesn't look like a spreadsheet.

The humidity, separate from the flooding entirely, is its own daily opponent — a wet 35°C that makes a strong case for scheduling anything physically demanding before 9am and conceding the rest of the afternoon to ceiling fans and cold tea.

## Australia: Weather as a Slow-Motion Horror Film With Excellent Production Values

Australia's relationship with its own climate is a masterclass in collective denial dressed up as stoicism. Summer arrives, temperatures nudge toward the mid-40s, and the national response is to have a barbecue. This is either magnificent or deeply concerning, and after a decade of visiting I've landed on both, simultaneously.

The heat itself is almost the smaller story. The real main event is fire season — a months-long, slow-building dread that peaks with total fire ban days, apocalyptic orange skies hundreds of kilometres from any actual flame, and a national Bureau of Meteorology fire-danger rating system that Australians check with the same daily reflex everyone else uses for the regular weather forecast. Entire regional communities now run fire-season routines with the grim efficiency of a place that has rebuilt before and will likely need to again: defensible space cleared around the house, a bag packed, a plan for the pets, a second plan for when the first plan's road is also on fire.

What's remarkable, and a little unsettling, is how normalised it's all become. People plan holidays around fire season the way other nations plan around a rainy month. Smoke haze gets its own category on the air quality app next to pollen count. The beach — Australia's great cultural pressure-release valve — becomes a genuine public health service during a heatwave, less leisure than a nationally endorsed cooling strategy with lifeguards as its enforcement arm.

## The Verdict

Bangladesh's weather is older than its infrastructure and the country has built an entire culture of resilience around working with it. Australia's weather is increasingly unmoored from the climate its infrastructure was built for, and the country is still, charmingly, catching up to its own new normal with sunscreen and vibes. If I had to pick a seasonal disaster to live inside, I'd take the monsoon — at least the water recedes on a predictable timetable. Fire season just lingers, a held breath across an entire continent, for months.

## Conclusion

Both countries have made peace, in their own stubborn way, with weather that would send most nations into permanent crisis mode. Bangladesh does it through generations of inherited know-how and a shrug that says the water always goes down eventually. Australia does it through bravado, a Bureau of Meteorology app everyone actually reads, and an unshakeable belief that the barbecue must go on. Visit either during peak season and you'll understand, within about a day, that "how locals cope" isn't really a tips list. It's a personality the entire nation has adopted, out of necessity, and now can't imagine living without.
