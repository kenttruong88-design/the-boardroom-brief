# Japan Runs on Time and Korea Runs on Wi-Fi, and Both Will Judge Your Phone Call

🇯🇵 Japan vs 🇰🇷 South Korea

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have been shoved onto trains in Tokyo by a man in white gloves and I have been ushered through glass doors in Seoul by a platform that looked as if it had been designed by NASA. Both experiences were perfect, and both left me quietly aware that my home country's transport network is a cautionary tale told to children.

Japan and South Korea are the two great commuter nations of East Asia, and they get lazily lumped together by people who think the only difference is the alphabet. It is not. One system is a miracle of private companies trading information at the speed of a nervous breakdown. The other is a public machine that has decided the passenger should not have to think. Let me explain why you will have an opinion by Friday.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇯🇵 Japan ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Put your phone on silent and keep calls for the platform | Take a call on the train and expect no one to notice |
| Get a rechargeable IC card and tap in and out | Queue at a ticket machine for every single journey |
| Download the station map for each operator in advance | Assume one map covers every company's line |
| Stand in the marked boarding lines and let people off first | Barge on and discover how silent contempt feels |

| 🇰🇷 South Korea ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Tap your transport card at every entry, exit and transfer | Forget to tap out and stand embarrassed at the gate |
| Use the numbered exits and platform doors to find your way | Wander the station guessing which exit is yours |
| Leave the priority seats for older and pregnant passengers | Sit in them and pretend to be asleep |
| Use the bike share for the last mile | Assume it is a tourist gimmick |

## 🇯🇵 Japan

Japan's trains are famous for punctuality, and the fame is deserved. A writer who rode both systems said you could set your clock by the Tokyo trains, and that is not a metaphor. A late train hands out a certificate so your boss will believe you. The system is a patchwork of different companies running different lines with different fares, and the genius is that the whole thing looks seamless on the platform and feels like a puzzle in your head. A single trip might cross two operators, two prices and two speeds, which is why every sensible resident has an app and a rechargeable card and the patience of a saint.

The unspoken rules are where Japan truly lives. Silence is a courtesy. You do not take calls. You hold your backpack in front of you. You fold into the corner like a polite origami swan. The rush hour is a sort of mass choreography in which thousands of strangers breathe in unison and do not look at each other, and I find it more moving than any cathedral. For the foreigner, the actual hazard is language: the big terminal maps are often in Japanese only, and one visitor admitted she had to lean on a companion who could read them. It is solvable, but it is a tax on your first month.

What Japan does not do is make it easy. It makes it excellent. There is a difference, and anyone who has stood in Shinjuku at 8.40 in the morning feeling like a lost parcel understands it. The ride is flawless; the finding of it is yours.

Tokyo's other quirk is its sheer mass of private operators, which a visitor discovers the hard way. Different companies mean different fares, different ticket logic and different transfer rules, and the rewards for mastering them are a lifelong feeling of being quietly clever. The writer who compared the two systems found fares roughly similar and suggested a rechargeable card for any longer stay, which is the only advice that matters. Add the pleasant discovery that trains in Tokyo, like in Seoul, do not run all night, and you quickly learn to treat the last train as a social deadline. Miss it and your evening becomes a taxi, a capsule hotel or a very long chat with a stranger.

## 🇰🇷 South Korea

South Korea took the same idea and decided to make it comprehensible. A single public system, a clear card, and signage in multiple languages that actually appears where you need it. Stations, exits and platform doors are numbered, which sounds boring until you realise you can say 'meet me at exit seven' and the entire problem of meeting strangers in a metropolis goes away. The writer who rode both found Seoul's English-language signs easy and Tokyo's a hunt.

Then there are the platform doors. Floor-to-ceiling glass at every station keeps people off the tracks and, as one former New York transit planner noted, makes the stations cooler and quieter. A Tokyo station often has a barrier about waist high, and some have nothing. Seoul has the full wall and the cars are wider, which means you are less likely to be eaten by your neighbour's elbow. Fares are modest, the bike-share scheme is vast and cheap, and the whole thing is run with the cheerful confidence of a country that built its subway only in 1974 and decided to leapfrog everybody.

The catch is the tempo. Seoul is faster, louder, ruder in the loveliest sense, and nobody is going to bow to you on the escalator. The ajumma will take your seat with the gravitas of a head of state. The trains are packed and the pace is ferocious, and you will learn very quickly to tap, move and not complain. But you will never be lost, which is more than Tokyo can say.

Seoul's other trick is the bicycle. Its public bike-share scheme, according to a former New York transit planner who lived there, has about half as many bikes again as New York's, and an annual pass costs roughly 35 dollars for two-hour rides, compared with 179 dollars a year and 45-minute rides in New York. That is the quiet genius of the Korean approach: the state pays for the unglamorous bits that make a city usable, from the card to the bike to the stairs. He also notes that national funding helps keep transit affordable, which explains why the system feels like a public good rather than a business.

## The Verdict

If I had to pick one for living, I would pick Seoul. I am not being sentimental about it. A country that numbers its exits has respect for the human mind, and a system that stops me falling onto the tracks without needing a guard is one I trust more than I trust most governments. For a foreigner without the language, Seoul is simply kinder.

Tokyo is the finer machine. Its punctuality is a national religion, its manners are the best I have encountered in a crowd, and its sheer scale is humbling. But kindness is not on the menu. Tokyo expects you to learn, adapt and suffer quietly, and rewards you with a ride so smooth you almost forget the humiliation of trying to find the right platform.

So the winner is Korea on usability and Japan on atmosphere. Anyone who wants both clearly does not commute. In Japan you will admire the trains; in Korea you will use them. One of those is a better way to live, and it is not the one that gets the documentaries.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Pink Pangea, Sarah Carey, former Fulbright teaching assistant in Korea — Seoul had plenty of English and multi-language signage, while in Tokyo the large terminal maps were Japanese only and I had to rely on a friend who read it; several companies run Tokyo's lines, so one trip can mean two routes with different fares.*

> *Phil Eaton's blog — Seoul has floor-to-ceiling platform doors at every station, Tokyo mostly has a barrier about three feet high and sometimes none, and both cities make you tap a card on entry and exit; neither runs all night the way New York does.*

> *Streetsblog NYC, Philip Mark Plotch, former MTA planner who lived in Seoul — Stations, exits and platform doors are all numbered, which makes navigation easier, and the platform doors keep people off the tracks and make stations cooler and quieter.*

## Conclusion

Here is the sting. Both countries have solved a problem the rest of us pretend is impossible, and both have done it by assuming that the passenger is an adult who can follow a rule. Japan assumes you will read the map. Korea assumes you cannot, and has printed the number on the wall.

Whichever you pick, buy the card on day one, silence your phone and never, ever eat on a Tokyo rush-hour train. The rest of us will keep waiting for a bus that was supposed to arrive twenty minutes ago and call it character. It is not character. It is a failure of imagination, and these two countries have none.
