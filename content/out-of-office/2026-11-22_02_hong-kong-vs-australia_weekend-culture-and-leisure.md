# Australians Treat the Weekend as a Right; Hong Kong Treats It as a Rumour

🇭🇰 Hong Kong vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Australia, the weekend is a national religion with its own liturgy: the Saturday morning sport, the barbecue with a sausage in a slice of white bread, the beach in the afternoon, and a long, guilt-free Sunday in which nothing is achieved and everyone feels virtuous about it. A work email on a Saturday is considered a hostile act. The country has the sun, the space and the attitude to treat leisure as the whole point of the exercise.

Hong Kong has the same instinct, compressed into a much smaller box and run on a much faster clock. The weekend arrives in fragments, between a Saturday meeting and a Sunday hike that begins before the heat does. Then it is gone, and you are on the MTR with a sunburn and a sense of achievement. I have lived in both. Australia gives you time. Hong Kong gives you intensity. Only one of them leaves you tired in an interesting way.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇭🇰 Hong Kong ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Start your hike early, before the heat and the crowds | Set off up the Peak at midday in June |
| Try the cooked food centres and dai pai dong | Assume a plastic stool means poor food |
| Take a ferry to an outlying island for the afternoon | Spend all weekend inside a mall |
| Book popular tables well in advance | Turn up for Sunday dim sum at noon without a plan |

| 🇦🇺 Australia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Swim between the flags | Ignore a surf lifesaver |
| Bring something to the barbecue, drinks or salad | Arrive empty-handed and critique the snags |
| Wear sunscreen, a hat and a long face about it | Fall asleep on the sand at noon |
| Leave Sunday for doing nothing | Schedule a work call on the weekend |

## 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

The common misconception about Hong Kong is that it is all towers and no trees. In fact it is half country park, and weekends are when the city escapes into them. Expat Arrivals lists the Hong Kong Trail, a 50 km route across Hong Kong Island from Victoria Peak to Big Wave Bay that passes through five country parks, and the Lung Fu Shan Morning Trail, a well-trodden path from near the University of Hong Kong up to the Peak. You can go from a glass lobby to a jungle in forty minutes, and then be back in time for a cocktail. It is absurd, and it is wonderful.

Water is the other pillar. The same guide mentions windsurfing at Stanley, Sai Kung and Cheung Chau, the last being the home of Olympic windsurfing champion Lee Lai-shan, and waterskiing and wakeboarding lessons around Tai Tam and Sai Kung. Ferries to outlying islands such as Discovery Bay and Mui Wo take you out of the city without leaving the MTR zone. And on a Wednesday evening, if you are looking for something louder, there is racing at Happy Valley, which the guide calls one of the city's best-loved evenings out. There is also the Hong Kong Sevens, the spring rugby tournament that moved in 2025 to the 50,000-seat Kai Tak Stadium.

Food is a pastime, not a punctuation mark. The cooked food centres and dai pai dong around Kowloon City Market, Bowrington Market, Queen Street and Java Road are cheap, loud and brilliant, and the city boasts more than 75 restaurants with Michelin stars. Nights belong to Lan Kwai Fong for the trendy crowd and to SoHo and Wan Chai's Peel Street and Star Street for a more local, music-led evening.

An Australian expat called Kate, interviewed by Expat Arrivals back in 2014, said the thing I would underline: no one leaves the office at 5.30pm, and Saturday work is not uncommon. Locals and expats balance long hours with a heavy dose of socialising, and bars and restaurants are busy late on weeknights. That was twelve years ago, so treat the details with care, but the culture has the same pulse. A Hong Kong weekend is not a rest. It is a carefully rationed burst of life.

## 🇦🇺 Australia

Australian weekends run on the assumption that the outdoors is free and the weather is on your side. Saturday morning belongs to sport, either played or watched with a flat white. Saturday afternoon belongs to the beach, the park or the barbecue, where a public grill, a patch of grass and a packet of sausages count as a civic amenity. Sunday is for brunch, a long lunch, and the unhurried certainty that tomorrow is a long way off.

The scale of it is hard to explain to someone who has lived in a dense city. A weekend can mean a four-hour drive to a coastal town, a day in a national park, or simply a walk to a beach at the end of the street. People do not so much schedule leisure as live in it. The coffee culture is serious, the pub culture is friendly, and the sense that nobody is in a hurry is pervasive, perhaps even smug. If you are used to maximising every hour, you will find the pace either liberating or maddening. I found it both, usually in the same afternoon.

There are costs. The sun is no joke, and the weekend ritual includes more sunscreen than a pharmacy. Distances are long, public transport on a Sunday can be a rumour, and a car is usually essential for the best of it. Social life tends to revolve around the home, the barbecue and the friend's backyard rather than the restaurant or the bar, which is warm but can be hard to enter as a newcomer. You will be invited eventually. You will be expected to bring a bottle.

Most importantly, the Australian weekend is protected. Work does not follow you onto the beach, and a manager who emails on a Saturday is considered faintly hostile. That cultural boundary is the real luxury, more than the sand or the surf. It says that your life is yours, and that the job is something you do on weekdays, between the better parts of being alive. After Hong Kong, that is the thing that will hit you hardest.

## The Verdict

Australia wins the weekend, and Hong Kong wins the Saturday night. If your idea of leisure is space, sun and the feeling that nothing is due, move to Australia and stop reading. The boundary between work and rest is firmer, the outdoors is bigger, and nobody will judge you for doing nothing. It is the more restful of the two cultures by a considerable margin.

But Hong Kong, bless it, gives you more per hour. In a single weekend you can summit a ridge, eat something extraordinary for the price of a sandwich, take a ferry to an island and watch horses race under floodlights. It rewards the curious and the energetic. If you want your leisure to feel like a series of small adventures, Hong Kong is the more exciting place to spend two days. Just do not expect to sleep in.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Kate, an Australian expat interviewed by Expat Arrivals (2014) — says the city "never sleeps" and that friends can be made anywhere, and advises new arrivals to avoid comparing everything with "home."*

> *Kate, same Expat Arrivals interview — says no one leaves the office at 5.30pm, Saturday work is not uncommon, and locals and expats balance long hours with a heavy dose of socialising.*

> *Elmer, a Filipino expat quoted in the Expat Arrivals lifestyle guide — advises newcomers to assimilate with the local culture, eat local dishes and explore the hiking trails and beaches.*

## Conclusion

Australia gives you the weekend. Hong Kong makes you earn one, and then makes sure you remember it. Pick the first if you want to rest and the second if you want to feel something. Wear sunscreen in both, take the early ferry, and never, under any circumstances, bring work to a barbecue. The sausage will be cold by the time you finish your sentence.
