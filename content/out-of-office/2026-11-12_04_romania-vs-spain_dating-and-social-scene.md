# Spain Flirts Until Midnight. Romania Wants to Know Where You Work First

🇷🇴 Romania vs 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Spain treats the social scene like an unscheduled public service. People stay out later than is medically advisable, kiss strangers on the cheek as a greeting, and consider a cancelled plan an interesting development rather than an insult. Romania treats the social scene as a family project: you meet people through work, school and relatives, and nobody is quite sure what to do with a stranger who simply turns up.

Dating in either country therefore begins with a decision about whether you are looking for romance or an introduction. In Spain, the two happen in the same bar. In Romania, one has to be arranged by somebody's cousin. I have moved fourteen times and made friends on every continent, and I can tell you that the countries that look warm are not always the ones that let you in.

**Romania**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |

| Be prepared for "where do you work" as the opening line | Take the question as an interrogation |

| Venture beyond the expat bubble in Bucharest | Socialise only with other foreigners |

## Romania

Romanian sociability is deeper than it looks and slower than it should be. The country guide notes that cafes, bars and cultural events anchor urban social life, that family gatherings are the centre of many celebrations, and that physical contact beyond a handshake is usually kept for close friends and relatives. Translation: you are not unwelcome, you are simply not yet in the file.

A team member of the Expat.com forum, who lives in Iasi, described the pattern years ago: social circles form mainly through family, work and school, and the first question at a gathering is where you work or study. Foreigners, she said, often end up in the expat bubble, especially in Bucharest. An American on the same thread found friends through a dog park, a gym he visited three times a week and a gaming group that met twice weekly and finished with beers. None of those are romantic solutions, but they are how a Romanian social life tends to start: repeated contact in a recurring setting.

An Englishman who spent nearly a decade in northern Romania on and off, with a Romanian wife, observed that people often stop to chat on cafe terraces. That detail matters. The Romanian social scene is not closed, but it opens on the terrace and not on the dance floor. Be a regular somewhere. Be visible. Be useful. After a few months you will be introduced to someone's sister, which is the Romanian equivalent of a dating app, only faster and with inbuilt references.

The honest drawback is that the forum evidence is old, and the country guide has no dating section at all. I would treat Romania as a place where the rules are not written down because everyone assumes you know them.

Even the Romanian guide hints at this reserve. It says physical contact beyond a handshake is usually kept for close friends and family, which tells you something about how courtship might begin: with conversation, gradually, and in company. A Romanian suitor is unlikely to gamble everything on a bar encounter, preferring a coffee, a mutual acquaintance and a polite interrogation about your plans. For a foreigner used to faster rituals this can feel cold. It is better understood as seriousness. Nobody wastes a family introduction on someone they do not intend to know for years.

## Spain

Spain has written rules, mostly because it has tourists. The city guides list language exchanges, called intercambios, as the standard way to meet people, so established that Madrid's tourist board publishes a list of venues. Seville's guide adds that after-work socialising with colleagues is the norm. Meetup groups, volunteering and expat Facebook groups fill in the rest.

A Canadian forum poster who had recently moved to the coast near Santa Pola admitted to feeling lonely and wanted nothing more than coffee, walks and beach chats. That is Spain's secret: the country is outwardly sociable, and newcomers can still find it hard to break in. Another poster, who travels widely, said dating varies by region, with the south more affectionate and the north more reserved. They described tardeos, the afternoon drinks that bridge lunch and dinner, as a way to meet people through friends, noted that bills are usually split, and added that arriving ten to fifteen minutes late is normal. Their reply read as quite structured, so treat it as general advice from an experienced resident.

An older thread had a newcomer who knew nobody and joined clubs, a walking group, a theatre group and a language class. Seventeen years on, that is still the advice: Spain rewards the person who turns up repeatedly to the same thing.

The dating-advice article from the Spanish press recommends an honest profile and proactive messaging, and then points readers to cooking classes, hiking, dance and art workshops. It is generic, and part of it is an advertisement, so I recommend the workshop and ignoring the sales link.

Spain's social scene has a gentler problem, which is that it can be hard to tell hospitality from friendship. Seville's guide says socialising with colleagues after work is the norm, and the Madrid guide notes that volunteer groups and expat clubs exist for those who want structure. But a newcomer can spend months in a convivial crowd and still not be asked home. The remedy is the same one that older forum posters give: pick a few recurring activities and be there so often that you stop being new. In Spain, familiarity is a plan, and the rest follows over a long lunch.

## The Verdict

Spain wins the social scene. Romania wins the sincerity. If you want to meet a lot of people on a Thursday night, Spain is the only reasonable answer. The late hours, the afternoon rituals, the language exchanges and the general understanding that evenings are for company make it the easier place to be single and curious.

Romania's reward is slower and arguably richer. Once you are inside a Romanian circle you are not a guest; you are a fixture. The same circle that withholds the hug will lend you the car. That kind of loyalty is rare in a Spanish bar, where friendship is cheerful, plentiful and sometimes disposable.

So the verdict depends on what you want from a month of effort. For a lively social calendar by Friday, Spain. For a relationship that will survive being a foreigner in a small city, Romania. I would take the Spanish bar for a fling and the Romanian terrace for a decade.

## Conclusion

Neither country will hand you a social life. Spain makes it look effortless and demands that you show up anyway. Romania makes it look difficult and rewards you for staying.

Join the thing. The language exchange in Madrid, the gym in Bucharest, the dog park, the walking group. Go every week until people stop asking who you are. Then, and only then, flirt.

Love abroad is not found by the lucky. It is found by the persistent, and by people who have learned to be ten minutes late without apologising for it.
