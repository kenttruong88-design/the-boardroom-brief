# One Country's Neighbours Take Years to Warm Up, the Other's Invite You In Before You've Knocked

🇧🇪 Belgium vs 🇲🇳 Mongolia — Neighbourhood and Community Life

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Belgium runs on tight, long-established social circles that formed in primary school and calcified by university, making genuine neighbourhood friendship one of the slowest, most quietly gate-kept processes in Western Europe. Mongolia, especially in its ger districts and rural communities, runs on an almost inverse principle — hospitality toward strangers, including foreign ones, that assumes you should be fed, sheltered, and included before anyone's bothered to ask too many questions about who you actually are.

I have lived in a pleasant Brussels neighbourhood for the better part of two years and been invited into precisely one neighbour's home, for a very polite thirty minutes, after an amount of small talk that would qualify as a formal diplomatic negotiation. I have also been waved into a Mongolian ger by a family I had never met, fed fermented mare's milk I did not ask for and will never forget, and sent on my way with directions, snacks, and what felt like a genuine, if brief, adoption into the extended family.

## Do's & Don'ts: Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a local club or association (sports, hobby, volunteer) — it's the real entry point into community life | Expect casual doorstep chats to lead quickly to friendship — Belgian social circles form slowly |
| Greet neighbours consistently and politely, even briefly — persistence is noticed over time | Take initial reserve personally — it's cultural distance, not dislike |
| Bring a small gift if invited to a neighbour's home — it's a genuinely appreciated, expected gesture | Assume a friendly conversation at a local café means an ongoing friendship has started |
| Be patient — Belgian friendships often take years but tend to be deep and durable once formed | Confuse Flemish and Walloon community norms — regional differences in openness are real |

## Do's & Don'ts: Mongolia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept food or drink offered in a ger, even a small amount — refusing outright can cause real offence | Enter a ger and walk on or step over the threshold — always step over it, never on it |
| Greet elders and hosts with genuine respect — hospitality norms here are taken very seriously | Assume rural hospitality means no privacy exists — reciprocal respect still matters |
| Bring a small gift (sweets, tea, useful household items) if visiting a family, urban or rural | Overstay dramatically without contributing or reciprocating in some small way |
| Expect genuine warmth toward strangers, especially outside Ulaanbaatar's busier districts | Rush an interaction — hospitality here is unhurried and expects real presence, not a quick visit |

## Belgium: Warm Once You're In, Glacial Getting There

Belgian neighbourhood life operates on a social architecture built well before you arrived — most people's core friend groups formed in childhood, secondary school, or university, and those networks tend to remain remarkably stable and closed for decades afterward, leaving relatively little structural space for a new adult neighbour to slot into easily. This isn't hostility; Belgians are, by most accounts, perfectly pleasant, polite, and willing to exchange friendly greetings on a regular basis. But the distance between friendly and friend is a genuinely long one here, often taking years of consistent, low-key interaction before it closes at all.

The most reliable path in runs through structured activity rather than casual proximity — joining a local sports club, a hobby association, a parents' committee, or a volunteer group provides a legitimate, socially sanctioned reason for repeated contact that a doorstep chat never quite achieves on its own. Belgians also draw a real distinction between social politeness and actual friendship that catches newcomers off guard: a warm, extended conversation at a local café doesn't necessarily imply an invitation is coming, and reading too much into it can lead to some quietly awkward miscalibration.

There's also a genuine regional texture to this — Flemish and Walloon communities carry somewhat different norms around openness and formality, shaped by broader linguistic and cultural distinctions within the country, and expats who've lived in both regions often report meaningfully different timelines and thresholds for being socially absorbed. What unites both, though, is the eventual payoff: Belgian friendships, once actually established, tend to be unusually durable, loyal, and low-drama, built on a foundation that took long enough to form that it rarely seems to crumble easily afterward.

## Mongolia: Hospitality as a Structural, Almost Sacred Default

Mongolian community life, particularly outside Ulaanbaatar's more anonymous urban core, runs on a hospitality tradition with genuinely deep roots — nomadic herding life historically depended on travellers being able to trust that any ger they approached would offer shelter, food, and basic care, a survival necessity that has calcified into an enduring cultural norm even as much of the country has urbanised. The practical result for a visitor, including a foreign one with no prior connection, is a level of unprompted welcome that can feel disorienting coming from more guarded cultures — being invited into a ger, offered food and drink, and genuinely included in ongoing family activity, sometimes within minutes of first contact.

This hospitality carries its own etiquette, taken seriously precisely because it matters so much culturally: guests are expected to accept offered food or drink, even a token amount, as declining outright can read as a real rejection of the host's generosity rather than simple politeness. Physical details matter too — stepping on the threshold of a ger rather than over it is considered bad luck and a minor breach of respect, one of several small rules visitors are expected to absorb quickly and without complaint.

Ulaanbaatar's rapidly growing ger districts — informal urban settlements where rural migrants have brought traditional gers into a city context — retain much of this communal warmth even under increasingly dense and difficult urban conditions, with neighbours checking in on each other and sharing resources in ways that echo the rural tradition even as the surrounding infrastructure struggles to keep pace with the growth. The overall texture of Mongolian community life reads as immediate, generous, and largely unbothered by the kind of social vetting that governs entry into a Belgian friend group.

## The Verdict

Belgium offers community life that's slow to open but genuinely solid once it does, rewarding patience and consistent, low-key effort over years rather than weeks. Mongolia offers something almost startlingly immediate — hospitality extended to strangers as a matter of deep cultural principle, no waiting period required. Belgium wins for anyone building a life somewhere long-term and willing to play a longer game. Mongolia wins for anyone who wants to feel, within a single afternoon, like they've been fed by someone who now considers you a small part of their family.

## Conclusion

Belgium teaches patience as a prerequisite for real community, a slow-building trust that ends up worth the wait once you're finally, quietly let in. Mongolia teaches something almost the opposite lesson, that hospitality doesn't need to be earned at all if the culture has already decided, generations ago, that a stranger deserves shelter and food before they're asked a single question. Go to Belgium and join a club. Go to Mongolia and just knock — someone will probably answer before you finish.
