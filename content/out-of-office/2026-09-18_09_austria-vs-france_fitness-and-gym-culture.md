# Austria Will Hike You Into the Ground. France Will Judge Your Gym Membership and Then Quietly Get One Too

🇦🇹 Austria vs 🇫🇷 France

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Fitness culture reveals more about a national psychology than almost any other lifestyle category, and Austria versus France is a genuinely instructive contrast. Austria never really needed the modern gym concept because it has 50,000 kilometres of hiking trail and a population raised to consider a brisk three-hour ascent to a mountain hut a perfectly normal Sunday activity. France, for decades culturally sceptical of the gym as an imported, faintly try-hard American concept, has recently and rather suddenly discovered it likes lifting weights after all — six million members and counting.

## Do's & Don'ts: Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the local "Wandern" (hiking) trail listings — nearly every town maintains them | Underestimate elevation gain; "easy trail" in Austria still means real vertical climb |
| Budget time for a proper hut stop — regional food and a view are part of the ritual | Rush the hike purely for exercise; the culture treats it as leisure first, fitness second |
| Join a local hiking group via expat or community forums for social integration | Assume gym culture dominates; outdoor recreation is the default, not the alternative |
| Pack proper boots and layers — mountain weather shifts fast regardless of season | Expect extensive English signage on smaller regional trail networks |

## Do's & Don'ts: France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Try a budget chain like Basic-Fit or L'Orange Bleue for a low-commitment entry point | Expect American-style 24/7 supersized gyms everywhere — many clubs are smaller, local |
| Join community fitness forums like INFOGYM for honest reviews of local clubs | Assume gym culture is universal; many French still prefer sport clubs or associations |
| Consider a sports association (association sportive) for a more social, less transactional feel | Skip proper gym etiquette — wiping equipment and re-racking weights are taken seriously |
| Expect steady growth in premium clubs like Club Med Gym in major cities | Assume fitness culture looks the same in Paris as it does in smaller regional towns |

## Austria: When the Entire Country Is Basically a Stairmaster

Austria's relationship with fitness has never needed to be formalised into gym membership statistics because the alternative was always right outside the door, free, and considerably more scenic. With over 50,000 kilometres of maintained hiking trail — Wandern, a national pastime treated with something approaching civic seriousness — ranging from gentle lakeside promenades to genuinely demanding multi-day alpine routes, the default Austrian approach to staying fit has simply been to walk uphill, regularly, for pleasure, since long before "cardio" was a word anyone needed.

What distinguishes Austrian hiking culture from the more performance-obsessed fitness trends found elsewhere is the deliberate, unhurried pace built into the tradition itself. Mountain huts — Almhütten, serving genuinely excellent regional food at altitudes that would make most gym-goers question their life choices — are not incidental rest stops; they're a core, expected part of the experience. A proper hike involves a proper break, often with a beer or a slice of Kaiserschmarrn, consumed while admiring a view that no StairMaster has ever come close to replicating. This is fitness as leisure, not fitness as punishment, and the distinction matters enormously to how Austrians actually relate to physical activity.

For newcomers, breaking into this culture is refreshingly low-barrier compared to more clique-driven fitness scenes elsewhere. Local hiking groups, often coordinated informally through community boards or expat forums in cities like Salzburg and Innsbruck, welcome newcomers with minimal ceremony — showing up with proper boots and a willingness to climb is generally sufficient credential. What catches visitors off guard is the elevation: an "easy" trail by Austrian standards still frequently involves real, sustained vertical climb that would qualify as a serious workout by most other countries' definitions, delivered with a deceptive gentleness that lulls unprepared visitors into starting later, packing lighter, and regretting both decisions by the second hour.

## France: The Reluctant, Increasingly Enthusiastic Convert to Gym Culture

For a long stretch of recent history, the commercial gym occupied an odd cultural position in France — recognised, present, but never quite embraced with the wholehearted enthusiasm found in, say, the United States or the UK. French fitness historically ran through other channels: sports associations, cycling clubs, the simple daily physicality baked into a culture that walks more and drives less than many of its European neighbours. The dedicated commercial gym, by contrast, carried a faint whiff of imported try-hard culture that sat somewhat awkwardly against French cultural instincts toward effortlessness.

That's changed, and changed fast. The French fitness industry is now a genuine multi-billion-euro market, with millions of members across thousands of clubs nationwide — a boom driven by budget chains like Basic-Fit and L'Orange Bleue making gym membership accessible and unpretentious, alongside premium operators like Club Med Gym serving the higher end, particularly around Paris. Notably, this isn't an entirely new phenomenon dressed up as one: France has its own homegrown fitness television history, with the 1980s aerobics show Gym Tonic predating much of the current boom by decades, suggesting the current enthusiasm is less a foreign import taking hold than a dormant domestic tradition reawakening.

What newcomers should expect varies considerably by city and class of club. The budget chains offer minimal frills, basic equipment, and genuinely low commitment barriers — ideal for anyone testing the waters. The more premium clubs lean into a broader lifestyle offering, incorporating restaurants, childcare, and a more social, less purely transactional atmosphere. Gym etiquette, once you're in, runs fairly standard by international norms — wipe your equipment, re-rack your weights — though the specifically French instinct toward unhurried, unbothered self-possession persists even mid-set, producing a gym floor that somehow manages to feel simultaneously serious and entirely unrushed.

## The Verdict

Austria never needed to invent fitness culture because it built an entire national infrastructure around outdoor movement decades before "wellness" became a marketing category. France, having spent years eyeing the gym with polite scepticism, has recently thrown itself into it with genuine, well-documented enthusiasm, budget chains and all. If your idea of a good workout involves a view, a hut stop, and zero membership fees, Austria wins outright. If you'd rather lift something heavy indoors, judge the form of the person next to you, and still somehow make it look effortless afterward, France has, somewhat to its own surprise, built exactly the infrastructure for that too.

## Conclusion

The real difference between these two fitness cultures isn't the equipment or the setting — it's the underlying attitude toward what physical effort is actually for. Austria treats it as inseparable from leisure, community, and the simple pleasure of being outdoors in genuinely spectacular terrain; the workout is a side effect, not the point. France, having spent years holding the commercial gym at arm's length, has recently decided the point can, in fact, be the workout itself, and has built a genuinely thriving industry around that quiet reversal. Neither approach is more legitimate than the other. What's certain is that showing up to either country expecting your home gym routine to transfer unchanged will leave you either badly underprepared for a mountain trail or mildly bewildered by a French club's unhurried, unbothered confidence. Adjust accordingly, and bring better boots than you think you need.
