# Canada Built a Second City Underground. Moldova Just Opened a Window.

🇲🇩🇨🇦 **MOLDOVA vs CANADA**

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is the difference between these two nations in one image: a Moldovan grandmother dragging a kitchen chair onto the pavement at 9pm because the flat is an oven and the street, mercifully, is not, versus a Canadian commuter descending thirty metres into a shopping concourse in October and not resurfacing until April, nodding in grim solidarity with strangers who have also decided that the sky is now optional. One country treats weather as something you simply endure, loudly, in public, with wine. The other has engineered an entire parallel civilisation specifically to avoid making eye contact with it.

I have shivered through a Chișinău heating season that started three weeks late because a municipal committee hadn't finished arguing about the gas bill, and I have walked the full 30 kilometres of Toronto's PATH system without once feeling February on my face. Both experiences taught me the same lesson: climate doesn't bother people nearly as much as the systems built to manage it do.

**Do's & Don'ts: Moldova**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a proper coat for January — Chișinău winters dip well below freezing with real wind off the steppe | Assume "Eastern Europe" means permafrost; July regularly hits the mid-30s and the city has nothing resembling British shade etiquette |
| Time any visit around the wine harvest in September–October, when the whole country's mood visibly lifts | Schedule anything bureaucratic for exactly when the municipal heating season is "about to start" — it isn't, and nobody knows when it will |
| Learn to love a plastic chair outdoors; café culture spills onto every pavement the second it's bearable | Expect central heating to arrive on the calendar date promised — it arrives when the city decides it has, which is a different thing |
| Pack layers you can shed fast — the shift from a 35°C afternoon to a cool evening is abrupt and constant | Panic during a heatwave warning; locals have been issuing them since before "climate alert" was a marketing term, and life continues |

**Do's & Don'ts: Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a coat rated for actual Arctic air, not "winter," if you're anywhere near the Prairies or the North | Trust a Canadian who tells you -15°C is "not that bad" — recalibrate your entire scale before believing anyone's tolerance claims |
| Learn your city's underground or skywalk network immediately; Toronto's PATH alone runs 30km and connects without ever requiring a coat | Assume everyone loves winter because the tourist board says so — barely a quarter of Canadians actually welcome it, by their own polling |
| Get a remote car starter before your first real cold snap, not after | Walk outside during an extreme cold warning expecting it to feel like "cold weather back home" — frostbite works faster than you think |
| Respect the heatwave too — Canadian summers in Toronto or Winnipeg can be properly, unexpectedly brutal | Pack for one season; a single Canadian year can swing from -40°C to +35°C, and the locals dress for both without blinking |

## Moldova: The Country That Shrugs at Its Own Thermostat

Moldova's climate is continental in the textbook sense and theatrical in the lived one: scorching, dry summers that regularly trip heatwave alerts up to 40–41°C, winters that drop reliably below freezing with the kind of flat, exposed wind that makes a modest -5°C feel personal. The country doesn't do gentle transitions. What it does instead is organise its entire culture around two extremes and treat the gap between them as somebody else's problem.

Summer coping is mostly aesthetic avoidance — shutters down at noon, errands at 8am or not at all, and a near-religious devotion to shade, melon, and chilled wine that has nothing to do with romance and everything to do with survival. When the mercury properly misbehaves, which it has been doing with increasing confidence in recent summers, authorities have opened heat-relief points in smaller towns, which tells you plainly that this isn't quaint, it's logistics.

Winter is where Moldova gets genuinely bureaucratic. Central heating in Chișinău isn't triggered by temperature — it's triggered by municipal decree, and the city has a long, well-documented habit of arguing with itself over exactly when the "heating season" officially begins, sometimes in mid-October, sometimes closer to November, occasionally earlier when politics demands a gesture of competence. Locals have adapted by not waiting: space heaters, extra blankets, and a philosophical indifference to whether City Hall has got round to the paperwork yet.

Autumn, mercifully, is the payoff. Wine harvest season turns the national mood genuinely cheerful — this is a country that produces serious volumes of wine and treats the September weather window around the harvest as closer to a national holiday than a date on an agricultural calendar. It's the one stretch where Moldova and its weather are, briefly, on speaking terms.

What you won't find is apology. Nobody in Chișinău is going to tell you the heat is "character-building" or the cold is "bracing." They'll tell you it's hot, they'll tell you it's cold, and they'll keep sitting outside anyway, which is either admirable stoicism or a complete failure to read the room. I'd call it the former on a good day.

## Canada: A Nation That Built a Basement to Avoid Its Own Country

Canada's relationship with weather is best understood as an engineering project with a national mythology bolted on top. The mythology says Canadians love winter — toques, hockey, a stoic grin into a -30°C wind. The engineering tells a more honest story: Toronto's PATH system alone stretches roughly 30 kilometres underground, linking offices, subway stations, and shopping concourses so thoroughly that an entire workday can pass without a single Canadian touching outdoor air. Montreal's underground city does the same job, bigger. These aren't novelties. They are load-bearing infrastructure for a population that has quietly decided the outdoors is a seasonal inconvenience rather than a daily requirement.

The cold itself is not exaggerated by anyone, least of all meteorologists. Environment Canada extreme cold warnings are a routine winter fixture across the Prairies and the North, and the country has produced genuine record-breakers — Saskatoon hit -42.5°C in February 2019, breaking a 112-year-old record, which is the sort of statistic that should end any conversation about whether Canadians are "built different." They aren't built different. They're built prepared: remote-start vehicles idling in driveways at -25°C, triple-glazed windows as standard rather than luxury, and an entire wardrobe economy — Canada Goose chief among the exports — built specifically around surviving the commute from house to car.

And here's the inconvenient bit the tourist board doesn't lead with: a national Ipsos poll found only around a quarter of Canadians actually say "bring it" when asked how they feel about winter. The rest are coping, not celebrating, which rather undercuts decades of maple-flavoured marketing about hardy northern spirit. Summers, when they arrive, can be startlingly hot and humid in cities like Toronto and Winnipeg, and the same population that built tunnels to dodge -30°C is, six months later, startled anew by +33°C, as though weather were a rumour rather than a recurring event.

## The Verdict

Canada wins on infrastructure, no contest — PATH and RESO are genuine engineering marvels, built because enough people decided that going outside in January was a design flaw to be solved rather than a fact to be lived with. That is either visionary urban planning or a four-billion-dollar admission of defeat, and I lean toward the latter.

Moldova wins on honesty. Nobody there is pretending the heat is charming or the cold is bracing — they complain, they adapt, they argue with the city council about when the radiators switch on, and then they sit outside with a glass of something local the second the weather allows it. There's no marketing campaign insisting Moldovans "love" their thermostat-dependent winters; there's just grumbling and getting on with it, which I find far more dignified than a national mythology propped up by a 23% approval rating.

If you want a culture that's built an underground empire to avoid admitting it dislikes its own climate, book Canada. If you want a culture that will simply tell you the weather is dreadful, pour you a glass of wine, and change the subject, book Moldova. One of these is self-aware. It isn't the one with the tunnels.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *talesmag.com, Post Report Q&A: Chișinău — the heating doesn't come on when it's cold, it comes on when the city decides it's cold, so budget for a space heater in October regardless of what the thermometer says.*

> *teamblind.com, "How cold is living in Toronto?" — nobody tells you the real skill isn't tolerating -20°C outside, it's learning to live an entire winter life underground or in a car and barely noticing the season changed.*

> *expat.com forum, Alberta winters thread — the locals aren't tougher than you, they just all have remote starters, heated garages, and zero shame about layering four pairs of socks before 7am.*

## Conclusion

Strip away the folklore and both countries are doing the same thing: managing discomfort and calling it identity. Moldova wears its weather on its sleeve — hot, cold, complained about, and then ignored in favour of a harvest dinner outside anyway. Canada has spent genuine public money pretending the problem doesn't exist by building whole cities where you never have to feel it. Neither approach is noble. One is just better funded.

So when someone tells you Canadians "love" winter, or that Moldovans are simply "hardy," smile politely and remember the receipts: a 23% approval rating and a city council that can't agree on when to turn the heat on. Everyone's coping. Only one of them had the decency to admit it out loud.
