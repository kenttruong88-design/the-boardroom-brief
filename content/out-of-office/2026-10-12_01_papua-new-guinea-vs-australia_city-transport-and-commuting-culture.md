# One Commute Might Get You Robbed. The Other Will Just Make You Twelve Minutes Late And Furious About It.

🇵🇬 Papua New Guinea vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Fourteen relocations in, I have come to believe that nothing reveals a society's soul quite like watching how it gets its own people to work in the morning. Some countries build gleaming trains that run so precisely you could set a Swiss watch by them and still somehow produce a population that complains about it daily. Others build no formal system whatsoever and instead let the market, the church, and several enterprising cousins with a fourteen-seat van sort it out, producing a population that has simply stopped expecting anything else.

Papua New Guinea and Australia share a border, a hemisphere, and roughly four hours of flight time, and yet their approaches to "how do I get from my house to my desk" could not be more philosophically opposed if one of them had been designed by a Swiss railway engineer and the other by a man who once won a bet. Buckle up. There may not be a seatbelt.

## Do's & Don'ts: Papua New Guinea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn your PMV route number and the hand signal for your stop before you need it in a hurry | Expect a printed timetable, a map, or anyone to apologise when the van simply doesn't come |
| Hire a trusted driver or arrange a company vehicle for anything after dark | Walk unfamiliar stretches of Port Moresby alone with a visible phone or bag |
| Keep small kina notes on you; exact change moves the line along | Flash a fresh K100 note and expect a PMV conductor to find change at 7am |
| Budget real time, not optimistic time, for any cross-town trip | Assume a "fifteen-minute drive" survives actual Port Moresby traffic |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Tap on and tap off your Opal, Myki, or Go Card religiously, or enjoy the fine | Make eye contact, small talk, or any sound above a polite cough on a morning train |
| Stand on the left on escalators unless you enjoy being tutted at by strangers | Block the doors, even for one stop, even if you're "basically getting off soon" |
| Check the app for "trackwork" before you commit to a weekend trip anywhere | Assume the bus replacement service runs anywhere near as often as the train it's replacing |
| Accept that a 30-minute delay will be announced with the calm of a man reading a shopping list | Expect anyone to riot about it. They will simply sigh, once, and open a podcast |

## Papua New Guinea: The Commute as Contact Sport

Port Moresby, population pushing past 400,000 and growing faster than its roads can politely accommodate, runs its daily movement almost entirely on PMVs — Public Motor Vehicles, which is a wonderfully bureaucratic term for a privately owned minibus that will leave when it is full, not when a schedule suggests, and will be full by a definition only the driver understands. There is no metro. There was, for a while, a hopeful city bus pilot, which the local press has covered with the weary tone of people reporting on a houseplant that keeps almost dying. The roads themselves are often the real obstacle: a trip that Google Maps insists takes fifteen minutes can take an hour once you factor in potholes the size of a dinner table and the fact that everyone else had the same idea about leaving at 7am.

What nobody tells you before you arrive is that the PMV system, chaotic as it looks to an outsider clutching a printed itinerary, has its own fluent internal logic — hand signals for stops, informal fare conventions, a social contract about who gets the window seat. Expats who stick around long enough to learn it describe something close to affection for it, in the way you grow fond of a slightly feral cat. Expats who don't tend to default to hired drivers and company vehicles instead, which solves the transport problem and quietly reintroduces the much older problem of never actually meeting your city.

Safety is the conversation nobody wants to have and everybody has anyway: particular routes and particular hours carry real risk, armed holdups on PMV runs are reported with grim regularity in the local press, and the sensible local advice — don't travel after dark, don't travel alone on certain routes, vary your patterns — is advice most Port Moresby residents absorb by osmosis rather than choice. It is not, whatever the brochure says, "character." It is a city that built its transport network around the assumption that everyone would simply adapt, and everyone did, because the alternative was staying home.

## Australia: The Commute as Silent Film

Australia, by contrast, has transport infrastructure so comprehensively planned it borders on the smug. Sydney's Opal card, Melbourne's Myki, Brisbane's Go Card — each city insists its version is superior, and each citizen will defend that position with a passion entirely disproportionate to how a tap-card actually works. Trains run on genuine timetables. Apps tell you, to the minute, when the next service arrives and whether "trackwork" — a word that strikes more dread into an Australian commuter than almost any other in the language — will replace it with a bus that takes twice as long and stops four times further from your actual destination.

The infrastructure is the easy part. The culture is the fascinating part. An Australian train carriage at 7:45am is one of the quietest rooms you will ever stand in that is nonetheless packed with several hundred fully conscious adults. Eye contact is a transgression. Conversation is reserved for genuine emergencies, which do not include a mildly interesting delay. Everyone has a podcast, a pair of headphones, and an expression suggesting profound inner peace that masks a seething, private fury about the 8:14 always running four minutes late. Escalator etiquette — stand left, walk right — is enforced not by signage but by the kind of collective tutting that would make a Victorian governess proud.

And here is the quiet cruelty of it: because everything mostly works, Australians have the luxury of complaining about the four minutes. Whirlpool forum threads about a late train run for pages, with the righteous fury of people who have simply never had to budget two hours of slack into a fifteen-minute journey because the van might not come at all. It is, I will concede, a nicer problem to have. It is still extremely funny to watch from the outside.

## The Verdict

If you want a transport system that works with the reliability of a Swiss clock and the emotional warmth of one, move to Australia and learn to love silence. If you want a system that will teach you, within one month, exactly how little control you actually have over your own schedule — and, improbably, a kind of resilience you didn't know you needed — Port Moresby will oblige.

I will not pretend these are equivalent inconveniences dressed up as "cultural differences." One system occasionally runs four minutes late. The other occasionally doesn't run at all, and the difference between those two realities is not quaint, it is infrastructure, and infrastructure is political whether anyone wants to discuss it at a dinner party or not. But I have sat in both the overfull PMV and the overfull Sydney T-line at peak hour, and I can report that neither experience left me enlightened about the human condition so much as simply late, sweaty, and — on one specific occasion in Port Moresby involving a van, a goat, and a disputed fare — slightly richer in material for future dinner parties. Pick your inconvenience. Budget accordingly.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat Exchange — a Port Moresby resident paraphrasing years of local advice: once you learn which PMV routes to avoid after 4pm, the rest of the system actually runs fine — the trouble is nobody writes that list down for you, you just have to get unlucky once.*

> *Tea O Maori News — a commuter describing a specific PNG route where holdups happen roughly every few hours on the same stretch, treated by regular riders less as news and more as a known occupational hazard of that particular road.*

> *Whirlpool Forums — a long-running Australian thread regular grumbling that a scheduled four-minute delay on the Sydney network somehow still manages to ruin an entire morning, a complaint repeated, with minor variations, roughly every single week for over a decade.*

## Conclusion

Here is what fourteen moves have taught me about commuting: the quality of a country's public transport is one of the most honest signals of what that government actually prioritises, and no amount of travel-blog whimsy should be allowed to launder that into "charming chaos" or "efficient but soulless." Papua New Guinea's commuters build genuine ingenuity and real community knowledge out of a system that has been chronically underinvested in. Australia's commuters build genuine, world-class infrastructure and then spend their one daily aggravation on a four-minute delay, because the system has given them nothing larger to be angry about. Neither country needs my pity or my golf-clap. Both deserve the honest comparison, which is this: one commute might cost you an afternoon. The other might cost you considerably more. Plan, and tip your driver, accordingly.
