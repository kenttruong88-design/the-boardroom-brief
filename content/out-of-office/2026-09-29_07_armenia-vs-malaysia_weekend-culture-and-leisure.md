# An Armenian Weekend Is a Four-Hour Lunch That Ends in a Toast. A Malaysian Weekend Is a Mall, a Mamak and Midnight Roti.

🇦🇲 Armenia vs 🇲🇾 Malaysia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The weekend is the most revealing institution in any culture, because it's the only time a country does what it actually wants. Monday to Friday is shaped by economics. Saturday and Sunday are shaped by the soul. Armenia and Malaysia have both perfected the art of the weekend, and both would consider the other's version somewhat mad.

An Armenian weekend is organised around a table: a long one, covered in herbs, cheese, lavash, grilled meat and apricot brandy, surrounded by family members who have driven two hours to argue about politics and propose toasts that last longer than some marriages. A Malaysian weekend is organised around air-conditioning and appetite: the mall, the night market, the mamak stall at 1am, a weekend trip to the highlands to escape the heat, and a food itinerary more meticulously planned than most military campaigns. Both are superb. One will leave you hungover and emotionally raw. The other will leave you full and faintly sticky.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇦🇲 Armenia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the invitation to a family khorovats (barbecue) weekend — it's an honour | Leave the table early. Lunch ends when the toasts end |
| Learn to make, or at least survive, a proper toast | Refuse a toast or empty your glass carelessly before the tamada (toastmaster) says so |
| Take a weekend trip to Lake Sevan, Dilijan or Garni in summer | Assume "a quick coffee" will be quick. It won't be |
| Stroll the Cascade and Northern Avenue in Yerevan in the evening | Compare Armenian food to its neighbours' cuisines unless you enjoy long debates |

**🇲🇾 Malaysia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check which weekend your state observes — some take Friday–Saturday off | Assume the whole country shares a Saturday–Sunday weekend |
| Eat at a mamak stall late at night like a local | Walk around in the midday sun if a mall with air-conditioning is available |
| Go to a pasar malam (night market) for food and people-watching | Arrive at popular brunch spots at noon on a Sunday without a reservation |
| Escape to Cameron Highlands, Penang or Melaka on long weekends | Criticise another state's version of your favourite dish. Food loyalty is fierce |

## Armenia: The Weekend as a Banquet With Speeches

In Armenia, the weekend is essentially a family reunion that happens fifty-two times a year. On Saturday or Sunday, grandparents, siblings, cousins and a handful of people whose connection to the family is never fully explained convene around a table in someone's garden, apartment or dacha, and the eating begins. Fresh herbs by the bunch, salty cheese, lavash, dolma, tolma, salads that nobody touched from the previous weekend, and eventually the khorovats — barbecue skewers of pork or lamb grilled over vine cuttings — which is less a dish than a national religion.

Then come the toasts. A tamada, or toastmaster, guides the table through a series of toasts that can last hours: to parents, to the dead, to friendship, to Armenia, to the guest from abroad who is now blinking slowly and trying to count how many glasses of cognac or mulberry vodka he has consumed. The speeches are long, emotional and often beautiful. Do not interrupt them. Do not, under any circumstances, drink between them.

Beyond the table, weekends in Yerevan are about promenading. The Cascade, a monumental staircase lined with art, fills with families in the evening. Cafés along Northern Avenue and Republic Square spill out onto pavements. In summer, people flee to Lake Sevan for swimming and fried fish, to Dilijan for forest walks, or to the Garni Temple and Geghard Monastery for an afternoon of history. In winter, Tsaghkadzor's ski slopes fill up.

What defines it all is intensity. Armenians don't do light weekends. They do weekends with emotional stakes — a birthday, a christening, a relative back from Los Angeles, a political argument that began in 1991. You'll leave exhausted, overfed and genuinely moved, having spent a day with people who treat time together as the most important thing in the world.

## Malaysia: The Weekend as a Food Itinerary

Malaysia's weekend starts with a question that sounds simple but isn't: which weekend? Some states — Kelantan, Terengganu, Kedah, and as of recent years Johor — observe a Friday–Saturday weekend, while most of the rest of the country, including Kuala Lumpur, rests on Saturday and Sunday. Foreign residents quickly learn to check their calendars before booking cross-state trips.

The weekend itself revolves around three pillars: food, air-conditioning and more food. Families head to enormous malls — the kind with ice rinks, cinemas, indoor theme parks and food courts the size of small towns — not as consumerism but as climate refuge. At dusk, the pasar malam (night market) sets up in car parks and streets, and neighbourhoods wander between stalls of satay, apam balik, grilled seafood and fresh fruit.

Then there's the mamak. The 24-hour Indian-Muslim eateries that dot every Malaysian city are where the country goes to be itself: roti canai and teh tarik at 1am, football on a giant screen, friends lingering over one drink for three hours with nobody asking them to leave. It's the Malaysian equivalent of a pub, minus the alcohol and plus the flatbread.

Long weekends — and Malaysia's multicultural calendar produces plenty — send people streaming towards Penang for food, Melaka for history, Cameron Highlands for strawberries and cooler air, or the islands for beaches. The tone is gentler than Armenia's: less emotional stakes, more gentle pleasure. You'll eat six meals, visit three places and never raise your voice.

## The Verdict

Armenia wins, and it wins because it asks more of you. A Malaysian weekend is a lovely, low-pressure sequence of excellent meals and air-conditioned relief, and I'd happily spend the rest of my life alternating between Penang hawker stalls and a mamak at midnight. But it's a weekend you consume.

An Armenian weekend is one you participate in. You're fed, yes, but you're also toasted, questioned, adopted, and occasionally reduced to tears by a grandfather's speech about a village you've never heard of. It's exhausting, emotionally demanding and sometimes painfully long. It is also the closest thing I've found to a culture that still believes the purpose of free time is to spend it with the people you love, loudly and at great length.

Malaysia feeds you. Armenia claims you.

## Conclusion

The weekend is where culture shows its hand. Armenia's hand is an overflowing table, a raised glass and an extended family treating every Sunday like a reunion — which, for a people scattered across the world by history, is often exactly what it is. Malaysia's hand is a food map, a cool mall and a mamak stall that never closes, a multicultural nation's quiet agreement that the best thing to do with free time is eat together without drama.

Both beat the Western weekend of errands, emails and a sad Sunday roast in front of the television. Both would regard our obsession with "productivity" at weekends as deeply suspicious.

So pick your poison. In Kuala Lumpur, bring an appetite. In Yerevan, bring an appetite, a strong liver and a toast prepared in advance. You'll need all three.
