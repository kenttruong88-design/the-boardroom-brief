# Bring Cash for the Private Clinic, Bring Patience for the Public One

🇭🇷 Croatia 🇰🇿 Kazakhstan
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Croatia and Kazakhstan both inherited healthcare systems from very different socialist pasts and have spent the decades since renovating them at wildly different speeds. Croatia's public system, EU-adjacent and increasingly polished, delivers solid basic care with the kind of bureaucratic patience-testing you'd expect from any European public health service, while a genuinely excellent private layer sits on top for anyone willing to pay. Kazakhstan, sprawling and resource-rich, has poured serious money into hospitals in Almaty and Astana that would not look out of place in Seoul, while healthcare in its smaller cities and rural steppe regions still lags noticeably behind.

I've been treated in both — a minor procedure in Zagreb that involved a lot of waiting and a genuinely competent doctor, and a private consultation in Almaty that felt more like a five-star hotel than a hospital, with a price tag to match. Neither country's healthcare is bad. Neither is uniformly good either, and knowing which tier you're actually in matters enormously.

## Do's & Don'ts: Croatia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get private supplemental insurance if you're staying long-term — public wait times for specialists are real | Assume EU healthcare reciprocity fully covers non-EU expats; check your specific status carefully |
| Use public emergency care without hesitation — it's genuinely solid and free at point of use | Expect appointment scheduling to move quickly outside emergencies; patience is required |
| Learn basic medical Croatian or bring a translator for smaller-city clinics | Assume every doctor speaks fluent English outside Zagreb and the major coastal cities |

## Do's & Don'ts: Kazakhstan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use private clinics in Almaty or Astana for anything beyond routine care — they're genuinely excellent | Assume rural or smaller-city facilities match the capital's standard; the gap is significant |
| Register for compulsory social health insurance if you're a resident — it's legally required | Rely solely on public facilities without private supplemental coverage if you can afford it |
| Carry cash or a card that works reliably — private care is billed directly and isn't cheap | Expect English to be widely spoken outside major private hospitals; Russian is often more useful than English |

## Croatia: Free, Solid, and Not in Any Particular Hurry

Croatia's public healthcare system, HZZO, delivers what you'd expect from a European public system that's had decades to mature: free or low-cost basic care, a genuinely competent base of doctors, many trained to EU standards, and a bureaucratic pace that will test the patience of anyone used to faster private systems. Emergency care is solid and won't bankrupt you. Getting a routine specialist appointment, on the other hand, can take weeks, occasionally months, particularly outside Zagreb, and that gap is exactly where the country's growing private healthcare sector has stepped in.

Private clinics, concentrated in Zagreb and the coastal tourist cities, offer faster appointments, more English-speaking staff, and a noticeably more polished experience, all for a price that remains, by Western European or American standards, genuinely reasonable. Most long-term expats end up running a hybrid system: public insurance as the safety net, private coverage or pay-as-you-go visits for anything that needs speed.

Where things get complicated is around EU reciprocity rules and non-EU residency status — the paperwork determining exactly what a given expat is entitled to under public coverage is not always intuitive, and getting it wrong means either overpaying unnecessarily or discovering a gap in coverage at the worst possible moment. Smaller-city and island clinics, meanwhile, can be genuinely excellent but thinner on English-speaking staff, so a bit of medical Croatian, or a translator app you actually trust, earns its keep fast.

## Kazakhstan: Five-Star Care in the Capital, a Different Story Beyond It

Kazakhstan has spent its oil wealth visibly on healthcare infrastructure in Almaty and Astana, and the results show: private hospitals there rival anything in Seoul or Dubai, with gleaming facilities, English-speaking specialists in the major private networks, and a service standard that can genuinely surprise visitors who arrived expecting post-Soviet austerity. It isn't cheap — private care here is billed directly and at rates that reflect the quality — but for anyone who can afford it, it's some of the best healthcare in Central Asia, full stop.

The catch is geography. Kazakhstan is enormous, and that capital-city investment doesn't extend evenly across the country. Smaller cities and rural steppe regions still rely heavily on older Soviet-era public infrastructure, with equipment, staffing levels, and English-language capability all dropping noticeably the further you get from Almaty or Astana. Compulsory social health insurance, a relatively recent system overhaul, is legally required for residents and covers a base level of public care, but most expats treat it as a supplement to private coverage rather than a substitute for it.

Language adds another layer most visitors don't anticipate: Russian remains far more useful in medical settings than English, even in some private facilities, and Kazakh is increasingly used in official documentation as the country continues its gradual shift away from Russian as the default administrative language. Anyone settling outside the two major cities long-term should genuinely prioritise Russian language basics, or a reliable translator, well before they need a doctor rather than after.

## The Verdict

Croatia wins on consistency — its healthcare quality doesn't swing as wildly by location, and the EU framework underneath it provides a baseline of reliability that's hard to overstate. Kazakhstan wins on peaks — nowhere in Croatia will you find private care quite as dazzling as Almaty's top hospitals, but nowhere in Kazakhstan outside its two major cities will you find the floor as solid as Croatia's smallest coastal clinic. If I needed routine, dependable care, Croatia. If I needed the absolute best specialist money could buy and happened to be within reach of Almaty, Kazakhstan, without hesitation.

## Conclusion

Croatia gives you a dependable floor with an optional, comfortable private ceiling above it. Kazakhstan gives you a genuinely spectacular ceiling in two specific cities and a considerably less certain floor everywhere else. Neither system is the horror story some outdated guidebooks suggest, and both have quietly become far more capable than their reputations. Just know which tier you're actually buying into before you need it urgently, because finding out mid-emergency is not the moment for surprises.
