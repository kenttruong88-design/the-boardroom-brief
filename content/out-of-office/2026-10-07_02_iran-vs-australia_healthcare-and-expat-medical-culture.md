# One Country Will Operate on You for the Price of a Handbag, the Other Will Make You Wait Six Months to Discuss the Handbag

🇮🇷 Iran vs 🇦🇺 Australia — Healthcare and Expat Medical Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nobody warns you that the two most wildly different healthcare experiences of your expat life will be a gleaming, astonishingly cheap private hospital in Tehran where a specialist sees you within the hour, and a public waiting room in Sydney where you will watch four seasons change through a window before anyone looks at your knee. I have used both systems in states of genuine medical distress, and I can report with total confidence that one of them treats you like a paying customer and the other treats you like a mild inconvenience to an otherwise excellent spreadsheet.

Australia will tell you, smugly, that its healthcare is "world-class," and it is — assuming you are either bleeding dramatically in an emergency room or prepared to wait the geological length of time required for anything that isn't. Iran, despite the international reputation its government has cultivated for itself, runs a medical tourism industry so good that Europeans and Gulf nationals fly in specifically for it. Hold those two facts in your head at once. It's uncomfortable. Good.

## Do's & Don'ts: Iran

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use private hospitals in Tehran or Shiraz for anything beyond a basic checkup — standards are genuinely high | Assume sanctions-related supply issues never affect imported medication; check availability before you need it urgently |
| Get proper international travel insurance regardless of how cheap cash-pay care is | Expect English-speaking staff everywhere outside major private hospitals in Tehran and Isfahan |
| Ask locals which private clinic they use — reputation travels fast and matters enormously | Walk into this assuming it's "basic" care; some of the region's best surgeons trained and stayed here |
| Carry cash or a working local payment method — international cards are unreliable due to banking restrictions | Ignore the pharmacy counter's advice; Iranian pharmacists are highly trained and genuinely useful first stops |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for Medicare the day you're eligible — the paperwork lag is real and the backdating is not generous | Expect to see a GP same-day without calling around; book ahead or use a bulk-billing clinic finder |
| Get private health insurance early if you want to skip public surgery queues — the waitlists are not a myth | Assume "bulk billing" is everywhere; plenty of clinics now charge a gap fee, even with Medicare |
| Use telehealth for anything non-urgent — it is fast, normalised, and often bulk-billed | Go to an emergency department for anything that isn't actually an emergency; triage will make you regret it |
| Build a relationship with one regular GP — continuity makes referrals and repeat scripts dramatically faster | Forget the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme discount exists; paying full price for prescriptions is a rookie expat mistake |

## Iran: Astonishingly Good Care, Astonishingly Complicated Money

Here is the sentence that makes every newly arrived expat do a double take: Iran has some of the best, cheapest, and fastest private healthcare available anywhere in the region, built on a surprisingly robust public medical education system that produces highly trained doctors, many of whom trained or practised abroad before returning. Cosmetic and reconstructive surgery, cardiac care, and dental work draw medical tourists from across the Gulf and Europe specifically because the quality-to-price ratio is almost absurd by Western standards. A consultation that would cost you a week's salary in London costs the equivalent of a nice dinner in Tehran, and you'll usually be seen that same day.

The catch, and it is a substantial one, is the money itself. International sanctions mean your foreign card almost certainly won't work, international insurers are patchy at best about settling claims directly with Iranian providers, and imported medications can occasionally vanish from pharmacy shelves for reasons having nothing to do with your actual health needs. You adapt by carrying cash, by building a relationship with a trusted pharmacist — genuinely one of the most useful medical relationships you can have here — and by asking locals, not expat forums, which private hospital they'd actually trust with their own mother. The Iranian Red Crescent Society runs extensive emergency response infrastructure that is more capable than its low international profile suggests, and the Ministry of Health has spent real money on infrastructure outside Tehran too, so don't assume the capital holds a monopoly on competence.

## Australia: The System Is Excellent, Assuming You Have Six Months to Spare

Medicare is, on paper, one of the most generous universal healthcare systems a country has ever built, and Australians will remind you of this constantly, usually while also complaining bitterly about how long they waited to use it. The emergency system is genuinely superb — fast, well-resourced, triaged with brutal efficiency — which is precisely why everyone keeps telling you not to use it for anything that isn't an actual emergency. The public system for everything else runs on waitlists that would make a Canadian blush: elective surgery queues stretching past a year in some states, specialist referrals taking months, and a GP landscape where "bulk billing" — the thing that makes a visit genuinely free — is quietly disappearing from clinic after clinic in favour of a gap fee that nobody mentions until you're at the counter.

Private health insurance exists specifically to let you skip the public queue, and most sensibly organised expats get it within the first month, treating it less as a luxury and more as the actual price of admission to a functioning system. Telehealth has filled in some of the gaps impressively — a same-day video consult for a repeat script or a minor ailment is now entirely normal and usually painless. The Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme caps what you pay for most prescriptions at a genuinely reasonable amount, assuming you remember to ask for it, because nobody at the counter is going to offer the discount unprompted. Register for Medicare on day one of eligibility. The backdating process for anyone who dawdles is a special, specific kind of bureaucratic misery.

## The Verdict

If you need something fixed today and don't mind navigating a banking system actively designed to frustrate you, Iran will see you faster, cheaper, and arguably with a more personally invested specialist than most Western systems can manage. If you need something fixed eventually, in a system that will never once ask you for cash upfront and will never once let you die in a waiting room, Australia wins on the floor, if not on the ceiling.

Australia's system is built for catastrophe and mediocre at convenience. Iran's system is built for convenience and occasionally held hostage by geopolitics it had no hand in creating. Pick your trade-off with your eyes open, and for heaven's sake get proper insurance either way — not because either system will bankrupt you outright, but because "proper insurance" is the only thing standing between you and finding out the hard way which gaps exist in both.

## Conclusion

Both of these countries will cheerfully let their healthcare reputation do the talking while quietly hoping you don't ask too many follow-up questions. Iran's is a genuine, underrated triumph wrapped in a banking system that seems engineered by someone who has never personally needed to pay for anything. Australia's is a genuine, slightly overrated triumph wrapped in a waitlist system that seems engineered by someone who has never personally been in pain. Sort your insurance before you land in either, befriend a pharmacist in one and a GP in the other, and stop believing anyone who tells you healthcare abroad is simply "worse." It's just differently, specifically inconvenient, and pretending otherwise is how you end up stranded, confused, and significantly poorer than necessary.
