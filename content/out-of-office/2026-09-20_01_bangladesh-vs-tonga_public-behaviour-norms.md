# One Country Has No Concept of a Queue. The Other Legally Shuts Itself Down Every Sunday and Dares You to Complain

🇧🇩 Bangladesh vs 🇹🇴 Tonga — Public Behaviour Norms
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular kind of culture shock that comes from discovering an entire nation has agreed, collectively and without much discussion, on a public behaviour rule that would never survive contact with a Western city council. Bangladesh has decided that queuing is a Western affectation nobody has time for. Tonga has decided that Sunday is legally, spiritually, and commercially closed, full stop, and if you didn't plan your grocery shop for Saturday, that is a you problem.

I have been swallowed by a Dhaka crowd that moved as a single organism with no apparent leader, and I have stood outside a locked, silent Nuku'alofa supermarket on a Sunday afternoon, genuinely uncertain whether I'd wandered into an apocalypse film. Both were formative. Neither was comfortable.

## Do's & Don'ts: Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect crowds to move with their own internal logic — go with the flow rather than fighting it | Expect a formal queue anywhere that isn't an airport or a bank with a ticket machine |
| Use your right hand for eating, greeting, and handing over money or documents | Use your left hand for any of the above — it carries real social weight as an insult |
| Dress modestly, especially outside Dhaka's more cosmopolitan pockets | Assume direct eye contact and blunt questions about your salary or marital status are rude — they're just curious |

## Do's & Don'ts: Tonga

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Do all your errands, shopping, and travel planning by Saturday evening | Expect shops, banks, transport, or most restaurants to be open on Sunday |
| Dress conservatively, particularly covering shoulders and knees near villages and churches | Swim, sunbathe loudly, or play sports in public view on a Sunday — it's taken seriously, not just quietly frowned upon |
| Greet elders and chiefs with visible deference — hierarchy is real and observed | Touch anyone's head, even a child's — it's considered the most sacred part of the body |

## Bangladesh: Organised Chaos That Actually Works, Somehow

Dhaka is one of the most densely populated cities on Earth, and it shows in the way public space functions, which is to say it doesn't function according to any rulebook a European city planner would recognise, and yet it functions. Queuing, in the orderly single-file sense, is a rumour. What you get instead is a kind of negotiated mass movement — at markets, bus stops, ferry terminals — where dozens of people converge on the same narrow point simultaneously, and somehow, through what looks like pure chaos, everyone more or less gets where they're going. It is loud, it is close, and it requires a complete recalibration of what "personal space" means, because the concept barely applies.

What foreigners tend to misread as rudeness is usually just directness. Bangladeshis will ask how much you earn, why you aren't married, and whether you have children, all within the first ten minutes of meeting you, and none of it is meant as an intrusion — it's simply how people build a picture of who you are, fast. The right-hand rule, however, is not a suggestion: eating, greeting, and exchanging money or documents with the left hand genuinely registers as an insult, a hangover from associations with hygiene that still carries real cultural force, and getting it wrong will earn you a wince even from people too polite to correct you.

Hospitality here is aggressive in the nicest possible sense — you will be fed, repeatedly, by people who have far less than you, and refusing outright is its own social misstep. The honest move is to accept a little, always, and let the crowd carry you, because it will, whether you consent to it or not.

## Tonga: The Only Country Where the Entire Economy Observes the Sabbath by Law

Tonga takes Sunday seriously in a way that will genuinely catch out anyone who hasn't done their homework. This isn't a soft cultural preference — it's written into the constitution, which explicitly protects the Sabbath, and the practical result is that shops, banks, public transport, and the overwhelming majority of restaurants and activities simply do not operate. Locals go to church, extended families gather for a large shared meal, and anyone who didn't stock up on Saturday is having a very quiet, very hungry Sunday. Swimming, loud sport, and anything resembling commercial activity in public view is genuinely frowned upon, not just theoretically discouraged, and visitors who ignore this find themselves the subject of very pointed, very silent disapproval.

Beyond the Sunday rule, Tongan public behaviour runs on a deep respect for hierarchy and the monarchy — Tonga remains one of the last Pacific nations with an active, culturally central royal family, and deference to chiefs and elders is visible everywhere, from how people speak to how they physically position themselves in a room. The head is considered sacred, and touching anyone's — even affectionately, even a child's — is a genuine cultural violation, not a minor faux pas.

What makes Tonga's version of public order distinctive is that it isn't chaotic restraint like Bangladesh's crowd logic — it's the opposite, an almost eerie collective stillness one day a week, a nation that has decided, as one, to simply switch off.

## The Verdict

Bangladesh throws you into the deep end of human density and trusts you to swim. Tonga hands you a calendar with one day circled in red and expects you to have read it in advance. Both demand the same thing from a visitor, really: humility, and the willingness to admit your own country's version of "normal public behaviour" is not the only one on offer. If forced to choose, I'll take Tonga's Sunday silence — at least it comes with a nap.

## What Nobody Warned You About

## Conclusion

Bangladesh doesn't organise its public space so much as trust its public space to organise itself, and against all Western instinct, it works. Tonga organises its public space so thoroughly that an entire day simply stops. Neither approach is wrong. Both will humble you within your first week, and if you're the sort of traveller who needs predictable public order to feel at ease, may I gently suggest a cruise ship instead.
