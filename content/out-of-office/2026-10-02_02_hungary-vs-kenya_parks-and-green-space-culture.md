# One of These Countries Put a Castle in Its Park. The Other Put Lions Next to Its Airport.

🇭🇺 Hungary 🇰🇪 Kenya
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country thinks its relationship with green space is normal, and every country is wrong in a different, delightful way. Hungary's idea of a city park involves a thermal bath complex, a replica castle nobody quite believes is fake until you touch the concrete, and enough manicured flowerbeds to make you suspect the municipal gardener has unresolved feelings about symmetry. Kenya's idea of a city park, in Nairobi specifically, involves actual lions, actual giraffes, and a skyline visible over the acacia trees, because someone decided decades ago that a capital city and a functioning savanna ecosystem could share a postcode and, astonishingly, made it work.

I have sat in Városliget with a coffee watching retirees practice tai chi near a dragon statue, and I have sat in a matatu on Nairobi's Langata Road watching a giraffe cross in front of traffic like it personally owns the right of way. Both experiences recalibrated what I thought a "park" was for. Neither country is doing it the "correct" way, because there isn't one — but they are doing it in ways that reveal exactly what each culture actually wants from its open space.

## Do's & Don'ts: Hungary

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat Városliget as a full day out — castle, baths, zoo, circus, all walkable | Don't rush through it like a checklist; Hungarians linger, and rushing marks you as a tourist instantly |
| Bring a book and claim a bench for hours — nobody will move you along | Don't expect manicured parks to mean sanitized ones; benches get used for everything from chess to arguments |
| Visit in winter for the thermal-adjacent park baths, not just summer | Don't assume every green space is a "park" in the English sense — many are formal gardens with rules about where you can actually sit on the grass |

## Do's & Don'ts: Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book a Nairobi National Park safari as a half-day trip, not a full expedition — it's inside the city | Don't walk the park on foot; it's one of the only national parks you genuinely cannot wander into unarmed and unescorted |
| Respect wildlife distance rules even when the animals seem used to traffic | Don't assume every green space in Kenya is wild — Nairobi's Uhuru Park and Karura Forest are entirely different, tamer registers |
| Visit Karura Forest for a proper walk, run, or cycle — it's the closest thing to Hungary's manicured model | Don't treat the park's proximity to the airport as a gimmick; it's a genuine conservation effort under real development pressure |

## Hungary: Green Space as Civic Theatre

Hungarians don't do green space quietly. Városliget — City Park, if you insist on the literal translation — is less a park than a civic stage set, complete with Vajdahunyad Castle, a building so committed to looking centuries old that it convinces first-time visitors it predates the actual medieval buildings it's modeled on. It isn't; it was built for a millennial exhibition in 1896, which somehow makes it more Hungarian, not less. Add the Széchenyi thermal baths at one edge, a zoo, a circus, and enough monument-dense walking paths to make an afternoon stroll feel like a guided history lecture you didn't sign up for but don't regret.

The culture around these spaces is deliberate and unhurried in a way that initially reads as stiff to anyone coming from a more casual park tradition, and then reveals itself as something closer to reverence. Hungarians use parks the way other cultures use living rooms: as an extension of domestic life, somewhere to read for three hours on a bench without anyone suggesting you've overstayed, somewhere chess games run on an unspoken schedule, somewhere old men argue about football with the specific gravity of people who have had this exact argument in this exact spot for thirty years. The flowerbeds are genuinely, almost aggressively maintained — this is a culture that takes municipal horticulture as a point of national pride — but the rules about where you can actually sit on the grass itself can be stricter than visitors expect, a small irony in a country this devoted to the outdoors.

What surprises newcomers most is the thermal-bath integration. A green space in Hungary is rarely just trees and benches; it's frequently trees, benches, and a steaming mineral pool complex that turns a park visit into something closer to a spa day. Try explaining to a Nairobi friend that your city's biggest park has a swim-up bar built into a historic bathhouse and watch them recalibrate their entire mental model of what "going to the park" means.

## Kenya: Green Space With Apex Predators

Nairobi National Park is, on paper, an absurdity: a fully functioning wildlife reserve with lions, giraffes, rhinos, and buffalo, sitting inside the boundary of a capital city of roughly five million people, close enough to the airport that landing planes and grazing zebras share a skyline. It should not work. It works, mostly, though the working is increasingly contested — urban development pressure on the park's migration corridors is a real and ongoing argument in Kenyan civic life, not a settled success story, and anyone writing it up as an unambiguous triumph is skipping the complicated part.

The culture here splits cleanly into two registers that have almost nothing to do with each other. There's the safari register — paid admission, ranger-escorted vehicles, binoculars, the specific hush that falls over a vehicle when a lion is spotted thirty meters off a city-adjacent road — which is treated with the seriousness of actual wilderness, because it is actual wilderness, government-managed and genuinely dangerous if you forget that. And there's the tamer, more Hungarian-adjacent register: Uhuru Park near the city centre, used for political rallies and lunchtime walks in equal measure, and Karura Forest, a genuinely lovely, bike-friendly, jog-friendly urban forest that spent years as a site of political struggle over land grabbing before becoming the well-maintained public asset it is today — a history worth knowing before you praise its paths too breezily.

What Kenyan green space culture does better than almost anywhere else is refuse to let you forget that "nature" and "city" were never actually opposites, just zoning decisions that could have gone differently. Hungary performs that same lesson through architecture and theatre. Kenya performs it through an actual lion, actually there, actually unimpressed by your presence.

## The Verdict

Hungary wins on comfort, reliability, and sheer bench-hours-per-visit — you will never once wonder if the flowerbed is going to charge at you. Kenya wins on sheer audacity: no other capital city on earth lets you watch a giraffe cross a road from a matatu window twenty minutes after clearing passport control. If you want a park that soothes you, go to Városliget. If you want a park that recalibrates your entire understanding of what a city is allowed to contain, go to Nairobi National Park, and then go to Karura Forest the next day to see the version of Kenyan green space that's closer to what Budapest is doing, so you can appreciate both countries doing their own thing properly rather than imitating each other badly.

## Conclusion

Both countries will quietly correct your assumptions about what "going outside" is supposed to feel like, and both will do it without apologizing for the inconvenience. Hungary insists the outdoors should be civilized, historic, and faintly theatrical, down to the fake-old castle and the bathhouse built into the landscaping. Kenya insists the outdoors was never fully tamed to begin with, capital city or not, and that any comfort you feel in its urban green spaces is a privilege, not a given. Visit both if you can manage it. Just don't make the mistake of expecting either one to behave like the other — Városliget will not produce a lion, and Nairobi National Park will absolutely not have a swim-up bar, and both of those facts are exactly as they should be.
