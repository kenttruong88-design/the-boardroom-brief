# Czechia Registers You With a GP Who Is Full; Poland Sells You Medicover

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇵🇱 Poland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Central European healthcare has a reputation for being cheap, competent and faintly Kafkaesque, and I am happy to report the reputation is deserved on all three counts. Both Czechia and Poland have decent doctors, serious hospitals and public systems financed by compulsory insurance. Both also have an enthusiastic private sector eager to relieve you of the wait, and a population of expats who learn within a fortnight to say "I have private cover" in the tone of someone mentioning a good tailor.

The difference is one of emphasis. Czechia hands you a system that works if you can get into it, and asks you to find a GP. Poland hands you a system that works for everybody, eventually, and quietly hints that your employer might like to buy you a clinic membership. I have been ill in both, briefly and with dignity, and my findings are that the doctors are fine, the paperwork is a character-building exercise and the receptionist is the true gatekeeper to the republic.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇨🇿 Czechia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a GP as soon as you arrive | Wait until you are ill to find one |
| Buy private cover if you hold a non-EU long-term visa | Assume the public system will take you by default |
| Learn that 112 is more likely to reach an English speaker than 155 | Call the ambulance line expecting English |
| Keep your eRecept details handy | Lose the e-prescription code and assume the pharmacy will remember |

| 🇵🇱 Poland ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your employer about private clinic cover | Rely on the public system alone for specialist care |
| Use the EHIC or GHIC for short visits | Treat a visitor's card as full cover for a long stay |
| Choose an English-speaking clinic if your Polish is shaky | Expect the public clinic to translate for you |
| Book early and be persistent | Assume a referral will arrive without chasing it |

## 🇨🇿 Czechia

The Expat.com guide, updated in July 2026, gives the outline. The country has around 160 hospitals and more than 25,000 outpatient practices and specialist offices, according to the national statistics it cites, and it ranks around fourteenth in the European Consumer Health Index. That is a respectable showing for a mid-sized state in the middle of the continent. If you are employed by a Czech company, you are normally in the public insurance system by default. The self-employed pay contributions based on 13.5 percent of their tax base. Non-EU long-term visa holders generally cannot use the public system unless they are employed locally and must buy private cover for their stay. An EHIC covers basic and emergency public care, but not private clinics or repatriation.

The part that catches expats is the GP. You must register with one, and while you may change GP once every three months, finding one with space is the difficulty. An Expats.cz piece from this summer, partly behind a paywall, makes the same point in its summary: Czech care is high quality and affordable but bureaucratic, GP registration is essential and often hard because of limited availability, specialist waits can be long and private services mainly offer faster access and English support. It also cites about 1,000 formal complaints a year against medical professionals, which I would take as a sign that the system has a working complaints process.

Practical tips are cheerfully mundane. The ambulance number is 155, and 112 is the EU-wide emergency line with a better chance of an English-speaking operator. Prescriptions are electronic, through eRecept, generally valid for 14 days. Private membership clinics, such as Canadian Medical, MyClinic and Unicare Medical, typically run from a few hundred to a few thousand euros a year, and they exist largely to give you an English-speaking doctor and an appointment this week.

I find this a perfectly sensible bargain. You pay modestly for the public pipes, you pay a bit more for convenience and you accept that the Czech state would like you to fill in a form first. The outcome is a system in which the care is good and the access is the puzzle. Do the registration before you get ill, and you will almost never need to think about it again.

## 🇵🇱 Poland

Poland tells a similar story with a louder private sector. Expat Arrivals' Krakow guide recommends private insurance because costs add up, and notes that public specialist queues can run for months while private facilities can see you the same day. It also claims that nearly 80 percent of locals who can afford it choose private care, a statement the page itself does not source, so I offer it as folklore with a plausible ring. It lists hospitals in Krakow, including John Paul II Hospital, and the EHIC and GHIC arrangements for short visits.

The most useful voice on the page is an unnamed expat, who explains the two-track arrangement. An employer paying contributions to the national health fund NFZ gives you public access. Corporate staff, though, typically need a private clinic alongside, and Medicover is recommended for English-speaking doctors and a decent app. That, in a sentence, is what Polish expat healthcare looks like: a state system in the background and a private subscription in the foreground, bought partly through your employer and used for everything that is time-sensitive.

I could not reach the Expat Focus Poland page, so I cannot tell you more about how GPs and referrals work than the Krakow guide does. What I can say is that, in common with Czechia, the quality of care is rarely the complaint. The complaint is the wait. A Polish public specialist appointment can take months, and anybody with a choice moves to a private clinic and pays a subscription or a fee. The system is two-tier in practice if not in law, and the expat is placed on the upper tier by default.

The upside is convenience. A private clinic in Warsaw or Krakow can give you English, an app, a same-week appointment and a polite receptionist, and your employer may be paying for it. The downside is the one you can see coming: if you are self-employed or a freelancer, the bill is yours, and the public queue is where you wait.

## The Verdict

Poland wins on convenience for the corporate expat, and Czechia wins on the integrity of its public system. If your employer will pay for private cover, Poland is brilliant: a modern clinic, an app and an English-speaking doctor within days. If you are on your own, Czechia's public system is probably the fairer deal, provided you secure a GP before you are ill.

I would pick Czechia for a family and Poland for a single professional with a good package, and I would pick neither for anyone who thinks healthcare means not thinking about healthcare. In both countries, the people at the sharp end are competent and kind. It is the logistics that are performance art. Register early, carry your insurance details and keep a private clinic in your phone.

## Conclusion

Czechia will insist that you register and make you chase the GP. Poland will let you queue, and then politely sell you a way out of it. Both will keep you alive and neither will hold your hand. Buy the insurance, learn the emergency number, keep your paperwork in one folder and treat the private clinic as part of the cost of living. It is cheaper than being clever.
