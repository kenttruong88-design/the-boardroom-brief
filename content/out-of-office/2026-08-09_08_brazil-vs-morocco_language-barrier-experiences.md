# Brazil Will Let You Fumble Through In Bad Spanish And Somehow Still Adore You For Trying. Morocco Will Switch Fluidly Between Four Languages Mid-Sentence And Expect You To Keep Up.

🇧🇷 🇲🇦 *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Language barriers rarely announce themselves as cleanly as you'd expect. In Brazil, the barrier is really a trapdoor: Portuguese looks close enough to Spanish that overconfident travellers wander in thinking they'll manage, only to discover the two languages are cousins, not twins, and the gap is wider than it looks on paper. In Morocco, the barrier is more like a moving target — darija (Moroccan Arabic), French, Modern Standard Arabic, Berber dialects, and increasingly English all operate simultaneously, often within the same conversation, often within the same sentence, and knowing which one to reach for changes by city, by generation, and sometimes by the specific shop you've walked into.

I've tried to order lunch in São Paulo using what I confidently believed was serviceable Spanish and been met with the polite, blank patience reserved for a well-meaning idiot. I've also stood in a Marrakech medina listening to a vendor pivot from darija to French to broken English to sell me the same teapot three times over, adjusting his pitch each time without missing a beat.

## Do's & Don'ts: Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn at least basic Portuguese pronunciation before arriving — nasal vowels and different stress patterns make it far less mutually intelligible with Spanish than assumed | Rely on Spanish and expect smooth understanding; "Portuñol" gets you partway but causes as much confusion as it resolves |
| Use apps or classes specifically for Brazilian Portuguese, not European Portuguese — pronunciation and vocabulary differ meaningfully | Be discouraged by initial confusion; Brazilians are famously warm about language mistakes and genuinely appreciate the effort |
| Pick up common gírias (slang) early — Brazilian Portuguese leans heavily informal even in semi-professional contexts | Assume English gets you far outside major tourist areas of Rio and São Paulo; it's far less widespread than commonly believed |

## Do's & Don'ts: Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn a handful of darija greeting phrases — even basic effort is noticed and warmly rewarded, especially outside tourist zones | Assume Modern Standard Arabic (learned from textbooks) will be understood in casual daily speech; darija is genuinely a different register |
| Lean on French in cities and with older generations — it remains a strong second language, particularly in business and administration | Expect consistency; language use shifts by region, generation, and context, sometimes within a single conversation |
| Use English confidently with younger Moroccans, especially in Marrakech, Casablanca, and Rabat — proficiency has grown quickly | Be surprised by rapid code-switching mid-sentence; it's completely normal, not confusion or inconsistency on the speaker's part |

## Brazil: The Deceptive Comfort Of Almost-Spanish

Portuguese and Spanish share enough vocabulary and grammatical DNA that Spanish speakers, or those who've studied Spanish, routinely walk into Brazil assuming they'll manage fine. The trap is real: written Portuguese is often genuinely readable to a Spanish speaker, which creates false confidence that evaporates the moment actual spoken Brazilian Portuguese arrives — nasal vowels, different stress patterns, and vocabulary that diverges sharply in exactly the everyday words you need most (obrigado, not gracias; a whole different verb conjugation logic underneath the familiar-looking sentence structure).

What saves the experience, consistently, is Brazilian attitude toward the attempt itself. Brazilians are famously, almost aggressively patient and warm toward foreigners butchering their language — mistakes get met with encouragement, not correction-by-embarrassment, and a fumbling "Portuñol" hybrid, while linguistically messy, is received as effort rather than failure. This cultural generosity does a lot of heavy lifting for newcomers who'd otherwise struggle badly.

English proficiency, contrary to what many expect from a country of Brazil's global profile, remains genuinely patchy outside upscale areas of Rio and São Paulo and major international business contexts. Outside those pockets, real Portuguese — even broken, halting, heavily accented Portuguese — is essential for daily functioning, and the payoff for learning it properly is a country that meets your effort with outsized warmth.

## Morocco: Fluid, Layered, And Genuinely Multilingual

Morocco's linguistic landscape is less a single barrier than a stack of them, operating simultaneously and switching fluidly depending on who's speaking to whom. Darija, Moroccan Arabic, is the everyday spoken language of the street, distinct enough from Modern Standard Arabic (the formal, pan-Arab register taught in textbooks and used in media) that visitors who've studied "proper" Arabic are often startled to find they can read a sign but can't follow a conversation happening two feet away. French remains deeply embedded from the colonial period, still dominant in business, higher education, and government, particularly among older and more urban Moroccans. Berber (Amazigh) dialects persist strongly in rural and mountain regions. English has surged rapidly among younger, especially urban, Moroccans, adopted enthusiastically as the language of global opportunity.

The result, for a newcomer, is genuinely disorienting in a way Brazil's more singular barrier isn't: a single transaction in a Marrakech souk might involve darija greetings, a French price negotiation, and an English aside to a foreign friend, all within the same two minutes, delivered by the same vendor without any apparent effort. This isn't confusion on the Moroccan side — it's a routine, practised, entirely normal form of linguistic multitasking that most Moroccans grow up doing instinctively.

For visitors, the practical strategy that works best isn't mastering any one language, but building a flexible toolkit: enough darija to greet and show respect, enough French to navigate formal contexts and older generations, and a readiness to lean on English with younger Moroccans in cities. Rigid commitment to a single "correct" language, ironically, works worse here than comfortable improvisation.

## The Verdict

Brazil offers a single, deep barrier softened by extraordinary cultural patience — get the Portuguese wrong and you'll still be met with a smile and genuine appreciation for trying. Morocco offers a shallower but wider barrier, a genuinely multilingual environment where flexibility matters more than mastery of any single tongue. If you want a country that rewards earnest, imperfect effort in one language, Brazil delivers warmly. If you want a country that will teach you to code-switch on instinct within a month simply by necessity, Morocco will get you there faster than any classroom could. I've embarrassed myself more thoroughly in Brazil. I've adapted faster in Morocco. Draw your own conclusions.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/brasil — a longtime expat noted that Spanish speakers often struggle more than true beginners, because false confidence leads to worse miscommunication than starting from genuine zero.*

> *Reddit r/Morocco — a traveller described spending a week using textbook Modern Standard Arabic to blank stares, until a local gently explained that nobody actually speaks it in daily conversation.*

> *Internations São Paulo — a member shared that attempting "Portuñol" in a taxi led to a full ten-minute detour, and recommended committing to proper, if broken, Portuguese from day one instead.*

## Conclusion

Brazil and Morocco both punish false confidence and reward genuine, humble effort, just through different mechanisms — one through a language close enough to fool you, the other through a shifting stack of languages that never lets you get comfortable in the first place. Learn real Brazilian Portuguese, not Portuñol. Learn a little darija, keep your French warm, and stay ready to pivot in Morocco. Either way, arrive humble. Both countries will find your assumptions within the first conversation, and both, in their own way, will forgive you for having them.
