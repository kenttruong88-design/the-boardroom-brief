# One Country Does A Weekly Shop, The Other Refuses To Commit

🇬🇧 United Kingdom 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country reveals its soul in the supermarket, and I say that as someone who has stood, bewildered, in grocery aisles on four continents trying to work out why bread is where the dairy should be. The UK and Spain both claim to be "European," a label that turns out to mean almost nothing once you compare how each actually buys its dinner. Britain has industrialised the weekly shop into a large-format, trolley-based ritual dominated by a handful of supermarket giants slugging it out for market share. Spain, even now, even with its own enormous supermarket chains, still can't fully let go of the daily mercado — the fresh market — where buying fish means making eye contact with the person who caught it, or at least the person who's pretending they did.

Britain's supermarket hierarchy is practically a class system, and expats learn it with the seriousness of learning local currency exchange rates: Waitrose and M&S at the aspirational end, Tesco and Sainsbury's dominating the middle as the reliable, slightly impersonal default, Asda and Morrisons doing solid value work, and Aldi and Lidl having spent the last decade dragging the entire market's price expectations downward, hard enough that the "Big Four" have had to respond with their own budget sub-brands just to compete. The whole system optimises for one giant trip a week, trolley loaded, click-and-collect or home delivery increasingly replacing the trip altogether. Spain resists this on principle, or at least on habit: even in cities with sprawling Carrefour and Mercadona branches, a meaningful chunk of the population still does the daily or near-daily run to the barrio's mercado — for bread that won't be fresh tomorrow, fish that came in that morning, and a conversation with the fishmonger that the supermarket self-checkout simply cannot replicate.

## Do's & Don'ts: United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the supermarket hierarchy fast — where you shop genuinely functions as a quiet class signifier, whether Britons admit it or not | Block the aisle with your trolley to check your phone — you will be tutted at, possibly audibly, definitely internally |
| Bring your own bags — the plastic bag charge made this second nature years ago and nobody's bringing cash for a new one | Assume Sunday trading hours match weekdays — many larger stores still close earlier by law on Sundays |
| Use self-checkout without hesitation — it's now the default for a small basket, and queueing for a till with ten items reads as mildly eccentric | Dawdle at the self-checkout scanning slowly — the queue behind you is silently, very British-ly, furious |

## Do's & Don'ts: Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take a numbered ticket at a mercado stall and wait your turn — jumping the informal queue is a genuine faux pas | Expect mercado stalls to keep supermarket hours — many close for a long midday break and shut entirely by early evening |
| Ask the vendor for a recommendation — "¿qué me recomienda hoy?" is not only accepted, it's often expected and appreciated | Buy a week's worth of fresh produce in one go — the culture assumes you'll be back tomorrow, so quantities and freshness are calibrated daily |
| Greet the stallholder ("buenos días") before ordering — skipping straight to your list reads as brusque | Be surprised that many neighbourhoods still support both a supermarket and a thriving mercado a block apart — they serve different purposes, not competing ones |

## United Kingdom: The Trolley Is Law

The British supermarket run has been optimised within an inch of its life. The "big weekly shop" is a genuine cultural unit — Sunday or Saturday morning, trolley, list, a specific route through a specific store that regulars can navigate half-asleep — and increasingly it isn't even a trip at all, with online grocery delivery slots booked days in advance during the busiest windows. What's fascinating to watch as an outsider is the sheer tribal loyalty attached to which chain you choose. Waitrose shoppers and Lidl shoppers are, culturally, read as different types of people, an entirely unspoken but universally understood hierarchy that has survived a full decade of the discounters proving, repeatedly, that their own-brand goods often beat the premium chains on blind taste tests. The self-checkout has become near-total in its takeover for small baskets, to the point that queueing at a staffed till with fewer than ten items now reads as a small act of either nostalgia or rebellion. And nothing reveals British retail law quite like the Sunday trading hours restriction — larger stores legally capped at six hours of trading, a genuine and mildly baffling surprise to newcomers who show up at 5pm on a Sunday expecting normal hours and find the big Tesco locked, the smaller Tesco Express open, because different rules apply to different footprints.

## Spain: The Mercado Isn't Nostalgia, It's Infrastructure

Spain has enormous, thoroughly modern supermarket chains — Mercadona, in particular, runs with an efficiency and own-brand quality that regularly gets discussed with genuine national pride — and yet the mercado has refused to become a heritage attraction. Madrid alone supports dozens of historic markets, from the tourist-heavy Mercado de San Miguel to the unglamorous, entirely local barrio markets where the tourists never wander, and the distinction matters: the barrio mercado is not performing Spanish culture for visitors, it's simply how a meaningful share of the population still buys fish, meat, and produce, daily or near-daily, on the logic that food bought today should be eaten within days, not weeks. The etiquette has real edges — a numbered ticket system at busy stalls, a genuine expectation that you'll exchange a few words with the vendor rather than silently pointing, and an assumption, baked into portion sizes and stock levels, that you're planning dinner, not provisioning a bunker. Supermarkets absolutely coexist alongside this — nobody's buying olive oil from the fishmonger — but the mercado's survival, well into an era when convenience should theoretically have killed it, says something real about how central fresh, daily, face-to-face food shopping remains to Spanish domestic life.

## The Verdict

Britain has built the more efficient system, full stop — one big trip, minimal friction, genuine choice across a real price spectrum, and self-checkout that respects your time. Spain has built the more *alive* system, one that asks more of you — memorise the ticket queue, learn the vendor's name, come back tomorrow — and rewards you with fish that tastes like it remembers the sea. If your priority is never thinking about groceries between Sundays, Britain wins outright. If your priority is a system that quietly insists food shopping remain a daily, human-scale ritual rather than a logistics problem to be solved once a week, Spain's mercado will not let you forget what you're missing.

## Conclusion

Britain optimised the weekly shop into something close to a solved problem — efficient, hierarchical, faintly tribal about which chain you're loyal to, and entirely comfortable never speaking to another human in the process. Spain never solved it, because it never saw grocery shopping as a problem needing solving; it's a daily social transaction that happens to end with dinner. Bring a trolley bag to London. Bring small talk to Madrid. You'll need very different muscles for each, and only one of them will actually make you a regular anywhere.
