🇰🇿 Kazakhstan 🇨🇦 Canada
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Kazakhstan will hand you a street sign in Kazakh, a menu in Russian, an official form that might be in either depending on which ministry printed it, and precisely zero expectation that English will rescue you from any of it. Canada will tell you, proudly and officially, that it has two national languages — and then let an entire anglophone province quietly operate as though French doesn't exist, right up until a federal form arrives that absolutely requires it.

I have mimed my way through a Almaty pharmacy transaction using nothing but hand gestures and a worrying amount of trust. I have also stood in a Canadian government office being handed a document in French with zero warning, in a city where I'd gone eighteen months without hearing the language spoken once. Both experiences taught me the same lesson from opposite directions: official bilingualism and lived bilingualism are not the same thing, and the gap between them is where every newcomer gets quietly ambushed.

## Do's & Don'ts: Kazakhstan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Russian before basic Kazakh for everyday urban life; Russian remains the dominant lingua franca in business and cities | Assume Kazakh alone will get you through daily Almaty or Astana life; Russian is still more broadly spoken in urban commerce |
| Carry a translation app and expect to use it constantly outside hotels and major international offices | Expect English signage or English-speaking staff as a default, even in reasonably central areas |
| Learn the Cyrillic alphabet, at minimum, to read signs and menus phonetically | Rely on Latin-script transliteration; Kazakhstan's transition away from Cyrillic is gradual and incomplete |
| Hire a local fixer or translator for anything bureaucratic — visas, leases, utility connections | Attempt serious paperwork solo without translation help; official processes are rarely available in English |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether a federal form, process, or job requires French before assuming English alone suffices | Assume English is sufficient nationwide just because it's dominant in your specific city or province |
| Learn that Quebec operates under its own distinct language law (Bill 96/the Charter of the French Language) affecting signage and business | Move to Quebec expecting English-first service as a given; French-language requirements there are legally binding |
| Expect federal government communication to often arrive bilingually, by law | Be surprised when a federal document, call centre, or form defaults to French in parts of the country |
| Treat "Canada is bilingual" as a legal and federal fact, not a lived daily reality everywhere | Expect everyday spoken bilingualism outside Quebec, New Brunswick, and specific bilingual pockets; it's far from universal |

## Kazakhstan: two languages, one expectation that you'll manage

Kazakhstan's linguistic landscape is a direct legacy of its Soviet history layered under a newer, deliberate nation-building push: Kazakh is the sole state language, enshrined constitutionally, while Russian retains official status as the language of interethnic communication and remains, in practice, dominant in business, administration, and daily urban commerce, especially in Almaty. The government has been steadily pushing Kazakh-language use and a planned transition from Cyrillic to a Latin-based script, but that transition is gradual and incomplete, meaning newcomers encounter a genuinely mixed signage and documentation environment, sometimes within the same building.

What this means practically for an expat is that English functions as essentially a non-factor outside hotels, major international firms, and a thin layer of urban young professionals. Everyday transactions — the pharmacy, the market, a utility connection, a lease — are conducted overwhelmingly in Russian or Kazakh, and expats who arrive assuming a reasonable baseline of English, the way they might in much of Western Europe or Southeast Asia, adjust fastest by simply accepting that learning functional Russian (not Kazakh, counterintuitively, for most urban daily needs) is close to mandatory rather than a nice-to-have.

Bureaucratic processes compound this: visas, residency registration, and property paperwork are conducted in the official languages with no English-language track offered, making a local fixer or translator not a luxury but close to a practical necessity for anyone trying to get through officialdom without months of frustration.

## Canada: officially bilingual, unevenly lived

Canada's bilingualism is a genuine, enforceable federal legal framework — the Official Languages Act requires federal services to be available in both English and French — but it coexists with enormous regional variation in how that plays out day to day. An anglophone newcomer settling in Vancouver or Calgary can go remarkably long stretches without encountering spoken French at all, while federal paperwork, passport services, and certain job postings will still, correctly, include French as a requirement or default, catching newcomers off guard who'd quietly assumed English alone covered them nationally.

Quebec operates on an entirely different footing again: its own provincial language law (most recently reinforced by Bill 96, strengthening the Charter of the French Language) makes French the mandatory default for signage, workplace communication above certain business sizes, and many public services, a legal regime distinct from and in some ways stricter than the federal bilingual framework. A newcomer moving from an English-dominant Canadian city to Montreal, assuming "Canada is bilingual" means the same thing everywhere, finds a genuinely different legal and social language environment waiting for them.

Statistics Canada's own census bilingualism data consistently shows English-French bilingualism concentrated heavily in Quebec, New Brunswick (Canada's only officially bilingual province), and specific border regions — meaning the comforting national narrative of "we speak both" is, for most of the country's geography, more aspirational than descriptive of daily life.

Education policy reveals how deliberately each country is managing its multilingual identity. Kazakhstan has been rolling out a trilingual education policy over the past decade, aiming to produce graduates fluent in Kazakh, Russian, and English simultaneously, a genuinely ambitious state project reflecting the country's wider geopolitical balancing act between Russian cultural influence, Kazakh national identity, and global economic integration. Canada's equivalent — French immersion programs available to anglophone families outside Quebec — is popular and well-regarded but entirely optional, chosen by individual families rather than mandated as national policy, meaning a child's functional bilingualism in Canada depends heavily on parental choice and local school availability rather than any uniform national expectation. An expat family comparing notes might reasonably conclude that Kazakhstan, for all its newcomer-facing language barriers, is actually pursuing a more coherent and ambitious long-term multilingual strategy for its own citizens than Canada's patchwork, opt-in approach ever quite manages to be — ambition born of necessity, in Kazakhstan's case, rather than the comfortable optionality Canada can afford as a country that assumes, rightly or wrongly, that English alone will usually carry its citizens far enough.

## The Verdict

Kazakhstan is honest about its language reality in a way that's almost refreshing — nobody pretends English will save you, so you adapt accordingly and fast. Canada's language situation is trickier precisely because the official story (fully bilingual nation) doesn't match most people's lived experience (largely monolingual pockets with sudden, legally mandated bilingual exceptions), which ambushes newcomers who took the national branding at face value. If I had to choose which system causes fewer unpleasant surprises, it's Kazakhstan — the barrier is obvious from day one. Canada's barrier hides in the paperwork, and it bites you exactly when you've stopped expecting it to.

## Conclusion

Here's the sting worth sitting with: Kazakhstan's language barrier is loud and visible, so you respect it immediately and adapt. Canada's is quiet and legal, buried in a form you didn't expect, in a province you thought you understood, and it catches people precisely because the country's own self-image oversells how bilingual daily life actually is. Neither barrier is crueller than the other — they're just differently disguised. One tells you up front that you'll need to work for it. The other tells you, officially, that you won't, and then quietly proves otherwise the moment it matters most.
