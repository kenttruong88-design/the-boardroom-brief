# One Country Turns December Into a Month-Long Street Party. The Other Closes Everything and Calls It a Holiday.

🇳🇬🇵🇱 By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office

Nigeria does not observe a public holiday so much as detonate one — "Detty December" alone has become a globally recognised season of concerts, weddings, homecomings, and street parties that turn Lagos into arguably the most alive city on the planet for four straight weeks. Poland, meanwhile, treats a public holiday as a solemn, near-total shutdown: shops close, streets empty, and the entire country seems to collectively agree that celebration means quiet reflection, mulled wine, and absolutely nowhere to buy milk after 2pm.

I have danced in Lagos traffic at 2am during a homecoming owambe that had somehow absorbed an entire street, and I have wandered a beautifully lit but eerily silent Polish town square on Boxing Day, hunting for a single open shop like a very well-dressed ghost.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇳🇬 Nigeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say yes to an owambe (party) invitation — they're often genuinely open to guests | Wear white to a wedding-adjacent event unless explicitly told to |
| Budget serious time for Detty December traffic — it is legendary | Assume events start "on time" — Nigerian time runs its own clock |
| Dress up — festival and holiday events are a real fashion occasion | Skip greeting elders properly at family or community gatherings |
| Carry cash for street festival food and impromptu vendor stalls | Expect quiet in December — it is, by design, the opposite |

**🇵🇱 Poland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stock up before a public holiday — nearly everything closes, genuinely | Expect shops open on Sundays either — trading Sundays are limited by law |
| Visit a Christmas market for mulled wine (grzaniec) and oscypek cheese | Assume restaurants stay open on major holidays — many close entirely |
| Learn the "12 dishes" tradition if invited to a Polish Christmas Eve | Be loud or rowdy at solemn holidays like All Saints' Day (Nov 1) |
| Visit a cemetery on November 1st — the candlelit tradition is genuinely moving | Confuse Polish reserve for coldness — warmth comes once you're inside |

## Nigeria: Holidays as Maximum Volume, Minimum Sleep

Nigerian festival culture operates on a single, unshakeable principle: if there is an occasion to celebrate, celebrate it at full volume, for as long as possible, with as many people as can physically fit. December is the undisputed peak — "Detty December," now practically a global brand of its own, sees the Nigerian diaspora flood back into Lagos for a monthlong stretch of concerts, weddings, owambes (a general term for lavish celebratory parties), and street festivals that reportedly inject genuinely significant sums into the local economy each year, per Lagos State's own tourism messaging.

The owambe deserves special mention as a cultural institution unto itself. These are not quiet gatherings — they are elaborate, often outdoor, sometimes street-occupying celebrations featuring live bands, professional MCs, aso-ebi (matching fabric worn by guests to show affiliation with the celebrating family), and food served in quantities that suggest the hosts are actively trying to feed the entire neighbourhood, which, functionally, they often are. Attending one as an outsider is one of the great privileges of visiting Nigeria — invitations are given generously, and the expectation is simply that you show up, dress well, and dance.

What nobody adequately prepares you for is Nigerian time, a cultural concept where a 4pm event start genuinely means sometime after 6pm, and nobody bats an eye. Combine this with Lagos's already legendary traffic, amplified tenfold during December's peak travel season as diaspora Nigerians flood back for the holidays, and you learn quickly that flexibility isn't optional, it's survival. Public holidays outside December — Independence Day, Eid celebrations observed by the country's large Muslim population, Christmas itself — follow the same pattern: loud, communal, generous, and thoroughly unhurried by clock time.

## Poland: Holidays as Solemn, Total Shutdown

Poland's approach to public holidays is built on the opposite instinct entirely: a holiday is a day the country deliberately, collectively stops, and that stopping is itself the point. Poland's trading restriction laws mean that most Sundays already see major retail closed, and public holidays intensify this considerably — supermarkets, pharmacies (beyond a rotating duty roster), and most restaurants simply shut, and newcomers who haven't stocked up in advance learn this the hard way, usually hungry, on a beautifully quiet street.

Christmas in Poland reveals the country's genuine warmth beneath the reserve, but it happens almost entirely indoors and among family. Wigilia, the Christmas Eve dinner, is a deeply codified tradition featuring twelve meatless dishes — one for each apostle — beginning only once the first star appears in the sky, with an extra place set at the table for an unexpected guest, a custom rooted in older Polish hospitality traditions. Being invited to a Polish Wigilia as an outsider is a genuine honour, and the warmth on display stands in real contrast to the empty, shuttered streets outside.

Then there's All Saints' Day on November 1st, one of the most genuinely moving public holidays I've witnessed anywhere. Poles travel, sometimes long distances, to visit family graves, lighting candles across entire cemeteries until the grounds glow orange after dark — a national ritual of remembrance observed with total, unhurried seriousness. Christmas markets, meanwhile, offer the loudest Poland gets during the holiday season: Kraków's and Wrocław's markets draw genuine crowds for grzaniec (mulled wine) and oscypek (smoked sheep cheese), though even these wind down by early evening, because Poland, fundamentally, does not do 2am.

## The Verdict

Nigeria wins for sheer, uncut, communal joy — nowhere on Earth does a holiday season feel more genuinely, deliriously alive, and the generosity of being folded into a stranger's owambe is unmatched. Poland wins for depth and meaning — its holidays carry a weight and quiet reverence that Detty December, gloriously, does not aim for and does not need.

If you want a holiday season that never sleeps, book Lagos in December and bring stamina. If you want a holiday that makes you sit still and actually feel something, Poland's candlelit cemeteries and Wigilia table will do that far better than any street party. Just plan your grocery shopping accordingly — one country will feed you at 3am from a street vendor, the other will not sell you bread after Thursday.

## Conclusion

Nigeria's public holidays are a masterclass in collective joy — loud, generous, unhurried by anyone's clock but their own, and genuinely welcoming to outsiders who show up ready to dance. Poland's public holidays are a masterclass in collective stillness — solemn, warm once you're indoors, and entirely unbothered by whether you managed to buy bread beforehand. Both cultures know exactly what they're doing. Only one of them will feed you at 3am, and only one of them will make you cry at a cemetery. Choose your December accordingly.
