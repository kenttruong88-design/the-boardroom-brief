# Czechia Wants You to Learn Czech; Switzerland Wants You to Guess Which Swiss

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Czechia, the language barrier is a wall with a small, grumpy door. Czech has cases, declensions and consonant clusters that look like an assault on the alphabet, and the people who speak it are lovely but will not pretend otherwise. You can get by in Prague on English, until the day you cannot, and that day arrives with a form, a stamp and a clerk who looks at you as if you had asked for the moon.

In Switzerland, the barrier is a moving target. There are four national languages, a dialect of German that nobody writes down and everybody speaks, and a general assumption that you will work it out. English is everywhere in the offices and nowhere in the post office. I have lived in places where I knew nothing and places where I knew too little, and I can report that both feel like being a toddler in a very expensive suit.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇨🇿 Czechia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn greetings, numbers and basic phrases | Open every sentence with "Do you speak English?" |
| Find a Czech friend or reliable translator for paperwork | Go to an office alone and hope |
| Say dobrý den when you enter a shop | Walk in without greeting anyone |
| Keep a translation app handy | Assume every official speaks English |

| 🇨🇭 Switzerland ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the standard language of your region | Try to imitate a local dialect too early |
| Greet people in the local language, even badly | Lead with English in a small village |
| Accept that offices may run in English | Assume the shop or the post office will |
| Be patient with regional differences | Treat Switzerland as one linguistic country |

## 🇨🇿 Czechia

Czechia is a country in which English is common among the young and in the big firms, and absent in places where it matters most. A January 2025 Expats.cz article, drawn from answers collected on Facebook and Instagram and edited for length, found most of the expats it polled advised newcomers to learn at least some basic Czech before moving. Chloe, from Andorra, said that the foreign police and the immigration office do not speak English, which is one person's report but a familiar warning. Catherine, from Italy, advised finding a Czech friend, because you will need one when sorting documents, and Oscar, from Portugal, advised finding a reliable translator from day one.

I want to be careful here: the page itself does not claim Czech is difficult, and one anonymous expat said that ninety percent of people in Prague speak English, which is a personal claim, not a measured figure. What the article does show is a pattern. In the daily bubble of cafés, restaurants and offices, English works. In the bureaucratic back rooms, it does not. The language barrier in Czechia is therefore less like a wall and more like a trapdoor, and you tend to fall through it at the exact moment you are carrying paperwork.

The social side is more subtle. Czechs are reserved with strangers, and a greeting in their language earns more goodwill than a perfect sentence in yours. Dobrý den on entering a shop, děkuji on leaving, and a willingness to look foolish will take you further than a month of grammar. The reward for effort is not applause but a small thaw, a slight easing of the shoulders, and sometimes a switch to excellent English, delivered with the expression of someone who was testing you all along.

My own experience agrees with the survey. The first year, I resented the language. The second year, I enjoyed being part of the joke. By the third, I could argue with a ticket inspector in broken Czech, and I will tell you that nothing, not a promotion, not a salary rise, felt as good.

## 🇨🇭 Switzerland

Switzerland has four official languages and a talent for confusing people about which one is in use. A forum member called rekapa, writing on Expat.com, argued that Switzerland has four official languages but no single common one, and that some people consider Swiss German a language when it is really a dialect spoken differently across cantons. They added that children learn High German in school and pick up some dialect, and that in most international firms the corporate language is English. That last point is the one that rescues the expat in the office. The first is the one that humbles you in the street.

IamExpat's 2022 guide makes a related point from the other side. English is widely spoken in Swiss cities, it says, but it is still important to try the local language, and Swiss-German phrases and slang do not always translate well with translation apps. That is an understatement. A phrase that works in Zurich may be baffling in Bern, and a word that makes sense in the Valais may be meaningless in Basel. The nuance is part of the culture, and it is not something an app can fix.

What distinguishes Switzerland from Czechia is how quickly the barrier moves. In the German-speaking north, you may be advised to learn standard German, since that is what you will write, read and be taught. In the French-speaking west, you will need French. In the Italian-speaking south, you will need Italian. And in each place, the locals will be friendly, precise and slightly amused by your efforts. In the Swiss case, the question is not whether you will learn the language, but which one.

For an expat, the practical solution is to identify your region early, find a good language course and be patient with the dialect. You do not have to speak it, but you do have to understand it, and the difference between those two is the difference between a pleasant life and a lonely one. And remember, if you are talking to someone from the next valley, you may both be speaking a different language entirely.

## The Verdict

Switzerland wins, narrowly, and only because its English works in the places where Czech English does not. In the office, the airport, the bank and the corporate flat, English will carry you a long way. You will feel competent, which is the most valuable feeling a foreigner can have. And you will have a clear path to learning the local language when you are ready.

Czechia is harder, but also more honest. Nobody there pretends the barrier does not exist, and the effort you put in is noticed and rewarded. I would argue it makes for a better story, and a deeper connection, if you are willing to be a beginner. If you want comfort, choose Switzerland. If you want to earn something, choose Czechia, and buy a phrasebook with a very large font.

## Conclusion

Czechia will make you earn your coffee. Switzerland will make you choose a language and then check your choice. Both will respect the effort and neither will fully forgive the shortcut. Learn the greeting, find a friend for the forms and keep a straight face when someone corrects your grammar. A language is a country you can carry, and the heavier it is, the more of the place you own.
