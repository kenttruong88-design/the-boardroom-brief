# Nigeria Pays at the Door and Ireland Makes You Wait Until You Have Forgotten Why You Came

🇳🇬 Nigeria vs 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Healthcare in Nigeria is a transaction conducted at speed. You go to a private clinic, you pay, you are seen. Public hospitals exist and the best of their doctors are heroic, but the savvy middle class has long since arranged its own system: the trusted family doctor, the private hospital, the pharmacist who knows what you need before you finish the sentence, and the relative abroad who sends the medication nobody can find locally.

Healthcare in Ireland is an exercise in managed expectations. There is a public system, a private system and a large grey zone between them, in which you will be told that you are entitled to something, shown a form, and placed on a list. It is not cruel and it is not incompetent. It is simply a country where waiting has become a form of national character, practised with great dignity and a strong cup of tea.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Nigeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Find a trusted private clinic or hospital before you need one | Wait for an emergency to look for a doctor |
| Keep cash or a payment method ready for consultations | Assume that someone else will cover the deposit |
| Ask your employer exactly what the health plan covers | Treat the insurance card as a magic wand |
| Keep copies of your own records and prescriptions | Rely on one clinic holding your history |

**Ireland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a GP as early as you can | Wait until you are ill to find a doctor |
| Check whether you qualify for a GP visit card or medical card | Assume the public system is free for everyone |
| Consider private health insurance, especially for hospital care | Expect to see a specialist quickly without it |
| Book appointments well in advance | Turn up unannounced and expect a slot |

## Nigeria

The Nigerian health system has two faces and most people live in the gap between them. The public side, which includes federal teaching hospitals and state facilities, offers real expertise but is strained by funding, strikes, shortages and the sheer number of people who depend on it. A national health insurance scheme exists and has been expanding its reach, but a large share of the population still pays out of pocket, and for many the question is not which doctor to see but whether they can afford to see one.

For the expat or the returning professional, the practical answer is private care. Private clinics and hospitals in the big cities offer shorter waits, modern equipment and doctors who answer the phone. Employers often provide a health plan, and the plan will tell you which facilities are in its network. Read the fine print. A plan that sounds generous in the offer letter can turn out to exclude the very thing you came about.

What Nigerians do very well is improvise. The pharmacist is a first point of contact, the family WhatsApp group is a second opinion and the cousin who is a nurse in Manchester is a consultant of last resort. It is a culture of self-reliance that treats health as a project you manage yourself rather than a service you receive. That can be empowering, and it can also be dangerous, because self-diagnosis and self-medication are common precisely because proper care is expensive.

Emergency care deserves a warning. Ambulance services are patchy, and in many places the fastest route to a hospital is a private vehicle. If you are relocating, know your nearest accredited facility and how you would get there. Plan it before the night you need it.

The cost of that independence is rarely mentioned in the glossy brochures. A private hospital stay can be expensive, medication quality varies between suppliers and the sensible expat keeps a small emergency fund and an evacuation plan for anything serious. Many experienced residents say the same thing: the care is good if you pay for good care, and you must know in advance who you will call.

## Ireland

Ireland runs a mixed system, and it is confusing enough that Irish people themselves explain it badly. The public system, run by the Health Service Executive, offers hospital care that is heavily subsidised but famously slow for non-urgent matters. Some residents qualify for a medical card or a GP visit card, which cuts or removes the cost of seeing a family doctor. Most adults do not, and they pay a consultation fee each time, which comes as a nasty surprise to expats from countries where a doctor's visit is either free or a bargain.

Private health insurance is widespread and, for many, close to essential. It does not cover everything, and it is not cheap, but it can shorten the wait for hospital treatment, and the difference between public and private waiting times is something every Irish dinner party eventually discusses. Expat forum threads on the subject keep returning to the same advice: sort out your insurance early, and understand what it actually pays for.

Registering with a GP is the first practical step, and it is harder than it sounds in areas where practices are full and not taking new patients. People circulate stories of calling a dozen surgeries before one agrees. It is a quirk of a system with a doctor shortage, and not a comment on you.

Once inside, though, the care is thoughtful. GPs are generalists with real depth, nurses are knowledgeable, and the culture is calm. You will be asked how you are, and the question will be meant. You will also be asked to wait, and that will be meant too.

Pharmacies are a small, welcome piece of relief. Pharmacists in Ireland are trained, approachable and happy to advise on minor complaints, and the culture of asking at the counter before booking a doctor is sensible and cheap. Prescriptions are carefully controlled, which will come as a shock to anyone used to buying whatever they like over the counter. Bring your records and a list of your medication.

## The Verdict

Nigeria is faster for the person who can pay. Ireland is more reliable for the person who cannot. That is the whole story, and it is a rather uncomfortable one, because it means that the better system depends entirely on your bank balance.

If you are an expat with an employer plan, Lagos or Abuja will give you speed and a degree of personal attention that the Irish system cannot match. If you are an ordinary resident without private cover, Ireland will give you safety, stability and a guarantee that you will not be bankrupted by an appendix.

I give Ireland the win on principle and Nigeria the win on convenience. The honest verdict is that neither country has solved the problem of being ill at an awkward moment. They have simply chosen different ways of making you feel guilty about it.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *ExpatExchange, 15 expats on Irish healthcare — recurring theme: a doctor's visit costs real money unless you hold a medical or GP visit card, and nobody tells you until the first bill.*

> *Boards.ie, GP card thread — recurring theme: the application process is slow, the rules are not obvious and the answer depends on income and circumstances.*

> *ExpatExchange, Irish healthcare question — recurring theme: private insurance is worth the cost mainly for shortening hospital waiting times.*

## Conclusion

Move from Lagos to Dublin and you will discover the strange peace of a system that, however slow, will not ask for a deposit before treating you in an emergency. Move from Dublin to Lagos and you will discover the equally strange peace of being seen by a doctor within the hour.

Neither is paradise. One makes you wait, one makes you pay, and both make you prepared. Buy the insurance, keep the records and find the doctor before you need one. That advice is the same in every language, and it is the only piece of medical wisdom I have never seen anyone regret following.
