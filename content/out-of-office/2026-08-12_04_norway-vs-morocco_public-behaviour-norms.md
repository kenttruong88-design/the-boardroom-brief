# Norway Will Give You Two Metres of Silence. Morocco Will Give You a Nickname by Lunch.

🇳🇴 Norway vs 🇲🇦 Morocco — Public Behaviour Norms

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Norway, standing too close to someone at a bus stop is treated as a minor act of aggression. In Morocco, a stranger will grab your arm mid-sentence to make sure you're really listening. Both are entirely normal within their own borders and entirely alarming the moment you cross into the other one unprepared.

Norwegians have built a public culture around the sacred principle of leaving people alone. Moroccans have built one around the equally sacred principle of never leaving anyone alone, ever, under any circumstances. I have been ignored more thoroughly in Oslo than anywhere else on Earth, and I have been more warmly, relentlessly included in Marrakech than I was at my own leaving party.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep a respectful distance at bus stops, checkouts, and public benches — at least an arm's length, ideally more | Make small talk with strangers unprompted; it reads as odd, not friendly |
| Queue in silence and wait your exact turn, even if the line isn't clearly marked | Comment loudly on someone else's appearance, purchases, or business, even kindly |
| Hold the door, nod, move on — brief courtesy without conversation is the norm | Take offence at the silence; it's social norm, not personal rejection |

### 🇲🇦 Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet shopkeepers and neighbours warmly, even briefly — it's expected, not optional | Rush a greeting or skip straight to business; it reads as cold and rude |
| Expect physical closeness in conversation — a hand on the arm is warmth, not intrusion | Photograph people in the souk without asking first |
| Dress modestly in medinas and away from tourist beach areas, regardless of the heat | Show visible impatience while bargaining; it kills the whole interaction |

## Norway: The Art of Militant Politeness

Norwegian public behaviour operates on one unbending principle: your personal space is sacred, and violating it — even with kindness — is a small transgression. Strangers do not make small talk on the bus. They do not comment on your shopping, your dog, your baby, or your weather-inappropriate coat. They will hold a door for you with a brief nod and consider the social transaction complete. Attempting to extend it into actual conversation marks you, instantly, as either a tourist or slightly unwell.

The famous Norwegian queue is a masterclass in unspoken order — no barriers, no numbers half the time, just a shared, silent agreement about whose turn it is, enforced purely through collective memory and mild telepathy. Jump it, even accidentally, and you won't get shouted at. You'll get something worse: a long, quiet look that somehow conveys generations of disappointment in under two seconds.

What surprises newcomers most is that this isn't coldness — Norwegians will happily help you if you're visibly lost or struggling, and will do so efficiently and kindly. It's specifically unsolicited social contact that's treated as intrusive. Comfortable silence is considered a sign of respect, not awkwardness; two Norwegians can sit next to each other for an entire train journey without a word and consider it a perfectly pleasant shared experience. Try this in most other countries and someone will assume you've had a fight.

Public space itself reflects the same instinct — buses have generous personal room by European standards, benches are spaced to avoid forced proximity, and even friend groups tend to leave a little more physical distance than you'd see in southern Europe. It's not hostility. It's a culture that has decided, collectively and firmly, that leaving people alone is the highest form of courtesy.

## Morocco: Warmth as a Public Obligation

Moroccan public life runs on the opposite premise entirely: ignoring someone is the rude act, and warmth is a social duty owed to strangers as much as friends. Walk through a medina and you will be greeted, engaged, invited for tea, and asked about your family within minutes, by people who have never met you and have no intention of selling you anything — though plenty will, and the two things are not mutually exclusive, which trips up a lot of visitors who assume all friendliness is a sales tactic.

Physical closeness in conversation is standard, not forward — a hand on the shoulder, a grip on the arm to underline a point, a level of proximity that would have a Norwegian quietly recalculating their exit route. This is warmth, not intrusion, and pulling away too visibly can genuinely offend, since it's read as coldness rather than a reasonable boundary.

Bargaining in the souk is public theatre as much as commerce, and it comes with its own behavioural code: enthusiasm, patience, and a willingness to banter are rewarded; visible irritation or impatience kills the interaction and, with it, any chance of a good price. Vendors expect a bit of back-and-forth performance, and refusing to play along reads as a small social failure on your part, not a savvy negotiating tactic.

Modesty in dress carries real social weight, particularly away from resort areas — this isn't really about religion so much as basic respect for the norms of the space you're standing in, and getting it visibly wrong draws stares that communicate disapproval more effectively than any spoken correction could. Greetings, meanwhile, are not optional pleasantries but genuine social infrastructure — skip them and go straight to business, even in a shop, and you'll be met with a coolness that, ironically, is the Moroccan equivalent of the Norwegian silent treatment.

## The Verdict

Norway offers you total freedom from unwanted social contact, at the cost of ever feeling truly included by a stranger. Morocco offers you constant, warm, occasionally overwhelming inclusion, at the cost of your personal space and your ability to walk anywhere without a conversation. Neither is wrong. Both are exhausting in their own specific way — Norway exhausts you with the effort of never presuming, Morocco exhausts you with the effort of never disengaging. I know which one left me more emotionally full and which one left me more rested. I will not tell you which is which, because you already know from the tone of this sentence.

## Conclusion

If public behaviour is a language, Norway speaks it in careful, respectful silence, and Morocco speaks it at full volume with both hands. You will need to unlearn something in either direction — Norwegians visiting Marrakech should brace for relentless, genuine warmth they'll initially mistake for a scam, and Moroccans visiting Oslo should brace for a silence they'll initially mistake for hostility. It's neither. It's just two very different answers to the same question: what do we owe a stranger in public? Norway says space. Morocco says tea. Pack accordingly.
