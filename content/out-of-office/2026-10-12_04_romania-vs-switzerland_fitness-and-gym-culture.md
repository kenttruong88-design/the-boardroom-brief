# One Country Will Charge You Handsomely For A Treadmill With A View. The Other Just Wants You To Use The Park Bars Like Everyone's Grandfather Already Does.

🇷🇴 Romania vs 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country has an opinion about the human body and what should be done with it after working hours, and nowhere is that opinion more nakedly visible than in its approach to exercise. Some nations have turned fitness into a polished, subscription-based lifestyle product with mood lighting and a smoothie bar. Others simply point at a set of rusted outdoor bars in a public park, shrug, and say: there, that's the gym, it's always been the gym, your grandfather used it too.

Romania and Switzerland sit at two very different ends of this particular spectrum, and having sweated — sometimes literally, sometimes just metaphorically over a price list — in both, I can tell you which one will build you a body and which one will simply bankrupt you trying.

## Do's & Don'ts: Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the free outdoor calisthenics parks (locuri de joacă pentru adulți) scattered through most cities | Assume a budget gym membership means budget equipment; many are better-stocked than you'd guess |
| Expect a no-frills, strength-focused training culture with genuinely serious lifters | Expect much hand-holding, orientation sessions, or a smoothie bar at the local sală |
| Try a local football match or a run along a riverside promenade for the social side of fitness | Be surprised that a monthly membership costs a fraction of what you paid back home |
| Bring your own towel, lock, and low expectations for air conditioning in older gyms | Skip the changing-room etiquette; shower before the sauna, it's taken seriously |

## Do's & Don'ts: Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget seriously; a basic business-district membership runs roughly CHF 60-70 a month | Expect a bargain. Switzerland is one of the most expensive countries on earth for a gym card |
| Book classes in advance through an app; popular slots fill fast even at this price point | Show up expecting a walk-in, pay-as-you-go culture the way some countries operate |
| Take hiking and lake swimming seriously as the "real" national fitness culture, free and glorious | Underestimate how central outdoor fitness is here; gyms are a city supplement, not the main event |
| Respect quiet, orderly gym-floor etiquette; re-rack weights, wipe down machines, don't hover | Assume Swiss reserve disappears once people are in activewear. It does not |

## Romania: The Park Bars Were Always the Gym

Romania's fitness culture has two faces that coexist without much friction. The first is the free, public, outdoor calisthenics setup — metal bars, parallel rails, the occasional pull-up station — that shows up in parks across Bucharest, Cluj, and smaller cities alike, used by teenagers, grandfathers, and the occasionally shirtless enthusiast with absolutely no self-consciousness about any of it. This is fitness as civic infrastructure: free, unglamorous, and genuinely well used, often from early morning onward.

The second face is the indoor sală, the gym proper, which skews heavily toward serious strength training rather than the boutique-fitness aesthetic common further west. Romanian gym culture takes lifting seriously in a way that can be mildly intimidating to a newcomer expecting a gentle orientation session and a staff member explaining how to use a cable machine; here, you are expected to already know, or to figure it out quietly, and the regulars will judge you more for bad rack etiquette than for bad form. Membership prices are a genuine relief after Western Europe — a fraction of what the same square footage of treadmill costs in Zurich or Geneva — though air conditioning, modern ventilation, and anything resembling a juice bar remain pleasantly optional extras you shouldn't expect.

What ties both faces together is an almost complete absence of fitness-as-performance. Nobody is posting a park-bar pull-up set for an audience. The culture, for all its genuine seriousness about strength, carries none of the self-promotional energy that saturates fitness culture elsewhere. You lift, you leave, you do not narrate it.

## Switzerland: Pay Handsomely, Then Go Outside Anyway

Switzerland's relationship with fitness is defined almost entirely by two competing truths: gym membership here is eye-wateringly expensive, and nobody who actually lives here seems to mind, because the real national fitness culture happens outdoors, for free, and the indoor gym is treated as a supplement rather than the main event. A basic business-district membership in Zurich runs somewhere in the region of CHF 60 to 70 a month — among the priciest gym markets on the planet, and Swiss gym attendance has, if anything, climbed past pre-pandemic levels despite the cost, which tells you something about either Swiss income levels or Swiss stubbornness, possibly both.

The outdoor side is where Switzerland's actual fitness identity lives: hiking trails that double as a national religion, lake swimming that starts the moment the ice has fully melted and sometimes slightly before, cycling infrastructure that would make most other countries weep with envy. This is not a culture performing wellness for a camera. It is a culture that has simply built fitness into the geography, so thoroughly that the expensive indoor gym becomes almost an afterthought — useful on a wet February evening, irrelevant the rest of the year.

Inside the gym itself, the famous Swiss reserve holds firm. Classes are booked precisely through an app, weights are re-racked without being asked, and the kind of chatty, high-energy gym-floor culture common elsewhere is replaced with quiet, efficient, slightly formal focus. Nobody is your friend here, but everybody, without exception, wipes down the machine when they're done.

Running culture tells its own version of this story. In Romania, running is cheap, unglamorous, and mostly solitary — a loop around a city park, headphones in, no particular performance attached to it, and certainly no expectation of a branded running club jacket. In Switzerland, running and hiking blur into something closer to a national pastime with real infrastructure behind it: waymarked trail networks (the famous yellow signs), mountain huts positioned for multi-day routes, and a culture where a casual "we went for a walk this weekend" might mean a six-hour alpine traverse that would be described, anywhere else, as a serious expedition. Swiss employers sometimes genuinely factor hiking fitness into team-building weekends in a way that would baffle a Bucharest office. Neither culture is performing for an audience; Romanians simply expect less fanfare from movement, and the Swiss have built so much accessible mountain infrastructure that strenuous outdoor fitness barely registers as effort at all, just Tuesday.

## The Verdict

If you want fitness that costs almost nothing and asks almost nothing of your ego beyond genuine effort, Romania's park bars and no-frills gyms will serve you well, cheaply, and without judgment about your technique improving in public. If you want fitness that costs a genuinely alarming amount indoors but is compensated for by some of the best free outdoor infrastructure on the continent, Switzerland earns its reputation, provided your bank account survives the membership fee first.

I have paid more for one month in a Zurich gym than for three months in a Bucharest one offering comparable equipment, and the Bucharest gym had better music. Make of that what you will.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com — a Geneva-based poster's blunt summary for newcomers asking about gym costs: budget double what you think is reasonable, then add a joining fee, and don't expect a short-term contract option to be cheap either.*

> *Tales Mag Real Post Report, Geneva — a posting spouse's observation that the real fitness culture in Switzerland happens on a mountain trail on Saturday, not in a gym on a Tuesday, and that locals treat the two as entirely separate categories of exercise.*

> *Reddit r/Romania — a regular at an outdoor calisthenics park noting that the unspoken rule is simple: show up, work hard, don't hog the pull-up bar, and nobody cares what you look like doing it.*

## Conclusion

A country's fitness culture is a surprisingly accurate X-ray of its relationship with money, space, and self-image. Switzerland has priced indoor fitness like a luxury good and quietly subsidised the outdoor version with some of the best natural infrastructure in Europe. Romania has kept fitness genuinely democratic, free in the parks and cheap indoors, with an unglamorous seriousness about actual strength that most boutique-fitness markets have long since abandoned. Neither needs the other's approval. Bring cash for one, bring hiking boots for the other, and in both cases, bring considerably more humility than you think you'll need.
