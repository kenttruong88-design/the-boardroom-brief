# Accra Still Haggles For Your Soul. Johannesburg Just Wants Your Loyalty Card.

🇬🇭 Ghana vs 🇿🇦 South Africa

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Shopping is the single fastest way to understand a country's actual relationship with money, time, and strangers, and West and Southern Africa have produced two of the more extreme answers I've encountered in sixty countries of doing exactly this. One nation still treats a purchase as a negotiation worth savouring. The other built roughly two thousand shopping malls and is now anxiously watching half its population quietly migrate to online checkout instead.

I've been overcharged, under-haggled, and gently mocked for my opening bid in Accra's Makola Market, and I've also stood in Sandton City watching people treat a mall food court with the reverence most countries reserve for cathedrals. Both experiences taught me something real about value.

## Do's and Don'ts: Ghana 🇬🇭

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Open your haggle at roughly half the quoted price and treat it as a "game," per local custom | Expect reliable card machines everywhere; ask first, and keep cash on hand as backup |
| Ask for the vendor's "last price" as the customary signal to close a negotiation | Stop in the middle of Makola Market to photograph it; move with the flow of foot traffic instead |
| Visit Kantamanto Market if you want world-famous secondhand clothing at genuinely steep discounts | Assume Accra Mall is "the real Ghana"; it's anchored by South African chains (Shoprite, Game) for a reason |

## Do's and Don'ts: South Africa 🇿🇦

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pick your mall by vibe, not just size; Sandton City skews affluent, Mall of Africa skews broader foot traffic | Assume malls have killed online shopping; just over half of SA adults now shop online weekly |
| Take Black Friday seriously; it's been imported wholesale from the US and genuinely overwhelms major malls | Underestimate Takealot; the local platform now outperforms several international rivals in recall and loyalty |
| Expect long dwell times; locals treat big malls as full-day social destinations, not quick errands | Expect to haggle in a formal retail setting; that instinct belongs at a market, not a mall storefront |

Ghana's shopping culture runs on two completely separate operating systems that rarely speak to each other. At street level, Makola and Kantamanto Markets in Accra remain the real engine room — Kantamanto in particular has become globally known as a secondhand-clothing hub so vast it's locally nicknamed "obroni wawu," literally "dead white man's clothes," a phrase that tells you exactly how the exported fast-fashion industry's leftovers are regarded on arrival. Haggling here isn't an unfortunate necessity; Time Out's own Accra guide frames it as "more of a game or verbal sparring match than an actual battle of wills," with vendors deliberately inflating opening prices for tourists and the savvy move being to open at roughly half, then ask for the "last price" to signal you're ready to close. A Talesmag diplomatic-community report captured the retail reality for expats more specifically: supermarkets stock US, European, and Middle Eastern goods well enough that "you can find almost everything you need," but prices run high and fresh produce can be unreliable, prompting many expats to simply import staples like flour and detergent themselves. Formal retail is newer and growing fast — Accra Mall, opened in 2008 as Ghana's first international-standard shopping centre, now draws an estimated 135,000 visitors a week, anchored not by Ghanaian retailers but by South African chains Shoprite and Game, with more South African brands (Woolworths, Mr Price, Truworths) driving roughly 110,000 square metres of additional retail space due within the next two years. Even the mall's own manager, Florence Menson, frames the appeal explicitly against the market alternative, calling malls "more inviting than going to a market where everything is open and it's crowded" — an admission that Ghana's retail future is, quite literally, importing its format from the country next door in this comparison.

South Africa has already finished the transition Ghana is mid-way through, and the scale is startling: roughly two thousand shopping malls nationally, over 24 million square metres of retail space, ranking the country 46th globally for retail-space density — Gauteng province alone accounts for 10.8 million of those square metres. Sandton City and Mall of Africa anchor the trophy tier, with Sandton drawing the wealthiest shoppers and longest dwell times while Mall of Africa more often wins on raw traffic; Black Friday has been imported wholesale from the US and genuinely strains the system, with tens of thousands of vehicles tracked at the country's busiest malls on the day itself. And yet the mall-mania narrative is quietly cracking under its own e-commerce data: The Herald reported that 50.1% of South African internet users now shop online weekly, nearly matching the 56% global average, with Statista forecasting growth from 11.7 million e-commerce users in 2025 to 21.5 million by 2029, and 77% of all online shopping already happening via smartphone. The home-grown winner is telling: Takealot, the local platform, posts a 62% recent-purchase recall rate against international rival Shein's 48%, suggesting South African shoppers are migrating online on their own terms rather than simply defaulting to whichever global giant shows up first. The two motivators cited most by Herald's survey — 70% wanting home delivery, 63% wanting to avoid crowds — sound less like convenience-seeking and more like a population that has spent two decades building the largest mall culture in Africa and is now, quietly, exhausted by it.

South Africa's Black Friday import deserves one more number, because it illustrates just how thoroughly the format has taken root: tens of thousands of vehicles were tracked at the country's two busiest malls combined on a single Black Friday, a traffic volume that would stop a mid-sized city's roads cold anywhere else and is treated, in Johannesburg, as simply the first weekend of the holiday shopping calendar. Ghana's trajectory is visible in the same data from the opposite direction — Broll Ghana's own estimate of 110,000 additional square metres of retail space arriving within two years tracks almost exactly the pace South Africa was moving at roughly a decade ago, suggesting Accra isn't inventing a new retail model so much as replaying Johannesburg's recent history on a short delay, down to the same South African retailers doing the building.

The real story here isn't "markets versus malls." It's that Ghana is racing toward the mall model South Africa built first, while South Africa is simultaneously racing away from it toward the smartphone checkout Ghana hasn't widely reached yet. Both countries are mid-transition in opposite directions, and meeting somewhere in the middle, whether they intended to or not.

## The Verdict

If you want shopping as genuine social theatre — a haggle, a last-price negotiation, a market that rewards showing up in person — Ghana's street-level culture still delivers an experience South Africa's mall-and-app economy has largely engineered away. If you want retail infrastructure, choice, and the sheer convenience of twenty-one and a half million people eventually just tapping "buy" from their phones, South Africa is years ahead. I'm giving this one to Ghana, because any shopping culture that still treats the "last price" as a meaningful social ritual understands something about commerce that a loyalty app notification never will.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Accra, paraphrased: card machines exist in Ghanaian supermarkets but are genuinely unreliable — "ask first" before assuming you can tap to pay, and keep cash as backup regardless of how modern the store looks.*

> *Time Out Accra, haggling guide, paraphrased: at Makola Market, the advice isn't to resist the crowd but to move with it; stopping to photograph the scene marks you instantly as a tourist rather than a shopper.*

> *BusinessTech, mall traffic analysis, paraphrased: average annual mall visits per South African vehicle fell from 139 in 2019 to 128 in 2023 and still haven't recovered to pre-pandemic levels, even as the malls themselves keep expanding.*

## Conclusion

Ghana will make you negotiate for the privilege of buying a plantain, and you'll walk away feeling like you won something, even when you absolutely didn't. South Africa will hand you a loyalty card, a food court, and an app notification, and you'll walk away having spent more than you meant to without a single word of conversation. Bring small bills to Accra and a data plan to Johannesburg. You'll need both, for entirely different reasons, and neither country will apologise for which one it chose to become.
