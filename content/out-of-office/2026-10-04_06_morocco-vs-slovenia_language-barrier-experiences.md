# One Country Will Talk to You in Five Languages, The Other Expects You to Try in One

🇲🇦 Morocco 🇸🇮 Slovenia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Morocco is a linguistic buffet — Darija (Moroccan Arabic), French, Berber dialects, Spanish in the north, and increasingly English, all deployed fluidly depending on who's talking to whom, often mid-sentence. Slovenia is a tidier affair: Slovene is the heart of everything, spoken by two million people with evident pride, and while younger urban Slovenians often speak excellent English, there's a quiet expectation that you'll at least attempt "hvala" before reaching for it.

I've had a Marrakech shopkeeper pivot through three languages in one transaction trying to find whichever one worked for me, and I've had a Ljubljana barista wait, patiently, while I mangled a single Slovene sentence, visibly pleased that I'd tried at all. Both are forms of linguistic generosity. Only one involves slightly more guesswork.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Morocco**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn a handful of Darija greetings — even imperfect attempts are warmly received | Assume Modern Standard Arabic will get you far in everyday conversation; Darija is quite different |
| Use French confidently in cities — it remains widely understood in business and tourism | Expect French to work equally well in rural Berber-speaking areas |
| Let shopkeepers switch languages to find common ground — it's normal, not awkward | Get frustrated when a conversation flips between three languages mid-sentence |
| Carry a translation app for Berber (Tamazight) regions specifically | Assume all of Morocco speaks Arabic as a first language — Berber speakers are a significant population |

**Slovenia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Slovene phrases — "hvala" (thanks) and "prosim" (please) go a long way | Assume English will always be offered first, especially outside Ljubljana and Maribor |
| Expect excellent English among younger, urban Slovenians | Assume the same fluency from older generations or in smaller towns |
| Try Slovene first, even badly — locals appreciate the attempt genuinely | Switch to English immediately without attempting a greeting |
| Note regional dialect variation — Slovene dialects differ significantly by region | Assume textbook Slovene will sound identical everywhere you go |

## Morocco: A Conversation in Several Languages at Once

Morocco's linguistic landscape reflects its history in the most immediate, lived-in way possible: a single interaction in a Fez medina might open in Darija, pivot to French when a tourist looks confused, swing briefly into Spanish if you're near Tangier, and land finally in broken English with hand gestures doing the rest of the work. This isn't chaos, it's extraordinary linguistic dexterity, practiced daily by shopkeepers, taxi drivers, and guides who've learned to read a stranger's face and guess their language within seconds.

Darija itself is the real heart of daily life — distinct enough from Modern Standard Arabic that visitors who've studied the "proper" classical form often find themselves still lost in an actual Moroccan street conversation, since Darija borrows heavily from Berber and French and moves at a conversational speed that formal textbooks don't prepare you for. French remains genuinely useful, a colonial legacy turned practical lingua franca across business, education, and tourism in cities, though it thins out noticeably once you're in rural Amazigh (Berber) regions, where Tamazight dominates and French recedes into a secondary, sometimes absent, option.

What's consistently moving is the generosity baked into this multilingual hustle. Moroccans rarely make a visitor feel stupid for not speaking Darija or French — the instinct is to keep switching languages until something clicks, treating the language barrier as a shared puzzle to solve together rather than the visitor's personal failing. Learn even a handful of Darija phrases — "salam," "shukran," "bisaha" — and the warmth multiplies instantly, less because the words themselves matter and more because the attempt signals respect for a conversation that's usually happening entirely on the host's terms.

## Slovenia: Small Language, Big Pride

Slovenia presents almost the opposite dynamic: a small, proud, linguistically cohesive country where Slovene isn't a fallback behind some larger regional tongue, it's the main event, spoken as a first language by the vast majority of the population and taught with evident national pride in schools. English fluency among younger, urban Slovenians — particularly in Ljubljana, Maribor, and the tourist-heavy coastal towns — is genuinely excellent, a product of strong education and heavy exposure to English-language media, which can lull visitors into assuming the whole country operates this way.

It doesn't, quite. Step into smaller towns, rural areas, or interactions with an older generation, and English becomes noticeably less reliable, while Slovene dialects themselves shift significantly by region — the Slovene spoken in Prekmurje sounds meaningfully different from standard Ljubljana Slovene, enough that Slovenes themselves sometimes joke about mutual incomprehension between regions. This dialectal pride runs deep; Slovenia has one of the highest dialect-density-to-population ratios in Europe, and locals take genuine, specific pride in their regional variant.

What visitors consistently underestimate is how much a small, sincere effort is rewarded here. Slovenia doesn't need you to speak its language the way Morocco's multilingual hustle doesn't need translation at all — but attempting "hvala," "prosim," or even a mangled "dober dan" (good day) shifts an interaction noticeably, less because it's functionally necessary and more because it signals you've recognised Slovene as worth the effort, in a small country that has spent real energy preserving a language spoken by barely two million people against much larger linguistic neighbours.

## The Verdict

Morocco wins for sheer linguistic survivability — you will almost always find some combination of languages that gets you understood, because an entire economy of hospitality has built itself around making that happen. Slovenia wins for the quiet satisfaction of effort rewarded — a single well-placed Slovene phrase buys you more goodwill, proportionally, than fluent French ever will in Marrakech, simply because fewer people try. If you want the path of least resistance, go to Morocco and let the switching happen around you. If you want the warm glow of having genuinely tried, learn five Slovene words and watch a Ljubljana barista's face change.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/morocco — paraphrased: a traveller recalls a single market haggling session conducted in Darija, French, and broken Spanish simultaneously, with the vendor seemingly fluent in reading confusion and adjusting language mid-sentence without missing a beat.*

> *Internations Ljubljana — paraphrased: a relocated professional notes that colleagues who defaulted to English immediately in meetings were seen as perfectly polite, but the ones who stumbled through Slovene first were remembered far more fondly months later.*

> *expat.com Ljubljana forum — paraphrased: a longtime resident warns that assuming English will be available in smaller Slovenian towns outside the capital leads to more than a few confused standoffs at rural train stations.*

## Conclusion

Morocco solves the language barrier through flexibility — a dazzling, practiced, generous willingness to meet you wherever your vocabulary happens to land. Slovenia solves it through pride and reciprocity — a smaller language, defended fiercely, that rewards even clumsy effort with real warmth. Neither approach asks you to be fluent. Both ask you to try. Show up in Marrakech and let the language shift around you; show up in Ljubljana and shift yours toward them, even slightly. Either way, "thank you" is always worth learning first, in whatever order the country hands it to you.
