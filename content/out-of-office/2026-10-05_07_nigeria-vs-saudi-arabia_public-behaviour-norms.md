# One Country Will Haggle With You Like It's a Love Language; the Other Has Quietly Rewritten Its Own Rulebook Mid-Decade

🇳🇬 Nigeria 🇸🇦 Saudi Arabia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nigerian public life runs loud, warm, and gloriously unfiltered — market haggling is a full contact sport, greetings are elaborate multi-step rituals you will get wrong at first, and personal space is a concept most Lagosians regard with polite confusion. Saudi Arabia, meanwhile, has spent the last several years quietly rewriting decades of public conduct norms — cinemas, concerts, mixed-gender public spaces that simply didn't exist a decade ago now do, even as plenty of older social expectations remain firmly, non-negotiably in place. I have haggled over a single mango in a Lagos market for four genuinely entertaining minutes. I have also sat in a Riyadh cinema watching a Hollywood blockbuster next to families who, ten years ago, would have had no legal venue to do so together at all.

Both countries reward a visitor who does their homework and punish, swiftly, one who doesn't. The homework required, however, could not look more different.

## Do's & Don'ts: Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet elders with visible, specific respect — titles and full greetings matter | Rush a greeting or skip straight to business; it reads as genuinely rude |
| Haggle enthusiastically in markets — it's expected, even welcomed as social sport | Accept the first quoted price; you will overpay and mildly disappoint the seller |
| Dress reasonably modestly and sharply in professional or formal settings | Raise your voice in anger publicly without expecting an attentive, vocal crowd to gather |
| Use your right hand for giving and receiving — it's a widely observed norm | Assume Lagos's loud, fast energy applies uniformly nationwide — regional norms vary significantly |

## Do's & Don'ts: Saudi Arabia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check current dress code norms before travelling — rules have genuinely relaxed but vary by venue | Assume everything from a decade ago still applies; plenty has changed, but not everything |
| Respect prayer times — some venues pause activity briefly five times daily | Public displays of affection beyond modest hand-holding in most settings — still broadly discouraged |
| Attend a concert, cinema, or mixed public event if curious — they genuinely now exist | Bring alcohol or assume it's available; the kingdom remains officially fully dry |
| Dress modestly in more conservative regions outside Riyadh and Jeddah's loosened norms | Photograph people, especially women, in public without clearly asking first |

## Nigeria: Volume as a Form of Affection

Nigerian public life operates on an energy level that can genuinely overwhelm a first-time visitor and then, within about seventy-two hours, becomes the thing you miss most when you leave. Greetings are not a formality to rush through — they are an actual social performance, layered with titles, well-wishes for family, and a warmth that assumes, correctly, that acknowledging another human being properly takes more than four seconds. Skip this at your peril; a transactional, Western-brisk "hi, how much" in a market will get you served, technically, but it will also mark you instantly as someone who hasn't done their homework.

Haggling deserves its own category of respect entirely. This is not an adversarial transaction — it is closer to a mutually enjoyable performance, with both parties fully aware of the eventual landing price and genuinely entertained by the journey there. Accept the first price offered and you haven't just overpaid; you've skipped the actual point of the interaction, and the vendor may look faintly disappointed on your behalf, as though you've missed out on dessert.

Public space in Lagos, Abuja, and most Nigerian cities runs loud by default — music, conversation, negotiation, preaching, all happening simultaneously and at volume, in a texture that can read as chaotic until you notice how functional the chaos actually is. Disputes get resolved publicly and vocally, often with an attentive crowd gathering to weigh in with genuine investment, because privacy in conflict is, culturally, a far less important value than communal involvement in resolving it fairly. Regional variation matters considerably too — the more reserved, religiously conservative north carries a meaningfully different public register than Lagos's maximalist energy, and treating the whole country as one undifferentiated vibe is a rookie mistake.

## Saudi Arabia: A Rulebook Being Rewritten in Real Time

Saudi public life has changed more in the past several years than in the previous several decades combined, and keeping up with which rules still apply has become something of a genuine skill. Cinemas reopened. Concerts, once unthinkable, now draw major international acts. Mixed-gender restaurants, cafés, and public events have become increasingly normal in Riyadh and Jeddah in particular, representing a social transformation that locals themselves often discuss with a kind of ongoing, visible recalibration — this is lived history happening in real time, not a settled culture you're simply observing.

What hasn't changed as fast, or at all, is the kingdom's fundamentally conservative social bedrock. Alcohol remains entirely prohibited, publicly and privately, with real legal consequences for violations. Public displays of affection beyond modest, married hand-holding remain broadly discouraged even in the most liberalised venues. Prayer times still structure the day visibly — shops may pause briefly, calls to prayer punctuate the hours audibly across every city — and showing basic respect for this rhythm, rather than visible impatience with it, matters considerably to how you're received.

The regional variation within Saudi Arabia itself deserves real attention: Riyadh and Jeddah have loosened considerably faster and further than smaller, more conservative towns, where older dress and interaction norms remain firmly, non-negotiably expected. A visitor who calibrates their behaviour entirely to a Riyadh nightlife district and then applies the same calibration in a smaller provincial town is setting themselves up for a genuinely uncomfortable correction, administered politely but very firmly.

## The Verdict

Nigeria wins on sheer textured warmth — public life there is loud because connection is loud, and the volume is, once you adjust to it, a form of collective affection rather than chaos. Saudi Arabia wins on sheer fascinating velocity of change — watching a society renegotiate its own public rulebook this visibly, this fast, is a genuinely rare thing to witness firsthand. If I want to feel embraced by a culture within a week, Nigeria, unreservedly. If I want to watch history being actively written in real time, Saudi Arabia is, right now, one of the most interesting places on Earth to simply pay attention.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations Lagos — paraphrased: a newcomer was gently scolded by a market vendor for accepting the first price offered, told plainly that haggling was "half the fun, why did you skip it."*

> *Reddit r/saudiarabia — paraphrased: a recent visitor described being surprised that a Riyadh mall felt almost indistinguishable from a Gulf shopping centre elsewhere, until a smaller town visit a week later required a complete recalibration of expectations.*

> *expat.com Riyadh — paraphrased: a long-term resident said the pace of social change has been so fast that even locals sometimes disagree amongst themselves about what's actually still expected in a given setting.*

## Conclusion

Nigeria and Saudi Arabia offer two completely different lessons in public behaviour: one is a deeply established, warmly chaotic social fabric that rewards loud, genuine engagement; the other is a society visibly mid-transformation, where yesterday's rule may already be out of date and tomorrow's isn't written yet. Do your homework for both. Just know that in Nigeria, the homework is cultural fluency. In Saudi Arabia, it has a shelf life measured in months, not years.
