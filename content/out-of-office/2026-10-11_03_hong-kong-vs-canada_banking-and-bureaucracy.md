# Hong Kong Will Open Your Bank Account in an Afternoon; Canada Will Open It Eventually, Pending Three Forms of Patience

🇭🇰 Hong Kong vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nothing reveals a country's soul quite like watching it process a stranger's money. Hong Kong treats banking the way it treats everything else: fast, slightly intimidating, and done the moment you walk in with the right paperwork. Canada treats banking the way it treats everything else: pleasant, apologetic, and somehow still requiring you to return next Tuesday for reasons nobody can fully articulate.

I have opened accounts in both. In Hong Kong I left with a working card and a mobile app in roughly ninety minutes, mildly traumatised by how many documents I'd needed. In Canada I left with a smile, a pamphlet, a "someone will call you," and a passive-aggressive sense that the bank and I were now in a long-term relationship with no defined milestones.

## Do's & Don'ts: Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring every document you own — passport, visa, proof of address, employment letter, soul | Expect a bank branch visit to take under an hour; compliance checks here are thorough and unhurried |
| Shop between HSBC, Hang Seng, and Bank of China (Hong Kong) — service and minimums vary wildly | Assume a tourist or short-stay visa gets you the same account options as a work visa holder |
| Set up FPS (Faster Payment System) immediately — it's how this entire city actually moves money | Ignore minimum balance fees; forget one month and the bank will remind you, loudly, in a letter |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book an appointment before you arrive — walk-ins exist but appointments exist for a reason | Assume "newcomer banking package" means instant; it still runs through full identity verification |
| Get your SIN (Social Insurance Number) sorted first — almost nothing else works without it | Expect weekend hours at most branches; Canadian banking still largely respects a 9-to-5 soul |
| Ask specifically about newcomer programs (CIBC, BMO, RBC all run them) — the perks are real | Be surprised when a "simple transfer" takes two business days; this is apparently still normal here |

## Hong Kong: Bureaucracy With a Deadline, Not a Feeling

Hong Kong's financial bureaucracy runs on a principle the rest of the world could learn from: move fast, demand everything up front, and never make the customer come back twice for the same reason. Walk into HSBC or Hang Seng with your passport, visa, proof of address, and employment documentation, and in one sitting — assuming you've actually got a stable visa status, which the city's post-2020 compliance tightening now checks with real seriousness — you can walk out with a functioning account, a card, and mobile banking active before your coffee's gone cold.

The catch, and there's always a catch, is the sheer compliance weight behind that speed. Anti-money-laundering rules here are aggressive, which means the bank wants to know everything: where your money came from, where it's going, and why you, specifically, need an account at all. Minimum balance requirements bite hard if you're not paying attention, and letters about falling below them arrive with a tone that suggests the bank is personally disappointed in you as a human being, not just a customer.

What makes the system genuinely impressive, though, is FPS — the Faster Payment System — which has turned peer-to-peer transfers into something closer to instant teleportation of money between phone numbers, QR codes, and even your Octopus card. Rent, splitting a dinner bill, paying your dim sum debt from three weeks ago: all instant, all free, all baked so deeply into daily life that explaining "wire transfers take three days" to a Hong Konger produces the same look you'd get explaining dial-up internet to a teenager.

## Canada: The Politest Possible Version of Making You Wait

Canadian banking is, structurally, not that different from Hong Kong's — strong compliance checks, real documentation requirements, a handful of dominant banks (RBC, TD, Scotiabank, CIBC, BMO — the "Big Five," spoken of with the hushed reverence usually reserved for royal families). What's different is the pace, and the pace is, charitably, "unhurried."

Newcomer banking packages are genuinely excellent on paper — fee waivers, credit-building programs, multilingual advisors who've clearly fielded this exact confused-expat conversation a thousand times before. But getting into the system requires a Social Insurance Number first, which itself takes processing time, which means your actual arrival-to-functioning-bank-account timeline often stretches into a week or two of "we'll be in touch," delivered with a warmth that makes the delay somehow feel like your fault for being impatient.

Then there's the transfer speeds, which remain, bafflingly, rooted in an earlier decade. E-transfers (Canada's beloved Interac system) are reasonably fast for small peer-to-peer amounts, but anything resembling an international wire, or occasionally even a domestic one between different institutions, can take "two to three business days" — a phrase Canadian bank staff deliver with the same calm as someone telling you the weather. Nobody is panicking about this. Only you are panicking about this. That's intentional, somehow.

There's a telling detail in how each system treats mistakes, too. Get something wrong on a Hong Kong bank form and you'll be sent back to the counter immediately, paperwork rejected on the spot, no appointment required because you'll simply be queuing again within the hour. Get something wrong on a Canadian bank form and you'll likely get a gentle phone call, a few days later, from an advisor who sounds genuinely apologetic about having to ask you to resubmit it. Same bureaucratic outcome. Entirely different emotional experience of being told you did it wrong.

## The Verdict

Hong Kong wins on speed and on genuinely leapfrogging most of the world with FPS — once you're in the system, the system works at the speed of an actual city that doesn't sleep. Canada wins on warmth and newcomer support that is, structurally, more generous and more thoughtfully designed than almost anywhere I've banked. But generosity delivered slowly is still slow, and I would rather be processed brusquely in ninety minutes than reassured kindly for ten business days.

Pick your poison: cold efficiency, or warm delay. Both will eventually hold your money. Only one will do it before you've forgotten why you needed it urgently in the first place.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Hong Kong forum — a recurring theme: newcomers on short-term visas are frequently surprised when a bank declines to open a full account, only offering limited options until a stable work visa is confirmed.*

> *r/PersonalFinanceCanada — newcomers consistently note the gap between "newcomer banking package" marketing and the real-world wait for a Social Insurance Number before any of those perks actually activate.*

> *Internations Toronto — members often remark that Canadian bureaucracy isn't difficult so much as slow, describing it as "friendly delay" rather than obstruction, which somehow makes the waiting feel both better and worse.*

## Conclusion

Both systems eventually work. That's the unsatisfying truth buried under all the complaining: Hong Kong and Canada will both, in time, hand you a working account, a card, and the quiet dignity of being a person with money who can prove it. The difference is simply how much of your patience each system plans to spend before it lets you have that dignity. Hong Kong spends it all at once, upfront, like ripping off a plaster. Canada spends it slowly, over two weeks, with a smile the entire time. Choose your trauma accordingly.
