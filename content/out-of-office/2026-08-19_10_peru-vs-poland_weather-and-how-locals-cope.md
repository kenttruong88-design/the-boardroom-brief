# One Country's Weather Might Give You Altitude Sickness. The Other's Weather Might Just Take Your Will to Live, Slowly, Over Five Months.

🇵🇪🇵🇱 By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office

Peru's weather problem isn't temperature so much as elevation — arrive in Cusco or Lake Titicaca without acclimatising and your body will stage a genuine, occasionally alarming protest at 3,400 metres above sea level, regardless of how fit you think you are. Poland's weather problem is duration — winter arrives in November, the sun essentially resigns by 3:30pm, and it does not meaningfully improve until March, by which point locals have developed a very specific, weary relationship with vitamin D supplements and the concept of hope.

I have sat gasping in a Cusco hotel room, coca tea in hand, genuinely questioning my cardiovascular fitness at what turned out to be entirely normal sea-level health. I have also stood on a Warsaw street at 4pm in January, already fully dark, watching locals walk past with the specific, hardened resignation of people who have simply decided winter is a season to be endured rather than enjoyed.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇵🇪 Peru**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Spend two to three days acclimatising in Cusco before strenuous activity | Fly straight into high altitude and immediately attempt a major hike |
| Drink coca tea (mate de coca) — it's a genuinely effective local remedy | Drink heavily at altitude — alcohol hits much harder and worsens symptoms |
| Pack layers for Lima's coastal "garúa" — grey, damp, deceptively chilly | Assume Lima is warm year-round just because it's coastal Peru |
| Stay hydrated aggressively at altitude, more than feels necessary | Push through severe soroche symptoms — descend if headaches worsen |

**🇵🇱 Poland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take vitamin D supplements from October — doctors here genuinely recommend it | Underestimate how early darkness falls; plan outdoor time before 3pm |
| Invest in proper thermal layers — Polish winter cold is a dry, biting cold | Assume a light coat "from home" will be sufficient — it likely won't |
| Embrace hygge-adjacent indoor culture — candles, tea, and cosy gatherings | Isolate indoors entirely; locals still walk daily, just bundled properly |
| Visit a Christmas market for a genuine mood boost during peak darkness | Expect your mood to be unaffected; even locals plan around seasonal dips |

## Peru: Weather as an Altitude Problem, Not a Temperature One

Peru's real weather challenge has almost nothing to do with hot or cold and everything to do with elevation. Cusco sits at roughly 3,400 metres, Lake Titicaca higher still, and SENAMHI's own climate data aside, the more relevant statistic for most visitors comes from Peru's Ministry of Health, which actively publishes soroche — altitude sickness — guidance because it affects such a substantial share of visitors regardless of fitness level. Headaches, nausea, breathlessness, and disrupted sleep are common in the first 24 to 48 hours, and the local remedy of choice, mate de coca (coca leaf tea), is offered almost automatically at hotel check-in across the highlands, a genuinely thoughtful, deeply normalised local response to a problem visitors rarely anticipate.

What surprises newcomers most is how indifferent altitude is to actual physical fitness — I have watched marathon runners struggle badly on their first Cusco morning while sedentary travellers sailed through, because soroche is a matter of individual physiology and acclimatisation time, not cardiovascular conditioning. Locals recommend, correctly, spending two to three unhurried days in Cusco before attempting anything strenuous like the Inca Trail, and avoiding alcohol entirely during that window, since it compounds symptoms significantly.

Lima presents an entirely different, almost comedic weather quirk: despite sitting directly on the Pacific coast, the city spends much of the year under garúa — a persistent, damp grey cloud cover that keeps temperatures mild but sunlight essentially absent for months at a stretch, confusing visitors who arrive expecting a sun-drenched South American coastal city and instead find something closer to a muted, temperate fog. Locals cope with genuine good humour about it, layering light jackets year-round and treating the rare sunny day as a small local event worth commenting on.

## Poland: Weather as an Endurance Test in Darkness

Poland's winter weather problem is less about extreme cold — though it does get properly, bitingly cold, a dry cold that Polish thermal wear is specifically designed to combat — and more about duration and darkness. IMGW's seasonal data confirms what every resident feels by December: sunset arriving around 3:30 or 4pm from November through February creates a genuinely long stretch where daylight barely registers around a standard workday, and Poland's Ministry of Health has, for years, actively recommended vitamin D supplementation during these months specifically because sunlight exposure drops so dramatically.

Locals have built an entire seasonal culture around coping with this. Christmas markets, which run through much of December in cities like Kraków and Wrocław, function as genuine, deliberate mood infrastructure — warm mulled wine, string lights, and communal gathering spaces designed specifically to counteract the creeping isolation of near-permanent darkness. Homes lean heavily into candles, warm lighting, and cosy indoor gathering culture through the coldest months, a close cousin of Scandinavian hygge that Poles have quietly perfected in their own right.

What genuinely impressed me was the collective, matter-of-fact resilience — Poles don't hide indoors for five months, they simply adapt thoroughly: proper thermal layering (a dry cold requires very different gear from a damp one), daily walks scheduled deliberately around the brief daylight window, and a cultural acceptance that seasonal mood dips are normal and worth actively managing rather than powering through silently. Newcomers who underestimate the psychological weight of that much darkness, expecting to simply "tough it out" the way they might a cold snap elsewhere, are consistently the ones who struggle most by February.

## The Verdict

Peru wins for sheer, acute drama — altitude sickness is dramatic, immediate, and genuinely educational about your own body, but it resolves within days and the reward, breathtaking Andean landscapes, is more than worth the temporary discomfort. Poland wins for the harder, slower endurance test — five months of compressed daylight is a genuinely serious undertaking, and the local coping culture around it, vitamin D and candlelit gatherings included, is a masterclass worth studying regardless of where you're from.

If you want weather that hits you hard and fast, Peru's altitude will do exactly that within your first 24 hours. If you want weather that tests your resolve slowly, over months, Poland's winter darkness will do that instead, thoroughly. I have never been so grateful for a cup of coca tea as I was gasping in Cusco, and I have never understood the true value of a Christmas market string light quite so deeply as I did surviving a Polish February.

## Conclusion

Peru's weather challenge announces itself immediately, loudly, and physically, then politely resolves within days, leaving you with some of the most spectacular landscapes on Earth as compensation. Poland's weather challenge is quieter, slower, and considerably more psychological, but the local culture built around surviving it, gracefully, is genuinely worth learning from. Pack coca tea patience for Peru and buy real thermal layers, plus a vitamin D bottle, for Poland. Neither country's weather is interested in your opinion about it, so best to simply prepare properly and get on with things.
