🇰🇪 Kenya vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ask a Nairobi resident what they're doing this Saturday and you'll get an itinerary: church, then a hike, then nyama choma with twelve relatives who weren't technically invited but came anyway. Ask a Canadian the same question and you'll get a shrug and, if you're lucky, a cottage address three hours outside the city where nobody will bother them until Monday. Both answers are, in their own way, completely correct expressions of what each culture believes a weekend is actually for.

Hofstede's Individualism score makes the theory embarrassingly tidy: Kenya scores 27, deeply collectivist, weekends organised around extended family and community obligation; Canada scores 80, one of the highest individualism scores recorded anywhere, weekends organised around exactly one person's preferences — their own. Neither country is wrong. They're just answering a completely different question about what rest is for.

## Do's & Don'ts: Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get out of Nairobi every few months, as multiple long-term expats specifically recommend — Naivasha, the Rift Valley floor, Ngong Hills are the established escape triangle | Stay inside the same small loop of expat bars and restaurants every weekend; one Talesmag respondent admits "you'll see them there basically every weekend" |
| Try Karura Forest for a genuinely cheap Saturday ritual — entry runs about 100 KES, a two-hour bike rental 500 KES | Bring single-use plastics to Karura Forest; the no-disposables rule is enforced, not a suggestion |
| Treat church attendance as the default Sunday institution for the roughly 85.5% of Kenyans who identify as Christian | Assume Nairobi's weekend social scene is uniformly secular or nightlife-driven; it mostly isn't |
| Join an organised day-hike through a forum or expat group rather than trying to plan the Rift Valley alone | Underestimate logistics; one documented Rift Valley hike needed two guides, a 6am departure, and roughly 4,000 KES for the group |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get access to a cottage if you possibly can — nearly 1 in 5 Vancouver homeowners and 1 in 6 in Toronto already own a second property for exactly this | Assume Victoria Day or other long weekends carry deep historical weight for most Canadians; only 22% say they actually hold real meaning |
| Accept that "going away for the weekend" here is an owned-asset norm, not a rental or day-trip habit | Expect your weekend to feel restful by default; 35.5% of Canadians aged 15+ rate their free-time satisfaction as low, worst among the 25-54 bracket |
| Try winter-specific leisure once the cold sets in — Canadians genuinely do drive out just to look at Christmas lights, per more than one new-arrival account | Over-plan a patio-hopping weekend expecting it to be the norm; only 16% of Canadians say they'd spend a long weekend specifically at a bar or restaurant |
| Get comfortable with wildlife turning up uninvited — deer, geese, and moose in residential areas are common enough to need licensing rules for fishing and hunting | Expect professors, bosses, or senior colleagues to keep formal distance socially; first-name address and genuinely flat hierarchy surprise many new arrivals |

## Kenya: a weekend that belongs to everyone else too

The structural fact underneath Kenyan weekend culture is Hofstede's Individualism score of 27 — a deeply collectivist number, and it shows in practice almost immediately. Roughly 85.5% of the population identifies as Christian, per Kenya's 2019 census data, and church remains the dominant Sunday institution, a structural anchor Canada's far more secular weekend simply doesn't have. Multiple long-serving expats, surveyed across years in Talesmag's Nairobi archive, converge on the same piece of advice without apparently coordinating: get out of the city every few months or you'll go quietly mad. One, writing in the aftermath of the Westgate attack, puts it starkly: "you have to get out of Nairobi." Another, years later, agrees that "the best fun comes from getting outside of the city and exploring the country."

Where that advice actually leads is a recognisable circuit — Naivasha for camping, with named spots like Sanctuary Farms recommended by name; Mount Longonot and the Ngong Hills for day hikes; the Rift Valley floor itself, where one Expat.com forum post documents an entire Saturday expedition in granular, almost accountant-like detail — a roughly fifteen-kilometre valley-floor hike to William Hill, five hours round trip, a 6am departure with carpooling, two hired guides for 4,000 KES plus a 200 KES entry fee per person. Closer to home, Karura Forest offers a cheaper, weekly version of the same instinct: a Reddit thread on r/Kenya describes a specific Saturday self-care ritual of a morning bike ride followed by an early lunch at the park's picnic site, with real, posted prices — 100 KES entry, 500 KES for a two-hour bike rental, 200 KES parking, 200 KES for the picnic site, and a strictly enforced no-disposable-plastics rule. Nyama choma — roast meat — functions as the weekend's culinary anchor, described in Kenyan press as "especially" a weekend ritual even as it creeps into weekday consumption too.

And yet there's a documented, self-aware risk baked into all of this for the expat community specifically: the same Talesmag archive includes a respondent flatly admitting the social circle is repetitive — "you'll see them there basically every weekend" — a small, honest confession that even a culture this socially rich can calcify into a rut if you let it.

## Canada: a weekend that belongs entirely to you

Hofstede's number for Canada — 80 on Individualism, one of the highest recorded anywhere in the world — translates into a weekend culture built almost entirely around personal, rather than communal, time. The clearest expression of this is cottage culture: a seasonal institution running from the Victoria Day long weekend (nicknamed, with real Canadian specificity, the "May Two-Four") through to Thanksgiving, and it is overwhelmingly an owned-asset habit rather than a rental one — roughly 20% of Vancouver homeowners and 17% of Toronto homeowners already own a second property specifically for this purpose, according to Globe and Mail reporting. And yet the holiday that kicks the whole season off carries almost no actual weight as a holiday: a 2007 Ipsos survey found only 22% of Canadians believe Victoria Day has genuine historical meaning, with the rest treating it as, functionally, just an extra day off; only 16% said they'd spend it specifically at a restaurant, bar, or patio, undercutting the easy assumption that Canadians patio-hop their way through every long weekend.

The uncomfortable twist in all this personal time is that it doesn't appear to be working especially well. Statistics Canada's own Q3 2024 release found 35.5% of Canadians aged 15 and over rate their satisfaction with free time as low, worst among the 25-54 age bracket at 46.8%, who average barely an hour a day of personal time once paid work, study, and unpaid labour are accounted for. A weekend built around individual choice, it turns out, is not automatically a weekend that feels restful — it just means nobody else is responsible for making it so.

What a newcomer actually notices first, though, is the sheer novelty of the leisure menu itself. A Kenyan international student's own account, posted to Twitter and reprinted on ThreadReaderApp, lists the culture shocks in rapid, genuinely funny succession: professors who insist on first names, students who socialise at the campus pub, locals who drive at night purely to admire Christmas lights and take selfies, and wildlife — ducks, geese, deer, moose — showing up often enough in residential areas that hunting and fishing require an actual license. His first Lethbridge, Alberta winter hit minus 20 degrees Celsius; he remembers, with real specificity, "hair in my nose was frozen." Hockey, Tim Hortons, and poutine round out the leisure landscape as the expected cultural touchstones, exactly as advertised, for once.

## The Verdict

Kenya's weekend is communal, cheap, and built on genuine institutions — church, forest, hike, extended family — that nobody has to maintain alone. Canada's weekend is private, often literally owned, and statistically not making anyone happier for all that personal space. If the goal of a weekend is to actually feel rested rather than simply unsupervised, Kenya wins this one on substance, not sentiment: a culture that drags you out to Karura Forest with your neighbours beats a culture that leaves 35.5% of its own population reporting low satisfaction with the free time it insists on giving you alone.

## Conclusion

The real divide here isn't leisure versus no leisure — both cultures take their weekends seriously, just in opposite directions. Kenya organises rest around other people and, perhaps counterintuitively, seems to be getting more out of the arrangement for it. Canada organises rest around the self and ends up, by its own national statistics agency's own admission, not entirely sure what to do with all that freedom once it's got it. Bring your neighbours to the forest. Leave the cottage key at home, metaphorically, until you've worked out what you actually want to do with it.
