# One Country Wants You to Meet the Family First, the Other Wants You to Pass an Audition First

🇦🇲 Armenia vs 🇰🇷 South Korea
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Dating abroad is always a masterclass in discovering how much of your home country's romantic instinct was actually just local custom dressed up as universal truth, and nowhere has that lesson landed harder than in Armenia and South Korea, two countries that could not have designed more different obstacle courses for two people trying to have a coffee together.

Armenia wants you to understand, roughly by date two, that you are being quietly assessed as a potential son- or daughter-in-law. South Korea wants you to understand that dating now runs through an app ecosystem so specific and so competitive that some platforms make you pass a screening panel of strangers before you're allowed to join. I have never felt so simultaneously charmed and surveilled.

## Armenia: Where the Family Is in the Relationship From the Start, Whether You Invited Them or Not

Yerevan's dating scene starts, misleadingly, like anywhere else's — cafes on every corner, plenty of them clearly built for lingering conversation, a genuinely lively spot to strike up something with a stranger over coffee. Where it diverges fast is intent. Armenian dating culture treats a relationship as a slow-building, deliberate thing aimed, more often than not, at marriage — not because everyone is in a rush to settle down, but because the culture doesn't really carry the Western concept of "casual dating" as its own separate category with its own separate rules.

That means the pace looks different from date one. Family isn't a distant milestone reserved for the six-month mark; it's present almost immediately, in conversation if not in person, because showing genuine interest in someone's background, their parents, their traditions, is simply how you signal you're serious rather than simply passing time. Respect for elders isn't a box to tick before a big meeting — it's baked into how the relationship is expected to be conducted throughout.

The younger generation, particularly in Yerevan, is visibly loosening this — more casual dating happens now than a generation ago, more people meet through apps and social platforms rather than family introduction or community proximity. But the underlying current hasn't disappeared, it's just quieter. Public affection remains understated by Western standards; a couple holding hands reads as established, anything more is a genuinely bold statement. And should things get serious, expect an actual, real vetting process from a family that will ask more direct questions about your intentions than most Western partners would dare after a year together.

## South Korea: Where Getting a Date Sometimes Requires Passing an Interview First

Seoul's dating scene is, on paper, hyper-modern — dominated by apps, driven by convenience, moving at a pace that should feel entirely familiar to anyone who's dated in a major Western city. Then you actually open one of the Korean-specific apps and discover the pace is the only familiar part. Amanda, one of the country's most popular platforms with millions of users, runs a scoring system where prospective members are rated by twenty existing users before they're allowed to join — an actual social audition, conducted by strangers, before your first match.

This isn't an outlier quirk; it's indicative of a dating culture that industrialised its courtship rituals faster than its underlying social conservatism could fully catch up. Apps like Glam and Noondate each carry their own verification rules, filters, and unwritten codes, and switching between them means relearning the game each time. Tinder exists and has adapted its approach specifically to appeal to Korean sensibilities, which tells you plenty about how differently the local apps were operating to begin with.

Once a match becomes a relationship, the milestones arrive fast and are treated with a seriousness that would bewilder most Western couples: 100 days together is a genuine occasion, marked, photographed, and — per the flourishing #커플그램 (couple-gram) culture on Instagram and KakaoStory — documented publicly for an audience that is absolutely paying attention. Nightlife districts like Hongdae and Itaewon remain the lower-pressure, in-person alternative to the app gauntlet, but even there, the underlying conservatism of the culture — one where dating apps only gained real traction once pandemic closures forced the issue — sits just beneath the modern surface.

## The Verdict

Armenia makes its intentions and its process transparent from the start — you know you're being assessed for the long term, and you know exactly by whom. South Korea hides an equally intense vetting process behind a slick app interface, which is somehow more unsettling, because at least Armenia has the decency to let you meet your evaluators in person, over coffee, rather than have twenty strangers silently rate your profile photo.

If I have to pick a winner, it's Armenia, purely on honesty. Being assessed by someone's grandmother over Armenian coffee is at least a process with a face attached to it. Being assessed by an anonymous jury of Amanda users before you're even allowed to start is a level of quiet, bureaucratic scrutiny I did not sign up for.

## Conclusion

Armenia and South Korea have both built dating cultures with real, functioning gatekeeping systems — one runs through grandmothers, the other runs through algorithms and anonymous juries, but the underlying message is identical: nobody gets in without being properly vetted. Romantics looking for something effortless should probably look elsewhere. Everyone else should bring patience, a decent photo, and — in Armenia's case specifically — a genuine willingness to answer questions about your five-year plan before dessert arrives.
