# One Country Will Introduce You to the Whole Family Before the Second Date; the Other Will Debate Philosophy With You for Three Hours and Call It Flirting

🇵🇾 Paraguay 🇫🇷 France
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Paraguayan dating culture moves through family first, almost always — meet someone, and within weeks you're at a Sunday *tereré* gathering being quietly assessed by aunts, cousins, and at least one grandmother with opinions. French dating culture, by contrast, moves through conversation first, sometimes exclusively — I have had actual romantic prospects in Paris spend an entire dinner debating Camus with genuine passion and never once touch my hand, and I have left those dinners both intellectually stimulated and completely unsure whether I'd been on a date or a seminar. Both cultures take romance extremely seriously. They have simply decided the proving ground is entirely different.

I've been folded into a Paraguayan family's extended social orbit within a month of meeting someone, invited to gatherings I didn't fully understand but was clearly expected to attend. I have also spent six dates in Paris with a man who never once called it dating, because apparently defining the relationship too early is considered gauche. Both experiences taught me things about love. Neither experience taught me the same thing.

## Do's & Don'ts: Paraguay

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to meet the family relatively early — it's a sign of serious intent, not pressure | Treat a Sunday tereré or asado invitation as optional once you're seriously dating someone |
| Show genuine respect to parents and grandparents — their approval genuinely matters | Rush physical affection in front of family gatherings; public modesty is still broadly expected |
| Learn a little Guaraní alongside Spanish — it signals real cultural investment | Assume dating apps dominate; social circles and family introductions remain the primary route |
| Be patient with group-based socialising; one-on-one time often comes later, not first | Expect a quick, casual dating culture; most relationships are understood as heading somewhere |

## Do's & Don'ts: France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Engage genuinely in debate and conversation — intellectual chemistry matters enormously | Ask "so are we exclusive?" too early; defining the relationship explicitly often comes later |
| Take dinner dates seriously as an actual event, not a quick coffee check-in | Assume a lack of PDA means disinterest; French affection is often more private, not absent |
| Let silences sit; not every pause needs to be filled with reassurance or small talk | Expect to meet a French partner's close friend group quickly; it can take real time to earn |
| Dress with intention — effortless-looking effort is genuinely noticed and appreciated | Over-explain your feelings early; a degree of ambiguity is culturally comfortable, not alarming |

## Paraguay: Love as a Family Enterprise

Dating in Paraguay rarely stays a two-person affair for long, and understanding this early saves considerable confusion. Meet someone with genuine romantic interest, and within a handful of weeks you'll likely find yourself at a Sunday family gathering, passed a gourd of *tereré* (iced yerba mate), and gently, thoroughly evaluated by an extended cast of relatives who all seem to have a stake in the outcome. This isn't intrusive by local standards — it's simply how seriousness gets communicated. A relationship that stays hidden from family for months isn't being protected; it's being quietly signalled as not that serious.

Guaraní, the indigenous language spoken alongside Spanish by the vast majority of Paraguayans, plays a real role in social and romantic life too — code-switching between the two carries emotional register, with Guaraní often reserved for warmth, humour, and intimacy in ways Spanish alone doesn't quite capture. A partner who picks up even basic Guaraní phrases, rather than relying purely on Spanish, signals a kind of cultural investment that lands deeply, disproportionately well relative to the actual linguistic effort involved.

Group-based socialising dominates the early stages of most Paraguayan relationships — asados, tereré circles, extended family football matches — before much one-on-one time ever happens. This can frustrate visitors used to dating cultures built around private dinners and solo outings, but it reflects something culturally central: love in Paraguay is rarely imagined as two people isolated from everyone else. It's imagined as two people being absorbed into each other's existing, extensive social fabric, aunts and all, immediately.

## France: Love as an Ongoing Intellectual Negotiation

French dating culture resists the very premise of clearly defined stages, and anyone looking for an obvious "we're officially dating now" moment may wait considerably longer than they'd like, because ambiguity itself is treated as a comfortable, even desirable, state rather than an anxious one. Conversation carries enormous weight — a dinner that runs three hours, moving fluidly between philosophy, politics, and gentle mutual provocation, counts as genuine romantic chemistry-building, arguably more so than any amount of physical affection would.

Public displays of affection run noticeably more restrained than their reputation suggests internationally — French romance is coded as private, interior, something performed primarily for the two people involved rather than for any observing public. This can read as coldness to visitors from more demonstrative dating cultures, but it reflects a different theory of intimacy entirely: the relationship's intensity is meant to live inside the relationship, not be broadcast outward for validation.

Cracking into an existing French friend group as a new partner takes real, sustained time, and attempting to accelerate the process typically backfires. French social circles, often formed in childhood, lycée, or university and maintained with real loyalty for decades afterward, extend trust slowly and deliberately. A new partner is observed, gradually included, and eventually, properly absorbed — but the timeline operates on the group's terms, not the new arrival's enthusiasm. Patience here isn't optional. It's the actual test.

## The Verdict

Paraguay wins on warmth and immediate, total belonging — being folded into a family this quickly, this completely, is a genuinely moving experience once you adjust to the scrutiny that comes with it. France wins on intellectual depth and the slow-burn satisfaction of a connection built through actual conversation rather than performed gestures. If I want to feel instantly embraced, Paraguay, without question. If I want a relationship that forces me to actually think, properly, for three hours over wine, France remains undefeated. Just don't expect either country to tell you clearly where you stand within the first month. Neither will. Ask the grandmother in Asunción or the philosophy graduate in Paris — both will simply smile.

## Conclusion

Paraguay and France both take love seriously, which is precisely why they look so different from the outside. One proves seriousness through immediate, total social integration — meet the family, join the circle, belong completely and fast. The other proves it through sustained, patient, conversational depth — earn the friend group, survive the ambiguity, let the relationship define itself slowly on its own unhurried terms. Pack patience for both. You'll just be spending it in very different rooms.
