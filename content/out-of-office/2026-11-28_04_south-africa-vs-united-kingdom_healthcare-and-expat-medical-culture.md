# South Africa Sells Healthcare by the Room, Britain Hands It Out by the Queue

🇿🇦 South Africa vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two ways to organise a nation's illness. You can ask people to pay for it, in which case the result is a very fine waiting room for those who can afford one and a very long queue for those who cannot. Or you can promise everyone care free at the point of use, in which case the result is a very long queue for absolutely everyone, rich and poor, bound together in shared national suffering. South Africa chose the first. Britain chose the second and has been apologising ever since.

I have been ill in both, and I am qualified to report that neither country is lying about its system. They are simply honest about different failures. One tells you the price. The other tells you to ring back at eight.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇿🇦 South Africa ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a medical scheme or take private cover before you need it | Rely on the public system if you have a choice |
| Ask the practice what your plan will cover before you book | Assume the hospital will bill your insurer directly |
| Keep your scheme card and ID with you at all times | Turn up at a private emergency unit with nothing |
| Use private pharmacies, which are easy to find | Wait for a repeat prescription to run out |

| 🇬🇧 United Kingdom ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a local GP as soon as you have an address | Wait until you are ill to find a surgery |
| Call 111 for non-emergency advice before heading to A&E | Treat A&E as a walk-in clinic for a sore throat |
| Book dental and optical care early, since NHS places are scarce | Assume your dentist will take you on the NHS |
| Check whether your visa required the Immigration Health Surcharge | Forget that you have already paid into the system |

## 🇿🇦 South Africa

South African healthcare is a story of two countries sharing a postcode. The public system serves the great majority of the population and is stretched, uneven and often admirable under pressure. The private sector serves a smaller group of patients, mostly those with medical schemes or personal insurance, and it is the one that expats and the wealthy actually use.

Private care is excellent. The major hospital groups run well equipped facilities in the big cities, specialists are easy to see, and waiting times are measured in days rather than months. You can book an appointment at a doctor's rooms, walk into a pharmacy on the way home and be treated with the briskness of a service that expects to be paid. The quality is a point of national pride, and the staff are among the best trained in the region.

The price is the catch. A medical scheme or insurance plan is almost a necessity for anyone who can afford it, and the paperwork that comes with it is a part-time job. Some procedures are covered in full, others partially, and some not at all, and the only way to find out is to ask before you sit in the chair. Many expats learn the difference between "covered" and "covered at the scheme rate" the hard way.

Public hospitals are another matter. They are staffed by committed people and are often overloaded, with queues, shortages and a patchwork of quality between provinces. For an expat, they are a last resort rather than a plan.

The pleasant surprise is how easy it is to be treated like a customer. Appointments are flexible, results come quickly, and a doctor will usually answer a follow-up question without making you feel like a nuisance.

South African expats in Britain often find the cultural gap in medical attitudes as wide as the structural one. At home, a patient expects to be told options, to be prescribed something and to be handed a bill with a smile. In Britain, a doctor may suggest rest and fluids and send you on your way, and the first reaction of many newcomers is that nothing has been done. The second, a week later, is that they feel fine.

## 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

The National Health Service is the closest thing Britain has to a religion, and like most religions it runs on faith, ritual and the occasional miracle. Care is free at the point of use for residents, funded by taxation, and visa holders who come for longer stays usually pay an Immigration Health Surcharge up front to join the system.

Your first act as an arrival is to register with a GP surgery, which is simple on paper and a minor test of character in practice. You fill in a form, wait for confirmation, and then discover that booking an appointment means ringing at eight in the morning and competing with every other patient in the postcode. Many surgeries now use online forms and triage systems, which helps if you can find the right button.

When it works, it works beautifully. A GP will refer you on, tests are done without a bill, and a hospital stay costs you nothing but patience. When it does not, you face waiting lists for specialist care, crowded emergency departments and a dentistry situation that is best described as a rumour. NHS dentists are hard to find in many areas, and expats often end up paying privately.

The culture of the consultation is polite and restrained. British patients apologise for wasting the doctor's time, and doctors treat you with a calm that can feel like indifference until you realise it is simply efficiency. Do not expect a long chat, and do not expect to be given antibiotics because you asked nicely.

Many employers offer private cover as a perk, and a surprising number of expats take it, mostly to skip the queue for the things that are not urgent.

Prescriptions and pharmacies work differently too. Private pharmacies in South Africa are plentiful and many medicines can be bought quickly, while in the UK a pharmacist is often the first point of contact for minor ailments, and a prescription from a GP is passed to a pharmacy of your choice. Charges for prescriptions vary by nation within the UK, so check the rules where you live.

## The Verdict

South Africa wins on speed and Britain wins on fairness, and I refuse to pretend those are the same thing. If you are rich enough, healthy enough to be insured and organised enough to read a policy, South Africa gives you a better experience than almost anywhere. You will be seen, tested and treated before the British system has finished sending you a text.

But the NHS is a different kind of achievement. It treats a plumber and a prime minister in the same queue, and it does so without handing either of them a bill. That is not a small thing, and no private hospital, however polished, can claim it.

If I were moving with a family and a decent salary, I would use both. I would register with an NHS GP for the everyday matters and keep a private plan for the things that cannot wait. It is the only sensible answer for an expat: take the equality at home and the speed on the side.

The sting is that both countries are exceptional at one thing and defensive about the other. South Africa boasts of its hospitals while ignoring who cannot use them. Britain boasts of its fairness while ignoring how long it takes to deliver it.

There is one more difference worth remembering. Britain's system is designed around general practice as the gateway, and you rarely see a specialist without a referral. South Africa's private market lets you go straight to one if your scheme allows it. The first is cheaper and slower. The second is faster and more expensive, and you will feel the gap on the day you need it.

## Conclusion

Healthcare is the part of expat life nobody plans for until it is too late. It is also the part where a country's values show up in the plainest way. South Africa believes in choice and pays for it in inequality. Britain believes in equality and pays for it in waiting.

Do the boring thing early. Register with the doctor, read the policy, keep your documents in a folder and know which number to ring. These are not exciting tasks, but they are what separate an expat with a plan from one with a story.

And if you are British and thinking of moving south, remember that you are about to learn the price of everything you once took for granted. If you are South African and heading north, remember that the free thing will cost you a morning.
