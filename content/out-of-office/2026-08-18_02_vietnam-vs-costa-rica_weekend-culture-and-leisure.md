# One Country Turns the Weekend Into a Market. The Other Turns It Into a Nap.

🇻🇳 Vietnam vs 🇨🇷 Costa Rica — Weekend Culture and Leisure

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Vietnam does not believe in rest, exactly — it believes in rearranging the noise. Weekends mean the streets fill even fuller than weekdays, motorbikes multiply like they're reproducing overnight, and entire families relocate their living rooms to the sidewalk to eat, gossip, and watch the chaos scroll past. Costa Rica, meanwhile, treats the weekend as a sacred deceleration ritual: plans are made in soft pencil, "pura vida" is deployed as both greeting and legal defence for showing up ninety minutes late, and the correct response to any urgency is a shrug pointed at the ocean.

I have spent weekends in both, hammered by heat, and only one of them made me feel guilty for sitting still. Vietnam will not let you be lazy in peace. Costa Rica will actively stage an intervention if you try to hustle on a Saturday.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get to the Hanoi Old Quarter night market early — by 8pm it's a solid wall of humans | Expect a quiet weekend anywhere near a lake, a market, or a karaoke bar |
| Join a plastic-stool sidewalk table without waiting for an invitation | Assume "closed on weekends" applies to anything — commerce here does not observe a day of rest |
| Rent a motorbike only after you've watched the traffic pattern for at least an hour | Try to cross the road hesitantly; commit fully or you'll be standing there till Monday |
| Wake up early for weekend markets; the good produce and the good gossip both go by 9am | Wear headphones while walking; you will need every sense you own to survive the sidewalk |

### 🇨🇷 Costa Rica

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build in at least a two-hour buffer for any weekend plan involving other people | Book a tight schedule; Costa Rica does not do tight schedules, ever, on principle |
| Check surf and tide reports before a beach weekend — the Pacific and Caribbean coasts behave differently | Expect shops in small towns to open at the posted hour; "abrimos pronto" is a spiritual concept, not a time |
| Bring cash to rural farmers markets (ferias) — card readers are optimistic at best | Rush a local into a decision about weekend plans; "pura vida" absorbs urgency like a sponge |
| Say yes to an invitation to someone's finca; it's the real leisure culture, not the resorts | Assume a "maybe" means no — it usually means yes, just not necessarily today |

## Vietnam: Leisure as a Team Sport

There is no such thing as alone time on a Vietnamese weekend, and if you want it, you will need to actively engineer it against the current of an entire culture that treats leisure as something done in a crowd, ideally a loud one. Saturday and Sunday mornings begin before dawn with tai chi groups colonising every park and lakeside path, followed by breakfast that is really the day's first social event — a bowl of phở eaten on a stool twelve centimetres off the ground, surrounded by strangers who will have opinions about how you're holding your chopsticks within the first five minutes.

By evening, the weekend fully detonates. Hanoi's Old Quarter closes its central streets to traffic and the entire population seems to relocate there simultaneously — beer served in plastic tables spilling into the road, families with toddlers weaving between vendors selling grilled everything, and a soundtrack that layers karaoke, traffic, and at least one wedding procession into a single continuous roar. Ho Chi Minh City does the same trick with rooftop bars and riverside walks, just with more neon and marginally less karaoke.

What nobody prepares you for is the sheer stamina required. A Vietnamese weekend does not taper off; it accelerates, and by Sunday night you will have eaten four meals you didn't plan, met eleven new people, and possibly bought a hammock you don't need, purely because leisure here is not a private recovery exercise — it's a communal performance, and everyone's invited whether they RSVP'd or not.

## Costa Rica: Leisure as a Constitutional Right

Costa Ricans didn't just adopt the phrase "pura vida" as a slogan — they built an entire relationship to time around it, and the weekend is where that philosophy reaches full, occasionally maddening bloom. Plans exist in a state of gentle suggestion rather than commitment. A "let's meet at ten" genuinely means "let's meet whenever the traffic, the weather, and my general mood align," and getting frustrated about this marks you instantly as someone who hasn't yet absorbed the actual lesson, which is that the schedule was never the point.

The national leisure activities are unambiguous: the beach, the rainforest, and doing absolutely nothing with tremendous conviction. Weekends empty cities toward the coast in a ritual migration that clogs the roads to Jacó and Manuel Antonio with a predictability Vietnamese traffic planners would envy, except nobody here seems remotely bothered by the delay — the destination is a hammock, and hammocks, famously, do not run late. Ferias, the weekend farmers markets, are less about efficient shopping and more about standing around a stall discussing your neighbour's new dog for twenty minutes before anyone mentions produce.

What nobody warns you about is how contagious the deceleration is. Arrive stressed, plan-obsessed, and calendar-driven, and within a month you will find yourself mysteriously unbothered by a two-hour delay, nodding along to "pura vida" with genuine sincerity instead of gritted teeth. It is either the healthiest thing that will happen to you abroad or a slow erosion of your Northern European punctuality, depending entirely on your priors.

## The Verdict

Vietnam wins on sheer sensory density — nowhere else turns a weekend into a full-body event this reliably, this loudly, or this deliciously. Costa Rica wins on actual rest — a country that has built a genuine national ideology around not rushing, which is rarer, and arguably harder to engineer, than a good night market. If you want your weekend to feel like an adventure whether you asked for one or not, go to Vietnam. If you want your weekend to feel like a held breath finally released, go to Costa Rica. Just don't try to bring a Costa Rican pace to a Hanoi sidewalk table — you'll get run over, metaphorically and possibly literally.

## Conclusion

Vietnam's weekend is a crowd you join whether you meant to or not, and Costa Rica's weekend is a hammock you eventually stop apologising for using. Both are, in their own way, a rebuke to the version of leisure most of us were raised on — the kind that's scheduled, optimised, and secretly still a form of work. Vietnam says rest can be loud and social and still count. Costa Rica says rest doesn't need a reason, a plan, or an apology. I've been ruined for ordinary weekends by both, in completely opposite directions, and I regret nothing.
