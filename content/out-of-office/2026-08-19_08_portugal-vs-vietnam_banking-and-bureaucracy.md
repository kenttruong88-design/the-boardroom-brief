# One Country Requires a Tax Number Before You Can Do Basically Anything. The Other Requires a Local Friend Before You Can Do Basically Anything.

🇵🇹🇻🇳 By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office

Portugal has built an entire bureaucratic ecosystem around a single nine-digit number — the NIF — without which you cannot open a bank account, sign a lease, get a phone contract, or, at times, seemingly purchase a sandwich with any dignity. Vietnam's bureaucracy operates on a different currency entirely: relationships. Show up with the right paperwork and no local connection, and you'll wait. Show up with a friend who knows the branch manager, and doors that were "permanently closed" this morning reopen by lunch.

I have queued for a Portuguese NIF appointment that took six weeks to book and eleven minutes to complete, and I have opened a Vietnamese bank account in under an hour purely because the colleague who accompanied me happened to know the teller's cousin. Bureaucracy, it turns out, runs on wildly different fuel depending on where you are.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇵🇹 Portugal**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your NIF sorted before attempting almost anything else administratively | Expect same-week appointments — booking lead times can run into months |
| Use a solicitor or relocation service if your Portuguese is limited | Assume email follow-ups get fast replies — patience is mandatory |
| Keep physical copies of every document — digital isn't always trusted yet | Lose your temper at counter staff — it will not speed anything up |
| Budget real time for anything involving SEF/AIMA or residency permits | Expect consistency — the same request can get different answers at different desks |

**🇻🇳 Vietnam**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a local colleague or friend when opening accounts or signing leases | Expect written rules to be applied identically everywhere — local discretion is real |
| Carry your passport and visa documents everywhere, always, without exception | Assume online banking apps are in English by default — many aren't fully |
| Build a relationship with one specific bank branch and staff member | Be surprised by cash preference — many transactions still happen in cash |
| Budget time for visa runs or renewals if not on a long-term visa | Skip getting a local SIM early — it unlocks much of the digital admin process |

## Portugal: Bureaucracy as a Single Chokepoint

Portuguese bureaucracy has a peculiar structure: almost everything routes through one document, the Número de Identificação Fiscal, or NIF — a tax identification number that functions as the master key to functioning adult life in the country. Banco de Portugal's own account-opening guidance makes clear that without a NIF, you cannot open a resident bank account, and without a bank account, you cannot sign a lease, get utilities connected, or set up a phone contract, creating a genuinely circular problem that every newcomer has to solve in exactly the right order or spend weeks stuck.

Getting that NIF, historically, meant an in-person appointment at a Finanças office, and demand has at various points overwhelmed the system so badly that booking appointments online became its own competitive sport, with newcomers refreshing booking portals at odd hours the way others chase concert tickets. A cottage industry of relocation consultants and solicitors has sprung up specifically to fast-track this process for a fee, and plenty of expats simply pay it, having learned the hard way that DIY bureaucracy here costs more in time than money ever would.

What makes Portuguese bureaucracy genuinely maddening rather than simply slow is its inconsistency — SEF, recently restructured into AIMA, has been notorious for giving different answers to the same question depending on which office, or even which staff member, you happen to speak to. Two friends applying for the same residency permit type, with identical paperwork, can have wildly different experiences and timelines, and there is no reliable way to predict which you'll get. The saving grace is that once you're through the initial gauntlet, the system does eventually work, and Portuguese civil servants, while slow, are rarely actively unhelpful — they're simply operating inside a system that seems to actively resist speed as a matter of principle.

## Vietnam: Bureaucracy as a Relationship, Not a Process

Vietnamese bureaucracy operates on a logic that confuses newcomers who arrive expecting a purely procedural system: written rules exist, certainly, but how smoothly they're applied depends enormously on relationships, local knowledge, and who's standing next to you at the counter. The State Bank of Vietnam has clear regulations for foreigners opening accounts, but in practice, the difference between a frustrating multi-visit ordeal and a same-day account opening often comes down to whether you brought a Vietnamese colleague, friend, or fixer who has a personal relationship with that specific branch.

This isn't corruption in the way outsiders sometimes assume — it's closer to a deeply relationship-based culture applied to administrative life, where trust built through personal connection genuinely smooths process friction that pure paperwork alone cannot. Expats who invest early in befriending one specific, reliable bank staff member, landlord's assistant, or local admin contact report a dramatically easier experience than those who show up cold, document in hand, expecting Western-style procedural consistency.

Visa logistics add their own layer of complexity — depending on your visa type, "visa runs" (brief exits and re-entries to reset visa status) remain a genuine part of expat life for those not on longer-term visas, and Vietnam Immigration Department rules around this have shifted periodically enough that expat forums function as genuinely essential, frequently updated resources rather than optional reading. Cash still dominates plenty of everyday transactions despite a fast-growing digital banking scene, and getting a local SIM card early unlocks a surprising amount of subsequent admin, since many services expect a local number for verification. The overall experience rewards patience, humility, and a genuine willingness to build local relationships rather than simply follow a checklist and expect it to work.

## The Verdict

Portugal wins for eventual consistency — frustrating and slow as the NIF gauntlet is, once you're through it, the system broadly works the same way for everyone, and that predictability has real value. Vietnam wins for warmth and flexibility — its relationship-driven bureaucracy can move remarkably fast for those who invest in the right local connections, offering a kind of humanity that Portugal's more impersonal, if consistent, system doesn't.

If you want bureaucracy that's slow but fair, Portugal delivers that, eventually, after testing your patience thoroughly. If you want bureaucracy that rewards genuine relationship-building and can move surprisingly fast once you have the right person on your side, Vietnam delivers that instead. I have never refreshed a booking portal with such single-minded desperation as I did for a Portuguese NIF appointment, and I have never been more grateful for a colleague's cousin than I was at a Hanoi bank counter.

## Conclusion

Portugal's bureaucracy is a single, brutal chokepoint you fight through once and then mostly forget about — slow, occasionally inconsistent, but ultimately fair to everyone equally. Vietnam's bureaucracy is a living, relationship-driven system that rewards those who invest in people over paperwork, and punishes those who don't with nothing worse than a slower afternoon. Bring patience and a solicitor's number to Portugal, and bring a genuinely good local friend to Vietnam. Neither country's system will apologise for how it works, and neither, honestly, needs to.
