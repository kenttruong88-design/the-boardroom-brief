# Cyprus Shops in Straight Lines. Morocco Shops in Arguments.

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Cyprus, you take a trolley, follow the aisles in the order the aisles have decided for you, and leave with exactly what was on your list, plus one halloumi you didn't plan on but regret nothing. In Morocco, you enter a souk with a list and leave with a rug, four kilos of dates you didn't need, a new friend, and a story about the man who sold you saffron that may or may not have actually been saffron. One country has turned grocery shopping into a logistics exercise. The other has turned it into a full-contact social sport.

Both, somehow, produce dinner. Let's discuss how.

## Do's & Don'ts: Cyprus

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Hit the big chains (Alphamega, Papantoniou) for reliable weekly stock-ups | Expect Sunday opening hours to match the rest of the week |
| Still visit a village market for produce — quality is noticeably better | Assume all supermarkets carry the same imported goods at the same price |
| Bring your own bags — plastic bag charges are standard | Show up right before closing expecting fresh bakery stock |

## Do's & Don'ts: Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Haggle at the souk — it's expected, not rude | Accept the first quoted price at a tourist-facing stall |
| Use supermarkets (Marjane, Carrefour) for fixed-price staples and imported goods | Assume souk vendors won't remember you if you overpay once |
| Learn a few numbers in Darija — it changes every negotiation | Photograph vendors or produce without asking first |

## Cyprus: Orderly, Air-Conditioned, and Quietly Excellent

Cyprus's supermarket culture is, on the surface, unremarkable in the best possible way — clean aisles, fixed prices, loyalty cards, the full EU-standard retail experience you'd get anywhere from Athens to Amsterdam. Alphamega and Papantoniou dominate, stocked with a genuinely impressive range given the island's size, from Greek staples to British expat comfort imports (Marmite has never been more emotionally significant to anyone than it is to a homesick Brit in Limassol). What elevates it is the parallel village market culture that hasn't gone anywhere: weekly farmers' markets where the produce is startlingly better than anything shrink-wrapped indoors, and where locals still do a meaningful share of their actual food shopping, treating the supermarket as the place for the boring stuff — detergent, tinned goods, the things that don't need a relationship with the seller.

The genuinely charming bit is how unbothered the whole system is. Cypriot supermarket culture doesn't try to perform authenticity or hustle; it just quietly works, closes on Sundays like it means it, and trusts you to know that the good tomatoes are two streets over at the market, not in aisle four. It's the retail equivalent of a shrug — competent, slightly Mediterranean-paced, entirely without drama. The only real adjustment for newcomers is recalibrating expectations around hours; this is not a 24-hour convenience culture, and turning up at 8pm expecting a bustling supermarket will occasionally leave you standing outside locked doors wondering where everyone went. They went home. It's Cyprus. Dinner can wait.

## Morocco: The Souk Is Not Optional, It's a Relationship

Morocco runs two parallel economies for the exact same tomatoes, and understanding both is the entire game. There are proper supermarkets now — Marjane and Carrefour chains with fixed prices, air conditioning, and the full Western grocery experience, genuinely useful for imported goods, packaged staples, and anyone who's had one haggling session too many that week. But the soul of Moroccan grocery shopping still lives in the souk, and pretending otherwise is a failure of nerve, not a lifestyle preference.

Haggling here isn't an unfortunate necessity to be minimised; it's a social ritual with its own etiquette, rhythm, and mutual respect, and vendors who sense you're trying to skip it entirely will often respect you less, not more. Quoted opening prices for tourists can run two, three, sometimes five times the eventual settled price, and the dance of counter-offering, walking away, being called back, and settling somewhere in the middle is genuinely how commerce here is supposed to feel — personal, theatrical, and faintly competitive in a way that a self-checkout machine simply cannot replicate. The Morocco National Tourist Office has increasingly leaned into souk culture as a headline attraction rather than a logistical hurdle for visitors, which tells you everything about how central it is to national identity, not just tourism marketing.

What catches expats off guard is the sheer relational depth of it — buy your vegetables from the same stall for three months running and you'll be remembered, greeted, occasionally gifted an extra handful of mint "for the tea," and quietly upgraded from "customer" to something closer to "regular." Skip the souk entirely for supermarket convenience and you're not just losing better prices on produce; you're opting out of one of the country's genuine daily social infrastructures.

## The Verdict

Cyprus wins on convenience, hygiene, and the sheer relief of a fixed price tag after a long day. Morocco wins on everything a supermarket cannot manufacture: theatre, relationship, and the quiet thrill of walking away with a bargain you actually earned. If I want groceries with minimum friction, I'm in Limassol. If I want groceries as an afternoon's entertainment and a small ongoing friendship with a spice vendor named Hassan, I'm in Marrakech, every time.

## Conclusion

Cyprus will teach you the quiet dignity of a fixed price and a Sunday closure you don't fight. Morocco will teach you that a grocery run can be a genuine social occasion, complete with negotiation, gossip, and mint tea you didn't ask for but definitely wanted. Bring patience to both — Cyprus for the hours, Morocco for the haggling — and never, under any circumstances, accept the first price in a Marrakech souk. Hassan is disappointed enough in tourists already.
