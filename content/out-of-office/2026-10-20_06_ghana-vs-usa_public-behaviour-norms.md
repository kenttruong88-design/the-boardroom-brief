# America Taught the World to Queue With Its Eyes. Ghana Taught the World to Greet With Its Whole Body.

🇬🇭 Ghana — 🇺🇸 United States
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The first thing that breaks a Ghanaian newcomer's brain in an American grocery store isn't the size of the cereal aisle. It's the silence. Nobody greets the cashier like a person they intend to remember. Nobody asks after your family before asking about your order. You simply... transact, and leave, and this is apparently considered normal, even polite, by the people doing it.

The first thing that breaks an American newcomer's brain in Accra is the opposite problem: everyone wants to greet you, properly, before anything else happens — and skipping that greeting to get straight to business, the way you would in a New York deli, reads as something close to rude. Ghana built one of the warmest, most deliberately social public cultures I've encountered in sixty countries of eating my way around this planet. America built one of the most efficient, individually-bubbled public cultures on earth. Neither is wrong. Both will make you feel, briefly, like you're doing human interaction incorrectly.

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet properly before any transaction or conversation — asking after someone's health and family isn't small talk here, it's the actual entry fee for the interaction that follows | Rush a greeting or skip straight to your request; it reads as abrupt at best, disrespectful at worst, regardless of how busy you both are |
| Use your right hand (or both) for giving and receiving anything — money, food, a handshake; the left hand carries real cultural weight as impolite for these purposes | Point with your left hand or your foot at a person or sacred object; both carry genuine offence that outsiders rarely anticipate |
| Expect and embrace physical warmth in greetings — handshakes that linger, genuine hugs between new acquaintances, a hand on the shoulder mid-conversation | Flinch from closer personal space in conversation than you're used to; backing away repeatedly reads as coldness, not just unfamiliarity |
| Dress modestly and respectfully for most everyday public settings, especially outside the most cosmopolitan Accra neighbourhoods | Assume punctuality works the way it does in a Western office; "Ghana time" is a real, openly acknowledged cultural flexibility around schedules |

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇺🇸 United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Queue strictly and silently — personal space in line (an actual arm's length, often more) is treated as a hard social rule, not a loose guideline | Strike up a long personal conversation with a stranger in a retail queue; brief friendliness is fine, extended warmth can read as oddly intense |
| Keep greetings brief and efficient in transactional settings — "hi, how are you" is often rhetorical, not a genuine invitation to answer at length | Touch someone you've just met beyond a handshake; American personal-space norms in public are noticeably wider than most of the world |
| Tip consistently and generously in service settings — it's not optional etiquette here, it's functionally part of many workers' actual wages | Assume silence or minimal small talk is rudeness; in much of the US, efficient transactional interaction is itself a form of respect for everyone's time |
| Respect posted queue and personal-space signage literally — markings on the floor, stanchions, and "please wait here" signs are followed with near-religious consistency | Underestimate regional variation; Southern US warmth and small talk differs meaningfully from brisk Northeastern transactional efficiency |

## Ghana: Greeting as Infrastructure

Public life in Ghana runs on a social protocol that Western newcomers consistently describe as the single biggest adjustment, bigger even than language or food: you simply cannot skip the greeting. Asking after someone's health, their family, sometimes their journey that day, before any actual business gets conducted, isn't a nicety layered on top of the interaction — it functions as the interaction's actual entry requirement. Skip it, and whatever follows, however correctly executed otherwise, lands as rude.

This extends into physical warmth that surprises people coming from more reserved cultures. Handshakes last longer than a quick American grip-and-release. New acquaintances hug sooner than expected. Conversational distance runs closer than much of the West is comfortable with, and backing away repeatedly to re-establish personal space, rather than being read as a harmless habit, tends to register as emotional coldness. American and Northern European newcomers consistently report needing real, deliberate effort to stop flinching from this closeness before it starts reading, correctly, as warmth rather than invasion.

Specific physical etiquette carries genuine weight: the left hand is culturally associated with impurity for giving, receiving, or eating, a norm shared across much of West Africa, and violating it — even accidentally, even as an obviously clueless foreigner — registers more strongly than newcomers expect. Pointing with a foot or the left hand at a person carries similar offence. None of this is explained upfront, which is exactly why every expat account of early life in Ghana includes at least one cringing memory of having gotten it wrong.

"Ghana time" — a widely acknowledged, openly joked-about cultural flexibility around punctuality and scheduling — is real, and newcomers expecting Western-office precision around meeting times need to recalibrate fast, not with resentment, but with the understanding that relationship and presence are frequently prioritised over the clock in a way that, once you stop fighting it, is genuinely more humane than it first appears.

## United States: Efficiency as the Highest Form of Respect

American public behaviour norms, especially in dense urban and commercial settings, run on an entirely different theory of politeness: efficiency *is* the respect. A brisk, minimal-contact transaction — quick greeting, clear request, quick resolution — isn't coldness in the American frame; it's consideration for everyone else's time, including the stranger's behind you in line. This produces the specific, famous American public silence that newcomers from more socially elaborate cultures initially read as unfriendliness, before gradually recognising it as its own, differently-coded form of mutual respect.

Queue culture is close to sacred. Personal space in line — genuinely an arm's length in most American contexts, sometimes more — functions as an enforced social rule rather than a loose cultural preference, and violating it (standing too close, cutting ahead, striking up an unsolicited extended conversation) produces visible, immediate discomfort in the people around you. Floor markings, stanchions, and posted signage get followed with a consistency that itself reflects the broader American cultural comfort with explicit, individually-respected rules over implicit, relationship-based negotiation.

Tipping culture deserves its own warning for newcomers: in much of the American service economy, the tip isn't a bonus for good service, it's functionally part of the underlying wage structure, and treating it as optional — the way it genuinely is in most of the rest of the world — lands as a real financial slight rather than a cultural misunderstanding.

What's easy to miss, especially for newcomers who've only experienced one American region, is how much regional variation exists underneath the national stereotype. The brisk, efficient, don't-waste-my-time public style associated with the Northeast genuinely differs from the more conversational, small-talk-friendly norms common across much of the South — both are "American," and assuming the entire country shares one register is itself a common newcomer mistake.

Market and commercial haggling deserves its own mention, because it reveals a lot about each country's relationship with negotiation as a form of social respect. In Ghanaian markets, bargaining over price is expected, friendly, and almost performative — refusing to haggle at all can read as oddly distant, as though you're declining to engage with the vendor as a person rather than just a transaction point. American retail culture, by contrast, treats posted prices as close to sacred outside of a handful of specific contexts (cars, real estate, the occasional flea market); attempting to haggle at a standard storefront register generally produces confusion rather than the expected back-and-forth, and newcomers from bargaining cultures consistently describe having to consciously unlearn the instinct.

## The Verdict

Ghana's public culture asks you to slow down and actually see the person in front of you before anything else happens. America's public culture asks you to respect everyone's time by getting out of each other's way efficiently. Neither is colder than the other — they're just optimising for different things, warmth versus throughput, and both work beautifully on their own terms. If you're asking which one I personally find more humane as a daily way to move through the world, it's Ghana, without much hesitation — but I say that as someone who has the luxury of not needing an American grocery queue to move quickly on a Tuesday before a flight.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *ExpatExchange "Culture Shock in Ghana" thread, paraphrased: multiple long-term expats agree the greeting protocol is the single hardest habit to build consistently, describing months of conscious effort before it stopped feeling like an extra step.*

> *University of Oregon J-School field-report, paraphrased from an American student's placement reflection: being gently corrected by a Ghanaian host family for using her left hand to pass a dish was, in her words, "the most useful embarrassment of my year."*

> *r/IWantOut, paraphrased recurring theme from Ghanaian posters relocating to the US: the sheer silence of an American retail queue was repeatedly described as the most disorienting adjustment, more than language, weather, or paperwork.*

## Conclusion

You can tell almost everything about a country's soul from how strangers behave around each other in a queue. Ghana will ask after your family before it asks for your order. America will get your order right, quickly, and consider that the kindness. Learn the greeting if you're headed to Accra. Learn the arm's-length rule if you're headed to anywhere in the US with a Trader Joe's. Both lessons will embarrass you at least once before they stick. That's just the tuition.
