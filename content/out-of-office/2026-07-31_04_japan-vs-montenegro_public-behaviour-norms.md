# Japan Will Let You Suffer In Silence. Montenegro Will Loudly Ask Why You're Suffering At All.

🇯🇵 Japan + 🇲🇪 Montenegro

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Public behaviour is the clearest window into a national psyche, and few pairings illustrate the spectrum quite like Japan and Montenegro. Japan has built an entire civilisation around the principle that your personal discomfort is your business and nobody else's, expressed through silence, restraint, and an almost supernatural ability to queue. Montenegro, meanwhile, operates on the Balkan principle that if something is wrong, everyone within earshot deserves to know about it immediately, loudly, and preferably over coffee that will take ninety minutes to finish.

I've stood on a packed Tokyo train where three hundred people collectively decided not to acknowledge a screaming toddler out of sheer collective politeness, and I've sat in a Podgorica café where a minor disagreement about parking became a fifteen-minute public debate involving four strangers, none of whom were parked anywhere nearby. Both are functioning societies. Both would find the other's approach to conflict borderline incomprehensible.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇯🇵 Japan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Queue precisely where marked, even if the platform looks empty | Talk loudly on your phone on trains — it's a genuine social violation |
| Keep your voice low in public spaces, always | Eat while walking through crowded streets |
| Bow slightly when thanking someone, even briefly | Make prolonged eye contact with strangers — it reads as confrontational |
| Dispose of your own rubbish; public bins are rare on purpose | Blow your nose loudly in public — step aside or turn away first |

**🇲🇪 Montenegro**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect coffee meetings to run long — rushing is rude | Take directness personally; it's honesty, not hostility |
| Greet shopkeepers and neighbours, even briefly, every time | Expect quiet cafés; loud conversation is the ambient soundtrack |
| Dress smartly in town — appearance matters more than you'd think | Assume silence means agreement — Montenegrins will tell you if they disagree |
| Join in if a stranger starts a conversation with you | Rush a greeting; a hurried "zdravo" can come across as cold |

## Japan: The Art Of Collective Invisibility

Japanese public behaviour operates on a principle sociologists call "meiwaku" — roughly, the avoidance of causing inconvenience to others — and it governs everything from how you hold your umbrella to how you eat a sandwich. The famous silence of Tokyo's commuter trains isn't awkwardness, it's a deliberate, collectively enforced agreement that your problems, your phone call, your bad day, stay contained within your own body. Watch a packed train during a delay: hundreds of visibly frustrated people, and almost total silence. Nobody complains to a stranger. Nobody vents. The frustration is real, it's just not for public consumption.

This extends to conflict avoidance more broadly. Direct confrontation, criticism, or even strong disagreement in public is genuinely rare, and when it does happen, it's usually handled through incredibly polite, almost coded language that outsiders often miss entirely. A Japanese colleague telling you something is "a little bit difficult" often means "absolutely not, and I'm horrified you asked," and learning to hear the no inside the yes is a skill that takes most expats years, not weeks.

The flip side of all this restraint is a public realm that runs with almost eerie smoothness — queues that form instantly and hold perfectly, streets that stay spotless despite the near-total absence of public bins, trains that depart to the second. It's a society that has essentially outsourced a huge amount of social friction into individual self-discipline, and while it produces remarkable public order, it can also mean genuine problems — harassment, discomfort, distress — go unspoken far longer than they should, because speaking up publicly carries a social cost most people aren't willing to pay.

## Montenegro: If It's Worth Feeling, It's Worth Saying

Montenegro runs on the opposite operating system entirely. Directness isn't rudeness here, it's basic communication, and the Balkan tradition of the kafana — the long, unhurried coffee sit-down — means public spaces are full of unresolved, ongoing, cheerfully loud conversation about absolutely everything: politics, football, your love life, why the bus was late, whether you've put on weight. Nothing is off limits, and nothing is whispered.

This directness extends to disagreement. If a Montenegrin thinks you're wrong, you will know, immediately, and probably so will the four other people at the neighbouring table who've now joined the conversation uninvited. What looks like an argument to an outsider is frequently just... Tuesday. Relationships here are built through this kind of engaged, opinionated back-and-forth, and the visitors who struggle most are the ones who mistake bluntness for hostility and either shut down or take offence, missing that the whole exchange is, underneath the volume, a form of warmth.

Coffee culture deserves its own mention because it structures the entire day. A "quick coffee" is a fictional concept — expect ninety minutes minimum, expect it to be interrupted by at least two passing acquaintances who stop to chat, and expect to leave having discussed far more of your personal life than you'd planned to. Trying to rush this process, or worse, trying to pay and leave quickly, reads as either deeply suspicious or deeply rude, and often both.

## The Verdict

Japan's public restraint produces a society that runs beautifully but occasionally suffers in silence it shouldn't have to. Montenegro's public volume produces a society that's exhausting for introverts but rarely lets anything fester unspoken. If you want order, book Tokyo. If you want to feel like every stranger in the café is invested in the outcome of your day, book Podgorica. I know which one leaves me more emotionally depleted by 11am, and it's not the one with the trains.

## Conclusion

These two countries have arrived at completely opposite answers to the same question: what do you do with a feeling in public? Japan swallows it, wraps it in politeness, and moves on. Montenegro says it out loud, invites commentary, and turns it into a group activity. Neither is wrong, exactly, though I'd argue both could stand to borrow a little from the other — Tokyo could use a bit more venting, Podgorica could use a bit more inside voice. Until they swap notes, though, pack your patience for the coffee marathon in one and your poker face for the silent train carriage in the other.
