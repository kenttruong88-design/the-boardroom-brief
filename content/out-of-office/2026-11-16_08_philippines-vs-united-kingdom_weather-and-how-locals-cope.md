# The Philippines Gets Twenty Typhoons a Year and the UK Gets One Long Drizzle and Calls It Character

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The Philippines has weather with muscle. Hot, humid, soaked in monsoon rain, and sitting squarely in the path of typhoons that arrive with an annual regularity and a cheerful female name. Filipinos have developed a resilience that borders on the unnerving: the lights go out, the water rises, the family moves upstairs, and somebody puts on a karaoke machine powered by a generator.

Britain has weather with mood. It is never extreme, rarely lethal, and perpetually, creatively disappointing. A grey sky, a thin rain, a temperature that cannot decide, and a population that has turned discussing all of this into the national art form. For hundreds of thousands of Filipino healthcare workers who have made the journey, the weather is the first thing to be survived and the last thing to be forgiven.

## The Philippines

A Philippine year is divided into the hot, the wet and the dangerous. The tropical heat is constant, the humidity thick enough to chew, and the rainy season brings downpours that turn streets into rivers within minutes. On top of that, the country sits in a typhoon belt, and roughly twenty storms a year enter the area the national weather service monitors, a figure often cited, though not every one makes landfall.

Locals cope with a mixture of preparation and humour. Houses in storm-prone regions are built or reinforced for the wind. Families keep emergency bags, candles and canned food. Neighbours check on each other, and after the storm the community clears the debris and gets back to work. The cultural trait, widely recognised, is bayanihan: the spirit of communal effort, the idea that a house is moved or a street cleared because everyone shows up.

The heat itself is a lifestyle. Offices and malls are cooled to arctic levels, and the standard Filipino accessory is a light jacket that you wear indoors in a tropical country. Umbrellas are used against both sun and rain. Midday is for staying in the shade, and the evenings, once the temperature drops, are when life really begins. If you grew up there, you carry the knowledge in your body: when to move, when to rest, and when to turn on the fan.

Which makes the departure to a British winter a genuine shock to the nervous system. You have spent your life managing heat and water. Now you are managing cold and dark, and nobody gave you the manual.

## The United Kingdom

British weather is a gentle assault. It does not kill you, but it does wear you down. Winters are cold, damp and dark, with daylight shrinking to a brief afternoon appearance. The cold is rarely dramatic, but it is persistent, and it gets into places a tropical person did not know could get cold.

The AllNurses forum has a thread titled "What should a Filipino nurse bring to the UK?", from a male nurse planning his move around an English-language test in October 2017. Replies cover the practical points: Scottish April can still be cold, UK sockets run at 240 volts so check your gadgets, and most supermarkets stock what you need, with specialist shops in larger towns. It is the kind of thread that tells you more than any glossy guide, because it was written by people who had already stood at a bus stop in the rain.

A diary on the Filipino UK Nurses site, written by a nurse who arrived in July 2013, offers a related flavour. She expected a posting in the Midlands and ended up in London, found British English differed from what she had learned, discovered that patients calling her "darling" was an everyday pleasantry, and had to learn basic household tasks and plumbing herself. The weather is only one part of the culture shock, but it sits behind all of it, because a cold, dark morning makes every other difficulty feel heavier.

British coping mechanisms are modest. Hot drinks, layered clothing, a heating timer, and a strong national instinct to discuss the forecast as a form of friendship. Heating costs are a serious matter, and many newcomers learn to be careful with the thermostat quickly. The advice is dull and indispensable: buy a proper coat, buy proper boots, dress in layers, get outside whenever there is daylight, and understand that the damp, not the cold, is what gets you.

## The Verdict

Objectively, the Philippines has the more dangerous weather and the UK has the more depressing weather. One tries to kill you spectacularly, and the other tries to bore you into surrender. If forced to choose a threat, I would take the typhoon, because at least it has the dignity of a plot.

For comfort, though, the British climate wins on safety and wins on stability. The Philippines requires real preparedness, constant vigilance and a tolerance for disruption that is hard to understand until you have lived through it. Britain offers a mild, manageable difficulty that can be solved with a coat and a lamp.

I give it to the Philippines for spirit and to the UK for survivability. A Filipino nurse in Manchester in February is likely to say the same thing: that she would trade every grey day for one sunny, humid, noisy afternoon at home. Climate is not just temperature. It is who is sitting next to you when the lights go out.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Filipino UK Nurses diary (2021) — the writer arrived in July 2013 expecting the Midlands, landed in London, and had to learn plumbing and household basics herself, a reminder that the weather is only one of the things nobody explained.*

> *Tinig UK (2023) — the cost of becoming a UK nurse is estimated at several hundred pounds before travel and food, so by the time you meet the first cold morning your wallet is already feeling it.*

## Conclusion

You cannot pack your homeland's weather, but you can pack its habits. Bring the Filipino instinct for community, the bayanihan reflex, the humour that gets a family through a flooded night. They work just as well in a British February, where the enemy is the dark, not the wind.

And if you are a Briton moving the other way, accept that you will sweat, that the rain will feel like a warm shower, and that the storm warnings are worth listening to. Get a good torch. Make friends with your neighbours. The weather will test you either way, and in both countries the best forecast is the person next door with a spare candle.
