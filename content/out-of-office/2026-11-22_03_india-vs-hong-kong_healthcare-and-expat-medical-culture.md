# India Has a Doctor on Every Corner; Hong Kong Has a Queue and a Credit Card

🇮🇳 India vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In India, healthcare is a bazaar. You walk into a private clinic without an appointment, tell a doctor what is wrong, argue about the price of the tablets, and call an uncle who knows a cardiologist. It is chaotic, personal and often brilliant, and anyone who has been treated for a fever in Bengaluru knows the answer will arrive with a second opinion and a free lecture on diet.

Hong Kong is a system. It has two lanes, public and private, and they are as different as a bus and a taxi. One is cheap and slow, the other is fast and expensive, and you are expected to know which one you are in before you feel unwell. I have been a patient in both countries and I can say this: India will treat you like a person, and Hong Kong will treat you like a file that is very efficiently handled.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇮🇳 India ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for a second opinion on anything major | Accept the first quote for a procedure |
| Keep copies of every report and prescription | Assume a clinic will share your records |
| Ask what a test costs before you book it | Treat a pharmacist's advice as a diagnosis |
| Check a hospital's reputation, not just its brochure | Skip insurance because treatment looks cheap |

| 🇭🇰 Hong Kong ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check what your employer's insurance covers | Assume the policy covers everything |
| Use the Primary Care Directory to compare consultation fees | Walk into a private specialist without knowing the cost |
| Book through the Go and Book app for a public clinic | Turn up at a clinic expecting an instant slot |
| Keep your HKID with you | Assume public rates apply to everyone |

## 🇮🇳 India

Indian healthcare, from the expat's side, is a two-tier affair with a lot of grey in between. The public system exists and serves enormous numbers of people, but the private sector is where an employed professional will almost always end up. Private hospitals in the large cities are well equipped, and doctors are often trained to a very high standard. What you will notice is the pace and the intimacy. You can usually see a general practitioner the same day, a specialist within days, and a lab report by evening. The doctor may give you a mobile number. The pharmacy next door will have the medication in stock, and will sell it to you with or without the paperwork.

That convenience is the system's great strength and its main risk. The patient is a consumer, a negotiator and often the sole project manager of their own treatment. You ask for the price, compare hospitals, check whether the surgeon is available on the day and keep a folder of everything. Insurance helps but is not universal, and cash on the table still talks. Family involvement is high, and relatives will sit through consultations, ask questions and offer their own diagnoses. It is warm, noisy and efficient in its way.

For someone arriving in Hong Kong from this world, the contrast is stark. India expects you to be your own advocate, and rewards the pushy. Hong Kong expects you to follow the process and rewards the prepared. Both can be navigated, but they require very different muscles. If you grew up in the Indian system, your instinct will be to ring someone, call in a favour or ask for an exception. In Hong Kong, that instinct will not help you, and you may find yourself shocked at how little room there is for negotiation at the counter.

## 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

Hong Kong's system is built around two pillars. According to the Hong Kong Talent Engage page, the Hospital Authority runs 43 public hospitals and institutions, 49 specialist out-patient clinics and 75 general out-patient clinics. Charges are low for those who qualify: an overnight stay in an acute general ward costs HK$300 a day, or HK$200 a day in convalescent, rehabilitation, infirmary or psychiatric beds, available to anyone holding a valid Hong Kong Identity Card. There is a fee-waiver mechanism for vulnerable groups, and about eighteen public hospitals have accident and emergency services. The page says Hong Kong was ranked 19th in the world and second in Asia in 2023 by the Legatum Institute's healthcare index.

The catch is access and waiting. An anonymous member on Expat Exchange, who had made the move to Hong Kong, wrote that public care is good and subsidised, but non-urgent treatment and specialist consultations come with long waiting times, language barriers can occur in public settings, and many expats choose private care for shorter waits at higher cost. That matches the government's own description of the private sector, which it says offers much shorter waiting times and a choice of specialist. The page also notes that there are over thirteen private registered hospitals, regulated by the Department of Health, and that a corporate employer's package will, chances are, include private cover.

I should flag a conflict. The Expat Exchange member says public care is heavily subsidised and accessible to residents and non-residents alike. The government page ties the low rates to Hong Kong ID holders and says nothing about non-residents, and I could not find the fees for non-eligible persons on either page. So do not assume that a newly arrived expat pays the cheap rate. Check before you get sick, which is the only moment at which no one wants to read a fee schedule.

Practical tools exist. The Primary Care Directory lets you look up doctors' consultation fees, and the Hospital Authority's Go and Book apps handle appointments at general out-patient clinics. That is the most Hong Kong sentence I have ever written: even your illness has an app.

## The Verdict

Hong Kong wins for predictability, and India wins for human contact, and I want to be honest that I do not think either wins outright. If you are a corporate expat with an employer-funded insurance package, Hong Kong is superb. You will see a private specialist quickly, in a modern clinic, with an English-speaking doctor who has read your file. The system is regulated, orderly and clean, and you know where you stand.

If you are an Indian professional used to a more fluid system, you may find Hong Kong's rigidity oddly restful, or oddly cold. India's private care is faster to enter and warmer to experience, but it demands more from the patient. Hong Kong's is smoother, and the price is the money you spend and the discretion you give up. My advice is boring and correct: get insurance, read the policy, keep a copy, and do not assume the cheap lane is open to you.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Anonymous member quoted on Expat Exchange, "a member who made the move to Hong Kong" — says public care is good and subsidised but with longer waiting times for non-emergency treatment and specialist consultations, and that many expats use private care for shorter waits at higher cost.*

> *Hong Kong Talent Engage (HKSAR Government) — describes "remarkably low hospital charges available to anyone holding a valid Hong Kong Identity Card," and says private care has much shorter waiting times and a choice of specialist.*

> *Hong Kong Talent Engage guide — points newcomers to the Primary Care Directory for doctors' consultation fees and to the Hospital Authority Go and Book apps for appointments.*

## Conclusion

India gives you a doctor who will argue with you. Hong Kong gives you a system that will not, and a receipt. Either way, the sensible expat does three things: gets covered, reads what is covered, and keeps a folder. Illness is a poor moment to discover that your plan has exclusions and your visa has a clause. Buy the insurance, learn which lane you are in, and be grateful you can complain about the waiting room in English.
