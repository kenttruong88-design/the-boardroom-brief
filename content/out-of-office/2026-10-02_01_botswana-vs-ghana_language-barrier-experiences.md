# Nobody Told You "Official Language" Is a Suggestion, Not a Promise

🇧🇼 Botswana 🇬🇭 Ghana
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every relocation brochure on earth will tell you, breezily, that English is the official language of both Botswana and Ghana, as though that settles the matter and you can skip the Duolingo app entirely. It does not settle the matter. It barely opens the conversation. I have stood in a Gaborone pharmacy understanding every individual word being spoken to me and still walking out with the wrong medication, because "official language" and "the language people actually use to talk to each other when they think you're not really listening" are two different documents filed in two different drawers.

What both countries share is a kind of linguistic diplomacy: English gets you through immigration, onto a lease, into a meeting. It will not get you invited to the thing that happens after the meeting, where the real decisions and the real jokes live. That's where Setswana and Twi take over, and where the gap between "fluent" and "included" becomes a canyon you can hear people laughing across.

## Do's & Don'ts: Botswana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn "dumela" and the extended greeting sequence — skipping it reads as rude, not efficient | Don't assume a one-word "hello" covers you; Batswana greetings are a small ceremony, not a formality |
| Use a translator app for anything medical or legal, even if the clerk's English sounds perfect | Don't nod along in Setswana-heavy meetings hoping context will fill the gaps later — it won't |
| Expect code-switching mid-sentence between English and Setswana in offices | Don't correct someone's English pronunciation unless they explicitly ask — it lands as condescension, every time |

## Do's & Don'ts: Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Twi even in Accra, where you'll hear more of it than English on the street | Don't assume Twi gets you everywhere — Ghana has dozens of languages, and Ewe or Ga speakers won't thank you for guessing wrong |
| Lean into Ghanaian English's own rhythm and vocabulary rather than correcting it toward British or American norms | Don't be surprised when "I'm coming" means "I'll be a while," not "I am currently approaching" |
| Ask which language a meeting will actually run in before you walk in | Don't treat pidgin English as broken English — it's a parallel system with its own grammar, not a mistake |

## Botswana: Fluent on Paper, Foreign in the Room

Botswana's English fluency rates are genuinely high, a legacy of a schooling system that uses English as the medium of instruction from upper primary onward, and the country's bureaucracy runs on it without apparent effort. Contracts, government forms, news broadcasts, court proceedings — all English, all comprehensible to anyone who paid attention in school anywhere in the Commonwealth. This is the part every relocation guide gets right, and it's also the part that lulls you into a false sense of linguistic security.

The Setswana layer underneath is where the country actually lives. Setswana isn't a backup language for people who can't manage English — it's the language of warmth, of humour, of the extended greeting rituals that open every real interaction, of the proverbs that get dropped into meetings to make a point land harder than any PowerPoint slide could. Skip the greeting sequence — the back-and-forth "dumela," the inquiry after someone's family, the unhurried pace of it — and you will get what you asked for, which is efficient, transactional, slightly cold service. Do the greeting properly, badly, with an accent that makes people laugh, and you get something else entirely: being treated like a person who's trying, which in Botswana buys more goodwill than fluent English ever will.

Workplace meetings are where the barrier gets real. English opens the meeting and closes it with the official minutes; everything substantive in between can slide into Setswana the moment the conversation gets interesting, urgent, or funny — and it slides back just as fast when someone remembers you're there, which is its own kind of insult if you let it be. The trick is to stop treating that switch as an exclusion and start treating it as a reliable signal: when the room drops into Setswana, something worth hearing is happening, and your job is to ask, afterward and without wounded pride, what you missed. Batswana colleagues will generally tell you, because the greeting ritual you bothered to learn bought you exactly that kind of patience.

## Ghana: One Official Language, Dozens of Real Ones

Ghana's relationship with English is more performative and more fractured than Botswana's, and far more interesting. Yes, it's the official language, the language of government, of the better newspapers, of anyone trying to sell you something with aspirations. But Ghana has, depending on whose linguistic census you trust, somewhere between forty and eighty indigenous languages, and the one you'll need changes depending on which part of which city you're standing in. Learn Twi, the most widely spoken, and you'll get a long way in Accra and Kumasi. Walk into a Ga-speaking neighbourhood or an Ewe-speaking one with your careful Twi and you will be met with the specific, weary patience of someone explaining, again, that Ghana is not a monolith.

Then there's Ghanaian Pidgin English, which foreigners consistently and condescendingly mistake for a degraded version of "proper" English rather than what it actually is: a distinct, rule-governed language with its own grammar, its own humour, its own entire register that moves faster and lands harder than formal English ever could. Dismiss it as broken English in front of the wrong audience and you will be quietly, correctly filed under "doesn't get it," and that file is hard to get out of.

Ghanaian English itself has its own vocabulary and rhythm worth respecting rather than correcting — "I'm coming" as a statement of eventual intent rather than current motion is the classic trap, and it will cost you real scheduling grief until you learn to translate it internally as "sometime, probably." The expats who do well here are the ones who stop importing their home dialect's assumptions about what English words mean and start learning Ghana's version as its own thing, because that's precisely what it is.

## The Verdict

If you want a tidier, more linear language-barrier experience, Botswana wins: one major indigenous language sitting under a genuinely high-functioning English bureaucracy, a clear ritual (the greeting) that unlocks most of the warmth, and a predictable reward structure for the effort you put in. It's the easier country to feel like you're making progress in, because the progress is measurable.

Ghana is messier, more fragmented, and considerably more fun if you're the sort of person who finds linguistic chaos energising rather than exhausting. You will never fully "solve" Ghana's language landscape the way you can meaningfully progress in Botswana's, because there isn't one landscape to solve — there are dozens, overlapping, code-switching mid-sentence, laughing at you with genuine affection the whole time. Ghana demands more humility and rewards it with considerably more texture.

Neither country is the problem here. The problem is every expat who read "official language: English" on a government website and decided that was the whole assignment. It was the syllabus. The actual exam is everything that happens after someone switches languages and watches to see if you notice.

## Conclusion

Here is the part nobody puts on the tourism website: fluency in the official language was never the finish line, it was the entry fee. Botswana will reward you for learning the greeting and staying humble about the rest. Ghana will reward you for accepting, cheerfully, that you are never going to fully catch up, and that this is the point rather than the problem. Either way, the barrier isn't the language you don't speak — it's the assumption, imported straight from whichever brochure sold you on "English spoken here," that comprehension and belonging were ever the same transaction. They weren't. They aren't. Pack the app, learn the greeting, and stop being smug about your IELTS score. Nobody out here is impressed by it, and nobody ever was.
