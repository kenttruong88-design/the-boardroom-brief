# One Country Fixes You Beautifully, the Other Fixes You Cheaply

🇸🇬 Singapore vs 🇲🇾 Malaysia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have been ill in a lot of countries. Food poisoning in Lima, a mystery rash in Tbilisi, a cracked tooth in Hanoi that I treated with optimism and then with a dentist. So when I say Singapore and Malaysia run the most opposite medical cultures on a single peninsula, understand that I have done the field work, mostly horizontally.

Singapore treats illness like a project that must be delivered on time, to specification, with an invoice. Malaysia treats it like a conversation, followed by a counter where you pay, followed by a small plastic bag of tablets. Both will cure you. Only one will make you check your bank balance before you faint.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇸🇬 Singapore ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy proper insurance before you land, with a real hospital limit | Assume the public hospital is the cheap option for foreigners |
| Ask for a written estimate and a Letter of Guarantee before admission | Walk into a private ward without asking about the deposit |
| Use a neighbourhood GP for coughs and sprains | Go to A&E for a sore throat because you're British and used to suffering |
| Check your employer plan's panel of hospitals | Take it for granted that your family is covered |

| 🇲🇾 Malaysia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use private clinics for routine problems and faster appointments | Treat the public hospital as your default if you're a foreigner |
| Ask what's in the unlabelled pouch of tablets and why | Swallow anything without asking about side effects |
| Keep the receipt, because insurers reimburse only up to your policy | Expect clinics to bill your insurer for you |
| Visit more than one pharmacy if the first lacks your medication | Assume every pharmacy stocks the same things |

## 🇸🇬 Singapore

Singapore has one of the best health systems on earth, and it will remind you of this at the till. The hospitals are clean, the doctors are brilliant, the queues are manageable, and the whole thing runs like a Swiss watch that has been to business school.

Here is the catch that every newly arrived expat discovers by accident. The subsidised wards, the famous B2 and C classes, are designed for citizens and permanent residents. Per Expatica's rundown, expats do not get those subsidies just by choosing a cheaper ward, and the A and B1 wards are unsubsidised for foreigners. So the public hospital is not the bargain you hoped for. It is merely the less glamorous way to be billed.

Go private and the numbers turn theatrical. Expatica lists single rooms at the big private hospitals starting at roughly 780 to 850 Singapore dollars a night, and maternity packages starting above four thousand, doctor fees excluded. Private hospitals may also ask for a deposit before they admit you, and ask for more if the bill climbs past it. One account Expatica cites describes a cash outlay of ten to twelve thousand dollars after an emergency caesarean and neonatal care, against a deposit of about four thousand. That is a lot of money to be handed a baby and a receipt.

The expat medical culture here is therefore built around insurance, and an almost spiritual reverence for the Letter of Guarantee, which can shrink your upfront deposit though it does not cover everything. Employers matter enormously. Your plan decides which hospitals will take you without a financial negotiation, and families are not always covered unless someone read the contract carefully.

Day to day, though, it is lovely. The neighbourhood GP will see you quickly, speak English, and charge you a sensible sum. Everything is digital, signposted, and punctual. The Ministry of Health itself says it is not trying to be a medical tourism hub, which is a charmingly Singaporean thing to announce while operating one. Be sick here, but be sick prepared.

## 🇲🇾 Malaysia

Malaysia runs two parallel universes. The public system serves most of the population, and, according to Expat.com's guide, about 65 percent of people use it while only 45 percent of registered doctors work in it. Do the arithmetic and you get the waiting rooms. Foreigners pay extra there, there are no reciprocal health agreements, and expats are generally steered to use government hospitals for emergencies only.

The private sector is where you will live. A private consultation costs around 125 ringgit according to the same guide, and a private clinic bed runs above 200 ringgit a day. That is not free, and it is not nothing, but it is a different planet from the Singaporean ward rates. The English is excellent, the appointments are quick, and the doctors are frequently trained abroad and frighteningly competent.

The culture is lovely and slightly chaotic. You see the doctor, you have your tests, then you queue at the payment window and collect medication in a plastic pouch with no leaflet and no original box. Ask what it is. Ask twice. Expat.com's guide notes rare cases of over-prescribing and counterfeit drugs, and while I would not let that stop you, I would let it make you annoying at the pharmacy counter. Not every pharmacy stocks the same items, so expect a small scavenger hunt.

Private clinics want payment up front, and your insurer reimburses only what your policy allows. An expat on the Johor Bahru forum talked about a child's check-up at a central clinic costing about 50 ringgit, which tells you the low end is genuinely low, though they also warned the memory was five years old and the price might have moved. Johor is, of course, where Singaporeans themselves wander for cheaper dentistry and check-ups, which is the most honest review either country gets.

There is one more thing worth saying about Malaysia, and it concerns attitude. Doctors here tend to treat you as an adult who can ask questions, which is refreshing after the sterile choreography of a Singapore consult room. The flip side is that nobody will chase you. If you do not ask for the explanation, the leaflet, the follow-up or the second opinion, you will not get them. Malaysia hands you the controls and wishes you well, and I find that more honest than a system that pretends to hold your hand while counting your deposit.

Insurance matters here too, but for a different reason. In Singapore it protects you from ruin. In Malaysia it protects you from paperwork, since you pay first and claim later, and the receipts become your small, tedious second job.

## The Verdict

Singapore wins on quality, reliability and the sheer comfort of never wondering whether the machine will work. Malaysia wins on price, access and the fact that you will not need to remortgage your dignity to treat a sinus infection. If you are rich, covered, or both, Singapore is the better place to be ill, and I say that as someone who has been ill in a hospital with an aquarium.

If you are on a regular salary with a thin policy, Malaysia is the saner choice, and I will not pretend otherwise. The Singaporean system is magnificent in the way that a Michelin menu is magnificent: you admire it right up until you check the right-hand column.

My personal ranking, and I reserve the right to be dramatic about it, goes like this. For a catastrophe, Singapore. For a Tuesday, Malaysia. The tragedy is that most of life is Tuesdays, and most expats buy insurance for the catastrophe and then pay cash for the Tuesday anyway.

Winner: Malaysia, narrowly, for anyone without a corporate benefits package. Singapore, loudly, for anyone with one.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expatica — an expat's emergency caesarean plus neonatal care in a private Singapore hospital ended up costing far more in cash than the deposit they had been quoted.*

> *Expat.com — medication in Malaysia arrives in plain labelled pouches with no leaflet, so you must ask the doctor about side effects yourself.*

> *Expat.com forum — a poster recommended a central Johor Bahru clinic for a child's check-up at about 50 ringgit, with the caveat that it was years ago.*

## Conclusion

The truth is that these two countries are not competing on the same terms. Singapore sells certainty and charges for it. Malaysia sells access and trusts you to ask the awkward question about the tablets. Neither is wrong, and both are smarter than whatever your home country is doing.

My advice, from fourteen relocations and a generous collection of waiting rooms. Sort the insurance before the boxes. Ask for the estimate before the surgery. Find the neighbourhood doctor before the fever. And never, ever assume the cheaper ward is for you.

Singapore will save your life with immaculate manners. Malaysia will save it before lunch and hand you a bag. Choose the one whose invoice you can survive, because the pills work the same either way.
