# Singapore Will Open Your Account Before Your Coffee Cools. China Will Open It After You've Proved You Exist

🇨🇳 China vs 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two kinds of bureaucracy in the world: the kind that wants to help you and the kind that wants to be sure you deserve it. Singapore is the first kind, run with the brisk efficiency of a hotel concierge who has read your file in the lift. China is the second, a system built on the sensible premise that every account must belong to a verified human being, and on the less sensible premise that this human being should bring every piece of paper he has ever owned.

Neither is wrong. Both are exhausting in different directions. I have opened accounts in more countries than I have owned umbrellas, and I can tell you the difference is not speed but temperament: Singapore asks what you need, China asks who you are. A source note before the sermon: the pages I could read cover Singapore only, and they are old, so the figures are indicative. The Chinese side below comes from general, widely documented features of mainland banking, and I flag it as such.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**China**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your passport, residence permit and a local phone number registered in your own name | Assume a foreign credit card will survive a week of daily life |
| Set up WeChat Pay or Alipay the moment a Chinese bank card exists | Carry only cash and expect shopkeepers to be pleased |
| Go in person, early, with your employer's letter if you have one | Send a colleague to do it for you |
| Keep your address registration paperwork in a single folder | Argue with a clerk about what the form "really means" |

**Singapore**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your employment pass, passport and proof of address | Present a hotel booking as your address |
| Check the minimum opening deposit before you queue | Assume every bank asks for the same amount |
| Wait for the working-day ATM delay without drama | Expect cash withdrawals the minute the form is signed |
| Compare banks, since their strictness genuinely differs | Treat DBS and its rivals as interchangeable |

## China

Mainland China runs on real-name registration, and that principle shapes every encounter. To open an account, a foreigner generally needs a passport, a valid residence permit or visa, a local mobile number and a physical appearance at a branch. The system also layers accounts by function and limits, which means a branch may steer you toward a basic account first and let you graduate to a fuller one later. This is general knowledge, not something from the pages I fetched, so treat the detail as a starting point and let your own branch correct it.

What matters in practice is not the account but what it unlocks. In Chinese cities, daily life runs through two apps. Cash is accepted in theory and regarded with mild suspicion in practice, and a foreign credit card is the financial equivalent of a fax machine. Until your local bank card is linked to WeChat Pay or Alipay, you are a person at the edge of an economy, waving politely.

The bureaucratic charm is its thoroughness. Banks, landlords and police registration offices all want the same documents in slightly different orders, and your address registration has a way of becoming the hinge on which everything else swings. A tidy folder is a survival tool. So is patience, preferably the kind that arrives with a flask of tea.

The sting is that it works. Once you are inside, the system is astonishingly convenient: you can pay for a dumpling, a taxi and a utility bill from a single phone without once touching a note. The price of admission is a morning spent being verified, and an afternoon spent wondering why nobody said so on the website.

Consider what this means for the timetable of a posting. A transferee landing in Beijing or Shanghai should treat the first fortnight as an administrative project: residence permit, local number, bank branch, payment app, in that order, because each step unlocks the next. Skip one and the chain stalls, and you spend a weekend learning why a foreign card is not a plan. The practical trick is to ask your employer's HR team which branch they use, since a branch that already knows your company letter will process you in a fraction of the time. Nobody tells you this, because the people who know it have forgotten it was ever hard.

## Singapore

Singapore operates on a different principle: assume you are a competent adult and process you accordingly. According to expat forum threads, an employment pass, passport and a minimum deposit, about S$500 at DBS or S$1,000 at UOB in the older posts, got an account opened on the spot. One replier reported being done in under half an hour once they held an employment pass and a FIN, while another counted nearly two hours at DBS.

Here is where the efficiency shows its teeth. DBS rejected a hotel address and accepted a signed rental agreement, which tells you the bank wants a proper domicile, not a vibe. Dependant pass holders reportedly cannot open an account alone, and cards take several working days to arrive. ATM withdrawals wait until the next working day. Small delays, all of them, and all of them posted in advance.

Corporate accounts are less breezy. One replier described Maybank asking for a S$1,000 minimum deposit, a passport and the company's Memorandum of Incorporation, taking about half an hour, though some business accounts came without a debit card. A brand-new firm with no credit history, the thread suggests, may struggle to get a card-linked account. Predictable, rule-bound, occasionally stingy, but never mysterious.

The Singaporean method is to publish the rules and then enforce them with a straight face. You will not be charmed into an exception and you will not be insulted into a refusal. The bank wants documents, you bring documents, and the transaction concludes before your phone battery has finished its sigh.

The practical lesson from the Singapore threads is to treat the minimum deposit as a negotiable detail in nobody's mind but a fixed one in the bank's. The older posts quoted S$500 at one bank and S$1,000 at another, and a replier said banks differ in strictness, with DBS the stricter. Check the current figure on the bank's own page, bring the originals of every document, and make the visit on a weekday morning. Do that and the entire exercise is, as one replier found, a matter of minutes. The only genuinely slow element is the debit card, which takes three to five working days to arrive, so carry some cash for the first week.

## The Verdict

Singapore wins, and not by a margin that requires a spreadsheet. A system that tells you what it needs, takes it, and lets you leave is a civilised system. The Chinese approach has its own logic, and the end state is dazzling, but the journey demands a tolerance for ambiguity that most expatriates acquire only after the third visit to the same counter.

That said, do not mistake Singaporean efficiency for generosity. It is efficient because it is strict. Arrive with the wrong address, the wrong visa class or the wrong minimum deposit and you will discover that politeness and flexibility are different things. China, oddly, can be more human about it: a determined clerk will occasionally find you a path through the forest, while Singapore points to the sign that says there is no forest.

If you are relocating a family, the dependant-pass detail matters more than any ranking. A spouse who cannot open an account alone is a spouse who needs your signature, and nobody enjoys discovering that at a counter. Read the rules before you arrive, in both countries, and carry originals plus copies.

## Conclusion

Banks are where a country tells you what it thinks of strangers. Singapore says: here is the list, bring it. China says: first, let us establish who you are. One of those is faster. The other, once you are in, is quietly spectacular.

Pack your originals, learn which apps run the town, and never, ever offer a hotel as your home address. The paperwork is not the obstacle. Your belief that it will be reasonable is.
