# India Has A Festival For Everything. Hong Kong Has A Spreadsheet For Everything.

🇮🇳 India vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two former British colonies, two radically different relationships with the concept of "a day off." One nation has so many festivals that the government had to invent a tier system — gazetted, restricted, take-it-or-leave-it — just to stop the whole economy collapsing into confetti. The other has distilled the entire question down to a government PDF, seventeen rows long, four of which aren't even paid.

I have spent Diwali being fed by seven different households who were each convinced I hadn't eaten, and I have spent Chinese New Year in Hong Kong watching grown adults recalculate, to the half-day, exactly how much annual leave it takes to stretch three public holidays into nine. Both approaches are, in their own way, completely deranged. Let's get into it.

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your *state's* holiday calendar, not the national one — Onam means nothing in Punjab and Baisakhi means nothing in Kerala | Assume "public holiday" means uniform. India has 28 states and 8 union territories, each issuing its own list |
| Accept every sweet offered to you during Diwali season without comment | Ask what's "actually" being celebrated in a tone that implies you'd like a shorter answer |
| Use a restricted holiday for the festival that matters to you — you get roughly one or two picks a year | Expect the office to shut for your festival just because it shut for someone else's |
| Vote on election day if you're eligible — it's a legally mandated paid holiday | Assume your employer enforces that law out of love; the fine for dodging it is a magnificently unthreatening ₹500 |

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the difference between "general holiday" and "statutory holiday" before you sign anything | Assume all 17 gazetted days off in 2026 are paid — only 13 are, for most staff |
| Build your year around the six long weekends 2026 hands you for free | Book flights the moment a Typhoon Signal 8 goes up — half the city had the same idea |
| Treat Lunar New Year as a three-day negotiation with your calendar | Expect shops, your favourite noodle stall, or anyone at all to be open on Lunar New Year's Day |
| Keep two personal leave days in reserve near Chinese New Year | Try to schedule anything important in the week either side of it — nobody is coming |

## 🇮🇳 India: A Festival Calendar That Refuses To Be Tamed

India does not have a holiday calendar. It has a holiday *negotiation*, re-litigated annually, state by state, deity by deity. The baseline is deceptively small: three truly national gazetted holidays — Republic Day, Independence Day, Gandhi Jayanti — on which the National and Festival Holidays Act legally shuts every establishment down, full stop, no exceptions without prior approval. Everything else is a free-for-all. Officially there are 17 gazetted holidays and a further 30 "restricted" ones on the central list, from which the ordinary salaried employee gets to cash in perhaps one or two, depending on what their particular festival calendar demands. Most companies land somewhere between 8 and 14 paid holidays a year, which sounds generous until you realise it means almost every one of your colleagues is off on a *different* day than you.

This is where the expat ego takes its first bruising. You cannot simply learn "the Indian holidays." You must learn Delhi's, and then discover that Chennai's look nothing like them, and that Kerala has quietly been running on an entirely separate emotional operating system called Onam, and that Punjab will not be answering its phones during Baisakhi, and nobody is going to send you a memo about any of this — you're expected to absorb it by proximity, like humidity.

Meanwhile corporate India has developed its own parallel tradition: the mandatory festival celebration that technically isn't a holiday at all. Offices now run Diwali parties and Secret Santa exchanges with the gleeful enthusiasm of a theme-park mascot, employees still expected to show up, dress appropriately, buy gifts for colleagues, and smile through a dance performance — all while the holiday itself remains exactly as short as it always was. Factor in an actual legal entitlement — a paid holiday on election polling day, under Section 135B of the Representation of the People Act — enforced by a penalty so toothless (₹500, functionally a parking fine) that most employers simply treat it as optional. India will give you a thousand reasons to celebrate and roughly none of the institutional backbone to make sure you actually get the day off.

## 🇭🇰 Hong Kong: Precision-Engineered Rest, With An Asterisk

Hong Kong, by contrast, has turned its public holidays into an administrative art form, and the administration is the point. For 2026, the government has gazetted exactly 17 general public holidays, six of which — through the sheer mathematical kindness of where they fall — create long weekends without you lifting a finger or burning a single leave day. Lunar New Year runs February 17 to 19, and if you're willing to sacrifice two annual leave days either side of it, you can manufacture a full nine days off. This is the city's signature move: holidays as a precision instrument, optimised down to the half-day, discussed in HR departments with the seriousness other cities reserve for interest rate decisions.

Here's the twist nobody mentions in the glossy relocation brochure: of those 17 general holidays, only 13 are *statutory* — meaning only 13 are guaranteed paid time off for most employees, assuming you've cleared the three-month continuous-employment threshold. The other four are commonly granted as a contractual nicety, not a legal entitlement, which means your actual day-off math depends entirely on what your specific employment contract says, not on what the government calendar implies. Read the fine print. Twice.

And then there's the one "holiday" that isn't on any calendar at all: Typhoon Signal 8. When it's hoisted, public transport stops, offices empty out, and the entire city treats it as a sanctioned, almost sacred pause — the only universally accepted excuse to simply not come in. Residents have turned it into its own micro-tradition, stocking up on instant noodles and beer the moment the forecast looks promising, half-hoping for the storm. It says everything about Hong Kong that its most beloved day off is the one the government didn't actually schedule.

## The Verdict

Hong Kong wins on efficiency, obviously — six engineered long weekends and a weather system that occasionally gifts you a bonus day is objectively superior scheduling. But efficiency isn't the same as joy, and Hong Kong's holidays, however cleverly spaced, are fundamentally transactional: a government optimising your productivity curve, not a culture inviting you to anything. India's system is a bureaucratic shambles — three real holidays, thirty maybes, and a different festival in every postcode — but it is a shambles built entirely out of actual celebration, actual food, actual people dragging you into their homes whether you asked or not.

If you want your time off managed like a logistics problem, move to Hong Kong. If you want your time off to feel like something happened to you, emotionally, against your will, in the best way — book the flight to India. I know which one I'd rather be homesick for.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *X, via Business Today reporting (Dec 2025) — paraphrased: a widely shared post argued Indian offices stage mandatory Diwali parties and Secret Santa exchanges specifically to keep staff content enough not to demand real time off, with one commenter noting they had to cut short visits home because there was never an actual multi-day break, only the party.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — paraphrased from recurring threads: staff working from home during festival season routinely report receiving no Diwali gift or bonus at all, while office-based colleagues get the dry fruit, sweets, or token cash — a split that resurfaces every festival season almost unchanged.*

> *expat.com Hong Kong forum — paraphrased from longstanding community discussion: new arrivals are consistently thrown by discovering, usually via their own payslip, that not every gazetted public holiday is actually a paid one, and that "ask HR before Lunar New Year, not after" is close to universal advice.*

## Conclusion

Here's the uncomfortable truth both cities would rather you didn't notice: neither holiday system was built with you in mind. India's was built by committee, state by state, over decades of competing claims nobody wanted to adjudicate cleanly. Hong Kong's was built by people who like their spreadsheets more than their small talk. You, the arriving expat, are simply expected to download the local version of "normal" and stop asking why it doesn't match the one you left behind. So check the contract, check the state calendar, stock up on noodles before the storm, and for heaven's sake stop calling any of this "exotic." It's just Tuesday, somewhere. Tuesday just looks a lot busier here.
