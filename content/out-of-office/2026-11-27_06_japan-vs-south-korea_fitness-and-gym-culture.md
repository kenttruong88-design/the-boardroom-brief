# Japan Whispers Its Way to Fitness and Korea Takes a Selfie on the Way

🇯🇵 Japan vs 🇰🇷 South Korea

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Fitness is the great leveller of cultures, because every country eventually puts a weight on a barbell and discovers what it believes about discipline. Japan believes that discipline should be quiet, ordered and slightly scary. South Korea believes it should be visible, social and lit like a fashion shoot.

I have been to gyms in both, and I have been stared at in both, though for different reasons. In Japan I was stared at for putting my shoes on the wrong floor. In Korea I was stared at for not looking the part. Neither made me love exercise, but both made me love the staff.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇯🇵 Japan ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wipe down the equipment after use and bring a small towel | Leave a sweat puddle for the next person |
| Change into indoor shoes if the gym requires it | Walk in with your street shoes on the training floor |
| Ask about tattoo policies before you sign anything | Assume tattoos are fine because they are fine at home |
| Read the contract carefully, including the cancellation terms | Sign a long-term plan on your first visit |

| 🇰🇷 South Korea ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Try the outdoor exercise equipment and the hiking trails | Treat the mountain as a flat walk |
| Dress sharply, because the gym is also a social stage | Turn up in your oldest T-shirt and expect to be ignored |
| Ask about trial passes and trainer packages | Sign up for a personal trainer bundle without comparing prices |
| Respect the age and seniority hierarchy in group classes | Address a senior member too casually |

## 🇯🇵 Japan

The Japanese gym is a place of rules, and the rules are the point. Members arrive, change, train and leave in a flow that nobody has to explain. Equipment is wiped, weights are returned and conversations are kept to a murmur. The atmosphere is calm and a little austere, with a quiet respect for everybody else's workout. Nobody grunts. Nobody films. If you drop a dumbbell, expect the whole room to experience it as a personal event.

The big surprise for newcomers is the tattoo question. Many gyms, pools and bathhouses restrict visible tattoos because of historic associations with organised crime, and although attitudes are slowly relaxing, a tattooed foreigner should check before paying. Contracts are another trap. Membership terms can be rigid, cancellation can require paperwork and notice, and the person at the desk will smile politely while explaining that no refund exists.

The strength of the system is the community around it. Municipal sports centres offer very good facilities at low prices, local pools are tidy and well kept, and running clubs gather on weekends along rivers and around the Imperial Palace. Public exercise also exists in its own form: radio calisthenics, the morning stretching routine, is still practised in parks and workplaces.

Japanese fitness suits people who like order and anonymity. If you want to be left alone to work, it is the best gym culture in the world. If you want to be seen, you may feel like a ghost.

The cost and contract culture is worth a short warning. Japanese gyms often prefer long contracts, fees for joining, and a surprising number of rules about when you can freeze or cancel your membership. Get everything translated before you sign. Korean gyms are often cheaper to join and more flexible, but they are known for sales pressure around training packages, so ask questions, take a trial, and do not feel bound to an offer that appears to expire in an hour.

## 🇰🇷 South Korea

South Korean fitness is louder, more social and considerably more visible. The country is obsessed with appearance and health, and the gym is part of the urban stage. Mirrors are everywhere, trainers are numerous, and personal training is a thriving business. People dress for the occasion, and the lines between sports gear and streetwear blur in the most photogenic way.

But the real Korean secret is outdoors. Mountains stand at the edge of nearly every city, and hiking is a national religion. On a weekend morning the trails are crowded with retirees in colourful technical gear that could outfit a polar expedition. Parks contain free outdoor fitness machines that locals use with enthusiasm, and the riverside paths in Seoul are packed with runners and cyclists in the evening.

Gyms are plentiful and often open late. A lot of fitness is tied into social life, with colleagues exercising together, friends joining the same class, and a general awareness that your fitness is a conversation topic. Seniority matters here too. Younger members defer to older ones, and politeness is built into the way you speak and the way you share the weights.

The cultural pressure can be intense. Body image is a heavy subject, and the expectations on appearance are strong. A newcomer should enjoy the energy and the excellent facilities, while ignoring anyone who treats a fitness plan as a verdict on your character.

The most useful thing about South Korean fitness culture is how it merges with daily life. People walk everywhere, take stairs without comment, and treat the mountain as a weekend appointment. There is also a well-developed running culture, with organised races and groups meeting along the rivers. Even office life has its moments, with stretching breaks and team hikes, and the sense that health is a collective project.

## The Verdict

South Korea wins, narrowly, for variety. The combination of good gyms, free outdoor equipment and mountains on the doorstep makes it hard to be sedentary. Japan has the better manners, and I will always value a gym where nobody sings along to the playlist. But the Japanese system punishes the casual member with paperwork and unwritten rules, while Korea invites you to climb something and feel better for it.

For anyone who finds the idea of a gym a form of punishment, the answer is simple. Move to Seoul and walk up a mountain on Sunday. For anyone who values silence, move to Tokyo and train at a municipal centre in peace. Either way, you will leave fitter than you arrived and, unavoidably, better dressed.

Compare the two for a moment as societies. Japan treats the gym as a private ritual, where you are responsible for your own improvement and no one else's. Korea treats it as a shared stage, where improvement is partly a performance and partly a conversation. Both are effective, and both are shaped by deeper attitudes to the group and the individual.

A practical reminder for newcomers: bring proof of address, a local phone number and a sense of humour. Registration at either type of gym can involve forms, deposits and the occasional misunderstanding about what a trial means. A friendly staff member who speaks a little English can be a great help, and a translation app can fill the gaps. Treat the first visit as reconnaissance, and sign nothing until you have seen the changing rooms.

One more cultural point is worth knowing. In both countries, the changing room is a place where etiquette carries real weight. Japan expects you to wash before entering any shared bath or pool, and nobody will explain it twice. Korea has its own saunas and bathhouses, where public nudity is routine and staring is not. Learn the rules, follow the crowd, and you will be fine, and if you are unsure, ask the member next to you.

## Conclusion

Fitness reflects what a society admires. Japan admires restraint, consistency and tidy habits. South Korea admires energy, effort and looking good while doing it. Both are impressive, and both will test your pride.

Pick the one that suits your temperament, then follow its rules. Wipe the machine in Tokyo. Climb the mountain in Seoul. And in both countries, never underestimate the person in the technical hiking trousers, because they will beat you to the top.

Whichever you choose, start slowly. The standards are high, the locals are fit, and the temptation to compete with a retired hiker is a path to injury.
