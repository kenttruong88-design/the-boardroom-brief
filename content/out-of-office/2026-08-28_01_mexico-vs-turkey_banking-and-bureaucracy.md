# Bureaucracy Doesn't Care About Your Feelings, and Neither Do These Two Countries

🇲🇽 Mexico vs 🇹🇷 Turkey — Banking and Bureaucracy

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country likes to pretend its paperwork is uniquely, spectacularly cruel, and every expat who has lived in more than one country knows this is nonsense — bureaucracy is a universal language spoken fluently by underpaid clerks the world over. But Mexico and Turkey have taken this shared inheritance and built two entirely different cathedrals of inconvenience, one out of patience-testing repetition and the other out of stamps, so many stamps, applied with the solemnity of a coronation.

I have opened bank accounts on four continents and been made to cry, quietly, in a lobby, exactly twice. Once in Mexico City, once in Ankara. Neither bank apologised. This is their story.

## Do's & Don'ts: Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your CURP and RFC sorted before you even think about a bank | Assume a printout of your passport photo page counts as an official document |
| Bring a proof-of-address utility bill dated within the last three months | Expect the branch that told you X yesterday to agree with itself today |
| Befriend one specific, named employee and return to them every time | Try to do anything bureaucratic in the last week of the month |
| Carry small bills everywhere — cash still runs half this country | Rely on your bank's app working the same way twice in a row |

## Do's & Don'ts: Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a tax number (vergi numarası) on day one — you need it for everything | Show up at a noter without an appointment and expect to leave before lunch |
| Use a Turkish e-Devlet account once you have residency — it fixes half your problems | Assume English will get you anywhere past the airport lounge |
| Keep physical copies of every stamped document, forever, in a folder | Trust a verbal promise from any official — get it stamped or it didn't happen |
| Expect genuine warmth once you're through the paperwork gauntlet | Lose your temper with a bank teller — it will not speed anything up, ever |

## Mexico: The Photocopy Economy

Mexico runs on an economy of paper that photocopies other paper. You will be asked for your passport, then a photocopy of your passport, then a photocopy of the photocopy, notarised, because apparently the ink itself needs credentials. Opening a bank account as a foreigner without a CURP (your national ID number, which itself requires an appointment you'll wait three weeks for) is close to impossible, and even with one, the actual account-opening ritual is less a transaction than a pilgrimage — three visits is standard, not exceptional. First visit: you find out what you need. Second visit: you bring what you need and discover it was the wrong version of what you need. Third visit: someone finally takes pity on you.

And yet — and this is the part visitors miss — nobody in the branch is actually being cruel. Mexican bureaucracy moves at the speed of a system built when everything had to be verified by hand and never got the memo that computers exist, but the humans trapped inside it are, almost without exception, patient, warm, and will call you "joven" or "querida" while delivering news that will ruin your afternoon. CONDUSEF, the consumer financial protection agency, exists precisely because banks here have historically loved a hidden fee, and disputing one requires the kind of documentation trail usually reserved for court cases. SPEI transfers between Mexican banks are instant and free, which almost redeems the whole system, right up until you need a wire from abroad and discover the receiving bank has quietly decided today is not the day.

The real skill in Mexican banking bureaucracy isn't patience — it's relationship management. Find one teller, one manager, one human being who remembers your face, and your life improves by an order of magnitude. Mexico doesn't run on rules so much as it runs on people who like you enough to bend them.

## Turkey: The Empire of the Stamp

Turkey has taken bureaucracy and turned it into a genuine visual art form. Every document needs a stamp. Every stamp needs a signature. Every signature needs to be witnessed by a noter — a notary whose office resembles a cross between a courtroom and a very slow post office, and whose fees for stamping a single sheet of paper would make a Zurich private banker blush. Want to open a bank account? You need a tax number. Want a tax number? You need an address registration. Want an address registration? You need a rental contract stamped by, you guessed it, a noter, who charges you for the privilege of watching you sign your own name.

But — and Turkey deserves real credit here — once you've cleared the gauntlet, the actual banking infrastructure is startlingly modern. Turkish banking apps are slick, FAST transfers move money between accounts almost instantly, and e-Devlet, the government's digital portal, means that once you're registered in the system, you can renew half your official life from your phone without ever seeing a human being again. The problem was never the technology. The problem is getting into the technology in the first place, a process seemingly designed by someone who has personally never had anywhere else to be.

Inflation has also turned Turkish banking into a national spectator sport — interest rates and currency swings are dinner-table conversation, and locals treat foreign-currency accounts the way other countries treat retirement funds. If you're not paying attention to the lira's mood, you will be, eventually, painfully.

## The Verdict

Mexico makes you suffer through inefficiency delivered with kindness; Turkey makes you suffer through formality delivered with stamps. If I had to choose which purgatory to be stuck in, I'm picking Turkey, purely because once you're through the noter's gate, the actual systems work — the apps, the transfers, the digital government portal are genuinely good. Mexico's system, charming as the humans inside it are, never fully rewards your patience; it just resets the clock and asks you to be patient again. Turkey punishes you upfront and lets you go. Mexico punishes you slowly, forever, with a smile. Pick your poison, but don't pretend either one is fast.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/expats — paraphrased: opened an account in Mexico City on the third attempt, only after the branch manager personally intervened because she felt sorry for how long I'd been coming back.*

> *Internations Istanbul — paraphrased: nobody tells you the noter fee for stamping a rental contract can cost more than the first month's deposit itself.*

> *expat.com Turkey forum — paraphrased: once you have your tax number and e-Devlet set up, everything gets shockingly easy — the hard part is entirely the getting-in.*

## Conclusion

Neither system is designed with you in mind, which is, frankly, the most honest thing either country will tell you. Mexico wants a relationship; Turkey wants a stamp. Both want your patience, and both will get it eventually, whether you offer it willingly or have it extracted from you one branch visit at a time. If there's a lesson here, it's this: bring photocopies, bring cash, and bring absolutely no expectation that "I did this yesterday" means anything at all today. Bureaucracy, like weather, is not something you argue with. You just dress for it.
