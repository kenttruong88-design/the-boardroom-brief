# One Country Whispers, the Other Insists You Say Good Morning Properly

🇦🇩 Andorra vs 🇲🇱 Mali — Public Behaviour Norms

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Andorra and Mali sit at opposite ends of nearly every measurable axis — wealth, population, altitude, rainfall, the number of ski resorts per capita — and yet both have built entire public behaviour codes around a similar underlying instinct: don't waste anyone's time, and don't skip the pleasantries either. Andorra does this through formality, quiet nightlife, and an almost pathological respect for punctuality. Mali does it through an elaborate, genuinely beautiful greeting ritual that treats skipping "how is your family" as a small act of social violence. Neither country will tolerate a rushed, transactional interaction. They've simply built opposite architectures to prevent one.

I have been quietly, wordlessly judged in Andorra la Vella for arriving four minutes late to a lunch reservation, and I have been gently but firmly corrected in Bamako for trying to ask a shopkeeper for directions before properly greeting him first. Both moments taught me the same lesson from two completely different angles: efficiency is not the same thing as respect, and assuming otherwise will get you nowhere fast in either country.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Andorra**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive on time, every time — lateness reads as a real social slight | Expect a loud, lively nightlife scene — evenings here are notably quiet |
| Use formal titles (senyor, senyora) until invited to use first names | Litter or disregard park and trail rules — environmental respect is deeply ingrained |
| Greet with a handshake or cheek kisses, matching the formality of the moment | Rush a business or social interaction — formal courtesy takes real precedence |
| Respect the country's small scale — reputations travel fast here | Assume rules are relaxed just because the country itself is small |

**Mali**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Always greet first, properly, before asking for anything — directions included | Make prolonged eye contact with elders — it can read as confrontational |
| Use your right hand for giving, receiving, and eating | Rush a greeting exchange — skipping it is considered genuinely impolite |
| Dress modestly and moderate public affection — Islam shapes public norms significantly | Assume Bamako's urban pace excuses skipping traditional greeting customs |
| Show visible respect toward elders and authority figures in speech and posture | Treat greetings as a formality to get through quickly — they're not |

## Andorra: Formality as the Default Setting

Andorra's public behaviour code makes a lot more sense once you understand the country's peculiar geography and history: a tiny principality wedged between France and Spain, historically reachable only through rugged mountain passes, which kept it relatively insulated and allowed a fairly formal, family-and-reputation-centred social code to calcify over generations largely undisturbed by outside influence. That formality shows up everywhere, starting with greetings — handshakes or cheek kisses depending on familiarity, formal titles used by default until someone explicitly invites first-name terms, written correspondence that leans notably formal even in fairly casual professional contexts.

Punctuality carries real social weight here in a way that catches plenty of newcomers, particularly those arriving from more relaxed Mediterranean timekeeping cultures just across the border, genuinely off guard. Arriving late to a social event or meeting isn't a minor inconvenience to be waved off with an apology — it registers as a small breach of respect, and Andorrans, while unfailingly polite about it, absolutely notice. Given how small and interconnected the country's population is, a reputation for chronic lateness or casual rule-breaking travels fast and sticks.

The nightlife scene reflects the same underlying restraint — quiet by design, not by lack of options, and entirely in keeping with a broader national instinct toward measured, unhurried, formally correct interaction. Add in a genuine, near-reverential respect for the country's natural landscape — littering or ignoring park rules lands as a real cultural offence, not just an environmental one — and you start to see the throughline: Andorra's public behaviour code isn't really about rigidity for its own sake. It's about protecting something small, beautiful, and easily disrupted, whether that's a mountain trail or a dinner reservation.

## Mali: Greeting as Sacred Ritual

Mali's public behaviour norms run on an entirely different but equally rigorous logic, one built around the idea that skipping a proper greeting is close to a genuine social transgression, regardless of how mundane the interaction that follows might be. Before asking a shopkeeper for directions, before requesting a favour, before conducting nearly any transaction at all, you greet — properly, unhurriedly, with real attention paid to the other person. "Bonjour" or "I ni sogoma," followed by "Ça va?" or "I ka kene?", isn't small talk to get through quickly on the way to the actual point. It largely is the point, at least at the outset, and rushing past it reads as a small act of disrespect that Malians will notice even if they're too polite to comment on it directly.

Eye contact carries an inverted meaning from what many Western newcomers expect: sustained eye contact, particularly with an elder or authority figure, can register as confrontational or challenging rather than as a sign of honest engagement, and adjusting that instinct takes real, deliberate practice for most foreigners. The right hand carries similar cultural weight — used for eating, giving, and receiving, with the left hand's use in these contexts considered genuinely impolite, a distinction that matters far more than most newcomers initially assume.

Family and elder respect thread through nearly every layer of daily public interaction. Islam shapes much of the wider social fabric too, informing expectations around modest dress and restrained public affection, even in Bamako's comparatively cosmopolitan urban core. What consistently surprises long-term expats, though, isn't the formality of any single custom but the sheer warmth underneath all of it — Malian social life, once you've properly learned to greet, tends to be genuinely welcoming, generous, and community-oriented in a way that rewards the initial effort many times over.

## The Verdict

Mali, for the sheer human warmth embedded in its formality. Andorra's public behaviour code is admirable in its own precise, mountain-village way, but it's fundamentally a code of restraint — don't be late, don't be loud, don't disrupt. Mali's code, despite requiring more upfront cultural homework, particularly around greetings and gesture, is fundamentally a code of connection — don't skip the part where you actually acknowledge the person in front of you. Both will make you a more careful, considerate visitor. Only one will make you feel, by the end of it, like you were genuinely welcomed rather than merely tolerated.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a ski-season worker in Andorra la Vella paraphrased: he said arriving five minutes late to a casual coffee meetup earned him a noticeably cooler reception than he expected, "like I'd genuinely disrespected someone."*

> *Tales Mag, Bamako Q&A — paraphrased: a longtime resident described the American Club and expat pool socials as good entry points, but stressed that real integration only happened once she'd properly learned Bambara greetings.*

> *Expat.com forum, Mali thread — paraphrased: a newcomer admitted he'd skipped the greeting ritual out of habit for weeks, assuming it was optional small talk, before a colleague gently but clearly corrected him.*

## Conclusion

Public behaviour norms are never really about the specific rule — the handshake, the greeting phrase, the punctuality — they're about what each culture has decided respect actually looks like in practice. Andorra decided respect looks like restraint, precision, and not making a fuss. Mali decided it looks like taking the time to properly see the person in front of you before you ask them for anything at all. Learn either code badly, and you'll be tolerated. Learn it properly, and you'll be welcomed, which was always the actual point.
