# One Country Queues in Total Silence. The Other Doesn't Really Queue At All.

🇲🇽 Mexico vs 🇸🇪 Sweden — Public Behaviour Norms

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Sweden has turned queuing into an actual piece of technology — a little paper ticket with a number on it, dispensed from a machine, so that not one single Swede ever has to make eye contact while waiting their turn. Mexico, meanwhile, treats the concept of a queue as a loose philosophical suggestion, more of a cloud than a line, negotiated in real time through a combination of vague hand gestures and the universal question "¿quién es el último?" — who's last? — shouted into a crowd until someone answers.

I have stood in both. In Stockholm I once watched a grown man take a numbered ticket for a queue of one person, because the system existed and he was going to respect it regardless of logic. In Mexico City I have been folded into a queue that had no beginning, no end, and no visible order, purely by asking one question and trusting the collective memory of eleven strangers to remember I was there. Both systems work. Neither system would survive a single day in the other country.

## Do's & Don'ts: Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask "¿quién es el último?" to find your place in an informal queue | Expect silence in public — conversation volume is high and normal |
| Greet shopkeepers and neighbours warmly, even briefly | Take offense at being physically close to strangers in public spaces |
| Accept that plans and lines flex around relationships, not just order | Assume a lack of a visible queue means chaos — there's a system, just not yours |
| Join in on impromptu music or celebration in public plazas | Complain loudly about noise — it rarely lands well |

## Do's & Don'ts: Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take a numbered ticket the moment you enter any service counter | Jump a queue, even accidentally — it will be noticed |
| Keep your voice down on public transport and in cafés | Strike up conversation with strangers without a clear reason |
| Respect personal space — a full arm's length is standard | Stand too close in a queue or on a platform |
| Wait for a clear pause before speaking in group settings | Assume friendliness from silence — it's simply the default state |

## Mexico: Order Through Relationships, Not Lines

Mexican public life runs on a logic that looks like chaos to outsiders and is, in fact, a highly functional social contract nobody bothered to write down. The queue — if you can call it that — is held together entirely by memory and trust. You arrive, you ask who's last, that person confirms, and from that moment you are woven into an invisible order that everyone present will honour, even if you wander off to buy a Coca-Cola and come back ten minutes later. Try explaining this system to a Swede and watch their face attempt to process a queue with no ticket, no line, and no visual confirmation of anything.

Noise, similarly, is not a failure of manners in Mexico — it's a feature. Plazas fill with music, vendors call out prices, neighbours chat across balconies, and nobody is coming to file a noise complaint about the mariachi at 9pm on a Tuesday, because public space is understood as shared and alive rather than a zone to be endured in silence. Personal space contracts accordingly. Strangers stand close on the metro, close in shops, close in conversation, and this closeness is warmth, not violation.

Where this trips up visitors is bureaucracy, ironically the one place Mexican informality collides with genuine, glacial formality. A bank queue with no visible order can still take two hours, and the lack of a numbered ticket system means you must physically remain present, alert, and occasionally re-assert your position in the queue verbally, which is exhausting if you've spent your whole life trusting machines to remember your place for you.

## Sweden: Silence as a Form of Respect

Sweden's queuing ticket machine, the *kölapp*, is not merely a piece of infrastructure — it is a philosophical statement. It says: your place in this world is guaranteed, numerically, without you needing to speak to another human being or defend your position. Swedes will take a ticket even when they are visibly the only person in the room, because to skip that step would be to introduce an element of social risk, however small, and Swedish public life is engineered to minimise social risk at every possible turn.

Silence in Sweden is not coldness — it's a form of consideration. Talking loudly on a train is understood as imposing your noise onto people who did not consent to hear it. Standing too close to a stranger is a mild trespass. Making small talk with someone waiting for a bus is, more often than not, met with a polite but clearly startled response, because the unspoken agreement is that everyone is entitled to move through public space without being drawn into interaction they didn't ask for. It's less "Swedes are unfriendly" and more "Swedes have built an entire social infrastructure around the principle of not making anyone's day harder than it needs to be, including through conversation."

The flip side is a public realm that feels remarkably calm, orderly, and — for anyone raised on chaos — almost eerily frictionless. Nobody cuts a queue. Nobody blasts music from a phone speaker on the bus. Nobody is going to corner you for twenty minutes about their opinions. It's peaceful in the way an empty church is peaceful, which some people find soothing and others find slightly unsettling.

## The Verdict

Sweden wins on predictability — you will never, ever be unsure of your place in a queue. Mexico wins on humanity — you will never, ever feel like a number, mostly because there are no numbers, only relationships and memory. If you want a public life engineered for maximum calm and minimum social risk, Sweden delivers it with a little printed ticket. If you want a public life that feels genuinely alive, loud, and connected — at the cost of occasionally standing in an ambiguous queue for two hours — Mexico wins, decisively, and with a lot more music.

## Conclusion

Neither Mexico nor Sweden has it wrong, exactly — they've simply optimised public life for opposite outcomes. Mexico optimises for connection, at the cost of ambiguity. Sweden optimises for order, at the cost of warmth. If you're the kind of person who needs a numbered ticket to feel safe in the world, book the flight to Stockholm. If you'd rather trust eleven strangers to remember your place in a queue while a mariachi band plays somewhere nearby, Mexico City is waiting, loudly, for you. Either way, don't bring your home country's queuing instincts with you unexamined — they will fail you spectacularly in both directions.
