# Malls Are for People Who've Given Up on Joy

🇨🇱 Chile · 🇱🇦 Laos
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Chile wants you to believe it is the Singapore of South America — all glass malls, loyalty apps, and a mall culture so total that the *centro comercial* has replaced the plaza as the place teenagers go to feel something. Laos, meanwhile, still runs half its retail economy out of a woman with a scale, a stool, and forty years of knowing exactly what your tomatoes are worth before you've said a word. One of these countries has decided that shopping should feel like an airport lounge. The other has decided that shopping should feel like an actual conversation with an actual human being who will roll her eyes at you if you try to underpay her by more than is polite.

I have shopped in both. Only one of them left me wondering if I'd accidentally wandered into a duty-free zone for the soul.

## Do's & Don'ts: Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a *tarjeta* loyalty card at any supermarket chain — the discounts are real and stack fast | Expect to bargain in a mall. Prices are prices, and asking will earn you a very patient stare |
| Visit a *feria libre* on the correct weekday for genuinely superior produce prices | Assume Jumbo, Líder, and Santa Isabel are meaningfully different — they're all owned by two conglomerates |
| Carry cash for the feria; card readers are still spotty at small stalls | Show up to a feria at noon expecting the good stuff — the serious shoppers clear it out by 9am |

## Do's & Don'ts: Laos

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go to the morning market (*talat sao*) before 7am if you want anything worth having | Bargain aggressively over genuinely small amounts — 2,000 kip is not the hill to die on |
| Learn to say thank you in Lao before you try to negotiate anything | Expect fixed pricing, receipts, or returns policies anywhere outside a Vientiane supermarket |
| Bring small bills — nobody at a market stall is breaking a 100,000 kip note for you | Photograph vendors without asking; it reads as extractive, not as charming travel content |

## Chile: Consumerism With a Loyalty Card and a Guilty Conscience

Santiago has more shopping malls per capita than a city that size has any business having, and the Chileans I've spoken to about this fall into two camps: the ones who genuinely love Costanera Center on a Saturday, and the ones who will tell you, unprompted and with real bitterness, that the mall killed the neighbourhood. Both camps are right, which is the most Chilean outcome imaginable.

The formal retail sector here is dominated by a small number of enormous players — Cencosud and Falabella between them own most of what you'd call a supermarket, a department store, or a home improvement chain, and they've built loyalty ecosystems so aggressive that a Chilean without at least three store cards in their wallet is either an ideologue or new to the country. The discounts through these programs are not cosmetic. A Wednesday at Jumbo with the right card can shave a genuinely startling percentage off a grocery bill, and Chileans plan their shopping days around which chain's promotion lands on which day of the week with the seriousness other cultures reserve for religious observance.

And yet — the *feria libre*, the open-air produce market that rotates through different streets on different days, remains the backbone of how most households actually eat. It is cheaper, the produce is better, and it operates on a completely different social contract: you don't bargain, exactly, but a good relationship with your regular *casero* gets you the occasional free bunch of cilantro thrown into the bag, which functions as Chile's version of a loyalty program with better vegetables. The trick, which no supermarket app will ever tell you, is that ferias are ruthlessly time-sensitive. Arrive at 8am and the tomatoes are perfect and the price is fair. Arrive at 1pm as they're packing up and you'll get a better price on tomatoes that have given up on life.

What nobody warns you about is the sheer scale of Chilean mall culture as social infrastructure. In a country where public space can feel thin and the weather in Santiago swings between smog and heat, the mall has become the default answer to "where should we go." Multiplexes, food courts, ice rinks — I've met Chilean families whose entire Sunday itinerary lives inside a single building with a parking structure attached. It is convenient. It is also, if you squint, a little bit sad, in the specific way that indoor climate-controlled leisure always is.

## Laos: The Market Is the Economy, and the Economy Knows You're a Tourist

Laos does not have a mall culture to speak of, and this is not a gap waiting to be filled — it's a structural feature of an economy where an enormous share of retail, particularly outside Vientiane, still runs through open markets, family-run shopfronts, and roadside stalls that open before dawn and close by mid-afternoon. The *talat sao*, the morning market, is less a shopping destination than a daily ritual that most of the country still organizes its life around, and if you show up expecting the leisurely 11am browse you'd do at a Western farmer's market, you will find half the good stalls already sold out and packed up.

Bargaining here is real, but it is also wildly overstated by guidebooks that make it sound like a blood sport. The actual etiquette is closer to a gentle, good-humoured negotiation over amounts that, converted to your home currency, are almost never worth the emotional energy foreigners bring to them. I have watched a backpacker spend four minutes fighting for the equivalent of twenty cents off a scarf while the vendor looked increasingly amused rather than adversarial — she wasn't going to lose the sale either way, she was just enjoying the theatre. Learn a few words of Lao, smile, offer a number that isn't insulting, and you'll get a fair price and a much better interaction than the haggling-as-combat approach produces.

Vientiane does have supermarkets now — a handful of South Korean and Thai-owned chains have moved in, selling imported goods at prices that make expats wince and locals mostly ignore. They exist primarily for foreigners and the small but growing Lao middle class chasing convenience, and they have exactly none of the social texture of the market. You go there for pasta and sunscreen, not for an experience. The real shopping culture — the one that feeds the country — still runs on cash, relationships, and vendors who remember your face by your third visit and adjust the price accordingly, usually in your favour.

## The Verdict

Chile wins on convenience and loses on soul. It has built an extremely efficient consumer economy that occasionally forgets shopping used to be a form of neighbourhood contact rather than a loyalty-points optimization exercise. Laos has the opposite problem in the opposite direction — the market experience is rich, human, and occasionally maddening if you need something specific at 3pm on a Tuesday and every stall has already gone home.

If I have to pick a winner, it's Laos, and it isn't close. A morning market where the vendor remembers you beats a mall food court every single time, and Chile's own citizens seem to half-agree with me — otherwise why would they keep showing up to the feria at 7am when the supermarket is air-conditioned and five minutes away? Convenience is not the same as satisfaction, and only one of these countries has fully realized that yet.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Santiago forum — a Belgian expat wrote that she didn't understand why her Chilean neighbours drove twenty minutes to a specific feria on a specific day rather than the supermarket two blocks away, until she tried the tomatoes and understood immediately.*

> *Internations Vientiane — one member described the morning market as "the only alarm clock that's ever actually worked" for him, since missing the 6am window meant an entire day of substandard vegetables.*

> *Quora — a longtime Vientiane resident answering "do you have to bargain at every market" noted that over-negotiating with vendors selling genuinely subsistence-level goods is regarded locally as more embarrassing for the tourist than for the seller.*

## Conclusion

Neither country is going to change for you, and that's the point. Chile will keep swiping your loyalty card while quietly mourning the feria it still can't quite give up, and Laos will keep opening its markets before you've had coffee, indifferent to whether you've adjusted to the time zone. If you want retail therapy with air conditioning, Santiago will oblige. If you want to remember that shopping used to mean something before it meant an app, get up before dawn in Vientiane and go stand in front of a woman with a scale. She will not care about your loyalty points. She will, however, remember your face.
