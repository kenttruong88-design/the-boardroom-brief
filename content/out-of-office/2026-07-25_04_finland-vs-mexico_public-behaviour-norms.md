# One Nation Queues in Total Silence, the Other Queues by Asking Who's Last

🇫🇮 Finland vs 🇲🇽 Mexico

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There's a photograph, beloved by every expat forum on earth, of Finns waiting at a bus stop spaced exactly two metres apart, in total silence, each apparently alone despite being clearly part of the same queue. I've stood in that queue. It's real, it's glorious, and it taught me more about Finnish public life than any guidebook could. Then I moved to Mexico City, where the first thing anyone taught me was to shout "¿quién es el último?" into a crowd to find my place in an invisible, noisy, entirely functional line.

Both systems produce order. One does it through silence and distance, the other through a kind of loud, communal choreography that looks like chaos until you realise everyone already knows exactly where they stand.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇫🇮 Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep a generous arm's length in any queue or public space | Make small talk with strangers unprompted |
| Speak quietly on public transport | Stand too close behind someone, even in a rush |
| Respect silence as a valid, comfortable social state | Assume silence means someone is upset |
| Wait your turn without asking who's next — order is assumed | Raise your voice in public, even when excited |

### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask "¿quién es el último?" to find your place in line | Assume a queue looks like an orderly single file |
| Expect physical closeness as normal, even among strangers | Take offense at loud conversation or laughter in public |
| Greet shopkeepers and neighbours warmly, even briefly | Be cold or overly formal — it reads as unfriendly |
| Embrace noise as a sign of life, not disorder | Stand rigidly apart from people in a crowd |

## Finland: Silence Is the Highest Form of Respect

Finnish public behaviour operates on a principle so foreign to much of the world it needs repeating slowly: distance and silence are not coldness, they are courtesy. Standing close to someone at a bus stop isn't neutral in Finland, it's an intrusion. The famous two-metre buffer isn't a stereotype exaggerated for laughs — it's a genuinely observed social contract, and violating it, even accidentally, produces a very particular Finnish discomfort that manifests as someone quietly shuffling further away without a word.

Queues in Finland don't need policing because nobody would dream of jumping one. Order is simply assumed, and the assumption holds because everyone shares it completely. There's no need to ask who's last, because the line itself, silent and single-file, makes the answer self-evident. This extends to public transport, where conversations are kept to a murmur and phone calls are, ideally, not taken at all.

What surprises newcomers most is how this reserve isn't hostility — it's actually a profound form of respect. Finns believe fiercely in not imposing on others, in giving people the space to exist undisturbed. Once you understand that silence is a gift rather than a rebuff, Finnish public life stops feeling cold and starts feeling like the most considerate system you've ever lived inside.

The sauna, unsurprisingly, is where all of this briefly dissolves — shared silence becomes shared comfort, and some of the most genuine Finnish friendships are built without a word being exchanged for the better part of an hour. It's proof that Finnish reserve isn't an absence of warmth, just warmth expressed through restraint rather than volume.

## Mexico: Noise Is Community, and Silence Is Suspicious

Mexican public life runs on the opposite assumption entirely: that proximity, volume, and constant social contact are signs of a functioning, healthy community, not a breach of anyone's personal space. Queues here look nothing like Finland's silent single file — they're loose, informal clusters where order is maintained through the simple, effective ritual of asking who arrived last. Once you've asked and been answered, your place is secure, and nobody will challenge it, however chaotic the crowd looks from the outside.

Noise is everywhere and it's not considered a problem to be solved. Mariachi drifting from a nearby plaza, family arguments conducted at full volume from an open window, vendors calling out their wares — this is simply the soundtrack of shared public life, and Mexicans generally find genuine silence a little unsettling, even suspicious. A quiet room reads as a room where something's wrong.

Physical closeness follows the same logic. Personal space, as Finns understand it, barely exists as a concept — greetings often involve a hand on the shoulder or a light touch on the arm, even between relative strangers, and standing close in a crowd is simply how crowds work. Warmth is expressed through contact and volume, not through careful distance.

What takes longest to adjust to isn't the noise itself but the underlying assumption behind it: that a lively, loud public space is a sign of trust and safety, not a lack of consideration. Once that clicks, Mexico's apparent chaos reveals itself as a deeply functional, deeply human social order — just one running on completely different rules.

## The Verdict

Finland offers a public life built on respect through restraint; Mexico offers one built on connection through contact. Neither is more civilised than the other — they're simply optimised for different things. Finland optimises for individual peace. Mexico optimises for collective warmth.

I've genuinely loved both, for entirely different reasons and often on the same difficult week. Finland's silence let me think clearly for the first time in years. Mexico's noise reminded me I wasn't alone in a city of millions. Suki's verdict: if your nervous system craves order, Finland will never once overwhelm you. If your nervous system craves connection, Mexico will never once let you feel invisible — and I've needed rescuing by both, at different points in my very disorganised life.

## Conclusion

Finland teaches you that silence can be the deepest form of consideration. Mexico teaches you that noise can be the warmest form of belonging. Move to Helsinki and you'll learn to treasure two metres of empty space like a small luxury. Move to Mexico City and you'll learn that a crowded, noisy room is where you're safest, not least. Whichever you choose, don't bring your old country's assumptions with you — they will not survive contact with either queue.
