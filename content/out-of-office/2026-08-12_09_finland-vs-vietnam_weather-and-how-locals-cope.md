# Finland Survives by Turning the Lights On. Vietnam Survives by Slowing Everything Down.

🇫🇮 Finland vs 🇻🇳 Vietnam — Weather and How Locals Cope

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Finland spends roughly two months a year in near-total darkness and treats this as a minor scheduling inconvenience to be managed with light therapy lamps and sheer collective willpower. Vietnam spends most of the year in punishing heat and humidity and treats this as an argument for doing absolutely everything slower, earlier, or under a fan, preferably all three at once.

Both nations have built entire daily rhythms around weather that would flatten most other cultures. Finns emerge from winter looking faintly triumphant, like they've personally defeated something. Vietnamese people simply route around the heat with a practical elegance that makes the rest of the world's air-conditioned complaining look a bit soft.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇫🇮 Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in a proper light therapy lamp before your first kaamos (polar night) hits | Skip vitamin D supplements through winter; deficiency is a genuine national concern |
| Embrace the sauna as a real coping mechanism, not just a novelty | Stay indoors all winter out of dread; Finns actively go outside, dressed properly, regardless of darkness |
| Layer properly with wool and windproof outer shells — cotton alone is a rookie mistake | Underestimate how disorienting weeks of near-total darkness genuinely feel on your mood |

### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shift your schedule earlier — mornings are for errands and exercise before the heat peaks | Plan strenuous activity for midday; locals actively avoid it and so should you |
| Carry a small towel and stay near shade or fans between tasks, like everyone else does | Wear heavy dark clothing; light, breathable fabric is the local norm for good reason |
| Rest during the early afternoon lull, especially in the south — it's a genuine cultural rhythm, not laziness | Assume the same coping strategy works in Hanoi's cold, damp winters and Saigon's year-round heat — they differ significantly |

## Finland: Manufacturing Light Out of Sheer Determination

Finnish winter isn't just cold, it's dark in a way that genuinely disorients anyone who hasn't experienced it — in the far north, the sun simply doesn't rise for weeks during kaamos, the polar night, and even in Helsinki, daylight can shrink to a grim five or six hours at the depths of December. This isn't background trivia. It shapes daily life completely, and Finns have developed an entire, largely unspoken toolkit for managing it.

Light therapy lamps are common enough that they're practically furniture in Finnish homes and offices — sitting in front of one for twenty minutes each morning is treated with the same unremarkable seriousness as brushing your teeth. Vitamin D supplementation is near-universal, recommended by health authorities and taken without a second thought, because the alternative is a population-wide slide into seasonal low mood that Finland has decided, quite sensibly, to actively prevent rather than simply endure.

The sauna deserves its reputation as more than a cultural quirk here — it's genuine physical and psychological infrastructure, a warm, private, screen-free space that Finns retreat to multiple times a week through the darkest months, and the ritual of heat followed by cold plunge is taken seriously as both physical and mental maintenance. Skipping sauna season is, for a Finn, roughly equivalent to skipping sleep.

What surprises visitors most is that Finns don't simply hide indoors waiting for spring. They go outside deliberately, dressed in serious wool layers and windproof shells, for walks, cross-country skiing, and ice swimming, treating the darkness as an obstacle to route around rather than a reason to disappear entirely. There's a quiet, stubborn refusal to let the weather win, expressed not through complaint but through sheer, well-equipped persistence.

## Vietnam: Building the Day Around the Heat

Vietnam's climate poses the opposite problem entirely — relentless heat and humidity across most of the country for much of the year, compounded by a genuinely brutal monsoon season, and the local response has been to restructure the entire daily schedule around avoiding the worst of it rather than fighting through it.

Mornings start early, often before dawn, precisely because the window between sunrise and the heat's real onset is when errands, exercise, and market shopping actually happen. Walk through Hanoi or Saigon at 6am and you'll find genuine bustling activity — group exercise sessions in parks, markets in full swing, motorbikes already moving — because everyone has collectively agreed that this is the usable part of the day, and the middle of it is not.

The early afternoon lull that follows is real, culturally embedded, and not remotely a sign of laziness, whatever a rushed foreign visitor might initially assume. Shops close, activity drops, and locals rest or retreat into shade during the hottest hours, a rhythm that makes complete physiological sense in humidity that can make even standing still feel like exertion. Fighting this rhythm as a newcomer, insisting on a Western midday schedule, just means fighting the actual climate on its worst terms.

Clothing choices follow the same practical logic — light, breathable, often long-sleeved fabric to protect from sun rather than heavy dark clothing that traps heat, and the ubiquitous small towel carried by nearly everyone isn't a quirky accessory, it's a genuinely essential tool for managing constant humidity. It's worth noting real regional variation too: Hanoi's winters bring a damp, penetrating cold that surprises visitors expecting tropical heat year-round, while Saigon stays warm and humid almost constantly, and locals in each city have developed distinctly different coping rhythms as a result.

## The Verdict

Finland copes with weather by manufacturing what's missing — light, warmth, structure — through deliberate, well-funded national effort. Vietnam copes by bending the entire day around what's already there, working with the heat's rhythm rather than against it. Finland's approach is expensive and technological. Vietnam's is ancient and endlessly practical. Both work remarkably well. Only one of them requires you to buy a lamp that mimics a sun you haven't seen in six weeks.

## Conclusion

Neither Finnish darkness nor Vietnamese heat is something you truly overcome — you simply learn, eventually, to build your life around it the way locals already have. Finland will teach you to manufacture your own daylight and take your sauna seriously. Vietnam will teach you that the productive day is shorter than you think, and that resting through the worst hours isn't weakness, it's the entire strategy. Ignore either lesson and the climate will simply outlast you. It always does.
