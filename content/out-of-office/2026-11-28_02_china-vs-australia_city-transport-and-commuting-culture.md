# China Moves Forty Million People by Lunch, Australia Moves Nobody Before the Timetable Says So

🇨🇳 China vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two kinds of commuter in the world. One kind is swept along by a system so large and so relentless that individual will is a rumour. The other kind stands on a platform in a suburb, watching a digital board that says the train is "approaching" and has been approaching since before the Olympics. China produces the first kind. Australia produces the second, with excellent coffee.

I have commuted on both sides of this divide, and the difference is not merely speed. It is a different theory of what a city owes you. China's cities say, "We will move you, cheaply and constantly, and you will not be consulted." Australia's say, "We will move you when we can, and please tap on and off properly."

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇨🇳 China ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Set up a mobile payment or transit app before you ride | Arrive at a gate with only a pocket of coins |
| Check which exit you need before you leave the platform | Surface at random and hope for a landmark |
| Move with the flow at the interchange | Stop dead in the middle of a transfer corridor |
| Keep your bag in front of you in a rush hour carriage | Expect a gap to open up when the doors do |

| 🇦🇺 Australia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Tap on and tap off with your transit card or phone | Forget to tap off and then wonder about the fare |
| Stand on the left of an escalator and let people pass | Block the right side with your luggage |
| Check the live app for cancellations before leaving | Trust the printed timetable on the stop |
| Say thank you to the bus driver when you get off | Stay silent and stare at your phone |

## 🇨🇳 China

Let me start with the thing that humbles every expat. China's big-city metro systems are, by scale, among the largest on earth, and they keep growing. Beijing and Shanghai alone run networks that dwarf most Western capitals, with trains every few minutes, clean platforms and fares that make you wonder what everyone else is doing with their taxes.

The system runs on your phone. Transit apps and mobile payments handle fares, and visitors are usually advised to set up an app or a card before they need it. Once you are in, signs are bilingual in the main cities, announcements are clear, and the logic of the network is easy to learn. The hard part is the human wave at rush hour.

Rush hour in a Chinese megacity is a lesson in compression. Platforms have lines painted on the floor, staff with flags and loudspeakers, and a collective discipline that is more impressive than it looks. Then the doors open and the discipline dissolves into elbows. Offer politeness at your own risk, and carry your backpack in front of your chest.

Above ground, buses, bike shares and cheap taxis fill the gaps, and high speed rail links cities at a pace that makes Australian domestic flights look like a sulk. The cost is part of the charm: most trips are a few yuan, which turns commuting from a budget item into a rounding error.

Expats complain about the crowds, the security checks at stations and the language on a bus with no English signage. These are fair complaints, and none of them has ever made me want to drive. When a city gives you a train every couple of minutes, you forgive a great deal.

Delivery and last-mile culture add to the picture. Electric scooters, shared bikes and ride-hailing apps are everywhere in Chinese cities, and a short hop from the station to the office is solved in seconds for pennies. The cost of that convenience is a kerb full of parked bikes and the occasional silent scooter at your heel, so keep your ears open and your elbows ready.

## 🇦🇺 Australia

Australian cities are built for the car and apologise for it in the timetable. Sydney, Melbourne, Brisbane and Perth all run rail, buses and ferries or trams, and the systems are clean, safe and perfectly civilised. They are also thin on frequency once you leave the core, so the suburbs run on a rhythm of one train every fifteen to thirty minutes and a fervent belief in the car park.

Payment is by transit card or contactless, depending on the city, and every state has its own scheme, which means a new arrival learns the local rules from scratch. In Sydney you tap with Opal, in Victoria with myki, and nobody on the platform will explain which is which. Forgetting to tap off is the classic rookie error, and the fare system will let you know.

The social etiquette is gentle. You queue, you stand to one side, you thank the driver, and you pretend not to hear the phone call next to you. There is an unspoken rule that quiet carriages exist even where nobody has designated one. It is a civilised experience until the day of a strike or a track closure, when the replacement bus becomes a lifestyle.

Cycling and walking are growing, particularly in the inner suburbs, but the weather and the distances keep most people in their cars. Expats from Asia tend to be shocked by two facts: the cost of a short taxi ride and the number of people who describe a forty minute commute as normal.

The honest truth about Australian transport is that it works when it works and reminds you of how far it has to go when it does not. The apps are good, the staff are decent, and the sunshine does a lot of heavy lifting.

Distance changes everything in Australia. Cities sprawl across enormous suburbs, and a commute of an hour each way is not unusual in Sydney or Melbourne. Many workers drive to a park and ride station and finish the trip by train, and the local lore around toll roads, parking costs and peak hour congestion is a national conversation. An expat quickly learns that where you live decides how you travel.

## The Verdict

China wins on speed, price and sheer ambition, and it is not close. A country that builds metro lines like other countries build shopping centres has a rare grasp of what a city is for. If your job is to get somewhere on time, in a clean carriage, for pocket change, Beijing or Shanghai will beat Sydney on every count but personal space.

Australia wins on comfort, courtesy and the dignity of a seat you can actually reach. The system is slower and the network thinner, but you are rarely pressed against a stranger's elbow, and the staff speak to you like a person rather than a flow rate.

If I had to commute for a living, I would take China on a weekday and Australia on a Sunday. One gets me to work, the other gets me to the beach, and only one of them is capable of making me late for the other.

The sting is that both countries think they have solved transport. China has solved the moving of crowds and Australia has solved the moving of individuals, and neither has solved the problem of what to do when the other is better at something.

Neither country is standing still. China keeps adding lines, and Australia keeps adding rail projects, each at a different tempo and a very different price. The honest comparison is that one is a sprint and the other a negotiated stroll, and a good commuter in either country has simply learned to read the pace.

## Conclusion

Commuting is a rehearsal for the whole country. China teaches you to move with the crowd and trust the system, Australia teaches you to wait calmly and trust the driver, and neither is ever quite what the brochure promised.

My advice is to learn the payment app before you land, learn the etiquette within a week, and learn to carry a book for the other kind of delay. In Beijing it will be the crowd, in Sydney it will be the signal fault, and in both it will be perfectly ordinary.

And please, for the sake of everyone behind you, stand on the correct side of the escalator. It is the closest thing the world has to a universal law, and you will be judged.
