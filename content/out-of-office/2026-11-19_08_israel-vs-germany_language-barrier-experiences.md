# Israel Will Argue With You in Four Languages, and Germany Will Politely Answer in Yours

🇮🇱 Israel · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Language barriers are usually described as a wall. This is wrong. A wall is honest. A language barrier is a revolving door that lets you in on the first floor, spins you round on the second and deposits you back on the pavement still holding a form. Israel and Germany are two of the best places on earth to experience this, and what makes them interesting is that the barrier has entirely different personalities.

Israel is a four-language country, Hebrew, Levantine Arabic, Russian and English, in which everyone is certain they speak the second one and rather fewer actually do. Germany is a country where the English is so good that learning German becomes a moral choice rather than a practical one, and where the forms, the pharmacy labels and the washing-machine dials will remind you of that choice every morning. One country makes you feel ignorant. The other makes you feel optional.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇮🇱 Israel**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the Hebrew alphabet early; being unable to read signs is deeply frustrating | Assume Tel Aviv English will carry you through every phone call |
| Look into ulpan, the subsidised Hebrew courses for new immigrants | Take one forum's ulpan price as gospel |
| Expect different languages in different regions; the north mixes Hebrew, Arabic and Russian | Assume every Israeli who says they speak English does |
| Budget around a year for basic conversation | Expect fluency outside Tel Aviv in a few months |

**🇩🇪 Germany**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your visa's language requirement; Blue Card needs none, others vary | Assume English is enough for every route |
| Take a recognised certificate from Goethe-Institut, telc, ÖSD, TestDaF, ECL, DSD or DSH | Treat any online quiz as proof of level |
| Expect documents by post and office visits in person | Assume you can handle it all online |
| Keep practising even when locals switch to English | Let the English-switch be the end of your German |

## 🇮🇱 Israel: The Four-Language Argument

Israel does not hide the language problem, it shouts it. The expat guides describe Hebrew as nearly indispensable for getting the most out of life, with Arabic holding special legal standing, and the forum record paints a country in which even basic Hebrew transforms mundane errands like sending a parcel or buying a bus pass. One poster estimated that the north of the country is roughly 40 per cent Hebrew, 30 per cent Arabic and 20 per cent Russian, which tells you that "Israel" is a collection of overlapping conversations rather than a single one.

The first obstacle is the script. A poster named Letitiat, newly arrived in Tel Aviv with her husband knowing only the alphabet and a few greetings, says that being unable to read signs or follow announcements is more frustrating than her other complaints. Another poster describes the problem as not being able to link a spoken word to its written form, and estimates a year before basic conversation is possible. Another reports that reaching everyday competence outside Tel Aviv takes "a year or more". That is a realistic timeline and an unhappy one.

The institutional answer is ulpan, the government-subsidised Hebrew course for new immigrants, with private language schools serving expats who don't qualify. A forum poster claims four days a week for a few hours at about NIS 1,200 for two months, and believes it is free for immigrants; treat that as an unverified claim, not a price list. Intensive ulpans exist in most cities, and the named resources include Duolingo, Memrise, Rutledge coursebooks and Ulpan Akiva in Netanya.

The most useful forum observation is a practical one: you can often get by without Hebrew in Tel Aviv, but calling companies is hard even with basic Hebrew, and many Israelis either don't speak English or overstate their skill. Another expat says everyday tasks are hard because many residents don't speak English. The honest summary is that Tel Aviv is a bubble and the bubble has a wall, and the wall appears at the exact moment you need a plumber.

## 🇩🇪 Germany: The Courteous Cul-de-Sac

Germany's language story begins with a paradox. The visa system is surprisingly forgiving: no German is required for the Blue Card, for skilled workers, for IT specialists or for self-employment. The Opportunity Card asks for A1 German or B2 English. Qualification recognition wants A2, vocational training B1, study usually B2, and healthcare B1 or B2 depending on the state. Accepted certificates come from Goethe-Institut, telc, ÖSD, TestDaF, ECL, DSD and DSH, measured against the six CEFR levels from A1 to C2. It is an orderly ladder, and it leads precisely nowhere if you do not climb it.

Because once you arrive, the practical pressure is of a different kind. A first-person essay by an American in Berlin reports that all important documents are sent by post, that you must visit offices in person, and that appointments for address registration and residence permits can take months to secure, with slots vanishing so fast that people refresh booking pages each morning. The same author notes that most Berliners speak English, so there is little pressure to learn German; locals switch to English the moment she tries German, and after a year her German had improved little. German labels at the pharmacy, supermarket and on washing machines also slow daily life.

I want to be careful here: I could not find a source confirming that immigration offices insist on German-language forms, so I will not tell you they do. What I can say is what the essay and the forum pages say. The expat page for Berlin notes that many expats find the German language barrier challenging, and little more. The barrier is real, but it is more social than legal. You are never refused. You are politely redirected into English and quietly excluded from the conversation that follows.

That, to me, is the cruellest form. In Israel, the barrier is loud and tells you where you stand. In Germany, it is courteous and tells you nothing. You can live in Berlin for years in English, have a perfectly pleasant life and never be asked what you think in German. It is a lovely country with a back door that stays locked unless you knock in the right grammar.

## The Verdict

Israel wins, because its barrier is honest. You know where the wall is, you know what it costs, and if you invest a year and a few hundred shekels in an ulpan the country opens up like a warm oven. Hebrew is difficult, the alphabet is humbling and the phone calls are terrible, but the reward for effort is visible and fast.

Germany is the harder case, because it lets you off the hook. The English is excellent, the visa rules are lenient, and the incentive to learn is therefore entirely internal. The people who learn German are the ones who decide to, not the ones who must. If you are that person, you will be rewarded with a country that takes you seriously. If you are not, you will be rewarded with a very comfortable exile.

My sting: Israel makes you learn the language because it needs you to. Germany lets you skip it because it doesn't mind. Only one of those is a compliment, and it isn't the one you'd like.

## Conclusion

I have been misunderstood in more languages than I have ordered dinner in, and the pattern is clear: a language barrier is a negotiation about how much of yourself the country wants to see. Israel asks for a year and gives you a seat at the table. Germany asks for nothing and gives you a very nice chair in the corridor.

Learn the alphabet in Israel. Learn the dative in Germany. And in both, treat every switch to English as a kindness you should politely decline.
