# Sweden Will Hand You a Ticket Number Before You're Allowed to Queue. Nigeria Will Just Let the Queue Happen to You, Loudly, From Several Directions at Once.

🇳🇬 Nigeria 🤝 🇸🇪 Sweden
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Lagos is loud in a way that has become, genuinely, a governance problem — authorities have at various points temporarily shut down churches and mosques over noise-pollution enforcement, which tells you everything about the scale of the issue and the lateness of the response. Personal space, in the sense a Scandinavian understands it, barely exists as a concept in daily Lagos public life: markets, traffic, queues (such as they are) all operate on a shared assumption that proximity, volume, and a certain amount of elbow are simply how public space functions.

Sweden has engineered the opposite system so thoroughly it borders on parody to an outsider: numbered ticket machines exist in pharmacies, banks, and delis specifically so that nobody has to stand in a queue close enough to another human to risk discomfort. "Lagom" — roughly, "just the right amount," never too much — isn't just a lifestyle aesthetic Scandinavians export via minimalist furniture; it's a genuine behavioural norm governing how loud you speak, how close you stand, and how much space you take up on a train. One country treats public space as a shared, noisy commons. The other treats it as a resource to be rationed in exact, silent portions.

## Nigeria, Deep Dive

The Lagos noise situation is serious enough that it's become an actual enforcement story, not just an expat observation: wire reporting has covered authorities temporarily shutting down churches and mosques specifically over noise-pollution complaints, in a city where religious gatherings, market commerce, generators (filling in for an unreliable power grid), and traffic all compete for the same acoustic space pretty much around the clock. The Nigerian Lawyer's own legal commentary on "the menace of noise pollution" makes clear this is understood locally as a genuine governance failure — rules exist, enforcement lags badly, and the gap is a live public conversation, not a secret.

What's genuinely useful for a newcomer to understand is that this isn't a lack of order so much as a different allocation of order. Queues in markets and at transit stops often look chaotic to someone expecting British- or Scandinavian-style single-file discipline, but there's an internal logic of assertiveness, visibility, and holding ground that locals navigate fluently — stepping back politely, the instinct many newcomers default to, often just costs you your place with nobody intending any unfairness by it. BusinessDay's own Life & Arts section has run self-reflective pieces, written by Nigerians, asking candidly what other nations do better on public order — a healthy sign that this is understood internally as a real, ongoing project rather than something only outsiders notice.

Personal space in crowded public settings — markets, public transport (danfos, BRT queues), street commerce — runs much tighter than Scandinavian or even typical Western norms. Close physical proximity during a transaction or a queue isn't an imposition; it's simply how shared space functions when a huge amount of commerce and movement happens informally, outdoors, at volume. Hawkers working traffic jams, conductors calling out routes over each other, and generators running outside shops to cover power cuts all layer onto the same soundscape simultaneously, and locals navigate it by tuning selectively rather than by expecting the volume itself to drop.

## Sweden, Deep Dive

Sweden has built an entire micro-infrastructure around avoiding the ambiguity, proximity, and noise that Nigerian public space absorbs as background texture. The numbered-ticket system — kölapp machines in pharmacies, banks, delicatessens, even some government offices — exists specifically so that nobody has to stand close enough to another person, for long enough, to generate the discomfort an unmanaged queue might otherwise produce. StudyInSweden's own newcomer orientation guide treats this as close to essential survival information, not a quirky cultural footnote.

"Lagom" is the organizing concept behind almost all of it: not too much, not too little, exactly enough — applied to how loudly you speak on a train, how close you sit to a stranger on a near-empty bus (take the next seat, not the adjacent one — ExpatExchange's Sweden faux-pas guide calls this out specifically), and how much space your voice and body are entitled to occupy in a shared public setting. This isn't coldness, and locals bristle, mildly, at the outsider assumption that it is — it's a consistently applied value about proportion, extended to volume and physical space as much as to consumption or ambition. The same logic governs queueing without a ticket machine present too — at a bus stop or outside a shop, Swedes will form an orderly, evenly-spaced line on instinct, with no signage or enforcement required, simply because the expectation is so deeply internalised that policing it would be redundant.

The practical trap for a newcomer coming from a louder, closer-contact culture is mistaking Swedish public restraint for unfriendliness, when it's better understood as a shared agreement to minimise unnecessary social friction by keeping a (literal and acoustic) buffer around everyone, always, as a matter of basic public courtesy rather than personal distance.

## The Verdict

Nigeria's public space runs hot, loud, and close, with order that exists but has to be read rather than seen — and the country itself is actively, visibly debating how to fix the parts that have genuinely gone too far (hence the mosque and church shutdowns). Sweden's public space runs cool, quiet, and spaced, engineered almost mechanically to prevent the friction Lagos simply absorbs. Neither is "ruder" than the other by any honest measure — they've just made opposite bets about whether shared space should feel communal and loud, or private and silent, even when you're standing in the same queue as everyone else.

## Conclusion

What you're actually choosing between, underneath the queue etiquette and the decibel levels, is two entirely different theories of what public space is for. Nigeria treats it as a shared, negotiated, noisy commons that everyone has an equal, assertive claim to. Sweden treats it as something closer to a shared resource to be rationed quietly, with a numbered ticket and an empty seat as the basic units of courtesy. Arrive expecting either one to resemble the other and you'll spend your first month either shouting into silence or apologising for existing too loudly. Learn the local operating system instead, and both, in their own way, start to make a strange kind of sense.
