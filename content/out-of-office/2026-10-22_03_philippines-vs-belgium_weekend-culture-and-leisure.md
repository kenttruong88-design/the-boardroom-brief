# One Country Throws a Party Every Weekend, the Other Country Closes the Shops

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇧🇪 Belgium

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is the first thing you learn about weekends once you've actually lived somewhere instead of visiting it with a lanyard and a return flight: nobody "relaxes" the same way twice. The Philippines treats the weekend as a civic festival with mandatory attendance, a riot of karaoke, lechon, and whichever saint's turn it is this month. Belgium treats the weekend as a quiet negotiation between a bicycle, a market stall, and the profound relief of every shop being legally required to shut so nobody has to pretend to want to buy anything.

I have lived in both. One of them left me hoarse from singing "My Way" at 1am to a crowd of people I'd met four hours earlier. The other left me standing outside a shuttered supermarket on a Sunday afternoon, car-free streets stretching out in every direction, wondering if the entire country had been raptured. Neither experience was relaxing in the way glossy travel brochures promise. Both were, in their own stubborn ways, the real thing — which is more than you can say for anyone who calls either country "exotic." It isn't. It's just somewhere else people live, on weekends they've organized with far more intention than you've ever put into yours.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Philippines:**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to the fiesta even if you weren't personally invited — the whole barangay is invited, that's the point | Decline food at a family gathering; refusal reads as an insult, not a diet choice |
| Learn at least one karaoke song you can commit to fully, badly, and loudly | Schedule anything important for a Sunday morning — Mass and family lunch outrank you |
| Bring a small gift (pasalubong) if you're joining someone else's family weekend | Assume "just family" means fewer than twenty people |
| Expect plans to shift by hours, not minutes — Filipino time is a feature, not a bug | Complain about the noise; next week it'll be your neighbor's turn to be loud |

**Belgium:**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy everything you need by Saturday evening — Sunday shopping is a myth in most towns | Expect a shop, bank, or even some pharmacies open on Sunday outside big-city exceptions |
| Get a bike; weekend cycling routes are a national pastime, not a niche hobby | Try to drive through central Brussels on a scheduled car-free Sunday |
| Linger at a café terrace for two hours over one coffee — nobody will rush you | Mistake quiet streets for a dead country; the market square is three blocks away and packed |
| Join a local market (Sunday brocantes and food markets are the real social hub) | Assume Flemish and Walloon weekend habits are identical; the regions diverge more than outsiders admit |

## Philippines

The Filipino weekend does not belong to the individual. It belongs to the barangay, the parish, and above all the family, and anyone who moves there expecting quiet, self-directed "me time" is going to have a difficult adjustment. According to Wikipedia's own exhaustive list of festivals in the Philippines, there is a fiesta happening somewhere in the archipelago on almost any given weekend of the year — each town celebrating its patron saint with processions, brass bands, street food, and enough noise to wake the dead, which is sort of the point, since several of these festivals exist to honor exactly that. Sun.Star's reporting on fiesta tradition frames it plainly: the fiesta is the organizing unit of community life, not an occasional indulgence. You don't attend a fiesta so much as get swept into it.

Sunday itself has its own gravitational pull. Cultural Atlas's breakdown of Filipino religion notes that Catholic Mass attendance remains deeply embedded in weekly life, and its section on family describes obligations to extended relatives — aunts, godparents, second cousins twice removed — as binding in a way most Western families would find suffocating and most Filipinos would find simply normal. Weekend lunch after Mass is not an invitation; it is an appointment you didn't know you'd accepted. Karaoke, meanwhile, is less a hobby than a competitive sport practiced at volumes that should require a permit — anyone who has lived near a Filipino household on a Saturday night already knows this is not hyperbole.

What outsiders misread as chaos is actually one of the most socially cohesive weekend cultures on earth. The fiesta and the family lunch exist to enforce belonging, loudly, repeatedly, and without an opt-out clause. It is exhausting. It is also, infuriatingly, one of the only weekend cultures I've lived in that nobody ever describes afterward as lonely.

There's also a logistics lesson buried in all this that most visiting consultants never learn: "Filipino time" isn't a punchline about tardiness, it's a scheduling philosophy that treats the plan as a suggestion and the gathering itself as the fixed point. A fiesta doesn't start on time because a fiesta doesn't have a start time — it has a direction, and everyone arrives whenever they arrive and gets absorbed regardless. Try explaining that to a German tourist with a laminated itinerary. I have. It did not go well, and it was, frankly, hilarious.

## Belgium

Belgium's weekend runs on an entirely different operating system: silence as infrastructure. Shop-opening-hours guides aimed at newcomers — Ghent's own expat leisure page among them — spell out, almost apologetically, that most retail simply does not open on Sundays outside a handful of tourist zones, a fact that catches nearly every new arrival off guard exactly once, usually while standing hungry outside a locked Carrefour. This isn't laziness; it's a deliberate civic choice that both protects workers' rest and manufactures an entire culture around not shopping.

What fills the vacuum is, somewhat magnificently, cycling and loitering — both elevated to near-ceremonial status. Brussels Times reporting on national mobility data shows bicycle use climbing steadily, with roughly a third of Belgians now cycling to work and weekend recreational cycling numbers in Flanders hitting record levels as e-bikes and new routes draw people out. Then there's Brussels' own official "Car-Free Sunday," documented by be.brussels, which periodically closes the entire city to traffic and hands the streets back to pedestrians, cyclists, and rollerbladers — an entire capital converted, for one day, into a very large, very orderly park.

And then there's the café terrace, the true seat of Belgian weekend government. The be.brussels "Brussels on Sunday" guidance gently steers visitors toward museums, markets, and long unhurried meals rather than shopping, because that's what the day is actually for. Add the weekend brocante (flea market) and the regional food market, and you get a culture that has essentially designed an entire day around doing nothing productive, extremely well, on principle. It is the most European thing I have ever witnessed, and I mean that as the compliment and the insult it simultaneously deserves.

Worth noting too: Belgium is not one weekend culture but at least two wearing the same passport. Flemish weekends lean toward cycling clubs, canal-side cafés, and an almost Dutch efficiency about leisure; Walloon weekends tilt slower, more market-and-conversation, more likely to end in a four-hour lunch nobody scheduled a follow-up to. Outsiders flatten this into one tidy "Belgian weekend," which is roughly as accurate as flattening Luzon and Mindanao into one undifferentiated fiesta. It isn't, and pretending otherwise just means you haven't stayed long enough to notice the seams.

## The Verdict

If you want a weekend that confirms you exist — loudly, repeatedly, surrounded by people who will remember your name and your second cousin's wedding — the Philippines wins without needing a recount. If you want a weekend that confirms civilization can occasionally shut up and sit down, Belgium wins by walkover. Neither is "relaxing" in the Instagram-caption sense; one just replaces stress with noise and the other replaces it with silence, and frankly silence is the harder sell to anyone under thirty.

But if I'm being honest, and I am always being honest because diplomacy is for people with something to lose, Belgium's weekend is the one that actually lets you breathe. The Philippines gives you belonging whether you want it or not. Belgium gives you a closed shop, a decent coffee, and the radical dignity of being left alone. I'll take the bicycle and the brocante. Fight me at the next fiesta.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Philippines — newcomers consistently underestimate how non-optional fiesta and family-lunch attendance really is; skipping one isn't seen as a scheduling choice, it's seen as a statement.*

> *Internations Brussels — expats repeatedly describe the shock of their first "forgot to shop Saturday" Sunday, arriving at a locked supermarket with nothing but a loaf of stale bread and a newfound respect for Belgian planning.*

> *expat.com Philippines forum — foreign partners folded into Filipino families describe weekend gatherings as warm but totalizing, where "just lunch" routinely means twenty relatives and zero exit before dark.*

## Conclusion

Neither of these countries is doing weekends "wrong," no matter how loudly my jetlagged brain insisted otherwise at 2am during a fiesta, or how smugly content I felt locked out of a Brussels Carrefour with nothing but a baguette and my own poor planning. The Philippines has built a weekend around never being alone, whether you like it or not. Belgium has built one around never being bothered, whether you like it or not. Both are coherent, both are intentional, and both will absolutely wreck your idea of a "day off" if you show up with Western-office-worker assumptions intact. So pack a karaoke song and a bicycle, leave your assumptions at customs, and stop calling either place exotic. It's just Tuesday somewhere, and somebody's weekend is already in full swing.
