# One Country Has Eleven Official Languages and Switches Between Four of Them Mid-Sentence. The Other Runs on Two Languages That Rarely Speak to Each Other at All.

🇿🇦 South Africa 🇫🇯 Fiji
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

South Africa constitutionally recognises eleven official languages, and in any genuinely mixed social setting you will hear several of them deployed within a single conversation, sometimes within a single sentence, a code-switching fluency most multilingual societies would consider showing off. Fiji's language landscape is quieter but just as structurally interesting: English, Fijian and Fiji Hindi coexist as the country's three major languages, but the Indo-Fijian and Indigenous Fijian communities who speak Hindi and Fijian respectively often live genuinely parallel linguistic lives, meeting primarily in English rather than in each other's mother tongue.

I have sat in a Johannesburg taxi where the driver fielded a phone call in Zulu, turned to greet a passenger in Afrikaans, and finished a sentence to me in English without missing a conversational beat. I have also sat in a Suva market stall where the Indo-Fijian vendor and the Indigenous Fijian customer conducted their entire transaction in English, a shared second language neither grew up speaking at home. Both countries are multilingual. Only one has made multilingualism into a genuine conversational sport.

## Do's & Don'ts: South Africa 🇿🇦

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn a handful of greeting phrases in whichever regional language dominates where you're living — Zulu, Xhosa, Afrikaans, Sotho — it's genuinely appreciated | Assume English alone covers you socially everywhere — it's an official and widely spoken language, but not everyone's first or most comfortable one |
| Get comfortable with casual code-switching in group settings — South Africans fluidly move between languages and won't always translate for you | Treat Afrikaans and English as interchangeable or politically neutral — the history and politics around language choice here run genuinely deep |
| Ask rather than assume which language someone prefers for a serious conversation — it signals real respect in a country still navigating its linguistic history | Be surprised when a conversation shifts languages specifically to exclude or include you — this happens, and it's rarely about rudeness |

## Do's & Don'ts: Fiji 🇫🇯

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Fijian greetings (bula!) regardless of who you're speaking to — it's the default warm, universal social opener | Assume Fiji Hindi and Fijian are mutually intelligible or interchangeable — they're entirely unrelated language families |
| Default to English in genuinely mixed Indigenous Fijian and Indo-Fijian company — it's the actual shared bridge language most Fijians use across communities | Assume the Indo-Fijian and Indigenous Fijian communities are linguistically or culturally the same — language is one of several real, distinct dividing lines |
| Notice and respect which language a specific community event or ceremony is conducted in — it carries real cultural weight either way | Expect widespread fluency in Hindi from Indigenous Fijians, or in Fijian from Indo-Fijians — genuine cross-fluency is less common than outsiders assume |

## South Africa: Eleven Languages, One Constant Negotiation

South Africa's constitution recognises eleven official languages — Zulu, Xhosa, Afrikaans, English, Sepedi, Tswana, Sotho, Tsonga, Swati, Venda and Ndebele — a structural acknowledgment of genuine linguistic diversity that few countries attempt at this scale. English functions as a practical lingua franca in business, media and government, but it's actually the home language of a relatively small minority of South Africans; Zulu and Xhosa each claim significantly more first-language speakers nationally, and regional dominance shifts dramatically depending on where in the country you are.

What makes daily linguistic life here genuinely distinctive is the fluid, constant code-switching that characterises ordinary mixed-company conversation. It's entirely normal to watch a single exchange move through English for a technical point, Zulu for an aside to a friend, and Afrikaans for a half-joke, often within the same two minutes, with speakers tracking and participating across all three without apparent effort. This isn't performative; it reflects genuinely overlapping, lived multilingual competence built from growing up in a society where monolingualism was never really an option for most people outside a narrow demographic.

The history underneath this fluency is not neutral, and foreigners who treat South African multilingualism as purely a charming cultural quirk miss something important. Afrikaans in particular carries a loaded political history tied to apartheid-era language policy, and language choice in certain contexts — which language a meeting is conducted in, which language a sign is printed in, which language a politician chooses for a speech — remains a genuinely contested, meaningful decision rather than a neutral logistical one. Navigating South African multilingualism well means understanding it's simultaneously a daily practical skill and an ongoing, serious national conversation about identity and power.

## Fiji: Two Communities, One Bridge Language

Fiji's linguistic landscape is structured around a different kind of complexity: not eleven languages jostling together, but two substantial, historically distinct communities — Indigenous Fijians and Indo-Fijians, descendants of indentured labourers brought from India under British colonial rule — whose respective languages, Fijian and Fiji Hindi, rarely function as each other's bridge. English, inherited from the same colonial period, ends up doing that bridging work instead, serving as the practical shared language across ethnic lines in business, education, government and increasingly casual daily interaction between the two communities.

This produces a quieter but genuinely notable language dynamic: rather than South Africa's constant, fluid multilingual code-switching within shared conversations, Fiji often features two communities running substantially parallel linguistic lives, each largely fluent in their own heritage language plus English, with comparatively limited cross-fluency into the other group's language. An Indigenous Fijian and an Indo-Fijian colleague might work together daily, entirely in English, without either developing meaningful fluency in the other's home language, a pattern that would look almost oddly formal by South African standards but is simply the unremarkable norm here.

Fijian itself carries deep cultural and ceremonial weight within Indigenous Fijian communities specifically — traditional ceremonies, village protocol, and the broader vanua (land and community) system are conducted in Fijian, and the language is inseparable from a wider set of customary practices that English, however practically dominant, doesn't touch. Fiji Hindi, meanwhile, has evolved its own distinct dialect over generations, meaningfully diverged from standard Hindi spoken in India, carrying its own literature, music and identity markers specific to the diaspora experience rather than a simple import. Both languages are living, evolving and culturally central to their respective communities; they simply don't need each other the way English needs to exist as the connective layer between them.

## The Verdict

South Africa wins on sheer linguistic dynamism — watching a fluent, constant, multi-language conversation unfold in real time is one of the more genuinely impressive social skills a society can collectively possess, loaded political history and all. But Fiji's quieter pattern reveals something South Africa's fluency can sometimes obscure: genuine multilingualism at the societal level doesn't automatically mean deep cross-community linguistic fluency at the individual level, and Fiji's two-community, English-bridged structure is arguably the more honest model of what actually happens when distinct communities share a small nation without fully merging. South Africa performs multilingualism brilliantly. Fiji quietly reveals its limits.

## Conclusion

South Africa's eleven official languages produce a society that treats fluid, constant linguistic switching as entirely unremarkable, a skill built from necessity and history in equal measure. Fiji's two major communities have settled into something calmer and more structurally separate, borrowing a colonial-era language to bridge a gap neither heritage tongue was ever going to close on its own. Both are working solutions to real linguistic complexity. Only one of them will leave you mildly dizzy trying to keep up with a single conversation.
