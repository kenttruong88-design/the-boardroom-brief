# One Country Loses the Sun for Months, the Other Has Entirely Too Much of It

🇸🇪 Sweden vs Morocco 🇲🇦
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Stockholm, I once experienced a December so dark that the sun, on the days it bothered to appear at all, rose around 9 a.m. and gave up by 3 p.m., a six-hour window of grudging grey light that Swedes navigated with a stoicism I found almost heroic. In Marrakech, I learned in my first July that "midday" isn't a time of day so much as a hazard to be actively planned around, and that any local caught outside between noon and four had clearly made poor life choices or had somewhere truly urgent to be.

Sweden's climate strips away light for months at a time and demands elaborate psychological infrastructure to survive the darkness intact. Morocco's climate delivers heat so aggressive in summer that entire daily rhythms reorganise around avoiding it. Both countries have built genuinely sophisticated cultural coping mechanisms around their respective extremes, and both will judge you, gently but firmly, for not adopting them fast enough.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇸🇪 Sweden**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in a proper light therapy lamp before your first winter | Skip the outdoor walk just because it's cold and dark — fresh air still matters |
| Embrace fika as genuine mental health maintenance, not just coffee | Assume you can power through winter without any daylight-mimicking routine |
| Buy real winter boots and layers, not fashion-first ones | Underestimate how early it gets dark from October onward |

**🇲🇦 Morocco**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Restructure your day around the heat — early starts, late evenings | Plan any strenuous outdoor activity between noon and 4 p.m. in summer |
| Follow the local siesta-adjacent slowdown in the early afternoon | Skimp on hydration and salt, especially if you're not used to dry heat |
| Dress in loose, breathable, covering fabrics rather than minimal ones | Assume air conditioning is standard everywhere — many older riads rely on thick walls instead |

## Sweden: Engineering Sanity Out of Darkness

Sweden's winter isn't just cold, it's dark in a way that genuinely reorganises daily life — in Stockholm, December days can offer as little as six hours of weak daylight, and further north in places like Kiruna, the sun doesn't rise above the horizon at all for weeks. This isn't a minor inconvenience, it's a real public health consideration, and Sweden has responded with a startlingly comprehensive cultural and institutional toolkit: light therapy lamps sold in ordinary pharmacies and recommended by ordinary doctors, workplace cultures that actively encourage a midday walk regardless of temperature, and a design aesthetic obsessed with candles, warm lighting, and cosy textiles precisely because the alternative is a home that feels as bleak as the sky outside.

Fika, the ritual coffee-and-pastry break woven into Swedish daily and work life, takes on a deeper function in winter than its cheerful reputation suggests — it's a scheduled, protected pause for connection and warmth that functions as genuine seasonal mental health infrastructure, not just a nice tradition. Swedes I've known treat their winter routines with a discipline that borders on ritual: the walk happens regardless of temperature, the lamp gets turned on first thing, the candles get lit the moment daylight fades, because the alternative — letting the darkness simply happen to you unmanaged — carries real psychological risk that everyone here seems to intuitively understand.

What surprises newcomers most is less the darkness itself than the sheer cultural normalisation of preparing for it. Nobody treats a light therapy lamp as an eccentric purchase. Nobody finds it odd that an office schedules its one shared social ritual around getting people fed, caffeinated, and briefly out of their own heads during the darkest months. It's simply survival, dressed up as cosiness, and it works.

## Morocco: Restructuring Life Around the Sun's Aggression

Moroccan summer heat, particularly inland in cities like Marrakech and Fes, regularly climbs past 40°C, and the local response isn't to fight it with air conditioning everywhere (many older medina buildings, riads especially, rely instead on centuries-old passive cooling — thick stone walls, interior courtyards, strategically placed fountains) but to simply reorganise the entire rhythm of the day around avoiding the worst of it. Shops and offices often shift their hours earlier and later, with a genuine, culturally sanctioned slowdown through the hottest stretch of early afternoon that functions much like a siesta even where it isn't formally named one.

The practical adaptations run deep and specific: loose, breathable, fully covering clothing turns out to be far more effective at heat management than minimal Western summer wear, a lesson every newcomer eventually learns the hard way after their first sunburned, dehydrated afternoon wandering a souk at 1 p.m. against every local's advice. Hydration culture here is serious and specific — mint tea, counterintuitively, is a genuine local cooling ritual, and older residents' insistence on salt and steady water intake through the day isn't folk wisdom, it's sound heat-management physiology that outsiders dismiss at their own risk.

Social life simply migrates to match the sun's schedule — evenings, once the heat breaks, come alive in a way mornings and early afternoons don't, with markets, cafés, and family gatherings stretching late into the night to reclaim the hours the heat had stolen earlier. Newcomers who insist on running their day by a Northern European clock — busy mornings, early nights — find themselves permanently out of sync with a country that has, quite sensibly, decided the sun doesn't get to dictate exhaustion on its own terms.

## The Verdict

Sweden's darkness is survived through deliberate, almost technical intervention — lamps, walks, scheduled warmth — applied with real institutional backing. Morocco's heat is survived through an older, more organic wisdom about simply not fighting the sun on its own schedule. I've found the Moroccan approach more instinctively appealing, there's something sane about a culture that just says "we'll live at night instead," but I've also never needed a light therapy lamp to get through a Marrakech July, and that tells you something too. Both climates will reorganise your entire relationship with daylight. Neither will negotiate.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/sweden — a first-winter newcomer describes buying a light therapy lamp on a Swedish coworker's direct, unembarrassed insistence, and being stunned at how normal and unremarkable the purchase was treated by everyone else in the office.*

> *Internations Marrakech — an expat recalls learning, after one disastrous midday walk in July, to simply plan their entire outdoor life around the hours before 11 a.m. and after 6 p.m., no exceptions.*

> *expat.com Morocco forum — a longtime resident explains that mint tea's reputation as a "cooling" drink sounded like nonsense until they actually tried it consistently through a full summer and noticed the difference.*

## Conclusion

Sweden will teach you that darkness is survivable with the right deliberate tools, applied consistently and without shame. Morocco will teach you that heat is survivable by simply refusing to argue with it and rearranging your entire day instead. Both countries have made peace with an extreme that would break most people, and both, frankly, do it with more grace than the weather deserves. I now own a light therapy lamp I use nowhere near Sweden, and I still instinctively nap through early afternoons wherever I am in the world. Consider your circadian rhythm fairly warned.
