# Jakarta Has a Park Target. Australia Has Parks, Barbecues and a Strong Opinion About Shade

🇮🇩 Indonesia vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Australia treats a park as a birthright: a green rectangle with a barbecue, a toilet block and a sign telling you what time the council will switch off the lights. Indonesia's capital treats green space as a precious, fought-over resource, a thing planned for and fretted about and rarely enough. The difference is not about attitudes towards trees. It is about how much concrete came first.

I have spent afternoons in parks across sixty countries, and the single best indicator of a city's mood is whether it lets you sit down without buying anything. Sydney does. Jakarta, in many places, asks you to find a mall. A source note: my retrievable pages are about Jakarta, not Indonesia as a whole, and not at all about Australia, so the Australian description is general knowledge.

## Indonesia

The numbers tell the story, and they are not flattering. An editorial in The Jakarta Post in June 2026 put green open space at about five to six percent of the capital, against a thirty percent target under a 2007 spatial planning law, and said reaching it would take nearly two hundred square kilometres more, when roughly ninety percent of land is already developed. A wire report the same month gave the figure as 5.59 percent as of April, against a twenty percent target in the same law, which tells you even the arithmetic is contested. The head of the city's parks agency said about 257 hectares had been added over the past year.

An urban planner interviewed in January 2026 said Jakarta's green open space is under fifteen percent by some measures, possibly under ten, and described the parks themselves. Taman Menteng is the busiest, with few covered seats. Taman Suropati is quiet, with no shade and nowhere nearby to park. At Lapangan Banteng, vendors are kept to the entrance. A Menteng visitor noted that most benches are open to the weather, and a canopy would help.

The social life of green space is therefore improvised. A 31-year-old office worker told the wire service that he meets friends at the Gelora Bung Karno complex for free, and added that many public spaces are located in malls, which means spending is expected. The Post's editorial proposes micro-parks on idle roadside lots, canal embankments and under flyovers, plus green walls, and argues that green access is a basic urban right.

The sting is the heat. A park without shade in an equatorial city is not a refuge but a test. The value of a green space there is measured in canopy, not acreage, and a bench in full sun is decoration.

The micro-park idea is more interesting than it first sounds. The Jakarta Post editorial proposes using idle roadside lots, canal embankments and the spaces under flyovers, plus green walls, which is a way of treating leftover land as civic assets. For a city with ninety percent of its land already developed, that is a realistic approach: you cannot build a Central Park, but you can plant a hundred small patches of shade. The test, as the planner interviewed in January pointed out, is whether those spaces are designed for use, with seating, canopy and sensible vendors, or merely for show. A park that looks good from a car window and cannot be sat in is an ornament, not a park.

## Australia

Australia's parks I know from general experience, not from a fetched source. The country's cities are built around generous public green space, from botanic gardens to coastal reserves to neighbourhood ovals, and the cultural expectation is that you can use them for free, at any hour, with minimal bureaucracy. Public barbecues, playgrounds, outdoor gyms, walking tracks and swimming areas are standard furniture of civic life.

What defines the culture is use. Parks are not scenery; they are the sitting room of the city. Weekends are for sport on the oval, birthday parties in the picnic shelter and cricket that nobody remembers starting. Dogs are walked in designated areas, joggers pass at dawn and the early-morning swim is practically a sacrament. A suburb without a decent park is considered underdeveloped.

The climate shapes it. The sun is fierce, the ultraviolet index is brutal and shade is a design value, so councils plant trees and build shelters accordingly. Seasonal fire restrictions can close barbecues and ban open flames, and signs are enforced with a gentle but unarguable firmness.

The sting is the sheer weight of rules about it all. You may not light a fire here, bring your dog there, or occupy the shelter without a booking. The Australian park is generous, but it is also administered, and the administration sometimes arrives with a clipboard.

Another contrast worth noting is who pays for it. In Australia, parks are paid for by councils and used as of right, which means a family with children can spend a whole Saturday in a park at no cost, and a company can host a team picnic with a free barbecue. In Jakarta, the worker quoted by the wire service says many public spaces are in malls, which turns leisure into spending. That is not a moral judgement, only an observation about the economics of space. If you are posted to the city, you will find free open space at Gelora Bung Karno and a number of parks worth discovering, but you will need to plan for them in advance.

## The Verdict

Australia wins on provision and Indonesia, specifically Jakarta, loses on it, and no amount of cultural charm changes the arithmetic. A city with five or six percent green space cannot compete with one built around parks. That is not a judgement on Indonesians, who make remarkable use of what they have, but on a planning history that let concrete get there first.

Where Jakarta earns respect is ingenuity. Free meeting spots, micro-park proposals and a public that treats every patch of shade as an opportunity all point to a city that wants more and is working for it. Australia's parks are a given; Jakarta's are a campaign.

For an expatriate with children, a dog or a running habit, the answer is obvious: Australia. For one interested in how people make public life out of scarcity, Jakarta is the more educational posting. Just pack a hat for both.

Finally, think about the daily routine rather than the weekend. In Sydney or Melbourne you can run before work in a shaded park, eat lunch on a lawn and walk the dog at dusk, all within a short distance of home. In Jakarta, the same routine takes planning: the best time is early morning, the best places are the ones with shade and the best companions are colleagues who know the hidden corners. It is a different relationship with the outdoors, and one that makes you value each patch of green properly.

## Conclusion

A park is a promise from a city to its people: you may sit here without paying. Australia keeps that promise at scale. Jakarta is still trying to afford it.

If you are posted to Sydney, use the barbecue and bring sunscreen. If you are posted to Jakarta, find the shade, learn the free spaces and, when you can, add your voice to the campaign for more. Green is not decoration. It is infrastructure.
