# India's Parks Exist Mostly on Paper. Botswana's Parks Are Real but an Hour's Drive From Your Actual Apartment.

🇮🇳 India vs 🇧🇼 Botswana
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

India has a national planning standard — the URDPFI Guidelines — recommending 10 to 12 square metres of open green space per resident. Trivandrum actually delivers 0.55. Botswana, meanwhile, protects a jaw-dropping 37% of its entire landmass as national park or game reserve, an area one tourism body compares to the size of New Zealand — and yet Gaborone, its own capital, was described by its national newspaper as having, in its own recreational facilities, "literally nothing."

Here is the pattern that took me fourteen relocations to notice: a country's relationship with nature on a map tells you almost nothing about whether you'll have anywhere decent to go for a walk on a Tuesday.

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Find your city's one reliable park loop and commit to it — Bangalore's Lalbagh and Ulsoor Lake are repeatedly named as dependable | Assume a "green" address on paper means a usable park nearby — some have been reduced to informal parking lots |
| Budget actual money for park access in some cities — Hyderabad's KBR Park charges enough to total roughly Rs 18,000 a year for a daily walker | Run outside a known park loop without expecting "a fair share of odd looks," per longtime running-forum advice |
| Expect clothing norms to be a live negotiation — shorts are becoming more common among runners, including locals, but full coverage is still the safer default | Expect free entry everywhere, or consistency between cities — fees range from nothing to real daily charges with no national standard |

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇧🇼 Botswana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat Kgale Hill, overlooking Gaborone Dam, as your de facto city park — an hour round trip, baboons included | Expect to walk to green space from your flat — nearly all of it requires a car to reach |
| Plan weekend trips to Mokolodi Nature Reserve or further out — this is where the real "nature access" pitch actually delivers | Confuse the country's 37% protected-land statistic with neighbourhood green space — it's almost entirely remote reserves, not city parks |

## India: Green on the Master Plan, Grey on the Ground

India's problem with parks isn't ambition — it's a planning document nobody enforces. The national URDPFI Guidelines from 2014 recommend 10 to 12 square metres of open space per person, a target most major cities fall catastrophically short of: Trivandrum manages just 0.55 square metres per person, Chennai 0.81, Amritsar 0.95, Mumbai 1.24 by its own draft Master Plan, Bangalore 2.01, Kanpur 3.76 — against WHO's own international benchmark of 9. A handful of outliers, like Varanasi at nearly 25 and Chandigarh at over 17, prove the gap is a choice, not an inevitability, which makes the shortfall in every other city feel less like scarcity and more like neglect.

What exists on the ground often doesn't match what exists on the record, either. In South Delhi's Katwaria Sarai, damaged park walls and gates have let residents drive cars inside and store household items in the remaining space, simply because the narrow internal streets can't fit cars anywhere else — a citywide survey found 56% of Delhi's 16,828 registered parks "dilapidated." Where parks are genuinely maintained, access can cost real money: Delhi's DDA briefly introduced entry fees at parks like the 36-acre Sector 16D Dwarka site before backing off under resident backlash, while Hyderabad's KBR Park charges Rs 50 a day or Rs 400 a month — a cost one regular walker calculated at roughly Rs 18,000 a year, prompting the entirely reasonable complaint: "Why should having to breathe fresh air cost so much?" Running itself is a spatial puzzle rather than a lifestyle choice — a long-running FlyerTalk thread on exercising in India flags that most Indian cities are poor for outdoor running "unless sticking to a proper park," naming Bangalore's Lalbagh and Ulsoor Lake as dependable while warning that Cubbon Park is "slowly disappearing," and that running outside a designated park loop in Delhi draws "a fair share of odd looks" regardless of your nationality. Clothing norms sit in a similar live negotiation — shorts are "becoming more common" among runners generally, including Indians, but full coverage remains the safer default outside beach towns.

What does survive, remarkably, is a real and committed morning-walk culture built almost in spite of the infrastructure rather than because of it. Outlets regularly cover organized "uncle" and "auntie" walker associations who petition municipal bodies over maintenance, and in Hyderabad specifically, that same community organized real pushback against the KBR Park fee — a grassroots green-space advocacy movement operating entirely around a broken system's edges, because nobody's waiting for the Master Plan to catch up.

## Botswana: Spectacular Wilderness, an Hour From Where You Actually Live

Botswana's green-space story inverts India's completely: the wilderness is real, vast, and seriously protected — roughly 37% of the entire country, an area the local tourism association compares to the size of New Zealand, spanning reserves like the Central Kalahari (52,800 square kilometres), the Kgalagadi Transfrontier Park, and the Okavango Delta. The problem is that almost none of it is actually inside the city where you'll be living. Gaborone's own municipal park infrastructure has been publicly, repeatedly criticized by the national press: a 2010 editorial in Mmegi called the city's recreational facilities "literally nothing," and described Tsholofelo Park — "the city's most popular park" — as neglected to the point of becoming a hangout associated with crime, only getting cleaned when booked for private events like weddings. A follow-up opinion piece a decade later, in 2020, was still arguing Gaborone needs its first genuinely public, free, well-maintained park, pointing at land already reserved in developments like the Botswana Housing Corporation's Phakalane estate that sits unused rather than becoming real city green space.

What fills the gap is Kgale Hill — a hill overlooking Gaborone Dam and a shopping mall, popular with walkers, joggers and picnickers on clearly marked trails, with baboon troops as the main visible wildlife, and a roughly one-hour round trip. A 2024 diplomatic post-report on Talesmag confirms the texture directly: "there are not many places around town that are good for running, but some people find a way," and accessibility is genuinely uneven, making a car practically essential even for outdoor recreation. For actual "nature access," the real answer is leaving the city entirely — the same report singles out Mokolodi Nature Reserve day trips and Khama Rhino Sanctuary camping as the real family outdoor routine, alongside weekend road trips to the Okavango Delta, Victoria Falls, Madikwe Game Reserve, and even Cape Town. Even the most commonly cited "accessible" nature spot, Mokolodi, is only 30 square kilometres — a cheetah-conservation-focused reserve, not an urban park substitute. A Reddit community FAQ for prospective movers frames Botswana's outdoor appeal almost entirely around this wilderness tourism — mokoro trips, elephant river cruises, the Makgadikgadi Pans — and notably never mentions in-city Gaborone green space at all, which is itself a telling absence from the people who'd know.

## The Verdict

India gives you a park on paper and, with real effort and a bit of luck, a decent loop to actually use near home. Botswana gives you an entire country that is functionally one enormous nature reserve, and almost nothing walkable once you're actually standing in its capital. If I want green space I can reach without starting a car, I'll take my chances with Bangalore's Lalbagh. If I want green space that will genuinely take my breath away, I'm booking a weekend in the Delta — just not expecting to find anything like it on my lunch break.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *FlyerTalk forum — paraphrased: posters warn that running outside a known park loop in Delhi draws "a fair share of odd looks," and that Cubbon Park in Bangalore is "slowly disappearing" even as a reliable option.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Gaborone (2024) — paraphrased: a posting family says good in-city running routes are scarce ("some people find a way"), but nature day-trips to Mokolodi and Khama Rhino Sanctuary are the genuine outdoor-life highlight.*

> *Expat.com forum, Botswana — paraphrased: one prospective mover says the real draw is "the bushveld" — wild landscape, not city amenities — while another is more struck by how slow and paperwork-heavy Botswana's bureaucracy is than by anything about green space.*

## Conclusion

Both countries teach the same quiet lesson from opposite directions: don't trust the headline statistic. India's open-space guidelines promise far more than most cities deliver, while Botswana's conservation numbers promise a wilderness paradise that, in practice, lives an hour's drive from your actual front door. Find the one reliable loop in India and defend it fiercely. Buy the car in Botswana and treat every weekend as the actual park visit. Neither will give you a corner green space to simply wander into — you'll have to go looking, on purpose, every single time.
