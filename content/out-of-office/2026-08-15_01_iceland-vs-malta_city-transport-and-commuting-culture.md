# Reykjavík Has No Traffic Because Nobody's There, and Malta Has All the Traffic Because Everybody's Stuck Behind One Bus

🇮🇸 Iceland 🇲🇹 Malta
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Let's get one thing out of the way: neither of these countries has what a sane person would call "public transport." Iceland has a bus system that exists mostly as a rumour, and Malta has a bus system that exists mostly as a traffic cone. Both nations have decided, independently and with total conviction, that the correct number of buses per hour is either zero or one, and that one bus should ideally be stuck behind a wedding convoy.

I have commuted — and I use that word generously — in both. In Iceland I owned a car within eleven days of arrival because the alternative was becoming a hermit. In Malta I owned a car within four days because the alternative was standing at a bus stop in October heat, questioning every choice that led me there. Neither country will apologise for this. Neither country thinks it needs to.

## Do's & Don'ts: Iceland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Rent a proper 4x4 the moment you land, even in "summer" | Trust a weather forecast further out than four hours |
| Learn to read a ring-road closure map | Assume the one bus per hour will actually arrive on schedule |
| Fill your tank whenever you see a station outside Reykjavík | Expect pavements, footpaths, or basic pedestrian dignity |

## Do's & Don'ts: Malta

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Download the Tallinja app and pretend the ETAs mean something | Stand in direct sun waiting for a bus that's "12 minutes away" for 40 minutes |
| Learn the back roads through Mdina before rush hour swallows you | Attempt a left turn in Sliema during peak traffic without a will prepared |
| Befriend anyone with a boat — the harbour is faster than the road | Assume roundabout etiquette is a real, shared concept here |

## Iceland: A Country That Built Roads for Nobody in Particular

Iceland has 370,000 people and roughly the road infrastructure of a mid-sized county, which sounds like it should be a disaster and instead is oddly serene, because there simply aren't enough humans to create a traffic jam. Reykjavík's rush hour is a genuinely charming twelve minutes of mild inconvenience that Icelanders discuss with the theatrical outrage of people who have never experienced an actual city. I sat in what a local co-worker called "brutal traffic" once. It was four cars.

The bus system, Strætó, operates on a schedule best described as aspirational fiction. It runs, technically. Whether it runs when the app says it will is a separate and unrelated question, one that seems to depend on wind speed, the driver's mood, and possibly tectonic activity. Icelanders don't complain about this because almost nobody who matters to the conversation actually rides it — car ownership is treated less as a lifestyle choice and more as a prerequisite for continued existence, the way you'd treat owning shoes.

What nobody tells you before you arrive is that "commuting" in Iceland frequently means driving through weather that would shut down an airport in most other nations, on roads that ice over sideways, past sheep who have absolutely no interest in your right of way. The Ring Road, Route 1, is simultaneously the most spectacular and most nerve-shredding daily drive a person can have, and Icelanders treat black ice on a coastal bend the way Londoners treat a slightly delayed Tube — a minor, familiar nuisance, not an event.

Reykjavík itself is walkable in the way a village is walkable, which is to say: charming for the six blocks that matter, useless the second you need anything outside them. There is no real cycling culture despite years of municipal promises, because cycling into 40 mph horizontal sleet is not a lifestyle, it's a punishment. Parking is plentiful and free in a way that would make a Londoner weep, and this, more than any infrastructure decision, tells you everything about how little pressure this transport system is actually under. It doesn't need to be good. There's nobody here to need it.

## Malta: A Country Where Every Road Was Built for a Different Country's Population

Malta is 316 square kilometres with over half a million residents plus a tourist population that seemingly never leaves, and this arithmetic produces the single angriest traffic culture I have witnessed outside of actual war zones. There are, per capita, more registered vehicles here than almost anywhere in Europe, all of them attempting to use roads laid out by the Knights of St. John, who — astonishingly — did not plan for the SUV.

The bus network, run under the Tallinja brand, is not lacking in ambition. Routes exist. An app exists. Real-time tracking exists, in theory. What doesn't exist is any relationship between the app's predictions and physical reality, because Malta's roads jam so comprehensively, so constantly, that a bus scheduled every 15 minutes becomes a bus that arrives in bunches of three, forty minutes apart, each one already full. Waiting for public transport in Malta in August, in full sun, on a pavement roughly the width of a dinner plate, is a genuine character-building exercise I would not recommend to anyone I liked.

Driving isn't better. Maltese driving culture treats lane markings as a starting suggestion rather than a rule, roundabouts as a form of assertive negotiation, and the horn as a full sentence in its own right. I once watched a three-point turn executed in the middle of a roundabout in Msida during rush hour, by a driver who seemed entirely unbothered by the eleven cars now stopped behind him. Nobody reacted. This, I was told, is Tuesday.

And yet — Malta has something Iceland doesn't: proximity. Everything is close. The entire island can be crossed in under an hour with no traffic, which never happens, but the theoretical possibility keeps everyone slightly hopeful. Locals compensate the way any sensible population under siege does: they move by boat where they can, they walk the old towns where cars can't reasonably follow, and they've developed an almost supernatural tolerance for gridlock that would send a Berliner into cardiac arrest.

## The Verdict

Iceland wins on pure functionality — you will get where you're going, eventually, mostly on time, mostly without honking. But it wins by forfeit: there's nobody else on the road. That's not infrastructure, that's just emptiness with tarmac. Malta loses on almost every practical metric — the buses lie, the roads were built for donkeys, and the traffic in Sliema at 5pm is a war crime — but it has soul, chaos, and the sheer commitment of a population that has simply decided gridlock is a personality trait now. If you want to arrive places, move to Iceland. If you want a story to tell at dinner, and possibly a small ulcer, Malta will oblige.

## Conclusion

Neither of these countries is going to fix this, and honestly, why would they. Iceland doesn't need a transport system because it doesn't have enough people to justify one — the roads are empty because the country is, and that's not a flaw, that's the whole appeal of the place. Malta doesn't need to fix its roads because everyone's already resigned themselves to the chaos with the weary affection of a person married thirty years to someone who still leaves the cap off the toothpaste. You adapt or you leave. Most people adapt, buy the car, learn the shortcuts, and stop expecting the bus to keep its promises. That's not defeat. That's just what living abroad actually looks like, minus the brochure.
