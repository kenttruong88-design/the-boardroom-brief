# Nobody Warns You That German Efficiency Stops At The Waiting Room Door

🇮🇩 Indonesia vs 🇩🇪 Germany — Healthcare and Expat Medical Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every expat arrives somewhere with a smug little theory about healthcare, usually formed from one dramatic anecdote a friend of a friend told them at a dinner party. Then they get sick abroad and discover the truth is both more mundane and more terrifying than the anecdote. Indonesia and Germany sit at opposite ends of a very particular spectrum: one system runs on cash, charm, and a startling amount of personal negotiation, the other runs on paperwork so dense it should qualify as a load-bearing wall.

I have had a kidney stone diagnosed in a Jakarta hospital corridor by a doctor who also, charmingly, asked about my flight home, and I have waited eleven weeks in Berlin for a dermatology appointment for a rash that had, mercifully, resolved itself somewhere around week four out of pure spite. Neither experience left me feeling especially cared for. Both left me with opinions, which is really all Out of Office promises you.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Indonesia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get international health insurance before you land, not after the ambulance ride | Assume BPJS (national insurance) covers you as a foreigner without proper residency paperwork |
| Carry cash or a card with a healthy limit — many private hospitals want a deposit before treatment | Expect a public hospital queue to move quickly, or at all, on a Monday |
| Ask specifically for a doctor who speaks English if you're not confident in Bahasa Indonesia | Assume every clinic has the specialist you need — Jakarta and Bali are well-served, elsewhere is a gamble |

**Germany**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a Krankenkasse (statutory insurer) within weeks of your Anmeldung | Skip the referral system and expect a walk-in specialist appointment |
| Learn the difference between gesetzlich (statutory) and privat (private) insurance before you sign anything | Assume private insurance is automatically better — switching back to statutory later is brutally hard |
| Keep every receipt and Rezept; German bureaucracy rewards paper trails | Show up to a Termin without your insurance card, or five minutes late, and expect sympathy |

## Indonesia: Warm, Chaotic, Occasionally Alarming

Indonesian healthcare operates on a two-tier system that expats figure out roughly the way you figure out a fire alarm is real — after the fact, usually mid-crisis. There's BPJS Kesehatan, the national insurance scheme, which is admirable in ambition and nearly impossible for most foreigners to access meaningfully without long-term residency status sorted well in advance. Then there's the private hospital network, which is where every sensible expat ends up, and which ranges from genuinely excellent in Jakarta and parts of Bali to "please just get me on a medevac flight" in the outer islands.

The private hospitals themselves are a peculiar hybrid of five-star hospitality and financial ambush. You'll be greeted with a warmth that borders on concerning given the circumstances, offered coffee in the consultation room, and then handed a bill that requires a small negotiation, sometimes literally, at the discharge desk. Deposits before treatment are standard, which is a fun thing to discover while doubled over. I once watched an expat friend argue down an MRI bill in the lobby of a Jakarta hospital with the calm confidence of someone haggling over a batik sarong, and she won, which tells you everything about how this system actually functions: not through policy, but through nerve.

Specialists are plentiful in the cities and vanishingly rare outside them, so anyone living in, say, Yogyakarta with a chronic condition eventually accepts they're doing a monthly pilgrimage to Jakarta whether they like it or not. Language is manageable in the major private hospitals — English is common among doctors trained abroad — but nursing staff and administrative desks are a different matter, and a translation app becomes as essential as your insurance card. What Indonesia does brilliantly is speed and access when you can pay: same-day specialist appointments, no six-week purgatory. What it does badly is any illusion of a safety net if you can't.

## Germany: Thorough, Glacial, Bureaucratically Sadistic

Germany's healthcare is, on paper, one of the best systems on the planet, and I want to be clear that this is true in the way "the food is excellent" is true of a restaurant with a four-month waitlist. The quality of care, once you're actually receiving it, is exceptional — precise, evidence-based, staffed by people who take their qualifications seriously in a way that borders on the devotional. Getting to that care is the problem, and it is a problem constructed entirely out of forms.

You cannot simply be sick in Germany. You must first be registered — Anmeldung, the bureaucratic rite of passage every expat complains about within their first fortnight — and only then can you enroll with a Krankenkasse, the statutory insurer that will govern the rest of your medical existence unless you make the fateful, often irreversible decision to go private. Choosing wrong here haunts people for years; several expats I know are still trapped in private insurance plans that seemed cheaper at twenty-six and are ruinous at forty, with no clean path back to statutory coverage.

Then there's the Termin system, under which seeing a specialist without a referral from your Hausarzt (family doctor) is basically fantasy, and even with a referral, "we have an opening in eleven weeks" is a sentence you will hear delivered without a hint of irony. Germans themselves navigate this by having relationships with doctors that predate their own memories and by understanding, from birth apparently, exactly which paperwork unlocks which door. Expats navigate it by suffering, mostly in silence, occasionally in a Reddit thread. The upside, and it is real: emergency care is fast, thorough, and won't bankrupt you. The downside is everything that isn't an emergency.

## The Verdict

If you want speed and don't mind negotiating your own hospital bill like it's a Balinese market stall, Indonesia wins on responsiveness — provided you can pay, and provided you never need a rare specialist outside Jakarta. If you want depth of expertise, rigorous standards, and the reassurance that whoever's treating you actually knows what they're doing, Germany wins, provided you can survive the eleven-week waiting room of the soul first.

Neither system is designed with the transient foreigner in mind, which is the real lesson here: healthcare abroad is never actually about the healthcare. It's about how well you can perform bureaucracy under duress, in a language you may not fully control, while also being ill. Indonesia asks you to be resourceful. Germany asks you to be patient, in both senses of the word, for a genuinely uncomfortable length of time. Pack good travel insurance either way, because neither country will forgive you for assuming otherwise.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Indonesia forum — a member described being asked for a cash deposit before an emergency appendectomy would even be scheduled, despite having insurance documents on hand.*

> *expat.com Germany forum — someone recounted spending four months on the phone trying to switch Krankenkassen after realizing their initial choice didn't cover a needed treatment, only to be told the switch couldn't take effect for another year.*

> *Quora — a Jakarta-based expat warned that "international insurance" sold locally sometimes excludes the exact procedures you'd actually need evacuated for, and recommended reading the exclusions list twice, sober.*

## Conclusion

Here's the uncomfortable truth Out of Office exists to say out loud: neither Jakarta's cash-and-charm hospitals nor Berlin's form-and-referral fortress were built with you in mind, and pretending otherwise is how people end up either bankrupt or eleven weeks into a rash that's already gone. Buy the insurance. Read the exclusions. Learn the word for "specialist" in the local language before you need it, not during. And accept that "efficient German healthcare" is a phrase that only applies once you've already cleared several rounds of paperwork purgatory — by which point, frankly, you've earned the diagnosis.
