# Lagos Will Haggle You Into Submission; America Will Just Let You Buy Forty Rolls Of Toilet Paper In One Go

🇳🇬 🇺🇸 *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nigeria taught me that shopping is a conversation, often a loud one, occasionally a performance, and that the sticker price is a rumour rather than a fact. America taught me that shopping is a logistics exercise, that bulk is a personality trait, and that a single trip to Costco can replace an entire relationship with human interaction for the following three weeks.

I have haggled a market woman in Balogun down from an insulting price to a merely cheeky one, and I have stood in a Walmart self-checkout queue behind a man buying exactly one banana using a card reader that rejected him four times. Both experiences, somehow, left me equally exhausted.

## 🇳🇬 Do's and Don'ts: Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Haggle. Always haggle. The first price quoted to a foreigner is a opening bid, not an insult to be paid in full | Pay the "oyinbo price" without at least a token negotiation — the vendor respects you more for trying, even badly |
| Download Jumia and PayPorte before you need them — Nigeria's e-commerce boom means half your shopping will happen on a phone screen anyway | Expect delivery windows to mean anything close to what they say; "today" is aspirational, "this week" is a promise |
| Carry small notes and coins everywhere — markets run on change you don't have and vendors who definitely don't either | Assume the mall and the market are competitors; most Lagosians use both, often on the same afternoon, for different things entirely |
| Trust your mama put and local market for fresh produce over imported supermarket versions — fresher, cheaper, and you'll meet your neighbours | Pay for anything by card without checking there's a surcharge first — cash remains quietly, stubbornly king |

## 🇺🇸 Do's and Don'ts: United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a Costco or Sam's Club membership immediately — bulk buying isn't a lifestyle choice here, it's the default operating system | Buy a "small" bag of anything; American retail portion sizes do not recognise the concept of small, only "personal" and "family" |
| Use the loyalty app for every grocery chain you frequent — the posted price is often a decoy for people who didn't scan the barcode | Expect haggling anywhere except a car dealership or a garage sale; a quoted price in a shop is a wall, not an opening offer |
| Learn the return policy before you buy — Americans return things with a confidence that borders on constitutional right | Forget sales tax is added at the till, not included in the sticker — the price you see is never, quite, the price you pay |
| Embrace online shopping as the default, not the backup — same-day delivery is normal, and malls are increasingly for browsing, not buying | Assume every state taxes, prices, or stocks the same way — shopping culture shifts hard crossing a state line, let alone an ocean |

## Nigeria: The Market Is A Relationship, Not A Transaction

Lagos shopping exists on three simultaneous planes, and most Nigerians move between all three without thinking twice. There's the open market — Balogun, Oyingbo, your local mama put stalls — where price is a starting negotiation and the real currency is rapport, not naira. There's the mall — Lekki's Circle Mall, Ikeja City Mall — gleaming, air-conditioned, fixed-price, and treated by the middle class as a genuine leisure destination, somewhere you go on a Sunday to be seen as much as to buy anything. And then there's Jumia, the platform that has spent a decade trying to drag Nigerian retail online, with a logistics network that compensates for the country's patchy addressing system by sending riders who call you from the gate, not the app.

What nobody tells you is how completely these three coexist. Someone will order imported skincare on Jumia, buy their tomatoes and peppers from the market woman who's known their mother for twenty years, and still go to the mall on a Saturday purely to sit in the air conditioning and eat suya. The market is social infrastructure disguised as commerce — you don't just buy yams, you catch up on the neighbourhood, negotiate a cousin's school fees discount through sheer force of charm, and occasionally get a free extra pepper thrown in because the vendor likes your face. Try that at a Tesco self-checkout.

The genuine culture shock for most expats isn't the haggling, which is at least fun once you commit to it. It's the cash dependency. Card machines fail constantly, mobile transfer apps occasionally seize up at the worst moment, and the entire economy has a backup plan involving a crumpled wad of naira that most Western shoppers simply don't carry anymore.

Jumia's own reporting on its growth has been candid about the specific logistical puzzle Nigerian e-commerce had to solve that Amazon never did: addresses that often don't formally exist. Business press coverage of the company describes riders navigating by landmark and phone call rather than GPS pin — "the blue gate after the generator repair shop" functions as a perfectly legitimate delivery address — and cash-on-delivery remains the dominant payment method precisely because trust in pre-paying a stranger online took years to build, and arguably still hasn't fully arrived outside the major cities. It's e-commerce bolted onto an economy that was never designed around formal addressing or default card trust, and the fact that it works at all, as well as it increasingly does, is a genuinely underappreciated feat of logistics improvisation.

## United States: Bulk Is A Love Language

American retail operates on an entirely different emotional register: abundance as reassurance. The Costco run isn't just shopping, it's a small act of civic participation — a cart the size of a canoe, a rotisserie chicken the size of a toddler, a 48-pack of paper towels that will outlive your lease. Scarcity anxiety, whether cultural memory or marketing genius, has built an entire retail ecosystem around the premise that buying more, less often, is both cheaper and somehow more responsible.

What actually disorients newcomers is the pricing theatre. The sticker price is never the price — sales tax gets bolted on at the register, varying wildly by state and sometimes by city, so the $4.99 you budgeted becomes $5.43 for reasons that feel personally targeted. Then there's the loyalty-app economy: the "regular" price on the shelf is increasingly a decoy for people who haven't downloaded the app and scanned in for the real, 30%-off price, turning every grocery run into a faint admission test of your technological compliance.

And nobody haggles, anywhere, ever, outside a used car lot or a yard sale — which makes the rigid fixed-price culture feel, after Lagos, almost insultingly polite. You cannot charm a Walmart cashier into a discount. You can, however, return almost anything, no questions asked, weeks after purchase, with a confidence Americans treat as a basic civil liberty rather than a retail courtesy.

The warehouse-club phenomenon specifically rewards a kind of social planning most newcomers underestimate: a Costco trip is often a household event, not an errand, with extended family or neighbours splitting a membership and a bulk haul of rotisserie chicken, paper goods and a suspiciously large jar of mayonnaise between two or three households. It's the inverse of the Lagos market's person-to-person rapport — the relationship here is between the shopper and the brand's implicit promise of bulk-discount reliability, not between the shopper and any individual employee, who increasingly exists mainly to scan a membership card and wave you through to the parking lot.

## The Verdict

Nigeria wins on texture, relationship, and the sheer theatre of a good haggle; America wins on convenience, scale, and the genuine relief of knowing the price on the shelf won't move regardless of your negotiating skill. If you want shopping to feel like a social occasion, go to Lagos. If you want shopping to feel like a mildly expensive act of stockpiling against an unspecified apocalypse, go to America. Both, disturbingly, will leave you with more plantain chips or Cheez-Its than any human needs.

The deeper tell is what each culture trusts to set a fair price. Nigeria trusts a human conversation, conducted in real time, informed by who you are and how well you negotiate — a system that rewards charm and punishes passivity. America trusts an algorithm, a loyalty database, and a sales-tax formula that varies by zip code but never, ever, by how nicely you ask. One system is more exhausting. The other is more honest about exactly how little room for exhaustion it's willing to offer you.

## The Parting Shot

Lagos will make you a better negotiator and a worse carrier of exact change. America will make you the proud owner of a pantry that could survive a siege, at a price that was never quite what the shelf promised. Neither country's shopping culture is designed for your convenience — Nigeria's is designed for relationship, America's for volume — and the sooner you stop expecting either to behave like the other, the sooner you'll stop doing that confused little math in your head at every single till.
