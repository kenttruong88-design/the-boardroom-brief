# Lagos Will Test Your Nerve. Lisbon Will Test Your Knees.

🇳🇬 Nigeria vs 🇵🇹 Portugal

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two cities, two entirely different definitions of "getting from A to B without incident." Lagos treats commuting as a full-contact sport played at fifty kilometres per hour in a vehicle with no discernible suspension. Lisbon treats it as a scenic stroll uphill, on cobblestones, in the rain, while a tram older than your grandmother rattles past close enough to clip your elbow. Neither city will apologise for this. Neither city should.

I have sat in Lagos gridlock long enough to watch an entire market spring up around my stationary car — a man selling phone chargers, a woman selling suya, a child selling bags of "pure water" — and I have also stood on a Lisbon hillside at 7am watching the number 28 tram fail to arrive for the third consecutive time while a busker played fado at a volume disproportionate to my patience. Both experiences taught me something. Mostly that public transport planning is, globally, an act of theatre.

## Do's and Don'ts: Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate your danfo or keke fare before boarding, not after | Assume there's a fixed timetable for anything — there isn't |
| Download Bolt or Uber before you land; ride-hailing is the sane option | Try to cross a Lagos expressway on foot like it's a suggestion rather than a threat |
| Keep small notes on you — nobody has change for a big bill | Expect air conditioning, legroom, or silence in a shared bus |
| Leave absurdly early; "traffic" is not an excuse in Lagos, it's a weather system | Get in an unmarked vehicle after dark without checking with a local first |

## Do's and Don'ts: Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a Viva Viagem or Andante card — paying cash per ride is a tourist tax on your own dignity | Stand where the tram turns; you will lose an elbow |
| Validate your ticket every single time, even if it feels performative | Assume the metro runs late enough to get you home after a proper night out — it doesn't |
| Wear shoes with actual grip; the calçada tiles are ice rinks when wet | Expect the elevadores (funiculars) to be anything but a tourist queue by 10am |
| Learn the difference between Carris buses and the private ones — routes overlap oddly | Confuse "quiet carriage energy" for Portuguese reserve on transport — it's just early-morning exhaustion |

## Nigeria: Commuting as Negotiation, Endurance, and Occasional Faith

Nobody in Lagos describes their commute as "fine." They describe it as a story, usually one involving at least ninety minutes, several forms of transport, and a moment where they questioned every choice that led them to that exact junction at that exact hour. The danfo — that yellow minibus held together by paint and optimism — remains the backbone of the city's movement, and riding one is less a transaction than an audition. You learn the conductor's shorthand, you learn to brace before every gear change, you learn that "final bus stop" is a philosophical concept rather than a literal one.

What foreigners get wrong immediately is assuming Lagos traffic is simply "bad." It isn't bad. It's a fully formed ecosystem with its own rules, hierarchies, and etiquette, none of which are written down and all of which you will violate at least once in your first month. The okada and keke drivers who thread through stationary traffic like it's a video game aren't being reckless — they're the only ones actually moving. Ride-hailing apps have genuinely improved life for anyone with a smartphone and a bit of cash, and I'd argue Bolt drivers in Lagos deserve some kind of civic medal for what they navigate daily without visibly aging.

The Lagos-Ibadan expressway and the BRT (Bus Rapid Transit) corridor have made a dent — a real one — but "dent" is doing a lot of work in that sentence when the city adds roughly the population of a mid-sized European country every few years and the roads have not kept pace. What impresses me isn't the infrastructure. It's the improvisation built on top of broken infrastructure: the informal route networks, the mobile money transfers to conductors, the entire shadow economy of getting people to work on time despite the state's best efforts to prevent it. Foreigners who arrive expecting Western punctuality standards leave either radicalised against public transport forever or, if they're smart, converted to the local gospel: leave early, expect nothing, tip generously, never act surprised.

## Portugal: Commuting as Aesthetic Suffering

Lisbon has built an entire tourism industry around the idea that its public transport is charming, and to be fair, it is — right up until you actually need to be somewhere on time. The number 28 tram, immortalised on every postcard and tea towel in the country, is objectively a nightmare during commuting hours: overcrowded, slow, frequently targeted by pickpockets who have clearly done market research on which route attracts the most distracted tourists. Locals avoid it entirely and take the bus, which nobody photographs because it looks like a bus in literally any other city.

Porto does this better, frankly, with a sleeker metro system and less performative charm, but Lisbon's seven hills mean that even a short commute becomes a cardio session, assisted by funiculars (elevadores) that are more tourist attraction than transit solution and are permanently besieged by queues. The actual metro is fine — clean, tiled, occasionally beautiful — but it doesn't reach nearly enough of the city, so most people's real commute is some combination of metro, bus, and a fifteen-minute uphill walk that leaves you sweating through whatever you'd ironed that morning.

What nobody warns you about is the punctuality gap between the schedule and reality. Portuguese transport apps will confidently tell you a bus arrives in six minutes. It will arrive in nineteen, or not at all, and everyone waiting will simply accept this with a shrug that borders on national identity. There's a specific kind of Lisbon resignation you develop — not anger, not stress, just a weary acceptance that time is a suggestion here, delivered with beautiful blue-tiled stations and genuinely good coffee at the kiosk while you wait. It's infrastructure failure with excellent production values.

## The Verdict

Lagos wins on sheer competence-under-fire — the city moves twenty million people a day with almost no functioning central planning, purely through collective ingenuity, and that deserves respect even as it destroys your nerves. Lisbon wins on aesthetics and does absolutely nothing else to earn its reputation; it coasts entirely on the fact that watching a yellow tram climb a hill at sunset makes people forget it was forty minutes late. If you want efficiency, neither city will give it to you. If you want a commute that teaches you something about the place you're living, Lagos wins by a landslide — you cannot survive it passively. Lisbon lets you sleepwalk through mediocrity as long as the lighting is flattering, and that, ultimately, is the more dishonest of the two.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/expats — "I moved to Lagos for work and genuinely didn't understand distance and time as concepts until my first Monday commute. Google Maps says forty minutes. It means two and a half hours. Adjust your entire personality accordingly."*

> *Internations Lisbon — "Everyone romanticises the trams until they're standing in one at 8:15am with someone's backpack in their face, running fifteen minutes late for a job interview because the number 28 decided today was a rest day."*

## Conclusion

Neither Lagos nor Lisbon will move you efficiently, and if you arrive expecting otherwise, that's on you, not them. Lagos will humble you loudly, immediately, and repeatedly, and somewhere around month three you'll find yourself weirdly proud of navigating it. Lisbon will humble you slowly, charmingly, through a thousand small delays dressed up in blue-and-white tilework, and you'll only notice you've been conditioned into acceptance when you stop checking your watch entirely. Pick your poison. Just don't tell either city its transport system is "quaint" — the Lagos commuter will laugh at you, and the Lisbon one will simply let the tram leave without you as punishment.
