# One Country Gave Everyone a Bicycle. The Other Gave Everyone a Green Vest and a Very Firm Helmet Law

🇳🇱 Netherlands vs 🇷🇼 Rwanda

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are more bicycles in the Netherlands than there are Dutch people, which tells you everything about a nation that decided, decades ago, that the car was a nice idea other countries could keep. Rwanda took a different route entirely — quite literally, on the back of a motorbike, wearing a mandatory helmet, negotiated fare already settled before the engine starts. Both countries have solved urban mobility. Neither solution looks anything like the other, and both will make you wonder why your own city's transport minister hasn't been fired yet.

## Do's & Don'ts: Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Rent or buy a bike within your first week — walking marks you instantly as a tourist | Wear a helmet unless you're a competitive cyclist; locals will assume you're either | 
| Signal turns with your arm, every time, without exception | Stand in a bike lane to take a photo — you will be run down, politely, without apology |
| Learn the hand-bell etiquette — one ring means "move," not "hello" | Assume pedestrian right of way near a cycle path; on a bike path, bikes rule absolutely |
| Use OV-chipkaart for trains and trams — it's genuinely seamless | Cycle against one-way bike traffic, even briefly, even "just this once" |

## Do's & Don'ts: Rwanda

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate or confirm your moto fare before the ride begins | Get on a moto without a helmet — it's mandatory, and drivers will refuse you anyway |
| Use a ride-hailing app for predictable, upfront pricing if you want it | Assume moto drivers accept large bills — carry smaller denominations |
| Flag a moto from a recognised station for a marginally safer pickup | Rush a moto driver through traffic — Kigali's roads reward patience, not urgency |
| Keep your phone secured while riding — pockets and open bags invite trouble | Expect widespread public transit coverage outside central Kigali's main routes |

## Netherlands: A Nation That Chose the Bicycle and Never Looked Back

The numbers alone should embarrass every other developed nation into silence: roughly 22.8 million bicycles for a population of about 18 million, a quarter of all journeys made by bike nationally, and 35,000 kilometres of dedicated cycling infrastructure that doesn't just exist but is actively, continuously expanded and maintained as core civic priority rather than a nice-to-have add-on squeezed in after the roads budget. Cycling commutes have grown even further in recent years, driven by better infrastructure and the quiet, unstoppable rise of the e-bike, which has made the whole system accessible regardless of fitness level, age, or how many groceries you're improbably balancing on the back rack.

What makes Dutch cycling culture genuinely remarkable isn't the infrastructure alone — plenty of cities have built bike lanes and called it a day. It's the egalitarianism. Everyone cycles: bankers in suits, toddlers in cargo bike front seats, elderly women doing their weekly shop, teenagers three-abreast having a conversation at speed that would terrify a Formula 1 driver. No helmet law exists for adult cyclists, and the near-total absence of helmets among everyday riders isn't recklessness — it's a reflection of infrastructure so genuinely safe that the protective gear became largely unnecessary, a fact that will offend the safety-conscious instincts of visitors from more car-centric nations within about their first hour on a rented bike.

The learning curve for outsiders is steeper than it looks. Dutch cyclists move with unhurried confidence through what appears to be chaos but is, in fact, a tightly choreographed system with its own rules, right-of-way hierarchies, and social contracts — including a bell-ring etiquette that tourists reliably misread as rudeness rather than the practical, efficient communication it actually is. Get in a Dutch cyclist's way and you will hear about it, briefly, directly, and then they'll be gone, three blocks ahead, entirely unbothered, because there's another beautifully engineered junction just ahead requiring their full and immediate attention.

## Rwanda: The Moto-Taxi as Genuinely Regulated Urban Backbone

Kigali's moto-taxis — universally called "motos" — are the closest thing the city has to a subway system, minus the tunnels and plus considerably more wind in your face. With an estimated 30,000 operating in the capital alone, motos function as the default mode of transport for most residents: fast, cheap at roughly one to three US dollars for a central trip, and genuinely convenient given Kigali's hilly terrain, which makes walking longer distances a proposition few locals or newcomers relish.

What distinguishes Rwanda's moto system from the informal, faintly chaotic motorbike-taxi cultures found elsewhere is how thoroughly regulated it actually is. Drivers wear standardised green reflective vests and matching helmets, are required by law to carry a spare helmet for passengers, and operate largely from recognised stations rather than purely opportunistic street hailing — though flagging one down mid-street works fine too. This isn't cosmetic regulation; Rwanda enforces its helmet and licensing requirements with a seriousness that would startle visitors from countries where "moto safety standards" is a punchline rather than an actual enforced policy.

Ride-hailing apps have gained real traction in recent years, offering upfront pricing that removes the negotiation step entirely — a welcome development for newcomers still calibrating what a fair fare actually looks like, since prices for the same route can vary noticeably depending on how confidently you negotiate versus how obviously foreign you appear the moment you open your mouth. Public buses exist and cover more ground each year, but for most point-to-point urban trips, the moto remains the default, the backbone, and honestly the far more entertaining way to see a city built dramatically across a series of hills.

## The Verdict

The Netherlands has built the platonic ideal of self-propelled urban mobility — quiet, egalitarian, dazzlingly efficient, and dependent entirely on infrastructure most countries will never have the political will to fund. Rwanda has built something scrappier and, frankly, more impressive given the constraints: a genuinely regulated, safety-conscious informal transport network that outperforms plenty of wealthier nations' chaotic, under-enforced motorbike-taxi scenes. If I'm judging by sheer engineering achievement, the Netherlands wins comfortably. If I'm judging by "solved an extremely hard problem with the resources actually available," Rwanda's moto system deserves far more international credit than it gets, and considerably less condescension from tourists who assume "motorbike taxi" automatically means "unregulated chaos." It very much does not, here.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *DutchPat cycling culture guide — paraphrased: a longtime expat admitted that learning the bell-ring etiquette took genuine embarrassment before it clicked — one ring means move now, not a friendly hello, and locals will not slow down to clarify.*

> *Kigali Newcomers — paraphrased: a newcomer was advised to always confirm the moto fare verbally before getting on, since prices aren't metered and a foreign accent can noticeably inflate the "negotiated" rate if you don't set it first.*

> *Living in Kigali forum — paraphrased: a longtime resident noted that ride-hailing apps solved the fare-anxiety problem entirely, but warned that app coverage still thins out fast once you're outside the central city grid.*

## Conclusion

Two countries, two entirely different answers to the same question — how do you move a lot of people through a city efficiently without everyone owning a car — and both got there by refusing to simply import whichever model a wealthier nation happened to be using at the time. The Netherlands spent half a century building infrastructure most countries still consider aspirational. Rwanda built a regulatory framework around an existing informal system rather than trying to replace it wholesale, and the result works better than it has any right to. Judge neither by comparison to your own city's transport failures. Judge both on their own terms, rent the bike, flag the moto, and stop assuming the country without the subway map is the one that hasn't figured it out.
