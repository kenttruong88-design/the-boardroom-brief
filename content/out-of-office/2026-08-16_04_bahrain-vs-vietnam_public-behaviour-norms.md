# Bahrain Wants You Composed. Vietnam Wants You in the Middle of the Action.

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Bahrain's public life runs on a quiet code of restraint — modest dress, measured volume, a general sense that display of any kind is faintly suspect. Vietnam's public life runs on the opposite principle entirely: everything happens outside, loudly, at knee height, on a plastic stool, with traffic flowing around you like you're a mildly inconvenient rock in a river. Learning to behave in either country means unlearning whatever you thought "in public" meant at home.

I've been quietly shushed in Manama and nearly flattened by a moped in Hanoi while sitting perfectly still. Both experiences were, in retrospect, entirely my fault.

## Do's & Don'ts: Bahrain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress modestly in public areas, especially outside international zones | Show public displays of affection — even hand-holding draws attention |
| Lower your voice and moderate behaviour in malls, mosques, and government spaces | Assume nightlife-district norms apply everywhere else in the country |
| Respect prayer times — some services and shops pause briefly | Photograph locals, especially women, without asking first |

## Do's & Don'ts: Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Embrace street-level life — plastic stools, sidewalk cafes, pavement dining | Point your feet directly at people or religious objects — it's disrespectful |
| Cross the road with slow, steady, predictable movement — traffic will flow around you | Raise your voice or show public anger — it causes loss of face for everyone involved |
| Bargain gently at markets, but expect fixed prices in most shops now | Touch anyone's head, even a child's, without real familiarity |

## Bahrain: Composure as a Public Virtue

Bahrain's public behaviour code isn't written down anywhere you'll be handed at the airport, but it is absolutely enforced by a thousand small social cues that newcomers pick up slowly and expats occasionally never pick up at all. The core principle is restraint: modest dress outside the malls and international hotel zones, moderated volume, minimal public affection, and a general aversion to any behaviour that draws attention to the individual over the group. This isn't oppressive so much as it is simply a different calibration of what "appropriate" means — Bahrain is genuinely one of the more socially liberal Gulf states, with a real nightlife scene and considerable Western expat presence, but that liberalism operates within a frame most visitors initially misjudge as stricter than it actually is, and then later misjudge as looser than it actually is, because the line moves depending on which part of the island you're standing in.

The Bahrain Tourism and Exhibitions Authority's own visitor guidance leans heavily on this zonal logic — different expectations in the souks and residential areas than in the international hotels and expat compounds — and getting it wrong tends to produce not confrontation but a kind of cool, polite distance, which is in some ways a harder signal to read than an outright telling-off. Prayer times, too, create small ripples through public life — certain shops pausing briefly, certain spaces going quiet — and locals navigate this so fluidly that visitors often don't notice until they've been standing awkwardly outside a shuttered shop wondering what they did wrong.

## Vietnam: Life Lived Entirely, Gloriously, at Sidewalk Level

If Bahrain's public norm is composure, Vietnam's is total, unselfconscious immersion. Public life in Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City happens almost entirely on the pavement — meals, haircuts, business meetings, naps, entire extended-family gatherings — conducted from tiny plastic stools at a height that seems specifically engineered to be uncomfortable for anyone over five foot six. Traffic, meanwhile, doesn't stop for pedestrians so much as flow around them, and the single most important public-behaviour lesson any newcomer learns is: walk slowly, steadily, predictably, and never, ever stop dead in the middle of the road. The moped swarm has already calculated your trajectory. Changing it is how accidents happen.

Beneath the visible chaos, though, sits a genuinely rigid etiquette code that catches Western visitors off guard precisely because the surface looks so loose. Pointing your feet at someone, touching a person's head, or causing someone public embarrassment — "loss of face" — are real, serious social violations, taken far more seriously than the apparent free-for-all of the street scene would suggest. Raising your voice in a dispute, even a minor one over a taxi fare, tends to backfire badly; the Vietnamese cultural preference is for measured, indirect conflict resolution, and a loud foreigner making a scene is remembered less as "in the right" and more as simply, permanently, "that person." Vietnam's public sphere is intensely communal and visually chaotic, but socially, it runs on rules just as precise as Bahrain's — they're simply invisible until you break one.

## The Verdict

Bahrain asks you to shrink slightly and read the zone you're in. Vietnam asks you to expand into the street and then quietly, carefully, not lose face while doing it. Both systems are more sophisticated than "conservative" or "chaotic" — those are just the lazy first impressions tourists leave with. My verdict: Vietnam's public life is more fun to actually live inside, all that sidewalk theatre and constant motion, but Bahrain's is easier to get right on your first attempt, because at least the rules hold still.

## Conclusion

Bahrain rewards a quiet, observant approach — watch the room, match the composure, and you'll rarely put a foot wrong. Vietnam rewards total physical commitment to the street and a genuine respect for a face-saving etiquette that hides beneath all that visible bustle. Get either one backwards — loud in Manama, stiff and awkward in Hanoi — and you'll be tolerated, politely, as the foreigner who never quite worked it out. Do the homework. Both cultures are watching more closely than the traffic noise or mall music would suggest.
