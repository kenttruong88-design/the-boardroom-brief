# One Country's Weather Changes With the Altitude, the Other's Changes Your Entire Personality

🇧🇹 Bhutan vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Weather abroad is rarely just weather, it's a full personality test disguised as a forecast. Bhutan's climate shifts dramatically depending on which valley you're standing in, subtropical heat and Himalayan chill separated by a single mountain pass, and locals simply dress in layers and get on with it, because arguing with altitude has never once worked. Canada's weather, particularly its winter, is less a climate and more a seasonal endurance event that the entire country has collectively decided to be almost aggressively cheerful about.

I've shivered through an unexpectedly cold Bhutanese evening after a warm afternoon twenty minutes down the road, and I've watched Canadians casually walk to brunch at minus twenty degrees like it was a mild inconvenience rather than a genuine threat to exposed skin. Only one of these countries has turned suffering into a national personality trait, and it isn't the one at altitude.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇧🇹 Bhutan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pack serious layers regardless of season; temperature swings by altitude are dramatic and fast | Trust a single weather forecast for your entire trip if you're moving between valleys |
| Acclimatise properly before strenuous activity at higher elevations | Underestimate how quickly evening temperatures drop even after a genuinely warm day |
| Respect monsoon season timing when planning travel between June and August | Assume Thimphu's climate represents the whole country; regional variation is extreme |

### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in genuinely proper winter gear; fashion-first coats will not save you in a real cold snap | Underestimate windchill; the actual temperature and the "feels like" temperature can differ by 15 degrees or more |
| Use the underground or skywalk systems in cities like Toronto and Montreal built specifically for winter | Assume all of Canada has the same winter; coastal Vancouver and the prairies are entirely different experiences |
| Take seasonal affective disorder seriously; light therapy and vitamin D are common, normalised coping tools | Skip winterizing your car or home; unprepared newcomers are consistently caught out by the first real freeze |

## Bhutan: The Weather Depends Entirely on Where You're Standing

Bhutan's climate is less a single national weather pattern and more a stacked series of microclimates, determined almost entirely by elevation. Drop into the southern lowlands and you'll find humid, subtropical heat; climb into the central valleys around Thimphu and Paro and the air turns cool and temperate; push further into the high Himalayas and you're suddenly in genuine alpine cold, sometimes within the same day's travel. Locals navigate this with a practicality that borders on nonchalance, dressing in adaptable layers, carrying a warm layer even on seemingly mild days, and treating a twenty-degree temperature swing as entirely unremarkable rather than newsworthy.

Monsoon season, roughly June through August, adds another layer of complexity, bringing heavy rainfall that can disrupt mountain roads and travel between regions, something the tourism infrastructure plans around carefully even if individual travellers sometimes don't. Outside monsoon, the dry, clear autumn and spring months are when Bhutan is at its most spectacular, crisp mountain air, clear valley views, and a level of comfortable coolness that makes the earlier heat and humidity of lower elevations feel like a different country entirely, which, functionally, it almost is.

What genuinely catches newcomers off guard isn't the heat or the cold individually, it's the speed of the transition. A warm, pleasant afternoon in a valley town can give way to a genuinely cold evening within a couple of hours as the sun drops behind the mountains, and travellers who packed for the daytime weather alone are left scrambling. Bhutanese locals, by contrast, seem to carry an instinctive internal forecast, always ready with an extra layer, never caught out, treating the altitude's mood swings the way someone else might treat a slightly unreliable friend: fondly, and with appropriate preparation.

## Canada: Suffering Rebranded as a National Personality

Canadian winter isn't weather so much as an annual, months-long event that the entire country has agreed to treat with a strange, almost defiant good humour. Temperatures across much of the country regularly plunge well below freezing for extended stretches, windchill making the "feels like" temperature dramatically worse than the number on the forecast, and yet Canadians will walk to brunch, take the dog out, and complain about it all with a shrug that suggests this is simply Tuesday, not a survival situation, which, generously, it sort of is.

The infrastructure reflects genuine, hard-won expertise. Cities like Toronto and Montreal have built entire underground pedestrian networks specifically so residents can move through downtown without stepping outside for extended stretches, a level of civic planning around cold that most newcomers have simply never encountered. Winter tires aren't optional, they're often legally required in certain provinces. Homes are engineered for serious insulation and heating in a way that countries with milder climates simply don't need to think about. None of this is dramatic to Canadians. It's just what living somewhere genuinely cold for five months a year requires, treated with the same unbothered practicality Bhutanese locals bring to altitude.

The psychological side gets discussed more openly than in many cold-weather cultures, too. Seasonal affective disorder is a normalised, widely acknowledged part of Canadian winter conversation, and coping tools, light therapy lamps, vitamin D supplements, deliberately scheduled indoor social activities, are treated as sensible, unremarkable self-care rather than a sign of struggling. Newcomers from milder climates consistently underestimate just how much a genuine Canadian winter affects mood and energy, and the ones who adjust well are usually the ones who take the mental health side as seriously as the physical gear side, rather than assuming a good coat alone will get them through it.

## The Verdict

Bhutan's weather challenges you with dramatic, fast-moving variation tied to altitude, and rewards anyone who packs thoughtfully and pays attention. Canada's weather challenges you with sustained, months-long extremity, and rewards anyone who invests seriously in both gear and mindset. If you want a weather challenge that resolves within a day's travel, Bhutan is manageable and genuinely beautiful for it. If you want a weather challenge that tests your entire personality for a third of the year, Canada will happily oblige, and hand you a toque and a strange sense of communal pride about it.

> *Internations Thimphu — paraphrased: An expat noted that altitude sickness caught them off guard on a trip to a higher valley, and that acclimatising slowly turned out to matter far more than they'd assumed before arriving.*

## Conclusion

Bhutan and Canada both demand real respect for their weather, just on completely different timelines. Bhutan asks you to adapt within a single day, sometimes a single afternoon. Canada asks you to adapt for an entire season and then do it again next year, with a smile, because apparently that's part of the deal. Pack properly for either. Underestimate neither. And if a Canadian tells you minus twenty "isn't that bad," reader, they are lying to both of you, cheerfully, as is tradition.
