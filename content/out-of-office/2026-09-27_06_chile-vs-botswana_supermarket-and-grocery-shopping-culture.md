# Chile Has Seventeen Kinds of Manjar. Botswana Has One Aisle That Actually Matters.

🇨🇱🇧🇼 Chile vs Botswana

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Chile's supermarket culture is a genuinely serious national infrastructure — a small handful of chains (Jumbo, Lider, Santa Isabel, Tottus) dominate a country famously stretched across 4,300 kilometres of coastline, and Chileans navigate their aisles with the practised efficiency of people who've turned grocery shopping into something between a weekly ritual and a minor competitive sport, especially when it comes to the sacred, non-negotiable manjar (dulce de leche) shelf. Botswana runs on something leaner and, honestly, more honest: a smaller number of chains (Choppies, Spar, Pick n Pay) serving a country with one of the lowest population densities on earth, where the supermarket is often the only game in town for a genuinely wide radius, and where the actual social centre of gravity for fresh food remains the informal and open-air markets that supermarkets have never fully displaced.

I've stood in a Santiago Jumbo watching a woman debate two brands of manjar with the seriousness of a wine sommelier. I've also driven forty minutes outside Gaborone to reach the nearest supermarket of any real size, and watched the entire transaction — from parking to checkout — take on the unhurried, slightly ceremonial quality of an event, because out there, it genuinely was one.

## Do's & Don'ts: Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your own bags — plastic bag bans are strictly enforced at major chains | Skip the feria (street market) entirely; produce there often beats the supermarket on price and freshness |
| Try at least one brand of manjar; opinions on the "best" one are held with real conviction | Confuse "Lider" with a discount chain purely by name — it's mainstream and comprehensive |
| Expect long, orderly checkout queues, especially before long weekends | Assume all regions have the same chains; remote areas rely on smaller local stores |

## Do's & Don'ts: Botswana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stock up properly on trips into town; smaller towns may have limited selection | Expect imported goods to always be in stock; supply can be genuinely irregular |
| Support open-air and informal produce sellers alongside supermarket runs | Assume a supermarket exists nearby everywhere; rural distances are real and significant |
| Budget for higher prices on imported items — Botswana imports a large share of packaged goods | Rush the checkout; it's often a social moment, not just a transaction |

## Chile: A Nation That Takes Its Supermarkets Seriously

Chile's geography — a country over 4,300 kilometres long and rarely more than 350 wide — has shaped its retail landscape in a genuinely distinctive way: national chains had to build logistics networks capable of servicing everything from the Atacama Desert in the north to Patagonia in the south, and the resulting supermarket culture is unusually sophisticated for a country of Chile's size. Jumbo and Lider (the latter owned by Walmart since 2009, a fact many Chileans still mention with mixed feelings) dominate the upper and mainstream tiers respectively, while Santa Isabel and Tottus round out a genuinely competitive, well-stocked field that most Chileans navigate with real brand loyalty and real opinions.

Nowhere is that brand loyalty more visible than on the manjar shelf. Dulce de leche, called manjar in Chile, occupies a section of nearly every supermarket with the gravity of a dedicated wine aisle, and asking a Chilean which brand is genuinely best will get you a confident, immediate, and often strongly-held answer — Chileans take their sweet spreads with a seriousness that borders on identity. Plastic bag legislation, banned at point of sale in large retailers since 2018, has also fully reshaped checkout behaviour: reusable bags are now simply assumed, and anyone caught bagless at a Jumbo checkout will be improvising with cardboard boxes rather than expecting a bag to materialise.

What supermarkets haven't managed to fully displace is the feria — the open-air produce market that persists in nearly every Chilean neighbourhood, offering fruit and vegetables that are frequently fresher and cheaper than the supermarket equivalent, sold by vendors many locals have bought from for years. The two systems coexist without much tension: Chileans will do their packaged goods, cleaning products, and pantry staples at the supermarket, then walk two blocks to the feria for produce, treating the division of labour between the two as simply how grocery shopping works, rather than a choice requiring justification.

## Botswana: Where the Supermarket Trip Is an Event

Botswana's retail geography reflects one of the most sparsely populated countries on earth — roughly 2.6 million people spread across an area the size of France, much of it Kalahari desert, with population concentrated heavily in and around Gaborone and a handful of other towns. Choppies, a homegrown Botswana-founded chain that's since expanded across the region, competes with South African imports like Pick n Pay and Spar, and together they form the backbone of formal grocery retail — but "backbone" understates how genuinely far apart the vertebrae can be for anyone living outside the main urban centres.

For rural and semi-rural residents, a proper supermarket run isn't a quick errand, it's a planned event, often combined with other town business and stretched across the better part of a day. This shapes shopping behaviour distinctly: bulk buying is the norm rather than the frequent-small-trips pattern common in denser countries, trolleys get genuinely loaded, and the checkout queue becomes as much a social moment — catching up with people you know, since everyone eventually recognises everyone in a town this size — as a transaction to get through quickly.

Supply consistency is a real, practical concern that shapes local shopping psychology: because a meaningful share of packaged and imported goods travels significant distances (much comes via South Africa), stock-outs of specific brands happen and are simply absorbed as normal rather than treated as a retail failure — Batswana shoppers tend to hold brand loyalty loosely, substituting readily rather than making a special trip elsewhere for one specific product. Fresh produce still leans heavily on informal traders and market stalls, particularly for those who can't justify supermarket prices on fruit and vegetables that a roadside seller offers cheaper and fresher, especially outside the capital. The overall culture prizes practicality over ceremony — nobody's debating brands of anything the way a Chilean debates manjar, because the more pressing question is usually just whether the thing you need is in stock at all today.

## The Verdict

Chile's supermarket culture is more sophisticated, better stocked, and frankly more fun — a country that turns a dulce de leche shelf into genuine cultural debate deserves credit for making grocery shopping feel like something more than a chore. But that sophistication comes with a certain sameness, a homogenised, Walmart-adjacent efficiency that, however well executed, isn't all that different from supermarket culture anywhere reasonably developed.

Botswana wins this one for sheer honesty about what a supermarket actually is: infrastructure, not entertainment, and precious infrastructure at that when the nearest one might be forty minutes away. There's something clarifying about a shopping culture where nobody has the luxury of being precious about brand loyalty, where the actual achievement is simply having the shelf stocked at all, and where the checkout queue becomes a genuine community touchpoint rather than something to be rushed through with headphones in. Chile shops better. Botswana shops truer to what shopping actually needs to be for most of human history — communal, practical, and a little bit of an occasion. I'll take the occasion.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/chile — paraphrased: a foreign resident said they didn't understand the intensity of the manjar-brand debate until they made the mistake of buying the "wrong" one for a family gathering and got genuinely ribbed about it for weeks.*

## Conclusion

Chile and Botswana both prove the same point from opposite directions: a supermarket tells you exactly what a country can afford to take for granted. Chile can afford brand loyalty, ceremony, and a genuinely competitive retail market stretched across one of the world's strangest-shaped countries. Botswana can't yet, and has built, instead, a shopping culture rooted in flexibility, bulk-buying sense, and a refusal to be precious about substitutions. One gives you seventeen kinds of manjar to argue about. The other gives you a checkout line where someone might actually ask how your mother's doing. I know which one sounds more like an actual community, and it isn't the one with the loyalty programme.
