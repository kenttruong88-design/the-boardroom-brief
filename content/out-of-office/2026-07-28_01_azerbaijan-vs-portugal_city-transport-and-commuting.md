# The Commute Is a Power Struggle in One Country and a Nap in the Other

🇦🇿 Azerbaijan vs 🇵🇹 Portugal

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Some cities make you fight for your seat. Others simply forget the bus was coming and hope you'll wander off and take a taxi instead. Baku and Lisbon sit at opposite ends of that particular spectrum, and having commuted badly in both, I can confirm neither will apologise for it.

Baku's metro is Soviet marble dressed up as a cathedral, moving people with the ruthless efficiency of a system that has never once considered your feelings. Lisbon's transport, by contrast, is a gorgeous idea let down by a timetable that appears to be more of a mood board. One will get you to work on time and resentful. The other will get you to work forty minutes late and strangely at peace with it. Let's dig in.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇦🇿 Azerbaijan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stand right on the escalator — Baku's are steeper and faster than they look | Block the left side, even for a second, unless you enjoy being tutted at in Azerbaijani |
| Offer your seat to elders without being asked | Assume the metro closes late — it shuts earlier than you'd like, plan around it |
| Keep a few coins or a top-up card for buses, which run separately from the metro system | Expect English signage everywhere outside the city centre stations |

### 🇵🇹 Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Validate your ticket every single time, even on trams packed with tourists | Trust the posted bus timetable as anything more than a suggestion |
| Learn the hills before committing to a route — Lisbon's geography punishes optimism | Take the 28 tram at rush hour expecting a peaceful heritage experience |
| Download a real-time app; the official one lies less than the paper timetable | Stand in the bike lane while waiting, Lisboetas on e-scooters do not brake for confusion |

## Azerbaijan: Punctual, Polished, and Absolutely Not Interested in Your Small Talk

Baku's metro stations look like someone crossed a Tsarist ballroom with a Bond villain's lair, all chandeliers and marble columns, and then ran it with the discipline of a Swiss watch factory. Trains arrive when they say they will. Nobody is late because nobody is permitted to be. The system processes over half a million riders a day through a network that, frankly, punches well above the size of the city it serves, and it does so without a hint of sentimentality.

What nobody tells you is how socially loaded the ride actually is. Azerbaijani commuting culture runs on quiet, firm etiquette: you give up your seat for an elder without being asked twice, you don't eat on the platform, and you absolutely do not block the escalator, because someone behind you has a train to catch and a look that suggests they will not hesitate to say so. It's not unfriendly, exactly. It's efficient in the way a factory floor is efficient — everyone has a job, yours is to not get in the way.

Buses are the messier cousin, running on a separate card system from the metro that catches new arrivals out constantly, myself included, standing at a barrier like an idiot while a queue formed behind me with the patience of people who have seen this exact scene a thousand times before. Traffic above ground is its own event — Baku's roads were not designed for the volume of cars now on them, and drivers treat lane markings as a philosophical suggestion rather than a rule. The metro, mercifully, doesn't care what's happening up there. It just keeps running, on time, indifferent to your jet lag and your feelings about marble ceilings. Expats settle into it fast because there's genuinely nothing to negotiate. You learn the rules once, and the city rewards you with predictability, which after a few months abroad starts to feel like the most romantic thing in the world.

## Portugal: A Beautiful System Having a Long, Sun-Drenched Breakdown

Lisbon's tram network is, visually, one of the great transport experiences on Earth: yellow carriages groaning up impossible gradients past tiled façades, bells clanging, the whole thing looking like a postcard that's somehow still operational. Ride it once as a tourist and you'll understand the appeal instantly. Ride it daily as a commuter and you'll understand why locals have developed a thousand-yard stare specifically reserved for tram stops.

The problem isn't the trams, or the metro, or even the buses individually — it's the collective commitment to timetables as an art form rather than a contract. Carris buses are famous among residents for simply not showing up, no warning, no announcement, just an empty stop and a growing crowd of people checking their phones with the same expression you'd wear at a funeral for a plan that never had a chance. The metro is more reliable, four lines doing honest work under a city that resists straight lines at every opportunity, but even that shuts earlier than a European capital its size really ought to allow.

And then there are the hills. Lisbon is built on seven of them, gloriously, and every commute becomes a small hike regardless of which vehicle you've chosen, because the vehicle will inevitably drop you somewhere below or above where you actually need to be. Locals cope with a mix of fatalism and genuine affection — they'll complain about Carris with real venom and then, in the same breath, tell you there's nowhere else they'd rather be stuck waiting. It's very Portuguese: mild despair, expressed beautifully, resolved with a coffee and a shrug.

## The Verdict

If you want your commute to function like a piece of well-oiled machinery that does not care about your day, Azerbaijan wins, decisively and without argument. Baku's metro is one of the most underrated transit systems on the planet, and its buses, once decoded, are perfectly serviceable. Portugal wins something else entirely — the aesthetics prize, the "I forgive you because you're beautiful" prize, the prize for making people fall in love with a system that routinely humiliates them. If you need to be somewhere at a specific time, book Baku. If you need to feel something on your way to the office, book Lisbon, and bring a book, because you'll have time to read several chapters while waiting for that bus.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/azerbaijan — paraphrased: A user warned newcomers that Baku metro cards and bus cards are entirely separate systems, and no one explains this until you're standing at a barrier holding the wrong one during rush hour.*

> *expat.com Lisbon — paraphrased: A longtime resident described giving up on Carris bus schedules entirely after a year, switching to walking everywhere because "at least my own legs post accurate arrival times."*

> *Internations Baku — paraphrased: An expat noted that Baku's metro etiquette is stricter than it looks from outside — standing on the wrong side of the escalator will get you corrected by a stranger within seconds, every single time.*

## Conclusion

Two cities, two philosophies, and neither one is going to change for you. Baku will get you there on time and mildly intimidated. Lisbon will get you there late and a little bit in love, possibly with a barista, possibly with the idea of never checking a timetable again. Neither is wrong. But if you've got a 9am meeting, I know which flag I'd be checking before I left the flat, and it isn't the one with the tram on the tourist mugs. Choose your commute the way you'd choose a relationship: know what you're actually signing up for, and stop pretending the charming one is also the reliable one. They rarely are.
