# Croatia Rents You a Room From a Grandmother, and Slovenia Rents You a Hayloft and Calls It Policy

🇭🇷 Croatia · 🇸🇮 Slovenia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A hotel is a promise made by a chain. A private room is a promise made by a family. These are different things, and Croatia and Slovenia, two neighbouring Adriatic-adjacent countries that share a border, have each decided which promise they prefer. Croatia sells you a room in someone's house with a plate of figs. Slovenia sells you a farm and tells you the cheese is the main feature.

Both are charming, and both are also quietly alarming in what they reveal. Croatia has so many private beds that its tourism board has begun to treat the count as a national anxiety. Slovenia has a regulatory regime so tidy that renting a flat requires either registration as a landlord or a sole-trader licence, and an unregistered landlord is described by the tax authority as engaged in undeclared work. One country has a flood of informal rooms. The other has a ledger.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇭🇷 Croatia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book July and August early; Dubrovnik, Hvar and Split fill months ahead | Arrive in peak season hoping to find a room by walking around |
| Consider a "sobe", a family room; many can be booked directly | Expect a sobe to be luxurious; they are often basic |
| Ask exactly how far "near the beach" is; it can be a steep 20-minute walk | Trust the brochure's adjectives |
| Check whether your host is registered; owners must use the eVisitor system | Assume every listing is properly licensed |

**🇸🇮 Slovenia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Try a tourist farm (turistična kmetija); there are about 900 | Treat a farm stay as a hotel with animals |
| Look for the Slovenia Green Label | Assume every rural stay is certified |
| Ask your host for a fiscally verified invoice | Accept a handwritten receipt as proof |
| Check Slovenian rules if you rent your own flat; registered landlords are limited to 150 days a year | Rent unregistered and assume nobody will notice |

## 🇭🇷 Croatia: The Bed Count Crisis

Start with the number, because Croatia has. According to Total Croatia News, registered commercial private-accommodation beds stood at 644,079 in 2024 and 667,598 in 2025, then fell to roughly 657,598 in 2026, the first drop, of about 10,000 beds. The tourist board's director said "uncontrolled capacity growth has been successfully stopped". The hidden number is bigger: non-commercial beds, holiday homes and free stays, rose from 651,175 in 2024 to 678,983 in 2025 to about 705,999, and now outnumber beds in formally rented private apartments, concentrated in Zadar County and Primorje-Gorski Kotar. Around 160,000 non-commercial facilities are estimated.

The law says owners of such places must register in the eVisitor system and pay a symbolic tourist tax even if they charge nothing, and the article says many don't comply. About 70 tourist inspectors oversee unregistered accommodation, and a draft Hospitality Act in public consultation would add customs officers and municipal guards and require a unique ID number on every listing, with proposed fines starting at 130,000 euros. That is the regulatory atmosphere: a country trying to catch up with its own hospitality boom.

For the traveller, the cultural centre of gravity is the sobe, the private room rented by a family, often basic, with a host who may offer homemade rakija or figs. Many are booked directly, by a sign on the house, the tourist office or a phone call. Rough Guides gives a price ladder: family-run sobe or budget rooms around €40 to 60, mid-range €80 to 140, luxury from about €170, dorm beds €25 to 35 and camping from €15, with rural retreats near Plitvice or Gorski Kotar at €150 to 200 including meals and activities.

The practical advice is wonderfully specific. Book July and August early, since Dubrovnik, Hvar and Split fill months ahead, with peaks around 25 June, 5 August and Easter week. "Near the beach" can mean a steep 20-minute walk. Cars are mostly banned in the old towns of Rovinj and Korčula. Guesthouse breakfast is usually bread, cheese, ham and coffee. I recommend all of this without reservation, and I recommend asking the host's name before you arrive, because in Croatia the person is part of the product.

## 🇸🇮 Slovenia: The Farm, the Form and the Fine

Slovenia takes accommodation seriously in a different way. The country's tourism board lists about 900 tourist farms, turistične kmetije, and over 400 local food products and dishes, with farm stays carrying a Slovenia Green Label. Types include family farms, cyclist and hiker farms, wine-growing farms and organic farms, with offers running from rooms and apartments to hayloft stays, home-produced food, horse riding and workshops, plus a spring Tourist Farm Week. Named examples include Tourist Farm Kranjc near Kobarid, rated 4.9 on TripAdvisor from 106 reviews, Lenar in Solčava, Pri Marku near Kranj and Pirc in Laško, which holds a perfect 5.0 across 34.

The regional architecture matters too: Karst stone yards, Gorenjska wooden dairy huts, Dolenjska zidanica vineyard cottages and Prekmurje thatched houses. A farm stay in Slovenia is a regional statement, not a generic one. It sells you a landscape and an appetite.

Behind the idyll sits a fastidious regime. The Slovenian tax authority says renting to tourists requires registration either as a registered landlord or as a sole trader. A registered landlord may rent for no more than 150 days, five months, a year to up to fifteen people, and unregistered renting counts as undeclared work with fines of €400 to 30,000. Rental income is self-employment income, with returns due via eDavki by 31 March. Stays booked through foreign platforms such as Airbnb carry 22 per cent VAT, with registration required even below the €50,000 threshold, and each guest needs a fiscally verified invoice. Failing to file a VAT return carries fines of €2,000 to 125,000. I flag that the page was last refreshed in January 2020, so verify before you act on it.

So Slovenia is, in effect, a country that tells you how to be a host, down to the invoice. The result is a hospitality sector that feels more curated and less improvised, and a traveller who is rarely surprised, for better or worse. If Croatia hands you a key and a story, Slovenia hands you a receipt and a cheese.

## The Verdict

Slovenia wins for the traveller who wants to know what they are getting, and Croatia wins for the traveller who wants to meet someone. The Slovenian system gives you farm stays that are certified, invoiced and verifiably charming. The Croatian one gives you grandmothers, figs and a lottery of quality. The first is a product. The second is a relationship.

I will take the relationship, narrowly, and with my fingers crossed. The sobe is the great expressive form of Croatian hospitality, and the bed-count statistics tell you how popular it has become, and how many hosts are doing it without telling the taxman. That is a problem for Croatia and an opportunity for you, since your host has every incentive to make you feel at home.

My sting: Croatia has a hospitality boom it cannot count, and Slovenia has a hospitality industry it cannot stop counting. Choose the chaos if you want a story. Choose the ledger if you want a receipt.

## Conclusion

I have slept in hotels, hostels, a hayloft and, once, a lighthouse, and the thing I remember is never the thread count. It is who handed me the key. Croatia understands that instinctively, even as its regulators scramble to catch up. Slovenia understands it formally, with certification and a Green Label.

Book early in Croatia and ask for the host's name. Book a farm in Slovenia and ask for the cheese. And if you are tempted to rent out your own place in either country, speak to the municipality first, bring a translator and keep every invoice, because the paperwork, unlike the figs, will outlast the summer.
