# Argentina Plants Its Parks With Mate and a Steak Sandwich. Brazil Closes the Road and Declares It a Park

🇦🇷 Argentina vs 🇧🇷 Brazil

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

South America's two giants share a border, a football grudge and a complete disagreement about what a park is for. Argentina inherited the big European idea: a grand green lung with a French landscape architect, a rose garden and a duty to be admired. Brazil, with a coastline to defend and a car problem to solve, looked at its streets and decided they could be parks if you just removed the cars.

Both approaches work. Which one suits you depends on whether you prefer your green space with a fence or without one.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Argentina**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring mate, a flask and something to eat on a Sunday | Arrive empty-handed at a picnic park |
| Walk to your nearest plaza for an afternoon | Treat plazas as anything but social spaces |
| Try the park-stand steak sandwiches with the long queues | Join the empty stall |
| Visit the weekend book and sticker swaps | Miss the fairs in Recoleta and Parque Centenario |

**Brazil**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Walk or cycle Avenida Paulista on a Sunday | Drive there and complain about the closure |
| Check the Rio street closures for your neighbourhood | Assume every street is car-free every day |
| Keep to the busy sections of Parque do Flamengo | Wander the park alone after dark |
| Go early on weekend mornings | Expect solitude at Ibirapuera |

## Argentina

Buenos Aires is a city that regards green space as a civic right, even if the city is mostly concrete. A research article in The Nature of Cities, based on a survey of 232 park and plaza users, found that plazas are small, up to a hectare, and used by locals, while parks exceed two hectares and draw people from further away. Physical and recreational activity accounted for 28 percent of use in parks against 8 percent in plazas, which were preferred for relaxing and walking. Most park visitors did not live nearby; they travelled to reach safe, high-quality green space. The authors trace the large parks to the late nineteenth century and French and English influence, while Spanish colonial cities had small, dry plazas.

The Buenos Aires Portal fills in the geography. Parque Tres de Febrero, the Palermo Woods, covers about 370 hectares, largely designed by Carlos Thays. Within it, Plaza Holanda has a mile-long running track, El Rosedal has some 18,000 roses, and the Ecoparque occupies the former zoo. The Reserva Ecológica Costanera Sur, at 21 hectares, is said to host around 75 percent of the city's birdlife. Parque Rivadavia's Paseo de los Libros has about a hundred second-hand book kiosks, and on weekends locals swap duplicate football stickers there. Artisan fairs run in Recoleta and Parque Centenario, and free weekend fitness classes are offered in Palermo.

The best first-person account is Lara Dunston's. The travel writer lived in Palermo and wrote about a spring Sunday spent walking ten minutes from her apartment to the parks. She named Bosques de Palermo her favourite and nicknamed the Botanical Garden "kitty-kat park" for its cats. Locals rollerbladed, cycled, did yoga and picnicked with mate gourds and flasks, empanadas in butcher's paper and park-stand steak sandwiches (her tip: pick the stall with the longest queue), washed down with Malbec or beer.

Argentine green space is a stage for the weekend ritual. It is designed, planted and organised, and it is used with warmth by whole families who bring their own supplies. A revitalisation programme over roughly a decade added free outdoor gyms and instructor-led classes, according to the research. The park, in Buenos Aires, is a civic habit.

## Brazil

Brazil's approach is cleverer and more improvised. It takes existing streets and gives them back. An Expat.com guide to São Paulo explains that Avenida Paulista, 2.7 kilometres long, is closed to cars on Sundays, usually from nine in the morning to four in the afternoon, for walking and cycling. The same guide describes Ibirapuera Park as free to enter for running, walking, cycling and picnics, with bike hire and some attractions costing extra. A Culture Trip essay says Paulistas flock to parks like Ibirapuera on weekends after weekdays among buildings and pavement. Further out, the Pedra Grande trail in Cantareira is about six or seven kilometres, steep, paid and sometimes closed in bad weather, and the Guarapiranga Reservoir offers urban "beaches" and a municipal sailing programme for children from seven.

Rio takes the idea further. The Rio Times reports that the Área de Lazer programme closes streets to traffic on Sundays and holidays from seven in the morning to six in the evening, seven in summer. The beachfront avenues, Atlântica in Copacabana, Vieira Souto in Ipanema and Delfim Moreira in Leblon, are included, and Avenida Infante Dom Henrique closes through the Aterro do Flamengo, where locals play sport. Residents who want their own street closed need signatures from two-thirds of the residential units and approval from the Military Police battalion. The city has published no footfall data.

The Aterro, or Parque do Flamengo, was designed by Roberto Burle Marx and has playgrounds, tennis and basketball courts and jogging and cycling paths. A Fodor's reviewer warns against wandering the park after dark and advises sticking to busy sections even in the daytime. I mention it because every Brazilian city has a version of the same caveat. The green space is magnificent, the safety is conditional, and the locals know exactly where to stand.

I found no first-person Brazilian expat accounts I could verify, so the Brazilian picture comes from guides and a lifestyle essay. The pattern is clear enough. Brazil's parks are a weekend event and a street takeover, while Argentina's are a weekly institution.

A last point on use. The research behind The Nature of Cities found that parks and plazas serve different purposes: plazas for sitting and walking, parks for activity. That matches what the Buenos Aires Portal describes, from the free weekend fitness classes in Palermo to the long, shaded avenues for running. Brazil lumps the two together in a single weekly street takeover, which is efficient, democratic and slightly chaotic. If you are a planner, you will find Buenos Aires soothing. If you are a spontaneous sort, you will find Brazil liberating.

## The Verdict

Argentina has the better parks. Brazil has the better idea. Buenos Aires gives you a formal, shaded, culturally rich green network, with a rose garden and a second-hand book market for good measure. Brazil's open-street approach is more democratic and cheaper to maintain, but it leaves you with a Sunday-only park and a few well-known zones to avoid after dark.

If you have children and want a regular, safe, predictable place to spend weekends, choose Buenos Aires. If you are fit, social and want your exercise on a coastal avenue, choose Rio. If you want the best of both, pick the Argentine routine and the Brazilian ingenuity.

My own vote goes to Argentina for the plazas and the picnic culture. A country that hands you a mate gourd and a steak sandwich in a park has understood what outdoors are for. Brazil, though, wins the prize for audacity. Closing a street and calling it a park is the sort of thing only a country with a sense of humour could come up with.

## Conclusion

A park is a promise a city makes to its people. Argentina keeps it with trees, roses and an impeccable picnic. Brazil keeps it with a closed road and a good attitude.

Take either and you will end up outdoors more than at home. That alone justifies the move.

Just bring your own flask, stay in the busy bits after dark, and never, ever join the short queue at the steak sandwich stand.
