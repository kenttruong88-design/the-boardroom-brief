# Switzerland Schedules Its Fun. Egypt's Fun Schedules You.

🇨🇭 Switzerland vs 🇪🇬 Egypt — Festival and Public Holiday Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Switzerland is the only country I've lived in where I have genuinely needed a spreadsheet to track which canton is celebrating what, because national unity is apparently for other people and Basel will absolutely observe a holiday that Geneva has never heard of. Egypt, meanwhile, runs on a lunar calendar that shifts eleven days earlier every year, meaning Ramadan can land in the depths of winter or the peak of a 45-degree summer depending entirely on the whims of the moon, and everyone just... copes.

One country treats its calendar like a Swiss watch — precise, cantonal, occasionally maddening in its regional variation but never actually unpredictable. The other treats its calendar like a living, breathing negotiation with the sky. Guess which one produces better street food.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇨🇭 Switzerland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your specific canton's holiday calendar — don't assume national uniformity | Plan a business trip assuming August 1st (National Day) behaves like it does elsewhere |
| Embrace Fasnacht if you're in Basel or Lucerne — it is deranged, glorious, and worth the sleep loss | Expect shops to open on a Sunday, holiday or not; Swiss Sunday closures are near-absolute |
| Book restaurants weeks ahead for any cantonal festival — locals plan with famous precision | Confuse a Protestant canton's calendar with a Catholic canton's; the gaps are real and old |
| Bring earplugs to any village Alphorn or cowbell festival — the volume is not a metaphor | Assume "public holiday" means banks, post offices, and pharmacies are all shut identically |

**🇪🇬 Egypt**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Adjust your entire schedule around Ramadan — working hours, traffic, and mood all shift | Eat, drink, or smoke visibly in public during fasting hours, even if you're not Muslim |
| Say "Eid Mubarak" generously — it costs nothing and buys enormous warmth | Expect a fixed date for Eid or Ramadan; the sighting-based lunar calendar shifts every year |
| Enjoy the iftar generosity — strangers genuinely will invite you to break the fast with them | Book anything requiring quick bureaucratic turnaround during the week before or after Eid |
| Lean into Moulid street festivals if you get the chance — loud, sugar-fuelled, unmissable | Assume Cairo's rhythm during Ramadan nights (very awake) matches its daytime rhythm (subdued) |

## Switzerland: Precision, Except When It Absolutely Isn't

Switzerland's reputation for order does hold — mostly. August 1st, National Day, is genuinely nationwide, genuinely reliable, and genuinely involves bonfires on hillsides in a country otherwise famous for extremely controlled use of fire. But the moment you look past that one unifying date, Switzerland reveals itself as twenty-six cantons that have collectively agreed to disagree about nearly everything else on the calendar. Basel's Fasnacht — three days of drumming, piccolo playing, and enormous papier-mâché heads starting at four in the morning — is one of the most disorienting, wonderful things I have ever witnessed, and it means absolutely nothing forty minutes away in a canton that didn't get the memo.

This regional patchwork traces back to old religious lines: Catholic cantons and Protestant cantons kept different saints' days and observances for centuries, and Switzerland's famous compromise-first political culture never bothered flattening them into one national list, because why would it, when the alternative is letting each canton keep exactly what it wants. The result is an expat learning curve nobody warns you about: you cannot assume a public holiday in Zurich applies in Ticino, and moving between German-speaking and French-speaking regions means effectively re-learning the festival calendar from scratch.

What never varies, gloriously, is the closure. Swiss Sundays are sacred regardless of canton — shops shut, and shut hard, holiday or not — and the discipline with which an entire nation of famously industrious people agrees to simply stop is, frankly, the most impressive display of collective willpower I've witnessed anywhere. You will not find a country more capable of throwing an enormous, chaotic, three-day street party and then returning to absolute punctual order on the Tuesday morning as if nothing happened.

## Egypt: The Calendar That Answers to the Moon, Not to You

Egypt operates on an entirely different logic, and adjusting to it is less a scheduling inconvenience than a philosophical realignment. Ramadan and the two Eids follow the Islamic lunar calendar, which shifts roughly eleven days earlier each Gregorian year — meaning the holiday you planned around last year lands somewhere completely different this year, and the exact start date is still, genuinely, confirmed by moon-sighting committees rather than fixed years in advance. You cannot out-plan this. You simply adjust, every year, along with 110 million other people.

What makes Egypt remarkable isn't the disruption, though there is plenty — working hours contract, government offices slow to a crawl, and traffic transforms into something with its own gravitational field in the hour before iftar as the entire country races to be home for the fast-breaking meal. What makes it remarkable is the generosity that comes with it. Strangers invite you to break the fast with them. Entire streets set up communal iftar tables, feeding anyone who shows up, tourist or local, without ceremony. The nights during Ramadan come alive in a way the days simply don't — cafés and markets bustling well past midnight, because the entire rhythm of daily life has flipped to accommodate the fast.

Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha bring their own explosions of color and sound: new clothes, sweets pressed into your hands by people you've just met, and Moulid festivals — saints' day celebrations layered with pre-Islamic folk traditions — that get genuinely, joyfully out of hand with music and sugar. Nobody schedules Egypt's festival calendar around your convenience. You schedule yourself around it, and the payoff for doing so with grace and respect is a level of hospitality that Swiss precision, for all its charms, has never once offered me.

## The Verdict

Switzerland gives you a calendar you can plan against, down to the canton, with the caveat that the plan changes depending on which side of an invisible cultural line you're standing on. Egypt gives you a calendar that refuses to be planned against at all, and asks you instead to surrender to its rhythm — a genuinely harder adjustment, and a genuinely richer one. Basel's Fasnacht will astonish you for three days a year. Cairo during Ramadan will quietly restructure how you think about hospitality, community, and the very idea of a "holiday" being something you experience alone.

If forced to choose the country that teaches you more about what a festival is actually for: Egypt, without hesitation. Precision is a Swiss gift. Generosity under a fasting sun is an Egyptian one, and it's the harder trick to pull off.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com Egypt forum — paraphrased: a new arrival was warned that their office would effectively grind to a stop for the two weeks bracketing Eid, with government paperwork best avoided entirely during that window rather than merely expected to run slow.*

> *Quora, thread on Swiss National Day — paraphrased: a longtime Geneva resident noted that August 1st bonfires and fireworks felt genuinely nationwide, but every other "holiday" they'd assumed was universal turned out to be a Zurich-only or Basel-only affair they'd mistakenly generalized.*

> *InterNations Switzerland community — paraphrased: a member admitted they'd shown up to work in a different canton on what they thought was a normal Tuesday, only to find every shop shuttered for a religious observance their home canton didn't celebrate at all.*

## Conclusion

Switzerland and Egypt sit at opposite ends of the same question: who controls the calendar, you or the culture? Switzerland lets you control it, mostly, provided you accept that "mostly" comes with twenty-six regional asterisks. Egypt takes control away from you entirely and hands it to the moon, and somehow that surrender produces the warmer, more memorable experience. I'll take a chaotic iftar table full of strangers over a beautifully punctual bonfire any day — though I will still be checking a cantonal calendar before I book anything in Zurich, because some lessons about precision are worth keeping.
