# One Country Will Sit Next to You Uninvited and Insist on Tea. The Other Will Cut the Queue, Shrug, and Consider the Matter Closed

🇯🇴 Jordan vs Vietnam 🇻🇳

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Jordan operates on the principle that personal space is a Western neurosis that Arab hospitality has thankfully never adopted, so expect company, conversation, and probably tea, whether you asked for any of it. Vietnam operates on the principle that a queue is a suggestion, noise is a form of togetherness, and traffic etiquette exists mainly to be politely, constantly ignored. Both countries will violate whatever boundaries you brought with you from home. Neither one is being rude. You're just not from here.

I've been interrupted mid-sunset by a stranger wanting to chat in Jordan and I've been elbowed out of a queue in Hanoi by someone twice my age who didn't even make eye contact. Both, apparently, completely normal Tuesdays.

## Do's & Don'ts: Jordan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept an offer of tea or coffee graciously — declining outright reads as genuinely impolite, not just distant | Expect anything resembling a formal queue — pushing forward is the norm, not an exception |
| Expect closer conversational distance than you're used to, and don't visibly recoil from it | Try to convey "I want to be alone" through body language alone — it often just doesn't register as a signal here |
| Bring a small token of appreciation if someone hosts you, rather than offering money, which can cause real offence | Assume a stranger striking up conversation wants anything from you — it's frequently just genuine hospitality |

## Do's & Don'ts: Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stay calm in traffic and in queues at ticket counters or restaurants, where more orderly behaviour is actually expected | Show road rage or visible frustration at queue-jumping — it accomplishes nothing and reads as an overreaction |
| Accept that cafés and public spaces run loud — 85 to 95 decibels in busy ones — and it's not considered a problem | Expect quiet conversation volume in shared public spaces; raising your voice slightly is often just how it's done |
| Recognise that some queue-jumping (supermarkets, petrol stations, airports) is simply the norm, not an insult aimed at you | Interpret staring or close proximity in a crowded café as rudeness — it's a different, non-hostile read of shared space |

## Jordan: Hospitality as a Boundary-Dissolving Force

Let's be direct about the thing that catches every first-time visitor off guard: personal space, as a concept, does not function here the way it does in the West, and this is not an oversight — it's a completely different value system, and a genuinely warmer one, even when it's inconvenient. Conversations happen at a proximity that would read as intrusive elsewhere. Strangers will sit down next to you at a viewpoint, at a café table, on a park bench, entirely uninvited, purely because they find you interesting and see no reason not to say so. Attempting to signal "I'd like to be alone" through the usual Western toolkit — closed body language, curt replies, avoided eye contact — frequently just doesn't register, because the entire cultural framework assumes company is welcome by default.

This same expansiveness shows up as genuine, structural hospitality that goes well beyond politeness into actual social obligation. Offering tea or coffee to a guest isn't a nicety — declining it can be read as a real rebuff, and Jordanians take real pride in making visitors feel instantly, thoroughly welcome. If you're invited somewhere, the correct response to that generosity is not cash — offering money for hospitality can be deeply offensive, treated almost as an insult to the sincerity of the gesture. A small gift, a token, something that acknowledges the relationship rather than transacting it, is the appropriate move.

Queuing, meanwhile, barely exists as a concept. Lines form loosely, if at all, and hanging back out of politeness simply invites someone else to step ahead of you — this isn't rudeness by local standards, it's just an entirely different social contract around shared resources, one where deference doesn't earn you anything and assertiveness isn't considered aggressive. Visitors who bring their home country's queue etiquette here will simply wait longer, get served later, and feel vaguely aggrieved about a rule nobody else in the room is actually playing by.

## Vietnam: Loud, Fast, and Entirely Unbothered by Your Discomfort

Vietnam's public behaviour norms make the most sense once you accept a single premise: this is one of the most densely populated countries on earth, and "personal space" as an organising social value simply never had room to develop the way it did in quieter, less crowded cultures. Cafés in cities like Hanoi and Da Nang routinely hit noise levels comparable to standing beside heavy traffic — 85 to 95 decibels — and this isn't treated as a design flaw or an annoyance. It's simply understood as what a lively, social public space sounds like. Local commentary even jokes that there's no such thing as a quiet coffee in Vietnam, and they're not wrong, and they're also not bothered by it in the slightest.

Queue behaviour splits in a genuinely interesting way depending on context. In more urban, formalised settings — ticket counters, restaurants — people do actually queue with reasonable patience. But in supermarkets, at petrol stations, in airports, passive nudging, mild shoving, and outright queue-jumping are expected, accepted, and not read as a personal slight against you specifically. It's simply how limited resources get allocated when everyone's in a hurry and nobody's especially precious about order for its own sake. Visitors who get visibly outraged by a cut queue are, correctly, seen as overreacting to something locals process as background noise.

Traffic deserves its own mention because it is, genuinely, a defining feature of Vietnamese public life — dense, constant, seemingly chaotic to an outside eye, yet running on an unspoken flow logic that mostly works as long as everyone stays predictable and nobody panics. Road rage here accomplishes precisely nothing except making you the visibly stressed outlier in a system everyone else has already made peace with. The correct move, always, is to stay calm, keep moving at a steady pace, and trust that the chaos resolves itself, because it reliably does.

## The Verdict

Jordan wins on warmth — a culture this insistently, structurally hospitable is rare, and even when it overrides your personal boundaries, it's doing so out of genuine generosity rather than indifference. Vietnam wins on honesty about density — a country this crowded simply cannot afford Western-style personal space norms, and rather than pretend otherwise, it's built an entire functional social fabric around loud, fast, occasionally pushy proximity that somehow still works. If you want to be genuinely, overwhelmingly welcomed whether you asked for it or not, Jordan. If you want a masterclass in staying calm while a queue, a café, and an intersection all simultaneously ignore whatever personal space you thought you were entitled to, Vietnam will happily teach you, loudly, immediately, and without apology.

## Conclusion

Jordan and Vietnam both dismantle the idea that personal space and orderly queues are cultural universals — they're not, they're just habits some countries happened to formalise and others never bothered adopting. One replaces distance with hospitality so sincere it borders on relentless. The other replaces quiet, orderly queuing with a dense, loud, functional chaos that mostly sorts itself out if you stop fighting it. Arrive in either expecting your usual boundaries to hold and you'll spend your whole trip irritated. Arrive willing to be pulled into a conversation or nudged out of a queue, and you'll actually start to understand what's going on.
