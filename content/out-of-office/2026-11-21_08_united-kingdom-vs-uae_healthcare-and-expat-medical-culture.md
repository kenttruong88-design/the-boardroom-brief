# In Britain You Queue for the Doctor; in the UAE the Doctor Queues for You, Pending Insurance Approval

🇬🇧 United Kingdom vs 🇦🇪 UAE

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The British relationship with the National Health Service is a love affair conducted entirely through complaint. Every Briton believes the NHS is falling apart, and every Briton would sooner give up their passport than see it changed. You register with a local GP, you wait for an appointment, you describe your symptoms in the first eight minutes of a ten-minute slot and you leave with a leaflet and a sense of stoic grievance.

In the UAE, the doctor is a service, the hospital is a brand and the receptionist is a salesperson with a tablet. You pick a clinic from your insurer's network, you book a slot the same day, you are greeted by someone who calls you sir or madam without irony, and you leave with a prescription and a bill whose co-payment is cheerfully small. It is a medical experience from a different planet, and the first time you have it, you will feel a pang of disloyalty to the country that brought you up. I know I did.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇬🇧 United Kingdom ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a local GP as soon as you arrive | Wait until you are ill to look for a surgery |
| Use pharmacies and 111 for minor problems | Go to A&E with a sore throat |
| Be precise and concise in your appointment | Bring a list of nine unrelated ailments |
| Keep up with your prescription reviews | Assume a repeat prescription renews itself |

| 🇦🇪 UAE ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check what your employer insurance actually covers | Assume dental and optical are included |
| Declare pre-existing conditions fully | Hide history to keep the premium down |
| Shop around until you find a doctor you like | Stay with the first clinic out of politeness |
| Check import rules for any controlled medicines | Bring a year of prescription drugs and hope |

## 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

The NHS is, for all its faults, a magnificent idea. It is free at the point of use, it does not ask about your credit limit and it has stood between countless Britons and catastrophe. Its defining feature is that it is a system of gatekeeping: you see your GP first, the GP decides whether you need a specialist, and a referral begins a waiting process that can take weeks or months. It is orderly, rational and, at its worst, agonising.

For the expat, the shock is in the structure. You register with a GP surgery in your catchment area, you are given an appointment in a window of time, and you are expected to have a reasonably firm idea of what you want from the encounter. The culture is understated to the point of stoicism. A British patient will announce that they have "a bit of a twinge" when they are in the throes of a medical emergency. There is a deep reluctance to waste anyone's time, which makes the system resilient and, in some cases, dangerous.

What you gain is security. A British expat returning home after years abroad knows that, whatever happens, they will not be asked to produce a card before receiving emergency care. The price of that security is patience: long waits, thin appointment times and an administrative machine that assumes you will cope. And to be fair to the machine, most of us do. I have met expats who have fled the waiting lists, and expats who have come home specifically to be ill in peace. It is the most British thing in the world to be annoyed with and devoted to the same institution.

The cultural gap is also about attitude. British patients are trained to apologise to their doctors, to under-report their symptoms and to leave without asking the one question they came to ask. It is a charming national habit and, I am told by clinicians, a slightly dangerous one. Patients in the Gulf tend to arrive better informed, more assertive and more inclined to request a scan, which is the point at which the British expat is torn between mild horror and quiet envy. You will get a scan, you will get a second opinion, and you will, quite possibly, get an upsell.

## 🇦🇪 UAE

The UAE system is built on insurance, and it works on the premise that choice is the main virtue. Health insurance has been mandatory for all residents since January 2025, according to ExpatFocus, and employers must provide it for private-sector staff. In Dubai the requirement covers employees; in Abu Dhabi it extends to family members. A basic plan costs about 320 dirhams a year, with a 25 per cent outpatient co-payment capped at 100 dirhams per visit, and inpatient care at 20 per cent, capped at 500 per visit and 1,000 per year. A private consultation, ExpatFocus reports, costs around 300 to 400 dirhams, against 100 to 200 at a public facility.

The most significant difference is that there is no catchment-based GP registration. You can walk into any clinic in your insurer's network, which makes the system feel remarkably fluid. One Expat Exchange member in the UAE said it took a few tries to find a doctor they were happy with and recommended shopping around, but noted two private hospitals within ten kilometres, a small fee per visit, generic medicines and antibiotics available over the counter. It is a consumer experience, and it is a good one.

The catch is that it is an insurance experience. Cigna's regional chief executive, Leah Cotterill, told Gulf News that the move from the NHS to an insurance-led system often comes as a surprise to new arrivals, who are used to getting mental health and maternity care without thinking about limits or approvals. She also noted that the average insured person visits a doctor about five times a year, over 20 per cent more than a few years ago, and urged patients to declare pre-existing conditions fully. This is an insurer talking, so apply a pinch of salt. But the warning is sound: dental and optical are excluded from the basic plan, prescriptions from overseas are not automatically recognised, and controlled medicines need prior approval.

The mix of people in a Gulf hospital is another surprise. The staff come from dozens of countries, and a typical clinic might have a Filipino nurse, an Indian specialist, a British GP and an Egyptian receptionist. That makes for a medical culture that is unusually international and, for an expat, rather comforting: there is almost always someone in the building who understands your accent. The medical fitness tests required before a residency visa can be granted are a quirky initiation, involving blood tests and, in Abu Dhabi, a chest X-ray. It is a rite of passage with a needle.

## The Verdict

The UAE wins on convenience, and Britain wins on the bit that matters if you are really ill. If you are young, healthy and employed, the Gulf system is delightful: quick, courteous and cheap in the way a good hotel is cheap. If you have a chronic condition, a complicated history or an employer who selects the cheapest insurer in the region, you may find that the beautiful hospital lobby disguises a bureaucratic maze.

I would not say that either system is better. I would say that they ask you different questions. The NHS asks: is it serious enough? The UAE asks: is it covered? It is a difference in philosophy, and it explains why British expats in the Gulf spend their first year being delighted and their second year reading the small print. My advice is to enjoy the first year and read the policy before the second begins.

## Conclusion

In Britain, you will be treated like a citizen and made to wait like one. In the UAE, you will be treated like a customer and invoiced like one. Both are valid arrangements, and both come with a hidden cost: the NHS costs you time, and the UAE costs you attention to detail. Whichever you choose, register early, declare everything, keep your paperwork and be nice to the receptionist. She knows where the appointments are, and she knows exactly how much you can complain.
