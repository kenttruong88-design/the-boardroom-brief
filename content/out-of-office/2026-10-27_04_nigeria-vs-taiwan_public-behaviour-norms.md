# Nigeria Fights For The Front Of The Line And Taiwan Will Never Forgive You For Breathing Too Loudly

🇳🇬 Nigeria vs 🇹🇼 Taiwan

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Everyone who has never left their hometown loves to tell you that Nigerians are "loud" and Taiwanese people are "reserved," as though public behaviour were a personality quiz you could answer from an airport lounge. It isn't. It's infrastructure, grief, colonial history, humidity, and population density wearing a human face, and it shows up most nakedly in the three places no guidebook prepares you for: the queue, the decibel level, and the distance a stranger is willing to stand from your elbow.

In Lagos, the queue is a theory, loosely observed, enforced mostly by whoever has the loudest voice and the least patience; in Taipei, the queue is a civic sacrament, marked out in floor paint, and breaking it is treated with the quiet horror usually reserved for arson. One country will talk over you with warmth and total confidence. The other will say nothing at all while recalibrating its entire opinion of your character. Neither is being polite. Both think they're right. I've lived in both long enough to tell you who actually is.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Nigeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet first, always — "good morning" before any request, or you'll be read as rude, not efficient | Don't assume a queue is binding; treat it as an opening position, not a commandment |
| Match the energy — a loud, animated negotiation is normal, not a fight | Don't flinch when someone touches your arm mid-conversation; it's enthusiasm, not aggression |
| Expect strangers, conductors, and security men to insert themselves into your business uninvited | Don't lodge a quiet complaint about 6am generator or church noise and expect sympathy |
| Haggle like you mean it — a flat "no" is an invitation to counter | Don't stand silently in a lift; greeting fellow passengers is the unwritten rule |

**Taiwan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Queue inside the painted floor markers at MRT doors as though your visa depended on it | Don't eat or drink on the MRT; you will be stared down by an entire carriage in perfect silence |
| Drop your voice in enclosed public spaces — trains, waiting rooms, lifts, lobbies | Don't touch, back-slap, or stand closer than arm's length to someone you've just met |
| Take your numbered ticket, wait your turn, trust that the system will actually work | Don't jaywalk or nose ahead at a crossing; fines are real and cameras are everywhere |
| Thank the bus driver audibly on your way out; it's expected, not optional | Don't raise your voice in a dispute — you lose the argument and your social standing in one move |

## Nigeria

Lagos does not have quiet hours, and it does not want them. The city runs on generators, okada horns, church loudspeakers, market callers, and the general conviction that if you have something to say, you should say it at a volume that guarantees it is heard over the generator next door — because there is always a generator next door, the national grid having spent decades auditioning for the role of "optional extra." Noise here isn't a failure of manners; it's a communication strategy in a country where infrastructure can't be trusted to carry information for you, so people carry it themselves, loudly, in person, with their whole chest.

The queue tells the same story. Nigerians will form an orderly line at an airport check-in desk with genuine sincerity, and then abandon it entirely the second a second attendant opens a window, because the social contract was always conditional on the system holding up its end — and the system rarely does. Lagos state authorities have periodically shut down dozens of churches, mosques and clubs over noise-pollution complaints, which tells you two things simultaneously: that the volume is loud enough to become a documented civic emergency, and that nobody involved considers this a reason to turn it down.

Personal space, meanwhile, barely exists as a concept, and this is not an oversight — it's intimacy by default. A market trader will grab your wrist to redirect your attention to better tomatoes. A bus conductor will physically rearrange you to fit one more body in. An auntie you've known for four minutes will comment on your weight, your marital status, and your life choices, standing close enough to share your next meal. The British obsession with a polite metre of distance reads, in Lagos, as coldness bordering on suspicion. You are expected to be touched, addressed, and occasionally hollered at, and the correct response is to holler back with equal warmth, not retreat into the silence you may have packed from home.

## Taiwan

Taipei runs on an almost devotional respect for the collective nervous system, and nowhere is that clearer than on the MRT, where painted lines on the platform tell you exactly where to stand, commuters split instinctively to let exiting passengers through before boarding, and an entire carriage will fall into library-grade silence that no amount of Western small talk can crack. Eating a packet of crisps on the train isn't just frowned upon — it's a punishable offence, and the fine exists because Taiwanese society decided, correctly, that shared space is a kind of shared body, and you don't season someone else's lungs with your lunch.

Queueing is not merely practised here; it is performed with the seriousness of ritual. People queue for bubble tea, for temple blessings, for the night market's best beef noodle stall, standing in lines that stretch around corners with nobody checking their phone in protest, because the queue is understood as fair, functioning, and worth the wait — unlike Lagos, where the system's unreliability makes jumping it a rational act of self-preservation. Taiwan's own Gold Card programme, in briefing newcomers on business etiquette, stresses exactly this: harmony, restraint, and reading a room before you speak in it, because a raised voice in a dispute doesn't win you the argument — it loses you face, and face, once lost, is expensive to buy back.

Personal space in Taiwan is a quiet perimeter most locals will not breach without an apology pre-loaded. Strangers do not touch you. They do not comment on your body. They will, however, judge you in total silence for talking too loudly on a phone call, for letting a door swing shut on someone, or for standing one centimetre too close in an elevator — judgment delivered via a look so brief and so devastating you won't notice it happened until you're lying awake that night wondering what you did wrong.

## The Verdict

If you want a culture that tells you exactly where you stand — loudly, immediately, with your elbow already being nudged out of the way — Nigeria wins on honesty. Nobody in Lagos is quietly resenting you; they're actively, audibly resenting you, which at least saves time. If you want a culture where the rules are fair, enforced, and genuinely make daily life function, Taiwan wins on competence, no contest. The MRT queue system alone does more for human dignity than most national infrastructure projects.

But "better public behaviour" isn't really the question — it's which discomfort you'd rather live inside. Nigeria will exhaust you with noise and closeness until you either toughen up or go home early. Taiwan will starve you of both until you start craving a stranger's unsolicited opinion on your love life just to feel something. I'll take Taipei's silence over Lagos's generator at 6am, every single time — my ears, unlike my tolerance for personal commentary, are not renewable.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat Exchange contributor — describes arriving in Lagos expecting "culture shock" to mean food or language, only to discover it actually meant recalibrating an entire sense of personal space and queue etiquette within the first week.*

> *A Tales from the State Department spouse community post on Lagos — recounts how generator noise becomes so constant that silence itself starts to feel suspicious, like something has gone wrong with the building rather than right.*

> *A Fund for Education Abroad exchange student's journal on Taiwan — notes that the hardest adjustment wasn't the language but learning to stand utterly still and silent on a packed MRT platform while every local instinct screamed to fill the quiet with small talk.*

## Conclusion

Here's the sting nobody wants served with their travel advice: both countries think the other one is doing it wrong, and both are partly correct. Nigeria thinks Taiwan's silence is cold; Taiwan thinks Nigeria's noise is chaos. Neither will apologise, and neither should, because both systems work exactly as designed for the people who built them — you're just the visitor who has to adapt, not the other way round. So drop the word "exotic," pack a thicker skin for Lagos or a quieter voice for Taipei, and stop pretending you can waltz into either expecting your home country's version of "normal" to apply. It won't. It never does. That's rather the point of leaving.
