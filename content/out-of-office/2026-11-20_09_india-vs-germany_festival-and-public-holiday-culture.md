# India Celebrates Everything at Once and Germany Celebrates Everything on a Schedule and Then Closes the Shops

🇮🇳 India vs 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

India has more festivals than a calendar can comfortably hold. Diwali lights the country, Holi turns it technicolour, Eid, Christmas, Navratri, Pongal, Onam, Durga Puja and a hundred regional celebrations fill the year with noise, sweets, colour and the sort of disruption that makes planning a meeting feel like an act of optimism. A festival in India is not an event you attend. It is a condition you live inside, along with your neighbours, your relatives and a loudspeaker.

Germany has public holidays the way it has trains: carefully scheduled, regionally variable and capable of shutting down the entire country with remarkable efficiency. A holiday in Germany is an instruction. Shops close, streets empty, a hush falls over the town and a person who has forgotten to buy milk stands in the dark outside a locked supermarket, reconsidering their decisions.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept festival invitations and bring sweets | Arrive empty-handed at a celebration |
| Plan around travel peaks and regional holidays | Book a train the week before Diwali |
| Join in, even if you are a spectator at first | Stand at the edge of the crowd with your arms folded |
| Respect local customs around dress and food | Assume that every festival is celebrated the same way everywhere |

**Germany**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the holiday calendar for your federal state | Assume public holidays are the same nationwide |
| Stock up on groceries before Sundays and holidays | Expect shops to open |
| Respect quiet hours, especially on Sundays | Hold a loud party or run a drill on a day of rest |
| Visit a Christmas market and a local festival | Treat them as tourist traps |

## India

India's festival calendar is the product of thousands of years, many religions and a spectacular disregard for the idea that one festival should wait politely for another. Diwali, the festival of lights, is perhaps the best known abroad: homes are cleaned, lamps lit, sweets exchanged and fireworks set off with enthusiasm. Holi, the festival of colours, spills into the streets. Eid, Christmas, Gurpurab and many others are woven into the shared life of a country where religion and community overlap in public.

Public holidays are a mixture of national dates and state-specific observances, and employers vary in how they handle them. The effect is that a typical year contains many more days of celebration than the official list suggests, and a city can change character overnight. Streets are decorated. Markets overflow. Families travel. Offices empty.

The emotional weight of a festival is hard to overstate. It is about family, food, memory and belonging. The sweet shop becomes the centre of the universe, and the act of visiting relatives is both a duty and a joy. For Indians living abroad, missing a festival at home is one of the sharpest forms of homesickness.

The cost is the chaos. Travel is crowded, noise is constant and the line between celebration and nuisance can feel thin, especially during the fireworks season. But the energy is infectious. Nobody in India celebrates quietly, and nobody expects you to.

Food is the heart of any festival, and in India it is inseparable from memory. Sweets are made in huge quantities, gifted in boxes and compared with the seriousness of a wine tasting. Even the simplest home becomes a restaurant for a few days, and no guest leaves hungry. Newcomers learn quickly that refusing food is not an option, and that the polite response to a third helping is a fourth.

## Germany

Germany's holiday calendar is built on a mix of national dates and state-specific ones, and which you get depends on where you live. Some states observe religious holidays that others do not, and a long weekend in one region can be an ordinary working week fifty kilometres away. University information sheets and expat guides all make the same point: check your state before you plan anything.

Sundays and public holidays are protected. Most shops, supermarkets and many services are closed, and the country takes the idea of a day of rest seriously enough to regulate noise. For an expat from a country where shops open at all hours, the first closed Sunday is a small culture shock, and the first holiday weekend without milk is a larger one.

But Germany does festivals very well, in a way that is both organised and heartfelt. Christmas markets, with their mulled wine, roasted almonds and glowing stalls, are a genuine highlight. Carnival, particularly in the Rhineland, is an exuberant explosion of costumes and parades. Beer festivals, harvest festivals and local fairs fill the summer. There is a strong tradition of community celebrations, and they are warmer than the stereotype of German reserve would suggest.

For Indian expats, the guides recommend a double approach: embrace German traditions, and find ways to celebrate your own. Diwali is not a public holiday in Germany, but communities hold events, temples organise gatherings and friends cook for each other. It will not be the same, but it can still be good. Bring the sweets to work and watch the colleagues learn the word for them.

Work and school calendars follow the holidays closely in Germany, and long weekends are planned months in advance. Bridge days, when a holiday falls next to a weekend and people take a single day off to link them, are a national sport. If you book travel at the last minute you will pay for it, so plan early and keep an eye on the school holiday dates of the state you are in.

## The Verdict

India wins on spirit. Nobody does celebration like it: the colour, the food, the sense of an entire society participating at once. The year feels like a procession.

Germany wins on rest. A country that closes properly on a Sunday is a country that understands recovery, and the public holidays, while fewer in spirit, are used rather than fought through.

I give the trophy to India for festivals and to Germany for holidays, and I would remind the expat that these are different things. One is a party. The other is a pause. A healthy life requires both, and the only truly unhappy people are those who have to move between them with an empty fridge.

## Conclusion

The calendar tells you what a country values. India values the gathering and Germany values the pause, and neither has much patience for the other's rhythms. An Indian in Germany will miss the noise. A German in India will miss the silence.

So celebrate where you are, honour where you came from and always buy your groceries on Saturday. It is the one piece of cultural wisdom that applies equally to both countries, and the only one that has ever prevented a Sunday disaster.
