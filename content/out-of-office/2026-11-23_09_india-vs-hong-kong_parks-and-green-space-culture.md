# Hong Kong Protects Its Mountains and Builds on Its Backyard

🇮🇳 India vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Hong Kong is the only dense city I know where you can finish a bowl of noodles and be on a ridge above the sea within the hour. People assume it is all towers and neon, and from the harbour it is, but the government has quietly set aside a big proportion of the territory as country park and given it a name, a warden and a set of long-distance trails. It is the great municipal contradiction: one of the most crowded places on earth, and one of the most hikeable.

India, by contrast, has parks the way a house with fifteen people in it has a quiet room: as a rumour. Its cities are crowded, loud and starved of public green space, and the parks that survive tend to be defended by morning walkers, yoga groups, retired colonels and a fiercely loyal supply of chai. I love them, because they are the last shared living room in a place that rarely has one. But the comparison is not flattering, and a decent column should say so.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇮🇳 India ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go early, because parks are at their best at dawn | Expect much shade or quiet at midday |
| Join the morning walkers and yoga groups | Treat a park as a private space |
| Carry water and a small snack | Rely on facilities being open or clean |
| Check whether a park is open to the public or a members-only club | Assume every green patch is free to enter |

| 🇭🇰 Hong Kong ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take the MTR and a bus to a country park trailhead | Assume you need a car to reach nature |
| Carry water, a hat and a charged phone | Underestimate the heat and the humidity |
| Try a section of the MacLehose, Lantau, Hong Kong or Wilson trails | Treat a hike as a casual walk in unsuitable shoes |
| Respect the barbecue and fire rules | Start a fire in dry weather |

## 🇮🇳 India

Let me be honest about my limits. I could not find a reliable, fetchable source on green space in Indian cities for this piece, so I will not quote a figure, and I would urge you to distrust any article that does so without a citation. What I can offer is observation, and the observation is that Indian urban parks are scarce, heavily used and culturally rich.

The park in an Indian city is rarely a place for quiet contemplation. It is a gym, a social club, a meeting hall, a temple annexe and a political forum. In the morning you will find walkers in pairs, laughter clubs, yoga circles, cricket practice and elderly men in a heated argument about the cabinet. In the evening the mood changes, and children and snack vendors take over. In Delhi, Mumbai and Bengaluru, the famous parks are famous partly because there are so few of them, and the pressure on each is intense. The ones attached to housing colonies or run by clubs can be pleasant and relatively empty, and the ones open to the public are lively to the point of exhaustion.

The deeper issue is that green space competes with everything else. Land is scarce, and the case for a playground is often weaker, in the eyes of a developer, than the case for another tower. The air quality in some cities adds a further complication: a walk in the park is not always a healthy act when the pollution levels are alarming. This is a real constraint on the culture of outdoor leisure, and I would not wave it away.

Yet the Indian relationship with green space is strikingly social. A park is not a place you escape people, it is a place you meet them. If you move to an Indian city as an expat, the best thing you can do is find the nearest park, arrive at dawn and let yourself be absorbed by the early-morning community. It is the cheapest, kindest and most reliable form of integration on offer. You will be talked to, advised and, in time, fed.

## 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

Hong Kong's story is different, and the government's own data lays it out. The Agriculture, Fisheries and Conservation Department says that, after Robin's Nest Country Park was designated in March 2024, there are 25 country parks and 22 special areas, mainly for nature conservation. Together they cover 44,842 hectares. About 11 million visits were recorded in 2025, most of them to hike, walk, exercise, barbecue, camp or enjoy a family outing, and there are four long-distance trails, named MacLehose, Lantau, Hong Kong and Wilson. An older piece of airline inspiration content puts the share at about 40 percent of the land inside country parks, and says three quarters of the territory is countryside. The Country Parks Ordinance came in 1976, and Lord MacLehose is credited as the champion.

That is a genuinely impressive piece of policy, and it explains why Hong Kong is such a good place to be a weekend walker. The trails are marked, the parks are reachable by public transport and the scenery is startlingly good for a city of seven million. As an expat, you can leave the office on a Friday and be in a country park on Saturday morning without hiring a car.

The sting is in the details. The South China Morning Post reports on what it calls backyard trails, the green belts between residential areas and country parks, which are largely overlooked by government and often among the first areas considered for development. Carine Lai Man-yin of the Wyng Foundation says green belt land is "passively implemented" and effectively left alone as a buffer, and that maintenance of benches and shelters focuses on easy, low-budget upkeep. A pilot project covering about 11 sites submitted its findings to government, and at the time of the article, no response had been received. The same airline piece mentions that enclaves of private land were left out of the parks, and developers have since eyed them.

So Hong Kong's country parks are magnificent, and the unremarkable green patch behind the housing estate is the one at risk. It is a pattern I recognise from half the cities I have lived in: the postcard is protected and the pavement is not.

## The Verdict

Hong Kong wins, and it is not close, if you measure access to proper nature by the hour. A city that sets aside around 45,000 hectares and keeps millions of visitors walking on managed trails has done something right. If you love hiking, view-hunting and a swim at the end of a path, Hong Kong is among the best-placed big cities in the world.

India wins on the social life of the park, and I mean it. A dawn walk among a hundred strangers who will be friends by the weekend is a different kind of green-space culture, and one I would not trade. But if the question is where I would rather spend a Sunday with a pair of boots, the answer is Hong Kong. If the question is where I would rather be found by a stranger with a flask of chai, it is India. Choose accordingly, and bring water for both.

## Conclusion

Hong Kong will give you a mountain by lunchtime and a developer's proposal by dinner. India will give you a crowded park, a stranger's opinion and a cup of tea. Both are part of the same story: cities that crowd people together and, in doing so, make green space a precious thing. Find the park, learn its hours and protect what is left, because in both places the land is always asking to be something else.
