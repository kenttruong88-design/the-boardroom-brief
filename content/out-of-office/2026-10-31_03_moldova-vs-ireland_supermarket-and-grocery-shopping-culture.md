# Nobody Needs a Loyalty Card When Your Nan Grows the Wine

🇲🇩 Moldova vs 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is a fact that will ruin someone's dinner party: the two most honest grocery cultures in Europe belong to a country most people can't find on a map and a country everyone thinks they understand because they watched one Richard Linklater film set in a pub. Moldova and Ireland have nothing in common on paper — one is landlocked, ex-Soviet, and quietly running the highest per-capita vineyard density on the continent; the other is an island with four supermarket chains locked in a blood feud over who gets to sell you yoghurt — and yet both have built entire identities around refusing to simply buy food like normal people.

I have shopped in both. In Chișinău I was publicly judged by a woman selling walnuts for choosing the wrong bag size. In Dublin I was handed a loyalty card before I'd said a word, like a passport I didn't know I needed. Neither country does "convenient." Both do "correct," according to rules nobody wrote down and everybody enforces.

**Do's & Don'ts: Moldova**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go to the piață (market) for fruit, cheese, and anything that was alive last week | Expect the supermarket to have better tomatoes than your neighbour's garden |
| Accept homemade wine if offered — refusing is the real faux pas | Call it "exotic." It is dinner, not an adventure |
| Bring cash and small bills to the market | Haggle like you're on a gap year — bargaining is a conversation, not a sport |
| Try Linella, Nr.1, or Fourchette for packaged goods and imports | Assume rural and urban Moldova shop the same way — they don't, not close |

**Do's & Don'ts: Ireland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pick a tribe — Tesco, Dunnes, SuperValu, or Aldi/Lidl — and defend it unreasonably | Expect loyalty everywhere; Dunnes famously never bothered with a card |
| Visit the deli counter like it's a religious obligation | Underestimate the breakfast roll. It is a cultural institution, not a snack |
| Shop SuperValu or Centra if "supporting Irish" matters to you | Assume German discounters are the budget option only — they're now mainstream |
| Expect prices and promotions to change mid-visit | Be shocked when the queue has opinions about your trolley |

## Moldova: The Market Still Wins, Quietly

Here is the thing nobody tells you about Moldova: the supermarket won on paper and lost in practice. Chains like Linella, Nr.1, and Fourchette have multiplied across Chișinău over the past decade, and industry surveys now put supermarket preference among urban shoppers at roughly 90 percent for routine buying. And yet the piață — the open-air market, the one with the walnut-selling enforcer and the woman who will not sell you her good cheese because she's saving it for a regular — remains the actual centre of gravity for anything that matters: tomatoes, plums, dill the size of a small tree, and the kind of cheese that doesn't survive a barcode. Moldovans didn't abandon the market for the supermarket. They split the labour. Supermarkets do logistics. Markets do dignity.

Then there's the wine, which is not a hobby here, it's infrastructure. A staggering share of Moldovan households make their own — vin de casă, house wine, fermented in basements and sheds with the seriousness other nations reserve for retirement planning — which means the "wine aisle" at the local supermarket is, to a meaningful fraction of the population, a tourist trap for people who failed to inherit a vineyard. I watched a man decline a supermarket bottle with the same expression I'd expect if I'd offered him a ready meal at his own wedding.

The uncomfortable statistic underneath all this charm: Moldovans have, in recent years, spent over 40 percent of household income on food — a figure that makes "shopping culture" sound less like culture and more like a structural condition. The market haggling isn't quaint. It's margin. When nearly half your income goes on dinner, you don't "pop out" for groceries — you negotiate for them, every single time, and you remember who overcharged you in March.

## Ireland: Four Chains, One Religion

Ireland's grocery culture runs on tribal loyalty disguised as practicality. Tesco holds the top spot by market share, according to Kantar data, but "top spot" in Irish retail means something closer to "most contested throne in Europe." SuperValu — Irish-owned, aggressively pro-local-sourcing — trades blows with it constantly, sometimes landing joint-first in share figures, while Aldi and Lidl have quietly stopped being the "cheap alternative" and become where a majority of shoppers now actually do a meaningful share of their shopping, loyalty-card sentiment notwithstanding. Dunnes Stores, in the middle of all this, built an entire brand identity on famously declining to bother with a loyalty card — a flex so Irish it should be on the coat of arms: we don't need to bribe you to come back.

But the real theatre isn't the big shop. It's the corner shop — Centra, Spar, the independent newsagent-slash-deli — and specifically the deli counter, where the breakfast roll is assembled with the gravity of a sacrament: sausage, rasher, egg, black pudding, hot sauce, into a roll, out the door, into a car, gone. Food writers describe the deli counter in genuinely devotional language, and they are not exaggerating. It is the one piece of Irish grocery culture that has nothing to do with price comparison and everything to do with being correctly fed by 9am.

Underneath the banter, Ireland's supermarket culture is a masterclass in loyalty economics — multiple surveys show shoppers professing fierce allegiance to one chain while their actual spending quietly migrates to whichever discounter currently has the better offer. Irish shoppers will defend Tesco at a dinner party and buy their weekly veg at Aldi on the way home. Nobody considers this hypocrisy. It's just Tuesday.

## The Verdict

Moldova wins, and it isn't close, because Moldova's grocery culture is honest about what it is: a negotiation for survival that happens to also be delicious. Nobody in Chișinău is pretending the piață is "charming" — it's just where the good food is, full stop, and the supermarket is the overflow valve for everything else. There's no theatre about it, except the theatre you get for trying to underpay for walnuts.

Ireland's culture, by contrast, is a beautifully maintained illusion — four companies locked in permanent, performative combat for your "loyalty," while everyone quietly shops wherever the offer is best that week and lies about it to their mother. It's charming. It's also a marketing department's fever dream made manifest in a trolley.

Moldova shops like it means it. Ireland shops like it's auditioning for a Kantar report. One of these is survival elevated to art. The other is survival with a Clubcard.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *The Cookbook Circle (Substack) — paraphrased: nobody explains, before you move to Ireland, that the deli counter isn't a convenience, it's a relationship. You will have a sausage roll order. The woman behind the counter will know it before you finish the sentence.*

> *Out of Place, Out of Context (Substack) — paraphrased: Moldovan hosts don't offer you wine, they test you with it. Decline the first pour and you have, somehow, insulted three generations in under a minute.*

> *r/Moldova (paraphrased, composite of recurring threads) — the supermarket is for shampoo and surprises. Nobody serious buys tomatoes there. If your tomatoes come from a supermarket, people will simply know, and they will be too polite to say so to your face.*

## Conclusion

What both countries understand, and most of the world has forgotten, is that where you buy your food is a confession. Moldova confesses through the market stall, through the wine nobody bought, through 40-odd percent of a paycheque spent on getting dinner right. Ireland confesses through the loyalty card it swears it doesn't need and the breakfast roll it would start a small war over. Everywhere else, grocery shopping is an errand. In these two countries, it's a personality test, and most people fail it quietly, in the cereal aisle, without ever realising they were being watched.

Go to either country and skip the supermarket tour entirely. Go to the market in Chișinău, go to the deli counter in Dublin, and watch how seriously people take something you've spent your whole life treating as a chore. Then try explaining to them, with a straight face, that back home you just "pick things up."
