# Poland Gives You 600 Banks and Czechia Gives You a Contract in Czech

🇵🇱 Poland vs 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Poland and Czechia sit side by side on the map, share a border, a mild suspicion of Germany and a deep fondness for dumplings. You would think their banks would be the same. They are not. One country built a vast financial market and a number that identifies every citizen. The other built a neat, conservative system and a legal tradition that assumes you can read the contract.

I opened accounts in both, a process which taught me more about the national character than any museum. Poland asked me for a number I did not have. Czechia asked me for a document I did have, and then handed me a contract in a language I did not. The lesson was the same: the system works beautifully for people who already belong to it.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇱 Poland ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask early about your PESEL number | Turn up without one and assume the bank will cope |
| Bring proof of residency to your first visit | Expect a quick fix by email |
| Allow up to two weeks for the account and card | Plan to pay rent in cash from day one |
| Check the fees before you use another bank's ATM | Assume every cash machine is free |

| 🇨🇿 Czechia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a passport and, for some banks, a second ID such as a residence card | Arrive with a photocopy and a prayer |
| Plan at least one in-person visit | Expect to do the whole thing online |
| Get a translation of your contract before signing | Sign a document you cannot read |
| Allow a week or two for the debit card and PIN to arrive by post | Assume the card is ready on the day |

## 🇵🇱 Poland

According to Xe's 2021 guide to banks for expats in Poland, there are up to 600 banks in the country and about 20,000 ATMs, with a fee for using another bank's machine. That is a competitive market by any standard, and for a foreigner it means choice and confusion in equal parts. Banks often ask for a PESEL, the national identification number, and proof of residency. Xe says the wait for an account, including card delivery, is between five days and two weeks. Xe also says that if you are in Poland, opening an account is very easy, as long as you can walk into a branch.

The PESEL is where the experience splits. If you have one, the process is fluid. If you do not, you will spend a week of your life discovering that you need the number to get the account and the account to get the number, or near enough. It is a perfectly rational system, built for citizens and residents who are already inside it. For everyone else it is a cheerful maze.

Poland's bureaucracy has a reputation for being slower and more paper-heavy than it needs to be, and that reputation is partly earned and partly nostalgic. Many offices have improved dramatically. Online services work. Banks are modern, with good apps and fast transfers that make British banks look like a museum exhibit. But there is still a point at which a form must be stamped, and when you get there, you will know.

The upside is competition. Foreign-owned banks tend to be more expat-friendly, with English-speaking staff and sensible onboarding. A newcomer who shops around will find something that works. It is a market built for people who like to compare.

Xe also notes that foreign-owned banks tend to be more expat-friendly, and that English service varies by branch, so a flagship in Warsaw may be a very different experience from a small office elsewhere. The sensible approach is to ask by phone or email before you go, and to bring every document you can find, since a missing page is a reason for a second visit, and a second visit is a reason for a new queue.

## 🇨🇿 Czechia

Czechia is a smaller, quieter and more conventional system. According to Expats.cz in May 2025, Česká spořitelna requires only a passport to open an account, while MONETA Money Bank asks for a passport plus another ID, such as a residence card. Expat.com, updating its guide in July 2026, says deposits are covered by the EU deposit guarantee scheme up to 100,000 euros per client per bank, that opening usually requires at least one in-person visit and that the initial deposit is usually a few hundred to a few thousand koruna.

The card and PIN, says the same guide, are usually posted and take one to two weeks, with activation taking a few business days more. That is slow, but it is predictable, which counts for a great deal. You will not be shocked by anything in the process. You will simply be waiting.

Where Czechia bites is language. Expat.com says account contracts are often provided only in Czech, and that daily life is noticeably easier once you have a Czech account. Eduard Malyarchuk, chief executive of NAWI Europe, is quoted in a sponsored Expats.cz piece saying many foreigners cannot open accounts because they do not know where to start with documents. That is a promotional article, and it should be read as such, but the point is real. A contract in a language you cannot read is a risk you have to manage, whether by translator, friend or sheer nerve.

The quiet strength of the Czech system is that, once you are in, it is stable. Fees are low. Apps are good. The bureaucracy is orderly, with offices that open at fixed hours, in rooms with numbered tickets, and an unshakeable belief that if you have the right paper the world will unlock itself.

Both countries share a quiet truth about bureaucracy: the written rule and the practised rule are not always the same. A branch may ask for a document its own website does not list. A clerk may accept something another branch rejected. Expats who succeed tend to be the ones who stay calm, return with a better folder and treat each refusal as information rather than insult. It is not efficient, but it is learnable, and the people who learn it fastest are usually the ones who ask a local colleague to come along.

## The Verdict

Czechia wins by a narrow margin, mainly because it does not ask you for a number you do not have. A passport and a visit will get you an account. That is a rare piece of simplicity in a region that loves forms.

Poland deserves credit for competition and speed. Once you are in the system, its banking is better than most, with excellent apps and a huge market. But the PESEL obstacle is a real barrier for new arrivals, and the process can feel like a loop until someone breaks it.

If I could pick one lesson from both, it is that a foreign bank account is a test of patience, and the country that makes it easiest to start usually wins the argument. Czechia, for all its contracts in Czech, lets you start sooner. Poland rewards those who stay. I would take the country that opens the door first and trusts me to find the light switch.

## Conclusion

A bank account is the first real piece of belonging a foreigner gets. It means rent, salary and a card that works in the shop, and it is the moment you stop being a visitor. In Poland it asks for your number. In Czechia it asks for your patience.

So prepare. Get your passport, your residence card, your proof of address and your translator. Ask your employer for help and your colleagues for advice. Take a book to the branch. And when the clerk finally smiles, take the card, say thank you in the local language and leave quietly before anything else changes.
