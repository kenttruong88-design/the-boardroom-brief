# Korea Writes Everything in a Script You Can't Read. America Assumes You'll Learn English by Osmosis

🇰🇷 South Korea vs 🇺🇸 USA

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are three kinds of language barrier: the one that stops you, the one that embarrasses you, and the one that quietly steers your whole life without your noticing. South Korea gives an English speaker all three before breakfast. Hangul is a famously logical script, which does not help when you are staring at a bottle in a shop and cannot tell whether it is conditioner or shampoo.

America, for its part, speaks English at a volume and confidence that assumes the rest of the world is secretly rehearsing. It offers newcomers a language environment so total that the barrier is almost invisible to those who built it. This is a comparison between a country where the written world is closed to you and a country where it is open but unforgiving. A source note: my fetched pages describe an American's experience in Korea, not a Korean's in America, so the US side comes from general knowledge.

**USA**

|---|---|

## South Korea

The evidence is old but consistent. In a 2009 survey of one hundred foreign business executives by the Korea Chamber of Commerce and Industry, thirty-five percent felt troubled while travelling, with the top complaints being the language barrier at about twenty-seven percent, then traffic, lack of signs, high costs and poor service. A professor quoted in the piece called for one national sign system and accurate English at bus and train ticket offices. An American IT specialist, Joseph Landau, said downtown Seoul was easy but outside areas were hard, mainly because of the language barrier and the lack of signage.

The everyday version is more intimate. A blogger listing twelve common barriers includes hair products, cleaning products, appliances, bill payment, medicine, menus, ingredient labels and forms. She bought a conditioner thinking it was shampoo and only realised about two weeks later. She gave her co-teacher her bank and residence card details so bills could be paid automatically, which is a sentence that makes every compliance officer reach for a sedative. Her tips are practical: use the camera feature on a translation app, ask someone to walk you through appliances, and visit large stores that sometimes keep English aisles.

An American teacher in Incheon, interviewed around 2012 or 2013, said that a positive, patient attitude softens the frustration, and that it is easy to end up in a little English bubble. Her friends were mostly other expats, and many foreign teachers lived in her building. That is the quiet danger: the barrier does not just block you, it sorts you into a comfortable enclave where you never have to climb over it.

The sting is the speed with which the barrier shapes your habits. You begin by pointing, then by avoiding, then by developing a very particular loyalty to the three shops where the staff speak a few words of your language. Hangul takes an afternoon to learn. The courage takes longer.

The tips from the Korean side are worth repeating because they are practical rather than inspirational. Use a translation app with a camera for labels and forms. Ask a Korean colleague to walk you through the washing machine, the hot-water boiler and the bill payment system. Visit a large store, since some keep English aisles. None of this requires fluency, and all of it reduces the daily friction that otherwise accumulates into a deep, quiet fatigue. The executive who treats the language barrier as a personal failing will burn out. The one who treats it as a logistics problem, to be solved with tools and favours, will be fine, and will probably have learned to read the alphabet within a month.

## USA

I will keep this honest: none of my sources covered the opposite direction, so what follows is general knowledge. The United States presents a different problem. English is everywhere, written and spoken, and the barrier is rarely absence of signage but speed, accent, slang and the vast machinery of forms, contracts and automated phone systems that assume native fluency.

A person arriving from Korea with textbook English will often find that the classroom version and the street version are two languages. Accents vary by region, idioms are unexplained and casual speech runs fast. A drive-through order, a pharmacy counter or an insurance call can be harder than a business meeting, because nobody slows down and the stakes are unclear.

The social side is more generous. Americans are famously willing to talk to strangers, and a visible effort is usually met with patience and encouragement. Community groups, language classes, faith organisations and workplaces provide structured places to practise. There is no shortage of people who will help, if you ask.

The sting is the paperwork. Health insurance, rental agreements and tax forms are written in a dialect of English that confuses natives. The language barrier in America is not a wall; it is a fog, and the fog is thickest where the money is.

There is a mirror-image point about America that many transferees miss: the language of institutions. A bank, a landlord or an insurer in the United States will assume you understand terms such as deductible, escrow and co-pay, and will not explain them. A newcomer with good conversational English can still be baffled, because the barrier is not vocabulary but convention. The sensible remedy is to ask for a written summary, to bring a friend to important appointments and to resist the social pressure to nod. Americans are generally happy to explain, if you ask directly. The trouble is that no one tells you which questions you are supposed to ask.

## The Verdict

South Korea has the harder barrier, America the harder fog. If you are an English speaker, the American environment is vastly easier on day one, and the Korean environment vastly more rewarding by month twelve, because you will have been forced to learn something. That is the dull, true answer.

For the corporate transferee, the practical issue is what the barrier costs. In Korea the cost is friction: time, errors, the occasional wrong bottle. In America, for a non-native speaker, the cost is risk: a misunderstood contract, a misread form. A conditioner mistaken for shampoo is a lesson. A lease misread is a lawsuit.

I will give it to Korea, narrowly, on the grounds that a barrier you can see is a barrier you can climb. America's is subtler, and it has a way of making you feel stupid for a reason nobody can name. Bring a translation app to Seoul and a patient friend to Chicago.

## Conclusion

A language barrier is a tax on every ordinary transaction. Korea charges it up front, in confusion and conditioner. America collects it later, in forms and fine print.

Learn the script, ask the colleague and read before you sign. The language will not be gracious about your timetable, but the people, in both countries, mostly will.
