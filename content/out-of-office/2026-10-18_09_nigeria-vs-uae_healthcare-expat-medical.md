# One Country Will Fly You Home In A Medevac Jet. The Other Will Just Fly You, Full Stop.

🇳🇬 Nigeria vs 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Let's dispense with the pleasantries: no healthcare system on earth is designed around your convenience, and the two in question here have simply chosen different ways to disappoint you. Nigeria's approach is Darwinian — figure it out, pay cash, pray your insurance reimburses you before the exchange rate eats the difference. The UAE's approach is bureaucratic — here is a government-mandated policy, a tiered package system, and an app, and you will comply with all three or your residency visa will quietly stop existing. One country has 90% of its population with zero insurance. The other has made insurance a legal condition of breathing the air. Neither of these facts should comfort you.

I have moved fourteen times. I have had a kidney stone diagnosed in a language I didn't speak and a sprained ankle dismissed in a language I did. What I've learned is that "the healthcare here is great, actually" is a sentence expats say exclusively about the one hospital they personally used, once, for something minor. It is never a system-wide endorsement. Keep that in mind for everything that follows.

## Do's & Don'ts: 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy standalone international medical insurance with evacuation cover before you land | Trust an employer's "local" health scheme to cover anything beyond a sniffle |
| Identify your nearest Lagos or Abuja private hospital (Lagoon, Paelon, St Nicholas) before you need one | Assume the hospital your coworker swears by is near where you'll actually be living |
| Carry cash or a card that works instantly — reimbursement claims can take weeks | Wait for a "serious" symptom to start asking where the good doctors are |
| Budget for air evacuation as a real, non-optional line item | Assume a diagnosis means the equipment to treat it is in the same building |

## Do's & Don'ts: 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm your employer's policy covers the emirate you actually sleep in, not just Dubai | Assume your dependants are automatically covered — in Dubai, by default, they often aren't |
| Get your Emirates Health Services card alongside insurance for discounted public-facility rates | Pick the cheapest "basic package" and assume it includes more than catastrophic emergencies |
| Read the co-insurance clauses — 20–30% patient contribution on outpatient visits is common | Assume a policy valid in Dubai travels with you to Sharjah, Ajman, or RAK |
| Use private hospitals for speed if you can afford the 30–40% markup over public care | Treat "mandatory insurance" as the same thing as "good insurance" — it is a floor, not a ceiling |

## 🇳🇬 Nigeria

Here is the number that should be tattooed on every HR onboarding deck for Lagos: one doctor for every 40,000 Nigerians. That's not a typo, and it's not a developing-nation platitude — it's Nigeria's own Coordinating Minister of Health, Prof. Muhammad Ali Pate, stating on television that the country has roughly 55,000 doctors for over 200 million people, and that figure keeps shrinking because "japa" — the now-institutionalised phenomenon of skilled Nigerians emigrating — has reportedly taken 16,000 doctors out of the country in the past five to seven years. The doctors training in Lagos teaching hospitals are frequently the same doctors interviewing for jobs in Manchester and Riyadh six months later. You are not imagining the staffing gaps. They are structural, documented, and getting worse.

And yet — and this is the part nobody tells you before you accept the posting — the private hospitals that exist for the expat bracket are genuinely good. Lagoon Hospital, Paelon Memorial, St Nicholas: clean, competent, staffed by doctors trained abroad or at Nigeria's better medical schools. One expat on expat.com's forum, posting as Gentlemaniac, put it with admirable precision: care at the right private facility can reach "up to European standards" — but the catch is getting there. If your employer's "local" health scheme restricts you to a specific panel of hospitals, which happens constantly with Nigerian-registered companies, those contracted hospitals are "usually not the best," and the arrangement is, in Gentlemaniac's words, "not advisable for more serious treatments." The system doesn't fail you dramatically. It fails you by quietly routing you to the B-tier facility your employer negotiated a discount with.

The real story in Nigerian healthcare, per the National Health Insurance Authority's own Director-General Kelechi Ohiri speaking in Abuja in September 2025, is that more than 90% of Nigerians have no health insurance at all. Ninety percent. You, the expat, are not in that 90%, which is precisely the problem with how you'll experience this country — you'll be floating several income brackets above the system you're observing, treated well at a premium while the infrastructure underneath you is visibly, statistically collapsing. Arrange your own international insurance with evacuation cover. Don't negotiate. Don't "see how it goes." The gap between adequate and catastrophic in Lagos is one missing piece of diagnostic equipment, and evacuation to Johannesburg or London is not a contingency plan — it's the plan.

## 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

The UAE has taken the opposite philosophical position: healthcare should be mandatory, tiered, and administered with the bureaucratic thoroughness of a customs form. As of 1 January 2025, health insurance is legally required in all seven emirates — Dubai and Abu Dhabi got there years earlier, and Sharjah, Ajman, Umm Al Quwain, Ras Al Khaimah, and Fujairah have now caught up. Your employer must buy you at least a basic package before they can issue or renew your residency visa. No insurance, no visa. It's refreshingly honest about the transaction, even if the transaction itself is a tangle of regulators — the Dubai Health Authority in Dubai, the Department of Health in Abu Dhabi, the Ministry of Health and Prevention everywhere else — each with its own rules about what "mandatory" actually covers.

Here's where the shine comes off: mandatory does not mean generous. The basic package in Dubai covers you, the employee, full stop — spouses and children aren't automatically included, and Gulf News documented real expat frustration over exactly this gap. A Dubai-based father named Ahmad bought his son a Dh650 Takaful Emarat policy, discovered it covered emergencies only when he tried to use it for a routine Sharjah consultation, and asked the obvious question: "What is the use of this coverage [for] Dubai visa holders living in Sharjah?" He wasn't about to brave Sharjah-to-Dubai traffic for a doctor his policy would actually pay for. An unnamed broker in the same piece summed it up better than any brochure could: plenty of dependant policies sit wasted, because "who wants to run to a Dubai clinic when a child is sick?" That's the UAE's quiet trick — mandate coverage, then let its geography be so narrow that having insurance and having access to a doctor become two separate achievements.

Where the UAE undeniably wins is spectacle and infrastructure: Dubai pulled in more than 691,000 international medical tourists in 2023, according to the UAE's own state news agency WAM — people flying in specifically for treatment, to hospitals with the latest equipment and multilingual staff. You will get excellent, fast, private care here. You will also pay 30–40% more for it than the public alternative, and discover your "comprehensive" plan has a 20% co-insurance clause buried in clause nine. It is a five-star hotel with an asterisk.

## The Verdict

Here's the sting: the UAE is actively flying in the world's medical tourists while Nigeria is actively losing the doctors who'd treat its own population — two countries moving in opposite directions on the same axis, and somehow landing on comparably infuriating outcomes for the expat in the middle. Nigeria gives you genuine access to excellent individual doctors wrapped in a collapsing system with zero safety net; the UAE gives you a legally bulletproof safety net riddled with coverage gaps engineered by actuaries who have clearly never had a sick child in Sharjah. If I'm handing out a trophy, it goes to the UAE, by a nose — because "my insurance has an annoying co-pay" is a Tuesday-afternoon problem, and "my country lost 16,000 doctors and I need surgery" is not. But don't mistake this for an endorsement. Mandatory insurance in a country that still makes you fight your insurer is not healthcare. It's paperwork with excellent production values.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com — Gentlemaniac, posting in the Nigeria forum, on employer-provided health schemes: staff on local contracts get routed to a fixed panel of hospitals that are "usually not the best," and arranging your own private cover is the only way to use Lagos's genuinely good facilities without restriction.*

> *ExpatExchange UAE thread — paraphrased from multiple respondents: care quality in the UAE "varies on your insurance coverage," and several noted that cost itself "filters out the people who cannot afford" better treatment, even inside a mandatory-insurance system.*

> *Gulf News, quoting Dubai resident Ahmad on his son's Dh650 dependant policy: it covered emergencies only, leaving him asking what the point of mandatory coverage was if he'd never make the Sharjah-to-Dubai trip to actually use it.*

## Conclusion

Neither of these countries will tell you the truth about their healthcare system before you arrive, because the truth doesn't fit on a relocation brochure. Nigeria's truth is that brilliance and collapse share a building. The UAE's truth is that compliance is not the same thing as care. I've eaten my way through sixty countries and relocated fourteen times, and the only rule that's ever held is this: read the policy document before you need it, not after, and never once describe either of these systems as "exotic." They're not exotic. They're just somebody else's insurance paperwork, with higher stakes and worse font choices. Pack accordingly, insure properly, and for God's sake, find the hospital before you need the ambulance.
