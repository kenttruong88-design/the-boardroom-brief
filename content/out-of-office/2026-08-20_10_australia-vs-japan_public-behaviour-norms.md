# Australia Will Talk to You in the Queue Whether You Like It or Not. Japan Won't Even Take a Phone Call on the Train

🇦🇺 Australia 🇯🇵 Japan
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Australia treats public space as an extension of the backyard barbecue, loud, friendly, and allergic to unnecessary formality, where striking up conversation with a stranger in a queue isn't an imposition, it's practically a civic duty. Japan treats public space as a kind of shared sanctuary that demands near-total self-effacement, where the highest form of courtesy is making your presence as unobtrusive as physically possible, silence included.

I've been drawn into a genuinely lovely, entirely unsolicited conversation about football with a stranger in a Melbourne coffee queue, the kind of encounter that would strike most of the world as either charming or faintly alarming depending on your tolerance for small talk. I've also sat on a packed Tokyo train where a phone buzzed, was silenced within a single ring, and the owner visibly reddened with something close to genuine shame at having disturbed the collective quiet for even that long. Both are functioning societies. Only one of them considers silence a form of respect owed to strangers.

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect and return casual small talk from strangers, cheerfully | Take offense at direct, blunt commentary — it's affection, not hostility |
| Queue properly — it's taken more seriously than the informality suggests | Jump a queue; it will be called out, loudly, on the spot |
| Match the volume — a bit of noise in public is completely normal | Assume reserved silence reads as politeness; it can read as standoffish |

## Do's & Don'ts: Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Silence your phone and avoid calls on public transport | Speak loudly on a train, even in a near-empty carriage |
| Queue in the marked lines on train platforms, without exception | Eat or drink while walking through busy public areas |
| Let others exit before boarding — it's non-negotiable etiquette | Assume brief eye contact or silence from strangers is unfriendliness |

## Australia: Friendly by Default, Direct by Design

Australian public behaviour operates on an assumption that strangers are just friends who haven't been introduced yet, and this plays out constantly in queues, on public transport, and in shops, where casual conversation with people you'll never see again isn't an intrusion but a mildly expected social lubricant. Ask a Melbourne barista how their weekend was and you might get a genuinely detailed answer; comment on the weather in a Sydney queue and you'll likely get a full, engaged response rather than the polite, closed-off nod that would follow the same gesture in more reserved cultures.

This friendliness comes bundled with a directness that can catch newcomers off guard. Australians will tell you, cheerfully and without malice, exactly what they think, deploying a bluntness wrapped in enough warmth and humour that it rarely lands as genuinely hostile, though it can take some recalibration for anyone raised on more diplomatic indirection. Queue-jumping, in particular, triggers a specific, immediate, and vocal social correction, because for all the informality elsewhere, the queue itself is sacred, and violating it will get you publicly, if good-naturedly, called out on the spot.

Volume in public spaces runs noticeably higher than in many other developed nations, and this isn't considered rude so much as simply how Australians exist in shared space. A rowdy group on public transport, animated conversation in a café, genuine laughter carrying across a train carriage, none of it draws the disapproving looks it might in, say, Tokyo or Zurich. The unspoken assumption is that public space is for living in, not merely passing through in careful silence.

What surprises many visitors is how this casualness coexists with a real underlying respect for fairness and courtesy. The directness isn't cruelty, and the noise isn't inconsideration, they're simply the texture of a culture that values authenticity and approachability over formal reserve. Reserved, quiet behaviour, ironically, is sometimes the thing that reads as slightly odd here, occasionally interpreted as standoffishness rather than the polite restraint it might signal elsewhere.

## Japan: Courtesy as Collective Silence

Japanese public behaviour operates on an almost inverse philosophy: the highest courtesy you can offer strangers is to disturb them as little as possible, and an enormous, largely unspoken social infrastructure exists to enforce exactly that. The train carriage is the purest expression of this. Phones go on silent mode by default, calls are simply not taken, and the near-total quiet of a packed Tokyo commuter train, hundreds of people, almost no conversation, is one of the more genuinely disorienting experiences for visitors accustomed to louder public norms.

This isn't coldness, it's a deeply held collective understanding that shared space belongs equally to everyone in it, and personal noise, personal calls, personal conversations imposed on that shared space represent a kind of small trespass against everyone else's right to quiet. JR East and other rail operators reinforce this constantly through manner campaign posters, but by this point the reinforcement is almost redundant, the norm having become thoroughly self-policing across generations.

Queuing culture takes this same collective consideration and applies exacting precision to it. Platform markings indicate exactly where to stand, exactly how to form lines, and the expectation that boarding passengers wait for alighting passengers to fully exit before stepping on is followed with a consistency that borders on choreography. Deviating from this, pushing forward early, standing in the wrong spot, doesn't usually provoke visible anger, but it does register, quietly, as a small failure of consideration that locals notice even when they say nothing about it.

Eating or drinking while walking through busy public areas carries a similar, if less rigid, social cost, generally considered a bit untidy and inconsiderate of shared space, which is why convenience store items are frequently consumed standing just outside the shop rather than carried off down a crowded street. What nobody quite prepares foreigners for is how this pervasive quiet coexists with genuine warmth beneath the surface. Silence and restraint in public don't mean disengagement, they mean a very particular, very consistent form of respect, one that simply doesn't require, or even want, verbal expression to be felt.

## The Verdict

Australia wins on sheer human warmth in public space, on making strangers feel like part of a shared, noisy, good-humoured community. Japan wins on collective consideration, on a society that has built an entire public behaviour code around genuinely not wanting to be a burden to anyone else sharing the space.

Give me an Australian queue for the conversation and a Japanese train for the peace. If you can't decide which philosophy suits you better, that's fine, just don't take a phone call on the Tokyo metro to talk it through. You'll know exactly how that goes down, and so will everyone else in the carriage.

## Conclusion

Australia has built its public culture around the assumption that strangers are worth talking to, loudly, cheerfully, and without much ceremony. Japan has built its around the assumption that strangers deserve your restraint, your silence, and your careful consideration of shared space above your own convenience. Both are, in their own way, deeply respectful cultures. They've simply arrived at opposite conclusions about what respect actually sounds like, and in Tokyo, it mostly sounds like nothing at all.
