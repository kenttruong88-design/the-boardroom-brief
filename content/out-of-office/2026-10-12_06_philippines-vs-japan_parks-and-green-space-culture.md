# One Country Will Hand You A Strict Rulebook For Sitting Under A Tree. The Other Will Hand You A Mall, Because The Park Situation Is... Complicated.

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇯🇵 Japan

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Green space is one of those things nobody appreciates until it's rationed, and few comparisons make that clearer than standing in a Tokyo park debating whether your picnic blanket's corner is technically over the designated line, immediately after having spent a week in Manila trying to locate a park that wasn't, functionally, a mall's air-conditioned atrium.

Japan and the Philippines have arrived at two wildly different relationships with public green space, one governed by meticulous etiquette and small entrance fees, the other largely improvised, privatised, or simply absent. Let's walk through both, ideally somewhere shaded.

## Do's & Don'ts: Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat Rizal Park, Luneta, and similar civic spaces as genuinely valuable; they're rarer than you'd expect | Expect every neighbourhood to have its own accessible green space the way many countries assume |
| Accept that malls function as the de facto public park in many Philippine cities; it's not a joke, it's infrastructure | Be surprised that air-conditioned mall common areas get used for walking, meeting friends, even light exercise |
| Visit provincial parks and plazas outside Metro Manila for a genuinely different, greener experience | Assume Metro Manila's limited park access reflects the whole country's relationship with nature |
| Support and use the few well-maintained public parks that do exist; usage itself helps protect their upkeep | Expect park infrastructure, benches, or shade to be consistently well-maintained everywhere |

## Do's & Don'ts: Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check specific park rules before a hanami picnic; some ban alcohol, some charge entry, all have norms | Assume one park's etiquette applies everywhere; Yoyogi and Shinjuku Gyoen could not be more different |
| Arrive early for a good cherry-blossom-season spot if you want any choice of location at all | Expect to easily claim a prime spot during peak hanami week without a plan or an early start |
| Bring a tarp, trash bag, and the intention to leave the spot cleaner than you found it | Leave litter behind; park cleanliness is enforced socially even where it isn't enforced by signage |
| Try a quieter park (Rikugien, Chidorigafuchi) if you want the beauty without the Yoyogi-level crowd noise | Expect peace and quiet at Ueno or Yoyogi during the official cherry blossom festival weeks |

## Philippines: When the Mall Is the Park

Metro Manila's relationship with green space is, to put it generously, strained. Luneta and Rizal Park remain genuinely important civic landmarks, and plazas and small barangay parks exist in patches, but the dense, rapidly developed urban sprawl has left large swaths of the metro area with minimal accessible, well-maintained public green space per resident, a gap long flagged by local urban planners and development researchers. Into that gap has stepped, somewhat improbably, the shopping mall.

This is not a punchline. Air-conditioned mall common areas across Metro Manila function as genuine substitute public space: people walk laps for light exercise, meet friends on benches near the fountain, let children play in atrium areas, simply sit somewhere free, shaded, and dry during the wet season. It reflects both the tropical climate's practical pressures — shade and air conditioning solve real problems heat-stressed green space often can't — and a genuine infrastructure gap that mall developers happened to fill, intentionally or not.

Outside the capital, the picture brightens considerably. Provincial towns and smaller cities often retain a more traditional plaza culture, genuine public parks, and considerably more breathing room per resident. The Manila green-space shortage is very much a story about one enormously dense metro area, not a verdict on the whole archipelago's relationship with nature, which — rice terraces, beaches, mountain trails — remains, away from the capital, genuinely abundant.

## Japan: The Etiquette Manual Is Real, and You Will Be Expected to Know It

Japan's parks operate on the opposite principle: abundant, well-maintained green space governed by a dense, mostly unwritten etiquette code that newcomers absorb through mild public embarrassment rather than any single posted sign. Hanami season turns this into high comedy for an outsider — Shinjuku Gyoen bans alcohol and charges a modest entrance fee, Hamarikyu Gardens strictly regulates picnicking, Showa Memorial Park provides actual barbecue areas, and Yoyogi Park, by contrast, is cheerfully rowdy, with office groups drinking under the cherry trees in a display of sanctioned, seasonal chaos that would be unthinkable the other fifty weeks of the year.

Crowd behaviour varies as dramatically as the rules. Ueno fills with families during the official blossom festival to a degree that tests anyone's patience for proximity. Nakameguro's canal-side stalls and crowds move at a pace best described as geological. Rikugien and Chidorigafuchi offer calmer, more contemplative alternatives for anyone who wants the blossoms without the crowd, and regulars know exactly which is which, planning visits around that knowledge the way other countries plan around weather.

What unites the whole system, chaotic Yoyogi included, is an underlying civic discipline: trash goes home with you (public bins are famously scarce), spots are respected once claimed, and the collective understanding that a park's beauty is a shared resource maintained by everyone's small compliance, not a council's enforcement budget. Nobody is watching, and almost everybody behaves as if they are.

There's a third, less obvious green-space substitute worth naming in the Philippines: the humble covered basketball court, found in nearly every barangay regardless of how little other public space exists nearby. It functions as park, gym, event venue, and informal community hall all at once, hosting everything from pickup games to birthday parties to emergency shelter during typhoon season. It is, in its own improvised way, just as central to Filipino public life as a Tokyo park is to Japanese public life, even though nobody would call it green space in any literal sense. Japan, meanwhile, layers its own informal green-adjacent culture on top of the formal park system through the convenience store, or konbini, which sells cheap, decent-quality bento and drinks specifically positioned near park entrances during hanami season, turning a quick stop into the easiest, least effortful picnic supply run in the world. Both countries, in their own way, have built clever infrastructure around the gaps their formal green space leaves behind.

## The Verdict

Japan offers an abundance of genuinely beautiful, well-maintained green space, wrapped in an etiquette code dense enough to require actual homework before your first hanami. The Philippines, or at least its densest urban core, offers a genuine, unresolved shortage that has been creatively, if imperfectly, patched over by shopping malls serving as unofficial parks.

I don't think this is a fair fight, and I won't pretend otherwise for the sake of false balance — one country has a green-space infrastructure problem, the other has a green-space etiquette opportunity. But I will say the mall-as-park solution is more ingenious, and more quietly sad, than most visitors ever clock on their first trip through a Manila atrium.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Philippines — a Metro Manila resident's recurring complaint: the nearest real park to many dense residential areas is a forty-minute commute away, so the mall genuinely functions as the default evening-walk destination instead.*

> *Japan Handbook — repeated advice to hanami newcomers: claim your spot early, mark it clearly with a tarp, and understand that stepping away for more than a short errand risks losing it entirely, politely but firmly, to the next group.*

> *Lonely Planet — a note on Tokyo park culture worth taking seriously: choosing the wrong park for your mood is a real planning failure, since Yoyogi's rowdy energy and Rikugien's quiet formality are, for all practical purposes, different countries.*

## Conclusion

A country's parks reveal exactly how much it has decided ordinary people deserve shared, unpriced, outdoor space. Japan has decided the answer is a great deal, administered with an etiquette code that borders on civic religion. Metro Manila's density has decided the answer is not much, at least not yet, and the shopping mall has quietly absorbed the slack. Neither outcome is really about nature. Both are about planning, money, and whose comfort gets prioritised in a crowded city. Bring a tarp for one, bring an umbrella for the other, and don't mistake either for a holiday brochure.
