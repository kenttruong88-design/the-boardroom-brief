# Ghana Is Hot in Two Different Ways and the Netherlands Is Grey in Every Possible Way

🇬🇭 Ghana vs 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ghana has weather the way other countries have personality. It is warm, it is humid, it announces itself, and it arrives in two flavours: the wet season, when the sky opens with theatrical conviction and the streets become rivers, and the dry season, when the harmattan blows dust down from the Sahara and everything acquires a fine, beige coating. Nobody in Accra discusses the weather as small talk. They discuss it as an adversary that has, to everyone's relief, always shown up on time.

The Netherlands has weather the way other countries have a mild, lingering cough. It is not dramatic. It is not dangerous. It is simply always there, grey, damp and unhurried, like a government office that has decided to stay open indefinitely. For a Ghanaian arriving in November, the experience is not so much a shock as a slow, personal betrayal by the sun.

## Ghana

Ghana sits close to the equator, and the weather follows a rhythm that locals can read like a clock. The south has two rainy seasons, a major one in the middle of the year and a shorter one in the autumn, and the north has one longer dry stretch. Temperatures stay warm all year, and the real variation is humidity. When the harmattan blows in from the north, usually in the cooler months, the air turns dry and hazy, mornings can feel almost cool and everyone's skin cracks like a map.

Ghanaians cope the way people cope with something they have known all their lives: without drama. Mornings start early, because the heat builds. Errands happen before midday. Markets, schools and offices adapt around the rain, and a downpour is a legitimate reason to arrive late. Clothing is practical and often beautiful, and shade is a precious, negotiated resource. Everyone knows which side of the street has it.

There is a social layer to the climate, too. A rainstorm is an excuse to sit together under a shelter and talk. The dry season brings festivals, funerals and weddings in the cooler air. Weather shapes the calendar and the conversation alike, and nobody pretends to be above it.

What does catch out the newcomer is the flooding. Parts of Accra and other cities struggle badly when the rains are heavy, because drainage is often inadequate. If you are relocating, check whether your street is the sort that becomes a stream, and if it is, do not park your car in it.

Dress is the visible part of the adaptation. A Ghanaian who moves north soon learns that a coat is not a single object but a system: a base layer, a middle layer, a windproof shell, a hat, gloves and a scarf worn with conviction. Friends who have lived there longer will advise you on shoes, which matter more than you think, and the first sale in January is a national sacrament.

## Netherlands

The Dutch climate is temperate, maritime and relentlessly moderate. Summers are pleasant and rarely extreme, winters are cold but seldom brutal, and the main feature is the sky, which is covered for a remarkable proportion of the year. Rain falls often, in modest amounts, and wind sweeps in from the North Sea with the energy of something that has had a long journey and nothing to do.

The real difficulty is light. In midwinter the sun sets around half past four, and the days can pass without a single clear hour. Expat guides address this in a tone of practical concern: the shortage of daylight can affect mood, and some newcomers experience seasonal low spirits. The advice is consistent. Go outside daily, exercise, use bright light, keep a routine and speak to a doctor if it gets heavy.

Ghanaian media is full of stories about compatriots abroad who are astonished by the cold, and the headlines are almost affectionate in their disbelief. A man who relocated and cannot believe the fridge-like air. Someone who laments the weather and plans to return. The cold is only part of it. The grey and the early dark are what get under the skin.

The Dutch cope by embracing something they call gezelligheid, a coziness that involves candles, blankets, hot drinks and the company of friends. They cycle regardless, wear coats with the confidence of people who have never been warm, and treat a clear winter day as a national holiday. The attitude is neither cheerful nor gloomy. It is simply practical, and it works.

Summers can be lovely, with long evenings and terraces full of people who look as though they have just been released from prison. The sun rises early and sets late, and the sudden abundance of light lifts the national mood. That is the secret the Dutch keep: the dark months are the price of the glorious ones, and they pay it with a hot drink and a very good sense of humour.

## The Verdict

If we are discussing pure comfort, Ghana wins in a landslide, though it is a landslide of humidity. A warm climate is easier on the body, the wallet and the heating bill. Nobody in Kumasi has ever had to buy a thermal base layer.

But the Netherlands wins on predictability. The weather is mild, rarely dangerous and never plagued by the flooding that keeps people awake at night in rainy-season Accra. The Dutch have not conquered their climate, but they have domesticated it.

My verdict is that Ghana is the better weather and the Netherlands is the better preparation for it. If you move to the Netherlands, accept that your first winter will be a rite of passage, buy the good coat and learn to love a candle. You will not love the dark. You will love the people who have survived it with you.

## Conclusion

Leave Ghana for the Netherlands and you will trade a sun that demands your respect for a sky that ignores you. You will learn to dress in layers, to check three weather apps and to be grateful for a single bright afternoon.

Go the other way and you will rediscover that warmth is a luxury you did not know you had been rationing. Either way, the weather will make a fool of you at least once. The only choice is whether you would rather be too hot or too cold when it does.
