# Oman Will Meet You Halfway in English. Croatia Will Let You Struggle Beautifully in Croatian Until You Learn to Order a Coffee Properly.

🇴🇲 Oman + 🇭🇷 Croatia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Oman's relationship with English is warm, practical and remarkably forgiving — a legacy of trade routes, international business and a genuine national investment in bilingual education that means most urban interactions happen in a language you probably already speak. Croatia has no such mercy. Croatian is a proudly, defiantly local language, spoken with pride in a country that has spent much of its modern history fighting to keep its identity intact, and it will not soften itself simply because you've shown up without having learned it.

I've fumbled through both — mangling Arabic pleasantries in a Muscat souk, butchering Croatian case endings in a Split konoba — and the emotional experience of failing at each language could not be more different. One country laughs with you. The other, gently, laughs at you, until you improve, at which point it laughs with you too, but you have to earn that.

## Do's & Don'ts: Oman 🇴🇲

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Arabic greetings anyway — even minimal effort is warmly, visibly appreciated | Assume English fluency extends to government offices or rural areas equally |
| Use formal titles and greetings; Arabic hospitality customs matter even in English conversation | Rush a greeting exchange — the "how are you, how is your family" ritual is genuine, not filler |
| Carry a translation app for older generations and rural interactions | Assume everyone under 40 speaks fluent English just because many urban Omanis do |
| Appreciate that most signage, menus and official documents are bilingual | Expect the same bilingual ease outside Muscat and major coastal cities |

## Do's & Don'ts: Croatia 🇭🇷

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Croatian phrases before arriving — effort is noticed and rewarded with warmth | Assume English will carry you smoothly through older generations or inland villages |
| Expect excellent English among younger, urban and tourism-industry Croatians | Assume that fluency extends to bureaucracy, healthcare or rural municipal offices |
| Try pronouncing names and places properly — mangling them repeatedly grates over time | Give up on pronunciation attempts; persistence is genuinely respected here |
| Use gestures and patience in smaller towns; charades work better than you'd expect | Speak loudly and slowly in English hoping volume will bridge the gap; it won't |

## Oman: Bilingual by Design

Oman's approach to English isn't accidental — decades of trade, a strong tourism sector and deliberate investment in bilingual education have made English genuinely functional across most of urban life. Street signage, menus, government paperwork and business communication are routinely bilingual, and in Muscat particularly, you can navigate daily life — banking, healthcare, shopping, even fairly complex bureaucratic processes — almost entirely in English if needed.

What makes the experience feel warm rather than merely convenient is the cultural layer underneath the language itself. Omani hospitality customs — the extended greeting ritual, the genuine inquiry into your wellbeing and your family's, the offering of coffee and dates before any real conversation begins — happen regardless of which language you're using, and skipping past them straight to business, even fluently in Arabic, reads as a minor social misstep. Learning a handful of Arabic phrases, even badly pronounced ones, tends to produce visible delight rather than the polite tolerance you might expect; it signals respect for a culture that doesn't need you to speak its language but clearly appreciates the gesture.

Where the ease drops off is outside the main cities. Rural areas and older generations rely far more heavily on Arabic, and government offices, particularly outside Muscat, can require either a translator or considerably more patience than the shiny bilingual signage in the capital might have led you to expect.

## Croatia: Earn It, Then Enjoy It

Croatia offers no such linguistic safety net by default, and it doesn't particularly want to. Croatian is a source of real national pride, tightly bound up with a modern history of asserting independence and identity, and the expectation — quiet but firm — is that residents, even foreign ones, make a genuine effort rather than assuming the world should accommodate English.

That said, the country isn't unwelcoming — it's conditional. Younger Croatians, particularly in tourist-heavy coastal cities and Zagreb, generally speak excellent English, a product of subtitled rather than dubbed foreign media and strong language education. Order a coffee in Split or Dubrovnik in English and you'll be served without a flicker of difficulty. But step inland, engage with older generations, or attempt anything bureaucratic — registering residency, dealing with healthcare admin, navigating a small-town post office — and the linguistic ease evaporates fast.

What genuinely moves the needle with Croatians is visible, sustained effort. Mangling grammatical cases (Croatian has seven, an genuinely brutal detail for English speakers) while clearly trying earns real warmth over time, in a way that simply defaulting to loud, slow English never will. The emotional arc is distinct from Oman's immediate welcome: Croatia asks you to struggle a little first, then rewards the struggle generously once it's clear you're not just passing through without any intention of engaging.

## The Verdict

For sheer day-to-day ease, Oman wins comfortably — you can build an entire functional life there without ever opening a Croatian-style seven-case grammar textbook, and the hospitality softens even total linguistic failure into something charming rather than embarrassing. But Croatia offers something Oman's easier bilingualism doesn't quite replicate: the deep, earned satisfaction of watching a shopkeeper's face change when you finally nail a sentence in their notoriously difficult language. Oman is kinder to beginners. Croatia is more rewarding to the persistent.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a relocated engineer wrote that daily life in Muscat runs comfortably in English, but a mid-sized municipal office two hours outside the capital required an Arabic-speaking colleague to accompany them.*

> *expat.com, Zagreb thread — a longtime resident said learning even broken Croatian transformed how shopkeepers and neighbours treated them, from polite distance to genuine warmth within a few months.*

> *InterNations Muscat — a member noted that skipping the traditional greeting exchange to get straight to business, even in fluent English, was gently but consistently corrected by Omani colleagues.*

## Conclusion

Oman and Croatia represent two different philosophies about what a foreigner owes a country linguistically. Oman has built a system that meets you most of the way, then quietly rewards any effort you make beyond that with real warmth. Croatia expects you to close most of the distance yourself, and reserves its warmest welcome for those who do. Neither approach is wrong. Just don't show up to Zagreb expecting Muscat's linguistic mercy — Croatian will not meet you halfway, and honestly, once you've earned your first unprompted compliment on your grammar, you won't want it to.
