# London Gyms Are Libraries With Dumbbells. Lagos Gyms Are Social Clubs With Squat Racks.

🇳🇬 Nigeria vs United Kingdom 🇬🇧 — *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

London gyms are libraries with dumbbells — headphones in, eyes down, a silent agreement that nobody will acknowledge anyone else's existence between sets. Lagos gyms are social clubs that happen to have squat racks — music loud, trainers shouting encouragement at total strangers, and a very real chance someone will ask about your business plan mid-deadlift.

I've trained in both. In London, I once went six months to the same 6am class without learning a single name. In Lagos, I learned someone's entire extended family history before I'd finished my warm-up. Only one of these felt like exercise. The other felt like exercise plus group therapy plus networking, all before 7am.

## The Do's & Don'ts

### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Train early — 5am to 7am is prime gym time before heat and traffic take over the day | Expect every gym to have reliable mains power; generator backup is a genuine selling point |
| Join a run club or outdoor bootcamp — Lagos has a thriving early-morning scene | Be shy about small talk; the gym is a social space first, a workout space second |
| Bring your own towel and water, always — not every gym supplies them | Underestimate the intensity of trainer-led group classes; "light warm-up" rarely means light |
| Expect loud Afrobeats and amapiano on the gym sound system, often at serious volume | Assume a boutique studio price reflects Western-equivalent facilities; check first |

### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book classes online in advance — spontaneous walk-ins are often turned away | Make eye contact or chat during a set; silence is the default social contract |
| Expect a strict etiquette around wiping down machines and queuing for equipment | Skip the induction session most gyms require before you touch the weights floor |
| Explore the UK's serious running and parkrun culture — free, weekly, and nationwide | Assume London gym prices are representative of the rest of the country; they're not |
| Pack headphones — it's less a suggestion and more a social requirement | Linger on a machine between sets if others are visibly waiting; it's a minor faux pas |

## Nigeria: The Gym as Community Hall

Lagos fitness culture runs on two engines: heat and hustle. Nobody trains at 2pm by choice — the city's brutal midday sun and gridlocked traffic push serious gym-goers into a 5am-to-7am window that has become genuinely iconic, with outdoor bootcamps, run clubs, and beachfront HIIT sessions along the Lekki and Victoria Island coastline packed before most of London has hit snooze once. Showing up for a 5:30am session and finding the car park already full isn't unusual; it's Tuesday.

Power is the quiet infrastructure story nobody tells you about in advance. Lagos's national grid is notoriously unreliable, so any gym worth its membership fee runs on generator backup, and "24-hour power" is listed on gym marketing material the way "rooftop pool" gets listed elsewhere — because it's a genuine, hard-won amenity, not a given. This shapes the whole vibe: gyms that can guarantee lights, air conditioning, and a working sound system all night become social anchors, places people linger in rather than just pass through.

And the social layer is the real differentiator. Nigerian gym culture is loud, warm, and deeply communal — trainers call out encouragement to people they've never met, strangers spot each other on the bench without being asked, and conversation flows freely between sets. Music matters enormously; expect Afrobeats, amapiano, and Burna Boy at a volume that would get a UK gym manager fired, and expect it to genuinely improve your workout rather than annoy you. Boutique fitness is booming in Lagos's wealthier districts, with serious, well-equipped studios charging prices that rival London's — but even at the premium end, the social warmth doesn't disappear. You will know people's names by week two, whether you intended to or not.

## United Kingdom: The Silent Treadmill Social Contract

British gym culture operates on an almost sacred principle: mutual, comprehensive avoidance of interaction. Headphones in before you've even reached the changing room. Eye contact treated as an accident to be apologised for. A full, unspoken etiquette exists — wipe the machine, re-rack the weights, don't hover, don't chat, don't, under any circumstances, offer unsolicited form advice to a stranger, however tempting. It's not coldness exactly; it's a deeply ingrained respect for other people's bubble of private, sweaty solitude.

Where the UK genuinely excels is in free, structured, community-run fitness that doesn't require a membership at all. Parkrun — the free, timed, volunteer-run 5k that happens every Saturday morning in parks across the entire country — is a genuine national institution, drawing hundreds of thousands of regular participants, and it's one of the few places British fitness culture actually turns warm: post-run chat over coffee, "parkrun tourism" where people travel to run different courses, a whole subculture of its own. It's the loophole in the national reserve, oddly enough, built entirely around not paying for anything.

Gyms themselves tend to run on tight administrative structure — mandatory inductions, booked class slots, strict cancellation windows — which rewards planners and mildly punishes anyone hoping to wander in on a whim. Prices vary wildly by region; London's boutique studios can rival Manhattan's, while a basic council-run leisure centre gym outside the capital might cost a tenth of that. The equipment is generally excellent and well-maintained nationwide. The conversation, generally, is not happening, and if you try to start one between sets, expect a polite, slightly startled nod and a swift return to the treadmill.

## The Verdict

Nigeria wins, decisively, on atmosphere — a gym in Lagos is a genuinely joyful, loud, communal place to be at 6am, generator hum and all, and you will leave knowing more about your fellow gym-goers than you know about some of your own relatives. The UK wins on infrastructure and consistency — reliable power is a given rather than a selling point, and parkrun remains one of the single best free fitness institutions on the planet.

If you want your workout to double as your social life, go to Lagos. If you want your workout to be a silent, efficient, headphones-in hour that nobody will ever interrupt, go to London. Just don't try to make small talk in a British gym changing room expecting Lagos-level warmth back — you'll get a nod, a towel adjustment, and absolutely nothing else.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/expats — paraphrased: "First week in a Lagos gym, a total stranger corrected my squat form, asked what I did for work, and invited me to his cousin's wedding. First week in a London gym, nobody looked at me once."*

> *Internations Lagos — paraphrased: a member described the 5am outdoor run club scene on Victoria Island as "more reliable than most of London's public transport," and said she'd never trained consistently until she found it.*

> *Quora — paraphrased, on UK gym quietness: a longtime London resident explained that talking to strangers mid-workout is treated roughly the same as talking loudly on a train — technically allowed, socially radioactive.*

## Conclusion

Both cultures have built genuinely effective fitness ecosystems around their own constraints — Lagos around heat, traffic, and unreliable power, turning all three into a thriving pre-dawn social scene; London around dense urban anonymity, turning silence itself into an entire unwritten etiquette.

Neither is wrong. One will make you fitter and more sociable in the same hour. The other will make you fitter and leave you blessedly, efficiently alone. Choose based on how badly you want a stranger's opinion on your deadlift form before 7am — Lagos will give it to you whether you asked or not.
