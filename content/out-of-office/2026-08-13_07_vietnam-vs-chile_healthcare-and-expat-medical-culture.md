# One Country Will Diagnose You in a Back-Alley Pharmacy for Two Dollars. The Other Will Make You Choose an Insurance Provider Before It Looks at Your Rash.

🇻🇳 🇨🇱 Vietnam vs Chile
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Vietnam and Chile represent two genuinely different philosophies of getting sick abroad. Vietnam runs on an informal, accessible, almost improvisational healthcare economy where a pharmacist will diagnose and medicate you over a counter for the price of lunch, no appointment, no insurance card, no waiting room. Chile runs on a formal, two-tiered system split between the public Fonasa network and private Isapre insurers, where getting seen quickly depends heavily on which piece of plastic is in your wallet, and navigating it as a newcomer requires actual paperwork literacy before you've even described your symptoms.

I have walked into a Hanoi pharmacy with a suspicious cough and walked out four minutes later, diagnosed, medicated, and five dollars poorer, and I have spent an entire afternoon in Santiago on hold trying to determine whether my Isapre plan covered a dermatologist or just made me feel like I was paying for the privilege of being confused. Both systems work. Only one of them will make you fill out a form first.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use pharmacies as a genuine first stop for minor ailments | Assume public hospitals will have English-speaking staff — bring a translator app |
| Get comprehensive travel/expat insurance for anything serious | Rely on local hospitals alone for major procedures — international clinics are safer bets |
| Carry cash; many private clinics don't take foreign cards easily | Skip vaccinations before arrival — ask your home doctor what's recommended |
| Use international clinics (Family Medical Practice, Vinmec) for anything beyond minor issues | Wait too long to seek care for dengue-like symptoms during rainy season |

### 🇨🇱 Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with Fonasa or get a private Isapre plan as soon as your visa allows | Assume Fonasa alone gets you fast private-style service — expect longer waits |
| Keep every receipt; reimbursement processes are paperwork-heavy | Skip travel insurance while sorting your local coverage — gaps happen |
| Use urgencias (ER) for anything genuinely urgent; it's efficient | Expect walk-in convenience like a Vietnamese pharmacy — appointments are the norm |
| Learn your plan's copay percentages before you need them, not after | Assume all private clinics accept every Isapre plan — check first |

## Vietnam: Healthcare as Accessible Improvisation

Vietnamese healthcare, at the everyday level, runs on a remarkably direct and accessible informal economy. Pharmacies are everywhere, staffed by pharmacists with real diagnostic knowledge who will assess a described symptom, sell you the appropriate medication — often things that would require a prescription elsewhere — and send you on your way for a fraction of what an equivalent Western consultation would cost. For minor ailments, this system is genuinely more convenient than most Western setups: no appointment, no insurance negotiation, no waiting room, just a conversation over a counter and a resolution within minutes.

For anything more serious, the picture splits sharply. Public hospitals are inexpensive but can be overwhelmed, under-resourced in some regions, and staffed with limited English, which makes navigating anything beyond routine care genuinely difficult without support. International clinics — Family Medical Practice, Vinmec, and similar — bridge this gap with English-speaking staff and Western-standard facilities, but at prices approaching what you'd pay at home, making comprehensive insurance a near-necessity rather than a nice-to-have for any expat planning to stay longer than a holiday.

What surprises newcomers most is the sheer range within a single system — the same country offering five-dollar pharmacy consultations and five-thousand-dollar international hospital stays, with very little standardised middle ground between them. Seasonal risks, particularly dengue fever during the rainy season, are real and taken seriously by residents but easy for newcomers to underestimate, and the informal, low-friction nature of everyday healthcare here can lull people into a false sense that all care is equally casual, right up until it very much isn't.

## Chile: Healthcare as Formal Infrastructure

Chilean healthcare operates through a genuinely well-built but bureaucratically dense dual system. Fonasa, the public option, is affordable and covers a real breadth of care, but comes with longer wait times and less choice over where you're treated. Isapre, the private insurance system, offers faster access and better facilities in exchange for meaningful monthly premiums that scale with age, health history, and plan tier — a structure that rewards early registration and can become genuinely expensive as you age into it, a fact many expats discover too late to easily reverse.

For newcomers, the paperwork is the real barrier rather than the medicine itself. Getting registered, understanding your specific plan's copay structure, and figuring out which clinics actually accept your particular Isapre plan requires real administrative effort before you've addressed a single symptom — a stark contrast to Vietnam's walk-up-and-be-seen pharmacy culture. Once you're through that setup phase, though, the actual quality of care, particularly in Santiago's private clinics, is genuinely excellent, comparable to Western European standards, with specialists, diagnostics, and facilities that wouldn't feel out of place in Madrid or Barcelona.

Emergency care, via urgencias, is a genuine bright spot — efficient, well-triaged, and accessible regardless of your specific plan in true emergencies. What catches expats off guard is less the quality of care and more the sheer amount of forward planning it demands — you need to have sorted your coverage, understood your reimbursement process, and ideally identified your preferred clinics before you actually need any of it, because Chile's system rewards preparation and mildly punishes improvisation, the exact inverse of Vietnam's everyday healthcare culture.

## The Verdict

Vietnam wins for sheer accessibility and low-friction everyday care — nowhere else have I been diagnosed, medicated, and back on the street within five minutes for the price of a coffee. Chile wins if you're planning to actually live there long-term and want genuinely excellent, Western-standard care once you've done the unglamorous work of setting up your coverage properly. I trust a Hanoi pharmacist with my cough more than I trust most GPs I've had back home. I trust a Santiago private clinic with anything requiring an actual specialist, provided I've done my Isapre homework in advance. Get both systems' paperwork logic backwards and you'll either underinsure yourself dangerously in Vietnam, or arrive in Chile expecting Hanoi-style walk-in convenience and be quietly, bureaucratically humbled.

## What Nobody Warned You About

## Conclusion

Vietnam and Chile have built healthcare systems that reflect their broader national instincts — one improvisational and immediate, the other formal and front-loaded. Neither is inherently better; they simply demand different things from you as a newcomer. Vietnam rewards spontaneity and punishes complacency about serious illness. Chile rewards preparation and punishes anyone expecting Vietnamese-style walk-in ease. Sort your Isapre plan before you need it. Find your trusted Hanoi pharmacy before you're too sick to explain your symptoms through Google Translate. Do both, and you'll be fine in either country. Do neither, and you'll learn the hard way, expensively, in exactly the system least forgiving of your particular oversight.
