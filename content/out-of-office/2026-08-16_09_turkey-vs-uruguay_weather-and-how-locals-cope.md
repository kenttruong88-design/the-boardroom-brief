# Istanbul Sweats Through Its Summers. Montevideo Just Gets Blown Sideways, Constantly.

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Istanbul's summer heat arrives with a specific, oppressive humidity that turns the entire city into something resembling a very beautiful sauna with better architecture, and locals respond with a rhythm of shaded tea gardens, late dinners, and an almost total surrender of the midday hours to indoor life. Montevideo's climate problem is subtler and, in its own way, more relentless: it isn't the heat, it's the wind — a near-constant coastal gale off the Río de la Plata that Uruguayans have built an entire posture, wardrobe, and mate-drinking culture around resisting.

I've melted gracelessly through an Istanbul August. I've also had a genuinely good coat torn from my hands in Montevideo by a gust that seemed personally offended I'd tried to walk in a straight line. Neither country apologises for its weather. Both have quietly, brilliantly adapted around it.

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in a proper windproof coat — Montevideo's gusts are no joke | Rely on an umbrella near the Rambla — the wind will destroy it instantly |

## Turkey: Surviving the Bosphorus Sauna

Istanbul's summer heat is less about raw temperature — often no worse on paper than plenty of Mediterranean cities — and more about a specific, punishing humidity that settles over the Bosphorus and refuses to leave for months at a time. The Turkish State Meteorological Service's own data shows Istanbul's summer humidity regularly running high enough to make even moderate temperatures feel considerably worse, and locals have responded not by fighting it but by rescheduling their entire day around it. Life shifts later: lunch stretches into a long, shaded affair, the streets empty out during the punishing early afternoon hours, and the real social and commercial life of the city resumes only as the sun drops and the temperature finally, mercifully, begins to ease.

Central to this adaptation is the çay bahçesi — the tea garden — a genuinely brilliant piece of vernacular urban design where shade, cross-breeze, and endless small glasses of tea combine into a legitimate, functional cooling strategy that predates air conditioning by centuries and, honestly, often outperforms it for comfort. Older buildings across the city frequently still lack air conditioning, a fact that surprises visitors expecting a thoroughly modern metropolis, and which makes these shaded outdoor institutions not a nostalgic tourist attraction but genuinely load-bearing infrastructure for surviving July. Go Türkiye's own seasonal guidance increasingly nudges visitors toward this local rhythm — plan around the heat rather than through it — which is really just official confirmation of what every Istanbul resident already knows: fighting a Bosphorus summer head-on is a losing and faintly ridiculous strategy.

## Uruguay: A Nation Permanently Windswept and Entirely Unbothered

Uruguay's climate rarely produces headline-grabbing extremes — no monsoons, no brutal winters, nothing dramatic enough to make international weather news. What it does produce, relentlessly, is wind. Montevideo sits exposed along the wide mouth of the Río de la Plata, and the resulting coastal gusts are a genuine daily fact of life rather than an occasional weather event, strong enough to render umbrellas essentially decorative and strong enough that INUMET, the national meteorological institute, treats wind advisories as routine seasonal content rather than emergency broadcasting.

Uruguayans have adapted with a wardrobe and posture built specifically around resistance — proper windproof coats treated as basic necessity rather than luxury, a walking gait that leans subtly and permanently into the gusts along the Rambla, and an entire cultural institution, mate drinking, that thrives specifically because it's a warm, grounding, sociable ritual perfectly suited to grey, blustery afternoons spent mostly indoors or huddled in doorways. The Uruguay Ministry of Tourism's seasonal guidance is refreshingly candid about this, warning visitors that mild average temperatures on paper don't remotely capture the lived experience of the wind chill along the coast. What's most striking, talking to locals, is the sheer lack of complaint about any of it — the wind isn't framed as an inconvenience to be endured but simply as Montevideo's permanent, defining character, woven so thoroughly into daily life that a genuinely still, windless day feels, to residents, faintly eerie.

## The Verdict

Istanbul's heat is dramatic, seasonal, and defeated with shade, timing, and tea. Montevideo's wind is quieter, constant, and defeated with wardrobe and sheer stubborn posture. If forced to pick which climate challenge I'd rather live inside, I'll take Uruguay's wind — at least it's honest about being there every single day, rather than ambushing you seasonally with a humidity that somehow gets worse every August you think you've adapted to it.

## Conclusion

Turkey teaches you to reschedule your entire day around a heat that arrives predictably and demands respect. Uruguay teaches you to simply hold your coat a little tighter and accept that some weather isn't a phase, it's a personality trait of the entire coastline. Neither country will change for you. Bring the tea garden mindset to Istanbul, and bring a coat with real conviction to Montevideo — the umbrella was never going to survive the Rambla anyway.
