# Canada Will Open Your Bank Account in an Hour and Take a Year to Answer Your Visa Email. Bolivia Will Make You Photocopy Your Soul.

🇨🇦 Canada vs 🇧🇴 Bolivia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Bureaucracy is the tax a nation charges for the privilege of existing in it officially. Every country levies it. Some collect it in paperwork, some in time, some in patience, and some — the truly gifted — in all three at once, with a smile and a rubber stamp. After fourteen relocations, I've come to believe that a country's bureaucracy tells you less about its efficiency and more about whether it trusts you. Canada and Bolivia answer that question in fascinatingly opposite ways.

Canada trusts your money almost immediately and your immigration status almost never. Its banks will welcome you with open arms, a newcomer package and a credit card before you've found a flat, while its immigration system will process your application at a pace that geologists would find leisurely. Bolivia trusts neither your money nor your documents — not until they've been photocopied, notarised, stamped, re-stamped and submitted in a queue that began before dawn. But it will, eventually, get it done, often with the help of someone whose entire profession is knowing which window to stand at.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇨🇦 Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Open a newcomer account at one of the major banks in your first week | Ignore your credit history — without one, renting and borrowing get harder |
| Apply for your Social Insurance Number (SIN) as soon as you're eligible | Share your SIN casually. It's highly sensitive |
| Learn Interac e-Transfer; it's how Canadians pay each other | Expect quick answers from immigration processing — check official timelines and wait |
| Keep copies of every immigration document, letter and receipt | Assume bank fees are negligible. Monthly account fees are common |

**🇧🇴 Bolivia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry multiple photocopies of your passport, visa and every document | Arrive at a government office without copies — you'll be sent away to make them |
| Get documents properly apostilled and translated before you arrive | Assume a document from home will be accepted without legalisation |
| Consider hiring a reputable tramitador (paperwork agent) for complex procedures | Hand money to an unofficial "fixer" in a queue promising shortcuts |
| Be patient, polite and early — queues form before offices open | Expect easy access to US dollars; foreign currency has been hard to obtain in recent years |

## Canada: Friendly Banks, Glacial Paperwork

Canadian banking is dominated by a small group of large banks, and they have become remarkably good at courting newcomers. Walk in with your passport and immigration papers and there's a decent chance you'll walk out with a chequing account, a debit card and a modest credit card, often with fee waivers for your first year. Branches are polite, staff are helpful, and online banking is excellent. Interac e-Transfer, the national payments system, means you'll be splitting dinner bills by email within a week.

The catch is credit history. Canada runs on it, and you arrive without one. Landlords, phone companies and car dealers will check your credit score and find nothing, which is treated with the suspicion normally reserved for a criminal record. Newcomers spend their first year carefully building credit through secured cards and small, dutifully repaid balances, like apprentices earning the right to be trusted with a mortgage.

Then there's immigration bureaucracy, which is an entirely different animal. The system is organised, largely digital, and entirely lacking in urgency. Work permits, permanent residence applications and citizenship files can take months or longer depending on the stream and the backlog, and the online status checker becomes a source of existential dread. Emails can go unanswered; phone lines can mean long holds. There's rarely hostility — Canada is too polite for that — just a vast, courteous, impenetrable waiting room.

The overall experience is a strange split personality: a financial system eager to give you a credit card and an immigration system that seems to have forgotten you exist.

## Bolivia: Paper, Stamps and the Art of the Trámite

Bolivian bureaucracy is not digital-first. It is paper-first, paper-second and paper-in-triplicate. Every procedure — a residency application, a foreigner's ID card, a bank account, a vehicle registration — involves a trámite, a ritual of forms, photocopies, notarised copies, fiscal stamps and signatures, often at multiple offices in a specific order that nobody writes down in full.

Photocopies are the currency of the realm. There are copy shops next to every government building for a reason: you will need copies of your passport, visa, previous copies, receipts for the copies. Documents from abroad typically need an apostille and an official translation, and sometimes further legalisation locally. The rules shift, and the version you read online may not be the version the person at window four is applying today.

Queues start early. In La Paz, Santa Cruz and Cochabamba, you'll see lines forming outside migration and bank offices before opening time. Many foreigners hire a tramitador — a professional paperwork agent — who knows the offices, the forms and the order of operations. Used carefully, a reputable one is worth every boliviano.

Banking itself is workable, with major banks and strong microfinance institutions such as BancoSol. But in recent years Bolivia has faced a shortage of US dollars, making foreign currency hard to obtain through banks, creating limits on withdrawals and transfers and a gap between official and street exchange rates. Expats who assumed they could move money in and out freely have had to rethink.

## The Verdict

Canada wins, but not for the reason Canadians would assume. Its banks are genuinely excellent to newcomers, and its bureaucracy, however slow, is transparent: the rules are online, the forms are consistent, and nobody asks you for a photocopy of a photocopy. You may wait a year, but you know what you're waiting for.

Bolivia's bureaucracy is harder, and its currency situation adds real risk for anyone whose income or savings are in foreign money. But I want to give it credit for one thing: it is human. There is always a person — at a window, in a queue, in a copy shop — who can help. Canadian bureaucracy is a polite wall. Bolivian bureaucracy is a maze with guides.

Canada is easier. Bolivia is more honest about how hard it is.

## Conclusion

Bureaucracy is where a country stops pretending. Canada's pretence is that it's a friendly, efficient place — and the banks live up to it, even if immigration offices have quietly decided that time is infinite and your life can wait. Bolivia doesn't pretend at all. It's paper, stamps, queues and patience, and anyone who tells you otherwise has hired a tramitador and forgotten to mention it.

The lesson for any expat is the same in both: bring every document, then bring copies of it, then bring patience. Build your credit in Canada. Build your relationships in Bolivia.

And when someone at home complains about waiting on hold for twenty minutes, smile gently and say nothing. They have no idea what a real queue looks like.
