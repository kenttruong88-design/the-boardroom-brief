# Hong Kong Charges You Three Months to Sleep in a Cupboard and Australia Charges You a Saturday Queue

🇭🇰 Hong Kong vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Accommodation is the first thing a corporate relocation promises and the last thing it delivers. The HR deck says "serviced apartment" and the reality says "a studio with a sofa bed, a bathroom you can use only by closing the front door". In Hong Kong the problem is space. In Australia the problem is competition. Both ask you to prove, repeatedly, that you deserve a roof.

Hong Kong sells you a view of a skyscraper from a flat the size of a London wardrobe, and charges you a handsome fee to sign the lease. Australia sells you a spacious house with a garden, then makes you queue with forty others for fifteen minutes on a Saturday to see it. One city is expensive and tiny; the other is huge and impossible. Take your pick.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Hong Kong**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget for agent fee, deposit and first month up front | Arrive with only one month's rent in cash |
| Check whether the flat is furnished, because most are not | Assume a bed and sofa are included |
| Consider a serviced apartment for the first weeks | Sign a two-year lease blind |
| Ask about stamp duty and who pays it | Ignore the small print |

**Australia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Prepare your ID and references before the inspection | Turn up with a passport and a smile |
| Expect to pay a bond of about four weeks' rent | Hand a deposit to a landlord directly |
| Arrange temporary accommodation for the first weeks | Count on finding a lease in a week |
| Check whether utilities are included | Assume the rent covers the bills |

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong's rental system is a transaction, and the upfront bill is the headline. According to MoveAndStay, an agency blog, you pay roughly half a month's rent as agent commission, a deposit of about two months and the first month in advance. At HK$25,000 a month that is HK$75,000 before the agent's fee. Expat Arrivals says the commission is split half and half between tenant and landlord, and that deposits of two to three months are standard. Most apartments are unfurnished, and furnishing one can cost HK$20,000 to 50,000. Leases are typically two years, often breakable after the first with two months' notice, and stamp duty is 0.5 per cent of annual rent, usually split.

The forum voices are blunt. One poster, in a thread about six years old, says HK$10,000 a month buys a very small flat and a room in a shared flat costs HK$5,000 to 8,000, and that with an agency fee of half a month, a two-month deposit and one month in advance, move-in day costs about HK$35,000 for a HK$10,000 rent. Another thread describes a roughly 430 square foot flat in Wan Chai at HK$28,000 and notes that many studios lack a Western-style kitchen. A Canadian poster in an old thread says HK$18,000 a month gets a decent serviced studio or a smaller one-bedroom, and warns that portal listings often omit prices.

The extreme end is worse. A Legislative Council research brief, using 2016 data, counted about 92,700 subdivided units in Hong Kong, with a median floor area of 5.3 square metres per person, against 18 in private flats. Rent for those households took nearly a third of income. That is not the corporate expat's world, but it is the same market, and it explains why the corporate world has services built around it.

A word on the lease itself. The two-year Hong Kong term, with a break clause after the first year, is a decent deal for a corporate assignment, because it matches the usual shape of a posting. The catch is the notice: two months, in writing, and the landlord's expectations about the state of the flat when you leave. Check the inventory on arrival and photograph everything, because a tiny flat has nowhere for a dispute to hide.

Serviced apartments bridge the gap. MoveAndStay says they require a one-month minimum and can cost up to twice a regular flat, but include utilities. For a two-month transfer, they are sensible. For a three-year posting, they are an extravagance with a concierge.

## Australia

Australian renting is a different kind of difficult. The deposit is called a bond, it is typically four weeks' rent in most states, and it is held by a government authority, not the landlord: Rental Bonds Online in New South Wales and the RTBA in Victoria, for example. Leases usually run six or twelve months, utilities are normally not included and furnished units come with basic furniture while unfurnished ones may have only a stove and an oven. Rent is quoted weekly and paid fortnightly or monthly.

The hard part is the process. An Expat.com guide to renting in Sydney says inspections last fifteen to thirty minutes, mostly on Saturdays, applicants need "100 points" of identification and a studio in the eastern suburbs starts around A$350 a week. A BrightTax blog says open inspections and waiting lists are the norm, routine inspections happen every three to six months and agents use platforms such as Snug and 2Apply. The same guides suggest organising about three months of temporary accommodation before committing to a lease.

I should be clear about the limits. I did not find a clearly stated agent-fee rule for tenants in Australia and I will not assert one. I also found no genuine expat voices from the Australian side, which is a gap, not a verdict. What the sources do show is a market where the pressure is on the applicant's paperwork, not their wallet, at least at the front door.

The Australian bond system is one of the more civilised things in this comparison. Because the money sits with a government body, a disagreement about the carpet becomes a procedure instead of a feud. That matters for a newcomer with no local network, no legal adviser and a flight home booked for a Sunday. The comparison sites suggest the details vary by state, so read your own state's rules, and note that one source's description of Queensland's thresholds was internally inconsistent, which is a good reason to check the official page.

The corporate expat's best friend in both places is a relocation agent, a temporary stay and a very low expectation of what the first apartment will be like.

## The Verdict

Australia wins on space, and Hong Kong wins on speed. In Hong Kong you can sign, pay and move in within days, if you accept the fees and the size. In Australia you may spend weeks refreshing listings and polishing a rental application, but when you win, you win a home with an actual room in it.

The cost structures differ too. Hong Kong front-loads the pain with a hefty commission, deposit and furniture bill, then leaves you alone for two years. Australia spreads the pain across a series of inspections and applications, but the bond is protected and the lease is shorter, which suits a mobile career.

There is one more difference worth stating, and it is cultural. In Hong Kong the apartment is an asset class and a status marker, so everyone knows what you pay and what you got. In Australia it is a home, so people talk about the light, the neighbours and the garden. A Hong Kong landlord asks about your employer; an Australian agent asks about your references and your pets. Neither is sentimental, but one is at least pretending to care whether you will be happy there.

I would take the Australian flat and the Hong Kong efficiency, but nobody offers that combination. Choose whether you would rather pay in money or in Saturdays.

## Conclusion

Where you sleep sets the tone for everything else. Hong Kong teaches you to love small spaces and read the fine print. Australia teaches you to be patient, organised and good at standing in a queue in your best shirt.

If you are heading to Hong Kong, bring cash, a tape measure and a sense of humour about the kitchen. If you are heading to Australia, bring documents, references and a plan B. In both cases, never sign anything you have not read, and never trust a listing photo taken with a fisheye lens.
