# Free Public Aerobics vs. a Gym Membership You Actually Need a Car For

🇰🇭 Cambodia · 🇬🇹 Guatemala
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

At dusk in Phnom Penh, every park in the city fills with synchronized public aerobics — free, loud, blaring Khmer pop remixes, open to absolutely anyone who wanders over and starts moving. In Guatemala City, fitness is a much more guarded, indoor, security-conscious affair, gated behind gym memberships and private complexes because the calculus of exercising outdoors alone is simply different there. Both countries care enormously about fitness. Only one of them has turned it into the best free show in town.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Cambodia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a public park aerobics session at dusk — genuinely open to all | Show up expecting silence; the speakers are part of the ritual |
| Try the outdoor gym equipment installed in most city parks — it's free | Assume it's only for locals; foreigners joining in is warmly welcomed |
| Go early or late to avoid the worst of the midday heat and humidity | Overdress; lightweight, breathable gear is non-negotiable here |

**Guatemala**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Choose a reputable gym with secure parking and vetted membership | Run alone in unfamiliar areas, especially before sunrise or after dark |
| Ask locals or expat groups for currently-safe running routes | Assume all neighbourhoods carry the same risk profile — they vary sharply |
| Consider a private trainer; it's common and relatively affordable | Leave valuables visible in a gym bag or car while exercising outdoors |

## Cambodia: The Park Is the Gym, and Everyone's Invited

Phnom Penh has quietly built one of the most genuinely democratic fitness cultures I've encountered anywhere. Every evening, as the worst of the day's heat finally breaks, public parks across the city transform into open-air aerobics classes — someone brings a speaker, someone else leads the choreography, and within minutes dozens of people of wildly different ages and fitness levels are moving in loose unison to Khmer pop remixed within an inch of its life. Nobody charges admission. Nobody checks whether you belong there. You simply walk up and join.

What makes this remarkable isn't just the cost — it's the sheer social range it pulls in. Elderly women who've clearly been doing this for a decade dance next to teenagers on their phones between reps, next to the occasional bemused tourist who wandered over out of curiosity and got roped into the routine by an enthusiastic grandmother. Cambodia's outdoor public gym equipment — the free metal contraptions installed in parks across Phnom Penh and other cities, government-funded as basic public infrastructure — gets genuine daily use, not the token, rusting-unused treatment similar installations receive in some other countries.

Riverside Phnom Penh in particular turns into a genuine fitness corridor by early evening — joggers, badminton games set up spontaneously with string tied between trees, groups of teenagers playing sei (a foot-volleyball game involving a woven rattan ball) with startling skill. The whole scene has an unforced, communal energy that gym-membership fitness culture almost never replicates. This is fitness as public good rather than private consumption, and it shows in how unselfconscious everyone is about participating.

The heat, it must be said, is a genuine obstacle. Cambodia's climate makes anything beyond a gentle evening session fairly punishing during the hottest parts of the year, which is precisely why the culture has organized itself around dusk and early morning windows rather than fighting the midday sun. Locals plan around it instinctively; newcomers usually learn the hard way, once, and then never again.

## Guatemala: Fitness, But Make It Secure

Guatemala City's fitness culture operates under a very different set of constraints, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest. Personal security concerns genuinely shape where, when, and how people exercise, and this isn't paranoia — it's a practical calculus that locals themselves navigate daily, adjusting routes, timing, and company based on real, specific neighbourhood knowledge that takes newcomers time to absorb.

The result is a gym culture that's more privatised and indoor-oriented than Cambodia's park scene. Private gyms — ranging from serious, well-equipped facilities to smaller neighbourhood setups — anchor most people's fitness routines, valued not just for the equipment but for the secure parking, controlled access, and predictable environment they offer. Personal trainers are common and relatively accessible by regional standards, and many Guatemalans, especially in Guatemala City's wealthier zones, build their entire fitness routine around a trusted trainer relationship rather than solo gym visits.

Running outdoors is absolutely still part of the culture — it's just more curated. Certain routes and neighbourhoods (parts of Zona 14, Zona 10, gated residential areas) earn reputations as genuinely safe for morning runs, and locals and expats alike share this knowledge fairly openly, almost as a form of mutual protection. What doesn't happen, sensibly, is spontaneous solo exercise in unfamiliar parts of the city — the improvisational, anyone-can-join spirit of a Phnom Penh park session just doesn't map onto Guatemala City's current security reality.

Outside the capital, particularly in smaller towns and around Lake Atitlán or Antigua, the picture shifts considerably — outdoor hiking and walking culture is genuinely strong, tourism-friendly, and far less encumbered by the security calculus that shapes the capital. Guatemala's fitness culture, in other words, isn't monolithic; it's sharply zoned, and knowing which zone you're in matters more than almost anywhere else on this list.

## The Verdict

Cambodia wins, decisively, on accessibility and sheer communal joy — a free, genuinely open fitness culture that asks nothing of you but a willingness to dance badly next to strangers at dusk. Guatemala's more guarded, gym-and-trainer-centred approach isn't a failure of culture; it's a rational response to a real security context, and within that context, it's actually well-organised and genuinely effective. If I'm choosing pure fun, I'm at the Phnom Penh riverside at 6pm. If I'm choosing where to train seriously and safely long-term, I'm getting a Guatemala City gym membership with secure parking, no argument.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations Phnom Penh — paraphrased: a newcomer describing being pulled into a park aerobics line by a stranger within their first week, and now attending most evenings out of genuine habit.*

> *expat.com Guatemala City forum — paraphrased: a resident explaining that choosing a gym is really about choosing a neighbourhood and a parking situation first, equipment second.*

> *Quora, on running in Guatemala City — paraphrased: a local runner listing three specific safe routes by name, and noting that straying even a few blocks off them changes the calculation considerably.*

## Conclusion

Cambodia proves that fitness culture can be entirely free, entirely public, and entirely joyful, provided you're willing to dance next to someone's grandmother at dusk. Guatemala proves that a security-conscious fitness culture isn't a lesser one — it's just more deliberate about where it happens and who's involved. Bring cash for a proper Guatemala City gym membership. Bring nothing but yourself to a Phnom Penh park at 5:30pm. Someone will hand you the choreography.
