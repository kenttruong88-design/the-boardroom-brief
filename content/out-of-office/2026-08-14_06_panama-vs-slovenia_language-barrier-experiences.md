# Spanish Gets You Fed in One Country, English Gets You Everywhere in the Other

🇵🇦 Panama 🇸🇮 Slovenia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Panama and Slovenia sit at almost opposite ends of the language-barrier spectrum, and the gap has less to do with how welcoming either country is and more to do with sheer geography and history. Panama runs almost entirely on Spanish, with English mostly confined to banking towers and canal logistics — step outside Panama City's financial district and your survival depends on functional Spanish, full stop. Slovenia, a country of two million people wedged between Italy, Austria, and Croatia, produces some of the best English speakers in Europe almost by necessity, because nobody else is going to learn Slovenian for you.

I've fumbled through both. In Panama, my broken Spanish got me fed, housed, and occasionally scolded by market vendors with real patience. In Slovenia, my attempts at Slovenian were met, almost every time, with a gentle switch to flawless English, which felt less like a barrier and more like being quietly out-competed by a nineteen-year-old barista.

## Do's & Don'ts: Panama

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn functional, practical Spanish before arriving — it is genuinely load-bearing here | Assume Panama City's international banking sector reflects the whole country's English level |
| Use Duolingo or a tutor for market and taxi Spanish specifically — it's a different register than textbook Spanish | Expect rural Panama, especially the interior and comarcas, to accommodate English at all |
| Lean on patience and gestures in a pinch — Panamanians are generally warm about language mistakes | Get frustrated audibly when misunderstood; it reads as rude regardless of intent |

## Do's & Don'ts: Slovenia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attempt Slovenian anyway, even briefly — the effort is noticed and appreciated, even if met with English | Assume everyone under 40 speaks flawless English just because most do — older generations often don't |
| Learn a few key phrases for rural areas, where English drops off noticeably | Rely purely on English in government offices — some bureaucratic processes still require Slovenian forms |
| Take advantage of Slovenia's high English fluency for daily logistics | Mistake fluent English for cultural sameness — Slovenian customs remain distinctly their own |

## Panama: Spanish Isn't Optional, It's the Whole Operating Manual

Panama City's skyline, glassy and dense with international banks, gives newcomers a false first impression — that this is a place where English quietly runs everything behind the scenes, the way it does in Dubai or Singapore. It doesn't. That impression survives about as long as your first trip outside the financial district, at which point you discover that taxi drivers, market vendors, pharmacy staff, and the vast majority of daily life run entirely on Spanish, spoken fast, with plenty of regional slang that no textbook prepared you for.

The interior of the country and the comarcas — the indigenous territories that make up a meaningful chunk of Panama's land and culture — operate almost exclusively in Spanish, and in some communities, in indigenous languages that predate Spanish colonisation entirely. Expecting English there isn't just unrealistic, it's a little tone-deaf about what Panama actually is beneath the canal and the banking towers.

The upside is that Panamanian Spanish, while fast, is generally considered one of the more approachable Latin American dialects for learners, and the warmth with which mistakes are received softens the learning curve considerably. Market vendors will correct your grammar mid-transaction, good-naturedly, the way you'd correct a child, and somehow it never feels condescending. You leave every interaction slightly more fluent than you arrived, whether you wanted the lesson or not. Panama doesn't hand you a shortcut. It hands you immersion, at speed, whether you're ready or not.

## Slovenia: Everyone Speaks Better English Than You Expect, and That's the Whole Problem

Slovenia presents the opposite challenge entirely: not a barrier, but a bypass so smooth it becomes its own obstacle. With a population under two million and a language spoken almost nowhere else on earth, Slovenians have made peace with the practical reality that international business, tourism, and media all run on English, German, or Italian, and the country's education system reflects that — English fluency, particularly among anyone under 40, is remarkably high, delivered with barely an accent, and offered instantly the moment you fumble a Slovenian sentence.

That instant switch is generous, and also, quietly, demoralising if you actually wanted to learn Slovenian. Attempt a phrase in a Ljubljana café and you'll often get a warm, encouraging smile followed immediately by a fluent English reply, which is lovely and also means you'll need real discipline to keep practising rather than defaulting to the path of least resistance every single time.

The gaps show up in specific places: rural villages, older generations who came of age before English became the default second language, and certain government or administrative processes that still require Slovenian-language forms regardless of how good your English is. Bureaucracy here, as in much of Central Europe, doesn't care how charmingly you butcher the local grammar — some processes simply require the paperwork in Slovenian, full stop, and that's where a translator or a genuinely patient local friend becomes essential rather than optional.

## The Verdict

Panama forces language learning on you through sheer necessity, and you come out the other side genuinely fluent, tested, and slightly exhausted. Slovenia removes the necessity almost entirely, which is more comfortable day-to-day but means most expats never actually learn the local language, because why would you, when every barista already speaks better English than your Spanish teacher ever taught you in Panama. If forced to choose, I'd take Panama's harder path — it left me with an actual skill. Slovenia left me with excellent small talk and zero Slovenian vocabulary beyond "hvala."

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/Panama — an expat said their carefully practised Spanish phrases were met with total confusion in the interior because the local slang bore almost no resemblance to what they'd learned from an app.*

> *Internations Ljubljana — someone admitted after two years they still couldn't hold a basic Slovenian conversation because every attempt got politely redirected into English within seconds.*

> *expat.com Panama — a longtime resident warned that assuming Panama City's English-speaking banking crowd represents the country is "the fastest way to get lost outside the capital."*

## Conclusion

Panama and Slovenia both prove that a language barrier isn't really about difficulty, it's about incentive. Panama gives you every incentive to learn because nothing else works. Slovenia removes the incentive almost entirely by being unfairly good at English, and in doing so, quietly denies you the fluency you thought you wanted. If you actually want to learn a language while living abroad, ironically, the harder country is the better teacher. Slovenia will just be nicer about letting you fail to try.
