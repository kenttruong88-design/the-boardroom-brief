🇮🇳 India vs 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nothing exposes a culture's actual values quite like watching how it exercises in public. India does its morning cardio in a sari. Ireland does its morning cardio reluctantly, in the rain, having paid more for the privilege than almost anywhere else in Europe. One of these approaches has roughly a century of continuity behind it. The other is a recent, extremely expensive panic response to modern life.

Hofstede's cultural data gives you the theory before you've even landed: India scores 77 on Power Distance and 26 on Indulgence — deferential, disciplined, not built around personal pleasure-seeking. Ireland scores 28 on Power Distance and 65 on Indulgence — informal, familiar, built for exactly the kind of casual self-indulgence a gym membership is supposed to represent. And yet it's Ireland, not India, that has turned fitness into a genuine financial burden. Explain that one to the spreadsheet.

## India: a tradition that predates the industry built to sell it back to you

Bangalore's Cubbon Park closes its internal roads from 5am to 8am every morning specifically to make room for walkers and joggers, and what's genuinely striking — documented by the Christian Science Monitor as far back as 2005 — is what they're wearing while they do it: saris, salwar kameez, burqas, baggy shorts, dhotis. Not athleisure. Not compression gear. A tradition that traces back to Bangalore's British-cantonment-era retired-military exercise culture, running decades before the modern gym boom arrived to monetise the same impulse. Headphones, notably, are frowned upon here — not because anyone's enforcing silence, but because the entire point of the walk is the gossip and networking that happens shoulder to shoulder with your neighbours, and a pair of earbuds is a wall nobody asked you to build.

The gym floor itself, where it exists, runs on a completely different social contract than its Western equivalent. An American expat's account of his New Delhi gym, written years ago but still recognisable in more recent testimony, describes a space an eighth the size of his old Los Angeles club, more crowded, costing roughly two and a half times as much, staffed by attendants who silently take your clothes and trainers who correct your form and hand out free advice entirely unprompted — a level of hands-on generosity a Ukrainian content creator living in India more recently confirmed with her own viral comparison: "Everyone's super warm, friendly, and always ready to help," she said, needling the European alternative where, as she joked, you pay for ten personal training sessions before anyone will even answer a question. Gym membership itself remains a minority pursuit — one widely cited, if loosely sourced, estimate puts formal membership at only around 5 million people nationally, a figure almost certainly undercounting the millions who simply walk the park instead and have never once considered that informal. Meanwhile a very current, very online anxiety has crept in at the top end: a doctor and twenty-year lifter, venting on Reddit's r/OffMyChestIndia, warns young Indian men chasing Instagram-driven physiques against daily multi-hour training and flags real medical risk factors — smoking and alcohol matter more for long-term health than muscle mass ever will, he argues, to predictable pushback from commenters who'd rather talk about protein access than overtraining.

## Ireland: devotion, priced like a luxury good

Ireland's gym economy is, by its own financial press's account, genuinely strange. A 2018 Irish Times analysis, citing a Deloitte/EuropeActive survey spanning 19 countries, found the average Irish gym costs €45 a month — the second most expensive in Europe, behind only Switzerland — and that gym costs eat up 2.9% of Irish disposable income, the highest proportion recorded anywhere in that survey, against a 2–2.5% norm elsewhere. Roughly 500,000 Irish people hold gym memberships generating some €300 million in annual revenue, with independent gyms — not international chains — accounting for 80% of that membership, a genuinely distinctive market structure. Budget operators run €25–30 a month; premium clubs charge €69–96; one Dublin franchise was cited charging roughly €90 a fortnight, which, converted into a monthly figure, should make anyone pause before complaining about Manhattan prices again. High Dublin retail rents, nearly double London's per the same analysis, are cited as the direct driver.

What makes this bearable, and even a little charming, is what sits around the gym rather than inside it. The GAA — the Gaelic Athletic Association — runs hurling and football pitches that function as much as community and social infrastructure as fitness venues, a genuinely different register from a paid membership model. One Indian software engineer who relocated to Dublin for work, profiled by Silicon Republic, names hurling specifically as something she came to love about her adopted country, alongside her employer's free gym access and the simple, specific pleasures of Phoenix Park and the river. Phoenix Park itself deserves the hype: 1,750 acres, one of the largest walled parks in Europe, an 11-kilometre perimeter wall, a 2.6-mile straight run along Chesterfield Avenue, and, almost incidentally, Ireland's zoo and the President's own residence inside its boundary. And then there's the pub, which an ExpatExchange culture-shock account names, not entirely jokingly, as the real entry point to Dublin social life — it takes roughly a year to make genuine friends here, the same account notes, and "the lack of sun can take a toll" in winter in ways no gym membership, however expensive, will fix.

## The Verdict

India offers a fitness culture that is older than the concept of a gym membership, free to join, and built on actual neighbourly generosity — a trainer who'll correct your form for nothing, a park that closes its roads for you, a stranger who'll warn you off a bad habit on Reddit for free. Ireland offers a fitness culture that costs more per month than almost anywhere else in Europe and still, somehow, manages to be genuinely lovable once you factor in the GAA pitch and the pub. If I'm paying, I want Cubbon Park's five free morning hours, not Dublin's €90-a-fortnight franchise bill. India wins on value. Ireland wins on vibes. Your bank balance should decide the rest.

## Conclusion

The honest comparison here isn't gym versus gym. It's a free, communal, decades-old walking tradition against a pricey, newly fashionable, individually-paid-for one — and the fact that Ireland's version costs more per month than India's costs per year tells you almost everything about which country built its fitness culture around community and which one built it around a business model. Go to Cubbon Park at dawn if you want to actually understand this. Go to a Dublin gym if you just want the receipt.
