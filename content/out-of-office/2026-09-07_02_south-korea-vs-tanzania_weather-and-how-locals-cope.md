# One Country Built an Umbrella Vending Machine Empire. The Other Just Waits It Out.

🇰🇷 South Korea · 🇹🇿 Tanzania
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

South Korea has engineered its way around the weather so thoroughly that you can walk from your apartment to your office to a department store to a subway platform without ever technically going outside, which some Seoulites treat as a lifestyle achievement rather than the mild dystopia it resembles. Tanzania, which deals with two genuinely dramatic rainy seasons a year, has instead cultivated something closer to acceptance — you plan around the rains because fighting them is a waste of the energy you'll need later for everything else. One nation has commodified weather avoidance into an art form. The other has simply decided the sky is in charge, and that's fine.

I've sweated through a Seoul monsoon in a suit I was not dressed for, and I've watched a Dar es Salaam afternoon go from blazing to biblical in the time it takes to order a coffee. Only one experience made me feel personally judged by an entire retail infrastructure.

## Do's & Don'ts: South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a cheap umbrella from a convenience store the second it starts drizzling — they're everywhere and cost almost nothing | Bother bringing a "good" umbrella during jangma season; you will lose it within a week regardless |
| Check the fine dust (*misemeonji*) index before deciding whether to wear a mask outside | Assume summer ends when the rain stops — the humidity often lingers well past the monsoon itself |
| Use the underground shopping arcades connected to subway stations to stay dry across entire neighbourhoods | Underestimate how seriously Koreans take heatstroke warnings — public cooling centers exist for a reason |

## Do's & Don'ts: Tanzania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan travel and outdoor work around the "long rains" (March–May) and "short rains" (Oct–Dec) | Expect punctuality from anyone, including yourself, once the roads start flooding |
| Keep a rain jacket rather than an umbrella — coastal wind makes umbrellas mostly decorative | Panic about a sudden downpour; locals will calmly wait it out under the nearest roof, and so should you |
| Drink more water than feels necessary during the hot, dry season between rains | Schedule anything time-critical during a known heavy-rain window if it can possibly be avoided |

## South Korea: Weatherproofing as a National Personality Trait

Seoul's relationship with its own climate is one of total infrastructural denial. The city gets a real monsoon season, *jangma*, typically landing in June and July, dumping enough rain to make umbrellas a disposable commodity rather than an investment — and Koreans have responded not by adapting their behaviour but by building an entire subterranean and interconnected retail world so you never technically have to experience it. Underground arcades connect subway stations to department stores to office towers across huge swaths of the city, and a determined commuter really can go days without direct exposure to weather they find inconvenient.

This extends to the dry cold of winter and the increasingly brutal summer heat, both of which get treated with the same engineering-first mentality. Public buildings are aggressively heated and cooled. Convenience stores — and there is one on nearly every block — sell single-use ponchos and folding umbrellas for the equivalent of pocket change, on the reasonable assumption that you will lose or destroy whatever you buy within days and simply purchase another. Nobody mourns a lost umbrella here the way a British person would; it's understood as consumable infrastructure, like a coffee cup.

What foreigners consistently underestimate is the *misemeonji* — fine particulate pollution that spikes in spring, often blowing in from industrial regions further west, and gets tracked with the same daily anxiety other countries reserve for pollen counts. Koreans check the fine dust index the way other nations check for rain, and a genuinely bad air day will see mask use spike back toward pandemic-era levels even among people who otherwise never wear one. It's a reminder that "weather" here means something slightly broader and more chemical than it does elsewhere — the sky itself is a data point to be monitored, not just observed.

## Tanzania: The Rains Run the Calendar, Not the Other Way Around

Tanzania's climate operates on a rhythm that predates any of the infrastructure built to accommodate it, and largely still does: the *masika*, or long rains, from roughly March to May, and the shorter *vuli* rains toward the end of the year, both of which can transform a passable dirt road into an impassable one within an afternoon. This is not a minor inconvenience treated as a fun local quirk — it genuinely restructures agriculture, transport schedules, and construction timelines across the country, and anyone doing business here learns quickly that a project plan which doesn't account for the rains is not a serious project plan.

What's striking, coming from a culture obsessed with weatherproofing everything, is the comparative calm with which most Tanzanians treat a sudden downpour. There's no scramble, no visible frustration — people move under the nearest available roof, wait, and continue their day once it passes, on the reasonable assumption that the rain, like most things, will end when it ends and not a moment sooner. Umbrellas exist but are treated with mild skepticism, partly because coastal areas like Dar es Salaam get enough wind that an umbrella is more liability than shelter; a proper rain jacket or simply accepting you'll get wet does the job better.

The dry season between the two rains brings its own challenge, less dramatic but more relentless: heat that builds steadily rather than announcing itself, particularly inland and up-country, where hydration becomes less a suggestion and more a survival requirement for anyone not used to it. Locals pace their days around it instinctively — heavier activity in the cooler morning hours, a slower rhythm through the hottest stretch of afternoon — in a way that looks, to an outsider fresh off a plane and eager to see everything immediately, suspiciously like laziness, until you try to keep their pace for a week and discover it isn't laziness at all. It's the only strategy that actually works.

## The Verdict

South Korea has built one of the most impressive weather-avoidance infrastructures on the planet, and I respect the ambition even as I find something faintly absurd about a nation that would rather tunnel under an entire city than simply own a decent raincoat. Tanzania has no comparable infrastructure and doesn't pretend to need one — it has instead built a cultural rhythm that bends around the sky rather than against it, which is, frankly, the more sustainable long-term strategy, climate change being what it is.

Neither approach is wrong, but only one of them will still work in fifty years without an even larger tunnel network. I'll take the country that already knows how to wait.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Seoul forum — a Canadian teacher wrote that she went through eleven umbrellas in her first jangma season before accepting that buying a ₩2,000 one from the corner store every single time was simply the correct strategy.*

> *Internations Dar es Salaam — one member described trying to keep a British sense of scheduling urgency during the long rains as "the fastest way to have a stress-related breakdown in your first three months."*

> *Quora — a respondent comparing Dar es Salaam humidity to Southeast Asia argued the coastal Tanzanian heat is "stickier but shorter-lived" than Bangkok's, since the sea breeze that makes umbrellas useless also cuts the worst of the afternoon swelter.*

## Conclusion

Seoul will keep building tunnels under itself rather than admit the sky occasionally wins, and Tanzania will keep organizing entire seasons around rains that show up whether or not anyone's ready. Both are, in their own way, forms of respect for a climate that isn't going to negotiate. If you want to disappear underground the second a drop falls, Korea has the infrastructure for you. If you'd rather just stand under a mango tree for twenty minutes and let it pass, Tanzania already figured out that this works just as well, for a fraction of the concrete.
