# One Country Will Hand You Excellent Private Care The Moment You Can Pay Up Front. The Other Will Hand You A Waitlist And A Very Sincere Apology.

🇵🇪 Peru vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Healthcare is the one cultural comparison where "charming local quirk" stops being an acceptable frame, because eventually, in every country you move to, something will actually go wrong with your body, and you will need the system to work rather than merely to be interesting. I have now tested two wildly different approaches to this problem, and both left me with opinions I did not expect to have about national health policy at a dinner party.

Peru and Canada represent two almost perfectly opposite philosophies: pay directly and get seen quickly, or pay through taxes and wait, sometimes for a very long time, for a system that is, on paper, free. Let's get into what that actually feels like from inside a waiting room.

## Do's & Don'ts: Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comprehensive private health insurance before anything goes wrong, not after | Rely on the public system (SIS/EsSalud) as your primary plan; both are built around Peruvian nationals |
| Bring a payment method for upfront fees; many private clinics expect payment regardless of insurance | Assume insurance paperwork will be processed before you're expected to pay at the counter |
| Stick to Lima's major private clinics for anything serious; Arequipa and Cusco have fewer English-speaking options | Expect rural or provincial clinics to have the staffing or equipment of a capital-city facility |
| Save the SAMU (106) and EsSalud (117) emergency numbers, but know many expats go straight to a private clinic instead | Expect a public ambulance to arrive quickly; many residents bypass it entirely for exactly this reason |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for provincial health coverage immediately upon establishing residency; processing itself takes time | Expect same-week access to a family doctor; shortages are a well-documented, ongoing national issue |
| Use a walk-in clinic or telehealth line for non-emergencies while you wait for a family doctor | Go to an emergency room for a non-urgent issue expecting a short wait; ER triage prioritises severity, not time of arrival |
| Consider supplemental private insurance for dental, vision, and prescription costs; provincial coverage often excludes these | Assume "free healthcare" means zero out-of-pocket cost for everything; several real gaps exist |
| Be patient and persistent; the system eventually delivers very good outcomes for serious conditions | Expect speed to match quality; Canadians themselves are the first to admit the wait times are a real problem |

## Peru: Pay the Clinic, Skip the Queue, Hope the Insurance Catches Up Later

Peru's healthcare landscape splits cleanly along a line that every long-term expat learns to navigate quickly: the public system, built around SIS for lower-income Peruvians and EsSalud for the formally employed, exists primarily for nationals, and foreign residents are steered, consistently and sensibly, toward private care instead. The private system, concentrated heavily in Lima, is genuinely good — English-speaking doctors, modern facilities at clinics like Clínica Anglo Americana, Clínica San Borja, and Clínica Ricardo Palma, and appointment speed that would make a Canadian weep with envy.

The catch, and it's a real one, is payment structure: many private clinics expect payment up front, at the counter, regardless of what your insurance policy promises to reimburse later. This isn't a scam or an inefficiency so much as a structural reality of a system that doesn't route insurance billing the way North American or European providers do, and newcomers who assume otherwise tend to learn the hard way, mid-appointment, with a bill in hand and a confused expression. The sensible, universally repeated advice is to get genuinely comprehensive private health insurance before you need it, confirm the clinic's exact payment expectations before your appointment, and keep enough liquidity on hand to cover the gap between paying and getting reimbursed.

Geography matters enormously here too. Lima's hospitals are, by a wide margin, the best-equipped in the country; Arequipa and Cusco have meaningfully fewer private, English-speaking options; and genuinely remote or rural areas may require evacuation to a city for anything serious. Emergency response follows a similar urban skew — public ambulance response can be slow enough that many residents simply drive themselves, or a private clinic's own ambulance, straight to care rather than waiting for SAMU.

## Canada: Free at the Point of Use, Expensive in the Currency of Time

Canada's system runs on the opposite trade-off entirely: healthcare is funded through taxation and delivered, in principle, free at the point of use to anyone with provincial coverage, which is a genuinely remarkable social commitment that Canadians, to their credit, defend fiercely as a national value. The lived experience of that commitment, however, is a well-documented, widely acknowledged wait-time problem that Canadians themselves discuss with more weary honesty than almost any other national healthcare debate I've encountered.

Finding a family doctor is, in many regions, the first real obstacle — shortages are significant enough that "I don't have a family doctor" has become a genuinely common admission rather than an embarrassing outlier, and newcomers can expect to rely on walk-in clinics, telehealth lines, or emergency rooms for issues that would, in a better-staffed system, be handled by a regular GP relationship. Specialist referrals and non-urgent procedures can carry wait times measured in months, sometimes considerably longer, a trade-off Canadians widely accept as the cost of a genuinely universal system but rarely pretend is painless.

What's easy to miss from outside is how much of the system's actual quality shows up once you're inside it: serious conditions, once diagnosed, tend to receive excellent, well-resourced care, and the financial catastrophe risk that haunts healthcare conversations in some other wealthy countries is genuinely, structurally absent here. The gaps that do exist — dental, vision, prescription costs, often requiring supplemental private insurance — are well known and widely discussed rather than hidden, which at least gives newcomers a fair, honest picture to plan around.

Pharmacy culture quietly reinforces each system's broader logic. In Peru, pharmacies (farmacias) are plentiful, well-stocked, and genuinely useful as a first stop for minor ailments — pharmacists will often recommend and dispense medications that would require a doctor's prescription elsewhere, which meaningfully reduces the need for a full clinic visit for everyday issues, and fits neatly into a system that generally expects individuals to navigate care more directly. In Canada, pharmacies are regulated more tightly and prescription requirements are enforced more strictly, which is safer in the aggregate but slower in the moment, reinforcing the broader pattern of a system that prioritises structured, gatekept access over individual speed. Dental and vision care round out the comparison unflatteringly for Canada specifically: both are frequently cited, by Canadians themselves, as the most-criticised gaps in an otherwise beloved universal system, generally requiring separate private insurance that many assume, wrongly, is already covered by their provincial plan until the bill arrives.

## The Verdict

Peru offers fast, high-quality private care for anyone who can pay and insure properly, with a real structural gap for anyone who can't or who finds themselves outside Lima. Canada offers genuinely universal coverage with real financial security, purchased at the cost of wait times that Canadians themselves describe, without much national defensiveness, as a serious ongoing problem.

If speed and immediate access matter most to you, and your budget supports comprehensive private insurance, Peru's system will treat you well. If financial catastrophe protection and long-term, no-bill security matter most, and you can tolerate real delays for non-urgent care, Canada's system, for all its well-aired flaws, will eventually come through.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat Exchange — a Lima-based expat's recurring advice in a healthcare thread: confirm a private clinic's exact payment policy before your appointment, not after, because "my insurance covers this" and "the clinic will accept that at the counter today" are frequently two very different statements.*

> *Reddit r/PersonalFinanceCanada — a commonly repeated frustration across many threads: moving to a new province and discovering you're on a multi-month waitlist just to be assigned a family doctor, with a walk-in clinic as the only practical option in the meantime.*

> *Global Health Insurance — standard guidance repeated across expat planning resources: factor private health insurance into your relocation budget for Peru from day one, and confirm medical evacuation coverage specifically if you'll spend meaningful time outside Lima.*

## Conclusion

Healthcare systems are a society's clearest statement about who it believes deserves fast care, guaranteed care, or both, and how it's willing to pay for the answer. Peru has built genuine speed and quality for those who can pay, alongside a real gap for those who can't, concentrated hardest outside Lima. Canada has built genuine universal security, alongside real, nationally acknowledged delays that strain patience without threatening solvency. Neither system is a villain. Both are honest trade-offs, made in public, that every resident eventually has to live inside rather than just read about. Insure properly, plan for the specific flavour of friction you're buying into, and don't get sick anywhere without first understanding which one you've chosen.
