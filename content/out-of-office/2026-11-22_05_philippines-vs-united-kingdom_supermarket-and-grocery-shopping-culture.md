# The Philippines Shops at the Wet Market and the Mall; Britain Shops at Tesco and Feels Nothing

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In the Philippines, grocery shopping is a social event. You may begin in a wet market, where a fishmonger knows your name and your budget, move on to a supermarket inside a mall that has air conditioning and a food court, and finish at a sari-sari store on your corner where you buy one egg, a sachet of shampoo and a piece of local gossip. It is loud, colourful, negotiable and layered.

In Britain, grocery shopping is a form of administration. You enter a large shed, take a trolley with one bad wheel, and execute a sequence of small tasks in total silence, then pay a machine that scolds you about an unexpected item in the bagging area. Nobody speaks. Nobody haggles. The only drama is whether the meal deal contains the right crisps. I have lived with both, and I can tell you that one is a performance and the other is a procedure.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇭 Philippines ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Haggle politely at the wet market | Pay the first price without a smile |
| Bring cash and small change for local stores | Assume every shop takes cards |
| Use the sari-sari store for small, urgent buys | Expect a neat aisle or a till queue |
| Go early for the freshest fish and produce | Show up at noon and complain about the selection |

| 🇬🇧 United Kingdom ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your own bags | Expect a free carrier bag at the till |
| Check Sunday opening hours before you go | Plan a big shop at 6pm on a Sunday |
| Compare prices at an Asian or ethnic grocer | Assume the big chains are cheapest |
| Use the self-checkout with patience | Argue with the machine |

## 🇵🇭 Philippines

Philippine grocery culture is built on proximity and variety. There is the wet market, where seafood, meat and vegetables are sold fresh from stalls and prices are open to negotiation, and there is the mall supermarket, which is more orderly, air-conditioned and surprisingly social. Families go together. Shopping is an outing, and often combined with lunch. The local convenience store, the sari-sari, is the connective tissue of the neighbourhood, selling small quantities of nearly everything, often on credit to people the owner knows by name.

What defines it is flexibility. You buy exactly what you need, when you need it, from someone who knows you. A packet of coffee, a small bottle of cooking oil, a single cigarette, a pinch of salt. It is a retail system designed around daily cash flow rather than weekly planning, and it works because the shop is a three-minute walk away. The big chains exist too, and they are growing, but the underlying habit is frequent, personal and small.

For a Filipino expat in Britain, the grocery shop is the first place homesickness bites. Where is the calamansi? Why is there no bagoong? Why does the fish sit in a plastic tray under a light as if it were embarrassed? The British supermarket is efficient and clean, but it has no mood. It does not know you, it does not speak to you, and it does not care that you came from a place where the vegetables arrived that morning on the back of a truck.

What you will find, once you search, is that the answer lies in the smaller shops. Britain has a thriving network of Asian, Turkish and other ethnic grocers, and a Filipino shopper can usually find rice, fish sauce, dried fish and frozen ingredients if they know where to look. That is, in the end, the great lesson of the British grocery trade. The chains are for convenience, and the shops with the handwritten signs are for home.

## 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

Expatica describes Tesco as the UK's largest supermarket chain, with around 4,000 stores and a range that favours price over quality, and puts Sainsbury's at over 2,000. In big cities such as London, Manchester and Edinburgh, supermarkets are generally more centrally located and smaller, and often slightly more expensive, while out-of-town stores are much bigger in size and range. Self-checkouts are increasingly popular, particularly in smaller stores, and shopping bags are not provided as standard, so locals bring their own and buy a bag at the till for under twenty pence if they forget.

Then there is the Sunday rule. Expatica explains that supermarkets over a certain size may legally open for only six hours on a Sunday, and most choose 10am to 4pm or 11am to 5pm. They must also close on major public holidays, including Christmas Day, Boxing Day and Easter Sunday. This catches every newcomer at least once, usually at 5.15pm on a Sunday with an empty fridge and a hungry child.

The forums are surprisingly eloquent on the subject of price. In a 2012 MoneySavingExpert thread, a user called concrete_kid reported an online price comparison in which an Asian grocer's basket cost £13 against about £32 at Tesco, and was shocked that a small family-run shop was £19 cheaper. Another user, dasophster, said Asian and Turkish grocers tend to offer better prices and better produce on ethnic foods than the big chains, and noted that Tesco had begun selling own-brand rice and oil with Asian-language labelling that were often cheaper in the independent shops. A third, chesky369, argued that the idea supermarkets pass on their bulk-buying savings is a myth, and that butchers, bakers, markets and ethnic shops often charge less. Those are anecdotes from 2012, so the numbers are old, but the instinct is sound.

Expatica agrees, with a caveat: products are generally cheaper in ethnic stores, but everyday items can be more cost-effective in the chains. That is exactly right. The British grocery landscape rewards a split strategy, with the big shop at the big supermarket and the specialist shop for rice, spices and fish. You will need two trips, three bags and a sense of humour.

## The Verdict

The Philippines wins on joy, and Britain wins on reliability. If you want groceries to be an experience, a conversation and a small daily adventure, the Philippine system is unbeatable. Fresh food, flexible quantities and friendly faces make it the better place to be hungry, and the better place to be a person. You will know your fishmonger.

Britain wins for the days when you are tired and just need it done. The supermarkets are clean, predictable and open, apart from Sundays and Christmas, and the choice is enormous. The secret, for any expat, is to ignore the idea that you must pick a single shop. Do your basics at the chain, find a good Asian grocer for everything that tastes like home, and accept that the price comparison will occasionally make you furious at the national supermarket. If you can do that, Britain is a perfectly decent place to eat.

## Conclusion

The Philippines will make you feel known. Britain will make you feel efficient. Neither is wrong, and both are better with a good bag and a long list. Bring your own bags, check the Sunday hours, find the nearest Asian grocer and stop pretending the supermarket is your friend. It is a shed with a loyalty scheme. It does not care that you miss the calamansi, but the man at the little shop on the corner probably does.
