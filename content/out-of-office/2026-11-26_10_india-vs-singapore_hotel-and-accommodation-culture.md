# India Sells You an Apartment and Singapore Sells You a View

🇮🇳 India vs 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nobody travels for business because they want to. Every executive who ever checked into a hotel at 11pm with a laptop bag and a headache will tell you that accommodation is a transaction with a pillow. India and Singapore have both turned that transaction into an industry, but they have done it in different directions. India has pushed the long-stay model, the serviced apartment with a kitchen, a sofa and a vague sense of home. Singapore has pushed the tower, the polished lobby, the infinity pool and the brisk, immaculate efficiency that tells you the cleaner has already been and gone.

I have slept in both, and I have learned that you can judge a country by what it puts on the bedside table. India gives you a thermos and a local phone directory. Singapore gives you a tablet that controls the lights and a note explaining how to use it.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇮🇳 India ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Consider a serviced apartment for any stay longer than a week | Pay nightly hotel rates for a month |
| Ask what the kitchenette and living room include | Assume every apartment comes with kitchenware |
| Check the location against your commute | Choose by brochure photos alone |
| Tip the housekeeping staff when stays run long | Treat staff as invisible |

| 🇸🇬 Singapore ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book in the downtown core, Marina Bay or Orchard Road for client meetings | Pick a hotel on price alone and then commute |
| Consider the shoulder seasons, March to May and September to November | Assume peak season prices are fixed |
| Look at serviced apartments for stays of a week or more | Pay for a pool you never visit |
| Plan for a hot, humid walk between buildings | Underestimate the air-conditioned malls |

## 🇮🇳 India

India's accommodation culture is built on the long stay. Business Traveller India, in a June 2015 article titled Home Away From Home, quotes Anil Chadha of ITC Hotels calling serviced apartments the more popular and economic choice for long-stay business travellers. The article says guests save 15 to 20 per cent compared with a hotel room and mentions that Mumbai had over 400 five-star serviced units at the time. The date is important, because this is a decade-old snapshot, but the model it describes has only grown.

The logic is straightforward. A traveller who is in a city for a month does not want to eat every dinner in a hotel restaurant. They want a kitchenette, a living room and a washing machine, and the serviced apartment gives them all three at a lower nightly rate. The industry has built its sales pitch around the idea that a long stay should feel like living somewhere, not like being parked.

There is also the Indian hospitality habit, which rewards a certain kind of attention. Staff remember your name, your tea order and which newspaper you prefer. A good hotel in India can make a guest feel like a minor dignitary and the best serviced apartments try to extend that to a lease. It is service as a relationship, not a script.

The weaker side is consistency. Standards vary hugely between cities, between brands and between one property and the next. A five-star in one town can feel like a four in another. The sensible traveller reads reviews, asks questions and does not take a brochure on faith.

The decision tree for an Indian business traveller is usually money, location and food. A serviced apartment near the office saves a daily commute and a daily restaurant bill, and many guests prefer to cook familiar meals on a long assignment, which is hard to do in a conventional hotel room. A kitchenette and a living room are also where small acts of ordinary life return: calls home, a quiet evening, a sofa that is not a bed. The industry sells comfort, but what it really sells is a sense of normality in a stretch of working life that does not have one.

## 🇸🇬 Singapore

Singapore's accommodation market is slick, dense and unashamedly commercial. Moveandstay says business travellers mostly stay in the downtown core, Marina Bay or Orchard Road, and names the Conrad Centennial Singapore and the Grand Mercure Singapore Roxy as examples. It recommends the shoulder seasons, March to May and September to November, for better value. It also says serviced apartments suit longer stays because they offer a kitchen, gym and laundry, which is the same argument India makes.

SilverDoor, a serviced-apartment operator whose guide is promotional by nature, says Singapore averages over 27 degrees every month, falling by about one degree between November and January, and that the city has stayed among its top five booked locations globally since its Asia-Pacific office opened in 2016. That is a sales line, so treat it as one, but it does say something about demand. Singapore is a business city, and its accommodation is built for people who arrive with a calendar.

The experience is polished. Hotels are clean, quiet and unnervingly efficient. Check-in is quick, the room is small but intelligently planned, and the air conditioning is set to a temperature that suggests someone has studied you. Everything is a few minutes from everything else, and most of it is connected by cool, covered walkways.

What Singapore lacks is a sense of surprise. It does not do eccentric, rumpled or idiosyncratic. It does very good, very expensive and very consistent, which is exactly what a corporate traveller wants and exactly what a person with a soul might find slightly sad. You will never be unhappy in a Singapore hotel. You may also never be moved.

One caution applies to both. The sources for this comparison are commercial: a trade article quoting a hotel executive, an accommodation booking site and a serviced-apartment operator whose guide is openly promotional. None of them is neutral, and all of them have something to sell. Treat the saving figures as a rough guide, not a guarantee, ask for the actual all-in price before you commit, and do not pay for amenities you will not use, however handsome the infinity pool looks in the photographs.

## The Verdict

India wins for the long stay and Singapore for the short one. If I am going to be somewhere for a month, I want a kitchen, a sofa and a person who knows my name. India's serviced apartments, whatever their variability, treat the traveller as a temporary resident. Singapore treats the traveller as a valued but fleeting customer.

If I am there for two nights, I want the downtown tower, the quick check-in and the room that works. Singapore delivers that without a flaw. It is a city that understands a business trip as a project with a deadline and builds accommodation to match.

My overall pick is India, and I admit it is partly because I am sentimental about tea. A hotel that remembers how you take it is a hotel I will forgive for a leaking tap. Singapore will never leak a tap, and will never remember your tea. That is the difference between a service and a welcome.

## Conclusion

A room is a room until you have to live in it. In India, the long-stay culture means you can unpack, cook something and feel less like a visitor. In Singapore, the efficient tower means you can sleep, work and leave without anyone noticing you were there.

So choose by the length of your stay. For a week or more, find a place with a kitchen and a washing machine. For a night or two, find the one with the best location and the quickest lift. And whichever you pick, read the reviews with a sceptical eye, because every brochure is written by a person who has never had to sleep in the room.
