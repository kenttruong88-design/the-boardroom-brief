# India Commutes Like It Is a Festival and Hong Kong Commutes Like It Is a Deadline

🇮🇳 India vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every city has a commute, and every commute is a confession. It tells you what people trust, what they fear and how much of their lives they are prepared to spend standing up. India's confession is improvisation. Hong Kong's is optimisation. One of them will get you to the meeting early and the other will get you there with a story.

I have commuted on both, and I can report that the Indian version is more fun and the Hong Kong version is more punctual. This is the central fact of the global commuting experience: you can have drama or you can have timetables, but the two have never been seen in the same carriage.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇮🇳 India ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the metro in the big cities and avoid peak road traffic | Assume your cab will arrive in the time the app promised |
| Keep small change and a charged phone for auto-rickshaw bargaining | Accept the first quote from every driver |
| Let passengers get off before you push on | Hesitate at the door when the crowd moves |
| Allow double the journey time for road trips in monsoon season | Schedule a client meeting an hour after landing |

| 🇭🇰 Hong Kong ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get an Octopus card on day one | Queue at a ticket machine with a pile of coins |
| Stand on the right of the escalator | Block anyone who is in a hurry, which is everyone |
| Learn the exit letters for your station | Wander through the underground passages guessing |
| Try the minibus and tram for short trips | Take a taxi across the harbour at rush hour |

## 🇮🇳 India

Indian city transport is not a system, it is an ecosystem. The metros in Delhi, Mumbai, Bengaluru and Hyderabad are modern, air-conditioned and excellent, and they carry millions of people with an efficiency that would embarrass several European capitals. Above ground, the picture changes. Buses, auto-rickshaws, motorbikes, cycle-rickshaws, cars and the occasional cow share the road according to a code that nobody wrote down and everybody understands.

Horns are conversation. A short toot means "I am overtaking you", a long one means "I am concerned", and two quick ones mean "please do not do that". Lane markings are decorative. Traffic flows like water, finding gaps, filling them, leaving no space that could be used by anything with wheels. For a visitor it looks like chaos. For a local it is a negotiation that happens in real time at thirty kilometres an hour.

Commuting hours are long. A cross-city trip in Bengaluru or Mumbai can eat two hours in each direction, and the working day is organised around that. People leave early, eat on the move, and treat the journey as part of the shift. Ride-hailing apps have made life easier, though surge pricing in the rain is a ritual of its own.

The metro is the great equaliser, and the women's coach is a sensible solution to a real problem. Anyone moving to India should take the metro wherever it goes and save the roads for when it does not.

A typical Indian commute is also a lesson in resourcefulness. People plan around the monsoon, the festival procession, the political rally and the sudden road closure. Most commuters carry a second option in their heads, whether it is a shared auto, a suburban train, or a friend with a motorbike. Flexibility is not a virtue here, it is a necessity, and the people who master it become strangely calm. If you have ever wondered how someone can remain relaxed in a traffic jam, the answer is that they have already accepted it.

## 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

Hong Kong's public transport is the thing the rest of the world quotes when it wants to make its own look bad. The MTR is fast, clean and frequent. Trains run every few minutes, stations are connected by walkways and malls, and the Octopus card means you can pay for the train, the tram, the ferry, a sandwich and a parking meter with one tap.

Then there are the extras. The Star Ferry crosses the harbour for little more than a coin. Double-decker trams rattle along the north shore. Green minibuses stop wherever you ask them to, as long as you shout the right word in Cantonese at the right time. Escalators run in long outdoor chains up the hillside, a piece of urban engineering that feels like a pleasant joke.

The price of this efficiency is pace. Commuters walk quickly, queue neatly, board decisively and expect you to do the same. The carriages are packed, but the packing is orderly. People hold the pole, mind their bag, keep their eyes on their phone and say nothing for forty minutes. If you stop at the top of the stairs to check your map, someone will glide around you without a word.

Most expats in Hong Kong do not own a car, and most do not miss it. The city is built so that the car is the slow option. That is the real flex.

The tram deserves special mention, because it embodies the Hong Kong ideal that transport can be both useful and delightful. The ride is slow, the fare is tiny, and the upper deck offers a front-row view of a city that mostly passes you at speed. Add the cross-harbour ferry and you have two of the best-value journeys in the world, and neither requires you to be anywhere in a hurry. The thing to remember is that all of this is organised by a precise public timetable, and the city expects you to respect it.

## The Verdict

Hong Kong wins the commute, and it is not close. If your priority is arriving on time with your shirt intact, you cannot beat the MTR. A transport system should be judged by how little you think about it, and in Hong Kong I forgot I was travelling.

But India wins the story. A commute in Delhi or Mumbai gives you something a clean train never will: a fresh anecdote every morning. The metro is excellent, the roads are theatre, and the auto-rickshaw driver will tell you his opinion of the government before you reach the next junction. If I had to pick for productivity I would pick Hong Kong. If I had to pick for character I would pick India. Nobody has ever written a memoir about a flawless transfer at Admiralty, which tells you everything.

There is also the question of what each system does to your temperament. After a year in Hong Kong, you become impatient with delays that would not have registered at home. After a year in an Indian city, you become relaxed about delays that would have ruined a Swiss morning. Neither is wrong, but one of them is better for your blood pressure, and it is not the one with the timetable.

I would add one more observation for anyone choosing a base. Housing near a station in Hong Kong commands a premium for good reason, because time is the scarcest resource in the city. In India, the premium is on a short drive or a walkable lane, and the sensible tenant weighs the daily journey before the rent. The cheaper flat that costs you ninety minutes a day is not cheaper.

## Conclusion

The commute shapes your whole day, so choose your city accordingly. India gives you chaos with a beating heart and a driver who will not let you pay for the chai. Hong Kong gives you a machine so well tuned that you will start resenting any other country's trains.

My advice is to take both seriously. In India, build in time, patience and a spare phone battery. In Hong Kong, tap in, keep right and keep moving. Do that and you will arrive at work on time in one city and with a story in the other, which is as good a definition of balance as I have found.

So when you plan your move, plan your commute first. Choose a home near a station in Hong Kong, and choose a home near a metro line in India, and your quality of life will improve more than any upgrade to the kitchen.
