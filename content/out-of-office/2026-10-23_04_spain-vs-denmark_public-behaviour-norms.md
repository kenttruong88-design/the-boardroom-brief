# A Nation That Shouts Out of Love and a Nation That Queues Out of Principle

🇪🇸 Spain 🇩🇰 Denmark
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Spain and Denmark have arrived, via entirely different routes, at two of the most confident public behaviour codes I've encountered in fourteen relocations, and both are completely unbothered by what outsiders think of them. Spain has decided that volume is a love language and silence is slightly suspicious. Denmark has decided that silence is a love language and volume is slightly suspicious. Put a Spaniard and a Dane in the same lift and you will witness, in real time, two entirely different theories of what it means to be a considerate human being.

Neither is wrong. Both are, in their own way, extremely polite — Spain's politeness just sounds like a heated argument about paella, and Denmark's politeness sounds like nothing at all, ever, which took me months to stop interpreting as quiet hostility. It is not hostility. It is Tuesday.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇪🇸 Spain**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect dinner conversation volume to rival a small political rally | Mistake raised voices for an actual argument, it rarely is |
| Embrace the terrace culture, loud and late is simply correct | Expect anyone to apologise for overlapping conversations at full volume |
| Greet shopkeepers and neighbours properly, it matters more than you think | Rush a greeting, a clipped "hola" without eye contact reads as rude |

**🇩🇰 Denmark**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Queue in total silence and trust the system, nobody will cut you off | Make small talk with strangers on public transport, it will unsettle them |
| Keep your voice down on trains and in cafes, it's the unspoken baseline | Stand too close in a queue, personal space is practically legislated |
| Respect quiet hours in residential buildings, they are taken seriously | Assume reserved behaviour means unfriendliness, it almost never does |

## Spain

Spanish public life runs at a volume that visitors initially mistake for conflict and locals experience as simply being alive. A family lunch at full tilt, three conversations happening simultaneously, someone laughing so hard they're slapping the table — this is not an argument escalating, this is Tuesday lunch, and it will happen again tomorrow with the same intensity regardless of what's actually being discussed. I spent my first few weeks in Madrid tensing every time a conversation got loud, certain someone was about to storm off, only to watch the same two people walk out arm in arm, still mid-sentence, still at full volume, heading to get a coffee.

The terrace culture deserves its own chapter in any serious study of human happiness. Spain has figured out, with total conviction, that eating and talking outside, late, loudly, with no particular end time in mind, is simply the correct way to spend an evening, and the entire infrastructure of the country bends around this belief — restaurants that don't start filling up until 9pm, bars that consider midnight to be mid-evening, neighbours who have long since made peace with the acoustic reality of living above a tapas bar. There is no apology economy here the way there is in quieter cultures; nobody lowers their voice because someone might be annoyed, because the underlying assumption is that everyone present is also enjoying themselves loudly, and if you're not, that's a you problem.

What's easy to miss, under all the noise, is how much ceremony Spain attaches to small interactions. A proper greeting — eye contact, a real "buenos días," maybe a cheek kiss depending on context — is not optional small talk, it's basic social maintenance, and skipping it reads as colder than any amount of volume ever could.

The siesta, much mythologised and much misunderstood abroad, is really just another expression of the same underlying value — life organised around when people actually want to be awake and sociable, rather than a rigid nine-to-five that ignores the reality of a 40-degree August afternoon. Shops shutting for several hours in the middle of the day baffles newcomers used to relentless opening hours, right up until they experience their first proper Spanish summer and understand exactly why nobody wants to be standing behind a till at 3pm. The same logic extends to dinner reservations rarely taking before 9pm and nightlife that barely stirs before midnight — Spain hasn't delayed its day, it's simply built an entire schedule around comfort and company over rigid productivity, and defends that choice without a shred of self-consciousness.

## Denmark

Denmark's public behaviour code operates on almost the exact inverse principle: the less noise and friction you generate, the more considerate you're being. Queues form silently and are observed with an almost spiritual discipline — nobody checks their phone anxiously, nobody hovers too close, nobody speaks unless spoken to, and the whole system runs with a quiet efficiency that initially feels cold and, over time, reveals itself as a very specific, very sincere form of respect: I will not impose my noise, my opinions, or my proximity on you, because your peace matters as much as mine.

Public transport in Copenhagen is close to silent most of the day, a shared agreement that this time belongs to everyone's own thoughts, not to anyone's conversation. Strangers do not make small talk in lifts. This is not rudeness, it's a boundary, and once you understand that Danish reserve is a form of courtesy rather than an absence of warmth, the whole culture clicks into place. Hygge, the much-exported concept, is really just this same principle turned inward — comfort and warmth cultivated quietly, deliberately, usually with candles and a blanket, rarely with noise.

Where Denmark does get loud, interestingly, is among close friends in private settings, where the same reserved public demeanour gives way to genuine, relaxed warmth. The volume dial exists, it's simply calibrated to context in a way Spain's is not — public space stays hushed, private space can get as warm and unguarded as anywhere else in Europe.

Danish directness, when it does appear, tends to show up in the one place you'd least expect it from an otherwise reserved culture: blunt, unvarnished feedback, delivered without the cushioning most cultures consider basic politeness. A Dane will tell you plainly that your idea has a flaw, or that the meeting ran too long, with no softening preamble, because in this context honesty is itself considered the respectful move — the same underlying logic, oddly, that drives Israeli directness in a completely different register. It took me longer to adjust to blunt feedback delivered in a near-whisper than to any amount of Spanish volume, purely because the mismatch between tone and content kept catching me off guard.

## The Verdict

Spain wins on sheer life force — nowhere else have I felt a city so collectively unafraid to be loud, present, and unapologetically delighted with its own dinner. Denmark wins on consideration as infrastructure — a whole society has agreed, without ever discussing it, to protect each other's quiet, and the result is one of the calmest public environments I've experienced anywhere. If you need noise to feel alive, Spain will give you that in excess. If you need silence to feel sane, Denmark will hand it to you, unbothered, every single day, no questions asked.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/spain — paraphrased: my Danish colleague thought my family was having a screaming match over lunch, it was a disagreement about which olive oil to buy, completely normal volume for us.*

> *r/Denmark — paraphrased: I said "good morning" to a stranger on the metro once out of habit and the whole carriage looked at me like I'd announced a fire, lesson learned.*

> *Internations Madrid — paraphrased: three years in and I still can't tell the difference between a Spanish argument and a Spanish celebration purely by volume, you just have to watch the hands.*

## Conclusion

Spain and Denmark have each built an entire public culture around a value they clearly consider non-negotiable — expression for one, consideration for the other — and both execute it with total conviction. You will, inevitably, misread both at first: too loud, too quiet, too much, too little. Give it a year and you'll find yourself craving a loud Spanish terrace one month and a silent Danish train carriage the next, having quietly accepted that both are correct, and that the real skill is knowing which volume the room is asking for before you open your mouth.
