# One Country Put Free Gyms in Every Park. The Other Is Still Negotiating Who's Allowed Inside the Gym at All.

🇲🇽 Mexico 🇪🇬 Egypt
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Mexico has quietly built one of the more admirable public fitness infrastructures in the Americas — free outdoor gym equipment installed in public parks across Mexico City and beyond, used constantly, by every demographic, at every hour, no membership required. Egypt's gym culture is booming in its own right, especially among Cairo's young, Instagram-literate middle class, but it's booming inside a much more complicated social negotiation over gender, space, and who gets to work out where, and with whom.

I have done a full outdoor circuit on free public equipment in a Mexico City park alongside a grandmother doing the exact same leg press I was struggling with, no judgment exchanged in either direction. I have also sat in a Cairo gym lobby watching a genuinely tense, polite negotiation over which hours were "ladies only" that day. Both countries care enormously about fitness. Only one has made it frictionless.

## Do's & Don'ts: Mexico 🇲🇽

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the free public outdoor gyms (gimnasios al aire libre) — they're genuinely well-maintained, heavily used, and open to anyone | Assume boutique fitness studios are priced like public options — CDMX's CrossFit, cycling and functional-training scene runs premium | 
| Show up early morning or early evening if you want the public equipment without a wait — peak hours get genuinely busy | Monopolise shared public equipment for a long solo session during busy hours — informal rotation etiquette is expected and enforced socially |
| Explore the growing "ejercicio al aire libre" group classes many parks now host for free — yoga, aerobics, Zumba, often donation-based | Expect the same equipment quality everywhere — maintenance and equipment age vary noticeably between affluent and lower-income neighbourhoods |

## Do's & Don'ts: Egypt 🇪🇬

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research a gym's specific policy on mixed-gender hours or ladies-only sessions before joining — it varies significantly by venue | Assume all gyms operate identically — Cairo's scene ranges from strictly segregated to fully mixed, often within the same neighbourhood |
| Join the genuinely vibrant CrossFit and boutique fitness scene among younger, urban Egyptians — it's growing fast and often mixed-gender | Wear what you'd wear to a Western gym without first observing local norms — modesty expectations vary by venue and clientele |
| Budget for a real membership — quality gyms in Cairo's upscale neighbourhoods (Zamalek, New Cairo) are priced closer to European rates than regional averages | Assume public outdoor fitness infrastructure exists at Mexico's scale — Egypt's public park fitness equipment is far less widespread |

## Mexico: Democratising the Gym by Putting It Outside

Mexico City's public outdoor gym programme is one of those quietly brilliant pieces of urban policy that doesn't get nearly enough international attention. Scattered through parks across the capital and replicated in cities nationwide, these installations — pull-up bars, leg presses, elliptical-style machines, all weatherproofed steel — require no membership, no fee, and no appointment. The result is a genuinely cross-generational, cross-class fitness culture: teenagers doing pull-ups next to retirees doing stretching routines next to someone's uncle attempting, heroically, a dip exercise clearly beyond his current capacity, all sharing the same equipment with an informal rotation etiquette nobody had to write down because everyone simply absorbed it.

Beyond the public infrastructure, Mexico's private fitness scene, particularly in Mexico City, Guadalajara and Monterrey, has exploded into genuinely sophisticated boutique territory — CrossFit boxes, cycling studios, functional training gyms charging prices that would be unremarkable in Los Angeles or London, catering to an affluent, increasingly fitness-obsessed urban middle and upper class. The contrast between the free park equipment and the premium boutique studio isn't treated as contradictory; Mexicans across the economic spectrum simply use whichever tier fits their budget and goals, and nobody considers the free option a lesser version of "real" fitness culture.

What's genuinely striking to an outsider is the lack of gatekeeping around the public equipment specifically. There's no gendered hour restriction, no membership-tier hierarchy, no sense that certain bodies or certain people belong at certain times. A woman working out alone at dusk in most well-trafficked park locations isn't treated as unusual or risky in the way it might be flagged elsewhere; the equipment's public, communal framing seems to have genuinely flattened a lot of the social friction that complicates fitness access in plenty of other countries.

## Egypt: A Booming Scene Still Negotiating Its Own Rules

Egyptian gym culture, especially in Cairo and increasingly in Alexandria and the newer satellite cities like New Cairo and 6th of October, has genuinely exploded over the past decade, driven heavily by a young, urban, social-media-literate demographic for whom fitness has become both health practice and identity signal, CrossFit boxes and boutique studios included. This is not a culture lacking enthusiasm or investment; gym membership among young professional Cairenes has become close to a default lifestyle marker in certain social circles, same as it has in plenty of Western capitals.

What makes the landscape more complex than Mexico's is the genuine, ongoing social negotiation over gender and space that shapes how that enthusiasm gets organised. Many gyms operate explicit mixed-gender hour restrictions, dedicated ladies-only sessions, or in some cases fully segregated facilities entirely, reflecting a spectrum of religious and cultural conservatism that varies significantly by neighbourhood, gym ownership, and clientele. A gym in Zamalek or New Cairo catering to a wealthier, more cosmopolitan clientele might run fully mixed with minimal restriction; a more traditionally-oriented facility a few kilometres away might maintain a far stricter separation, and newcomers genuinely need to ask rather than assume either way.

Public outdoor fitness infrastructure, Mexico's great democratising feature, exists in Egypt only in a much more limited form — some municipal parks in Cairo and Alexandria have installed basic equipment in recent years, part of a broader public health push, but nowhere near the density, consistency or cultural ubiquity of Mexico's park gym network. The upshot is that Egyptian fitness culture, for all its genuine energy and growth, remains more stratified by both class (can you afford a quality private gym) and gender negotiation (which hours, which venue, which dress expectation) than Mexico's considerably more frictionless, flattened public model.

## The Verdict

Mexico wins decisively on access and social flattening — a free, well-used, genuinely cross-demographic public gym network is a policy win most countries haven't managed, and it shows in how unremarkable working out in public has become there. Egypt's fitness scene has real energy and genuine growth, particularly among its younger urban population, but it's still working through social and infrastructural questions Mexico settled, almost accidentally, through a smart piece of urban planning. If convenience and low-friction access are the metric, Mexico wins outright; if you're curious about a fitness culture still actively negotiating its own identity in real time, Egypt is the far more interesting watch.

## Conclusion

Mexico didn't solve fitness access with a philosophy, it solved it with free steel equipment bolted into public parks, and the social ease that followed seems almost incidental to the policy rather than its stated goal. Egypt is building something with real energy and momentum, but it's doing so while still working out, gym by gym, neighbourhood by neighbourhood, who exactly the space is for and when. One country made the gym boring and universal. The other is still making it interesting, for better and occasionally for worse.
