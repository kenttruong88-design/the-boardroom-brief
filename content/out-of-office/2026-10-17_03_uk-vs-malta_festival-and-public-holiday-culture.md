# Eight Grey Days a Year vs. Fifteen Excuses to Set Off Explosives

🇬🇧 United Kingdom 🇲🇹 Malta

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every expatriate eventually has the conversation where a Brit explains, with the wounded pride of a man defending a war record, that the bank holiday is a sacred and ancient institution. It is neither. It is a Tuesday with slightly worse trains and a pub garden. Fly two and a half hours south, meanwhile, and you'll find an island nation that treats the calendar less like a bureaucratic inconvenience and more like a personal challenge: how many saints can we weaponise into a national day off, and how much gunpowder can a village of four thousand people legally detonate over one long weekend.

This is not a culture clash. This is a culture canyon, and I have lived in the ditch at the bottom of it. Britain gives you eight statutory holidays a year and calls it generous. Malta gives you fifteen, half of them accompanied by a marching band and a controlled explosion, and genuinely cannot understand why anyone would want fewer. Both countries think the other is doing it wrong. Both countries are partly right.

What nobody tells you before you relocate is that a country's relationship to its own calendar reveals almost everything else about it — how it feels about religion, community, noise, risk, and the idea that a Tuesday might be allowed to mean something. Judge a nation by its public holidays and you will never be surprised by anything else it does.

## Do's & Don'ts: United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept that "bank holiday weekend" is code for rain, regardless of forecast | Expect shops, trains, or anyone's goodwill to run normally |
| Learn the regional variations — Scotland and Northern Ireland get different days off | Assume St Andrew's Day or the Twelfth of July apply everywhere |
| Treat the August bank holiday as the unofficial end of summer and grieve accordingly | Book anything that requires sunshine with total confidence |
| Appreciate that an extra bank holiday (coronations, jubilees) is genuine national event | Expect it to happen more than once a decade |

## Do's & Don'ts: Malta

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check which village is having its festa before booking a quiet weekend anywhere nearby | Assume "nearby" means out of fireworks range — it doesn't |
| Join a procession at least once; the brass bands are genuinely moving | Film it on your phone the entire time like a tourist with no soul |
| Plan errands around Sunday and feast-day closures | Expect a supermarket run to save you on Santa Marija |
| Respect that this is UNESCO-recognised heritage, not noise pollution with a costume | Complain about the 6am fireworks to the people who grew up setting them off |

## United Kingdom: The Art of the Disappointing Tuesday

The United Kingdom has eight bank holidays a year across England and Wales, a figure the Trades Union Congress has publicly and repeatedly called the lowest in the European Union — four fewer than Spain's twelve, three fewer than France, and nearly half of what Slovakia, Slovenia, and Finland hand their workers. The TUC wants that number raised to twelve. Britain's response, in the grand tradition of British responses, has been to acknowledge the statistic exists and then do absolutely nothing about it for several more years.

What's remarkable isn't the scarcity, though — it's the British talent for making even the holidays they do have feel like administrative events rather than celebrations. A bank holiday is named, tellingly, after a bank. Not a saint, not a liberation, not a harvest — a financial institution's opening hours. Good Friday and Christmas aside, most of the calendar's "high days" are generic markers: Early May, Spring, Summer, August. There is no patron deity of Early May. Nobody processes through the streets of Preston carrying a effigy of Spring Bank Holiday. The holiday simply arrives, is observed with a slightly longer queue at the garden centre, and departs, having asked nothing of anyone and given rather little back.

Scotland and Northern Ireland at least have the decency to attach meaning to theirs — St Andrew's Day, the Twelfth of July, the Battle of the Boyne commemorations — which only sharpens the contrast with England and Wales, where the calendar is agnostically, almost apologetically, blank. The one occasion the country does festival properly, genuinely, unselfconsciously — Christmas — it executes with ruthless competence: weeks of buildup, a cultural monoculture of mince pies and bad telly, actual emotional investment. It proves the British can throw themselves at an occasion when they choose to. They simply choose to roughly once a year, and spend the other 51 weeks acting as though enthusiasm is slightly embarrassing, possibly foreign, and best kept for the FA Cup.

## Malta: Fifteen Saints and a Standing Army of Pyrotechnicians

Malta, by contrast, does not have this problem, because Malta does not believe in restraint as a civic virtue. The government's official calendar lists fifteen public holidays for 2026 — nearly double the UK's tally — and that's before you factor in that almost every one of Malta's roughly sixty parishes also runs its own unofficial, unpaid, deeply enthusiastic village festa between May and September, honouring its patron saint with days of processions, brass bands, and fireworks that make the national ones look like a warm-up act.

In 2023, UNESCO formally recognised the Maltese festa as Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity — which is either the most delightful validation a small island nation has ever received, or the most generous possible framing for what expat forums describe, with weary affection, as several consecutive weeks of being woken at eight in the morning by explosions. Threads on the expat.com Malta forum spanning more than a decade return to the same beats: newcomers counting ten firework displays in their first month and wondering, not unreasonably, whether the money might go toward bin collection instead; longer-term residents explaining that the tradition predates the Knights of St John and is not, in fact, up for debate; and a general consensus that you either make peace with the noise or you move to a part of the island with fewer churches, which in Malta is not many parts at all.

The religious calendar still structures daily life here in a way England abandoned decades ago — shops shutter on Sundays and major feast days, roughly 85–90% of the population identifies as Catholic, and freemalta.com's own expat guide calls the festa "the social and spiritual anchor of village life," which is not hyperbole, it's a job description. "Maltese time" — the looser relationship with punctuality outside the finance and iGaming sectors — compounds the sense that the calendar here serves the community's rhythm rather than the other way round. It's chaotic, loud, occasionally dangerous — fireworks factory incidents are, grimly, an annual feature of local news — and unmistakably alive.

## The Verdict

If you're measuring generosity in raw days off, Malta wins by a landslide the UK isn't even contesting — fifteen statutory holidays to eight, with a village-level bonus round running all summer. If you're measuring whether the British understand what a holiday is *for*, the answer is no, and they've known this about themselves since at least the Industrial Revolution, which is precisely when they started calling days off after banking hours rather than saints.

But generosity isn't quite the same as joy. Malta's festas are loud, communal, occasionally unsafe, and genuinely meant — UNESCO didn't recognise Britain's August bank holiday for a reason, and it never will, because there is nothing there to recognise. The UK's holidays are fewer and flatter, but at least nobody's house has ever caught fire because of one, which is more than Malta's fireworks factories can claim most summers. Pick your trade-off: noise and meaning, or quiet and tedium, delivered on schedule by a bank. I know which one I'd rather be woken up by at 6am, and it isn't the one that leaves me with nothing to tell my grandchildren.

## Conclusion

Here is the honest ledger. Britain hoards its eight bank holidays like a man rationing biscuits, names them after financial institutions, and is statistically confirmed — by its own trade union movement — to be stingier than almost everywhere else in the EU. Malta hands out fifteen, then layers an entire unofficial second calendar of village festas on top, complete with brass bands, saints, and enough fireworks to concern a NATO observer. One nation under-celebrates out of habit. The other over-celebrates out of conviction, and occasionally out of insurance-defying recklessness. Neither is balanced. Both are, infuriatingly, correct in their own context — which is the most British sentence I'll write all year, and I resent it.
