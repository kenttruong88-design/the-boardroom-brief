# Accra Will Dance With You. Abidjan Will Feed You First. Choose Your Fighter.

🇬🇭 Ghana vs 🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two capitals, one coastline, and a shared colonial habit of pretending the weekend is a Western invention they're merely tolerating. It isn't. West Africa has been doing "weekend" longer and better than anywhere I've lived, and I've lived in fourteen places, which means I've had fourteen landlords lie to me about the water pressure.

Accra and Abidjan sit a few hundred kilometres apart and could not be less alike on a Saturday. One is an Anglophone fever dream of Afrobeats and funerals that double as the social event of the season. The other is a Francophone fantasy of grilled fish, communal football screens, and a street that used to be the most famous nightlife strip in West Africa before anyone in London had heard of Afrobeats at all. Neither city will apologise for existing on its own terms, and frankly, why should they.

## Do's and Don'ts: Ghana 🇬🇭

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear your sharpest outfit to a Saturday funeral — it is the social event, not a chore | Assume Sunday morning plans are negotiable; church is load-bearing infrastructure |
| Head to Osu or East Legon for the actual nightlife, not your hotel bar | Expect a quiet December; festival season and diaspora homecoming season overlap hard |
| Learn to haggle-dance at a beach bar in Labadi over a cold Club beer | Walk the "shortcut" after dark; there are no sidewalks and the gutters are not decorative |

## Do's and Don'ts: Côte d'Ivoire 🇨🇮

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Eat at a maquis on a Saturday night — grilled fish, alloco, everyone watching the match together | Expect a quiet Grand-Bassam weekend trip in high season; everyone else had the idea too |
| Learn two sentences of French before you need them; effort is noticed and rewarded | Drive yourself around Abidjan expecting European lane discipline — it does not exist |
| Say yes to the invitation that gets issued twice; the second ask is the real one | Mistake a maquis for a "local curiosity" to photograph rather than a place to actually sit down |

Ghana first, because Ghana will insist on it and I have learned not to argue with a country that invented a word — "Homowo" — that literally means "hooting at hunger." Accra's weekends run on two engines most visitors clock far too late: church and death. Sunday morning is not a suggestion; something like seven in ten Ghanaians identify as Christian, and the fashion show that results — tailored, pressed, occasionally genuinely couture — is one of the more underrated free spectacles on the continent. Saturday, meanwhile, belongs to the funeral, which in Ghana is not a hushed affair but a full-scale production: custom coffins shaped like the deceased's profession, hired brass bands, printed obituary posters the size of billboards, white outfits if the departed made it past eighty because that's a life worth celebrating rather than mourning. Families compete, openly, to throw the better send-off. I have been to three Ghanaian weddings and none were as well catered as the funeral I crashed by accident in Kumasi.

Once the formalities clear, Accra gets loud in the way I respect. Osu's Oxford Street strip remains the backbone of the city's nightlife, though East Legon is quietly staging a coup with its newer rooftop bars and a clientele that has clearly discovered LinkedIn. Time Out ranked Accra 18th globally for nightlife this year — the only sub-Saharan entry besides Johannesburg — on the strength of affordability and a startling 75% of locals surveyed calling the going-out scene "welcoming." Beach weekends at Labadi and Kokrobite round things out, and December turns the whole city into a diaspora homecoming festival that makes Soho on New Year's Eve look like a church bake sale, which, in Ghana, it also technically is.

Côte d'Ivoire does its weekends with a French accent and a straighter face, until you find a maquis, at which point the face relaxes entirely. The maquis — an open-air local eatery that started in Ghana in the 1950s and became an Ivorian national institution — is where Abidjan actually lives on a Saturday: grilled chicken and fish, alloco (fried plantain doused in onion and chili you will regret and crave simultaneously), and an entire neighbourhood watching the football together whether they intended to or not. Historically, Yopougon's Rue Princesse held the title of West Africa's most legendary nightlife street and gave the world coupé-décalé, a genre built specifically for people who have decided dancing is a competitive sport. For the quieter crowd, Grand-Bassam — a UNESCO site and former colonial capital — and the beaches at Assinie are the standard weekend exit from the city, assuming you can find a free square foot of sand in high season.

The Ga calendar doesn't stay in Accra, either. Diaspora Ga communities in Portland, Oregon and across the UK run their own scaled-down Homowo observances, which means the "hooting at hunger" ritual has quietly gone international while most of the people doing it in London have never set foot in Osu. Back in Accra proper, the festival also pulls in "Soobii" — Ga visitors from other towns who arrive specifically on the Thursday of the climax week — turning what sounds like a single city's event into a whole diaspora's homecoming rehearsal months before December does the same thing at a national scale.

Abidjan's own nightlife history deserves a line Suki refuses to cut: Yopougon's Rue Princesse wasn't just a street, it was the actual birthplace of coupé-décalé, a genre built for people who treat dancing as a competitive sport rather than a Saturday pastime, and for a stretch of years it was the single most legendary nightlife address in all of West Africa — a title Lagos and Accra have both since tried, unsuccessfully, to quietly take off its hands.

Here's the honest difference, and I say this as someone who has eaten in sixty countries and lost patience with the word "exotic" somewhere around country twelve: Ghana performs its weekend outward, in costume, in public, for an audience including the ancestors. Côte d'Ivoire's weekend is quieter until it isn't, built around a table and a shared plate rather than a street parade, with a French-colonial café fluency Ghana never bothered acquiring and never wanted.

## The Verdict

If you need your weekend to feel like an event with a dress code, Accra will not let you down — just budget emotionally for a funeral invitation within your first month, because you will get one, and you will not regret going. If you want your weekend to feel like being folded, gently and permanently, into someone else's extended family over grilled tilapia, Abidjan wins outright. I'm picking Abidjan this round, purely because a culture that built an entire social institution around a fish stand understands leisure better than one that still thinks a PowerPoint-worthy funeral counts as "going out." Ghana, don't write in. You know I'm right and you had a lovely time doing it anyway.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations/Expat.com, Ghana — Accra, paraphrased: salsa nights at the Mexican restaurants have quietly become the city's real dance scene, and the embassy crowd's early-morning running groups will absorb you whether you asked or not.*

> *Expat.com, Ivory Coast forum — "Rebbie": "If you just show them that you at least trying to be part of them, they will take you." Markets are not to be attempted solo in your first few months, however good the prices look.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Abidjan, paraphrased: a community pool has sat shut since the pandemic and formal expat social events are rare — one respondent's single example of "a big night out" was the Marine Corps Ball, which tells you everything about who was answering the survey.*

## Conclusion

Neither city is going to arrange itself around your idea of a restful Saturday, and that's the point. Accra will dress you up, feed you at a funeral, and send you home at 3am smelling of palm wine and regret. Abidjan will sit you down at a plastic table, put a fish in front of you, and not let you leave until you've picked a side in a football argument you didn't know you had opinions about. Both will outlast your jet lag, your expectations, and quite possibly your liver. Pack accordingly, and for heaven's sake learn to haggle before you learn to drive — in both cities, you'll need the first skill far more urgently than the second.
