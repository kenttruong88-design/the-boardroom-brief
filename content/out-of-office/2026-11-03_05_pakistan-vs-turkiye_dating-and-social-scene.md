# Karachi Will Vet Your Biodata. Istanbul Will Argue About Whether the App Even Counts.

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇹🇷 Türkiye

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nothing clarifies a country's relationship with romance faster than watching it try to ban an app. Pakistan tried this in 2021, taking down Tinder, Grindr, Tagged, Skout, and SayHi in one legislative swing for "immoral content," which worked about as well as every other attempt in human history to legislate desire out of existence. Türkiye didn't bother banning anything and instead produced a population that uses dating apps constantly while telling researchers, with a completely straight face, that it disapproves of the whole concept.

I've dated across more borders than is strictly advisable for someone who still can't commit to a permanent mailing address, and these two countries gave me two of the more instructive crash courses in how a culture can be simultaneously modern, conservative, digital, and allergic to admitting any of it out loud.

## Do's and Don'ts: Pakistan 🇵🇰

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat a "rishta aunty" introduction as a legitimate institution, not a punchline — it has a real success rate | Expect mainstream dating apps to work; Tinder, Grindr, and several others were banned nationally in 2021 |
| Understand that a biodata document (family background, education, salary) is standard pre-match paperwork | Assume everyone under 30 is fine with arranged matches; a huge, app-literate youth cohort is quietly pushing back |
| Join a vetted Facebook marriage group if you're serious; some have genuine, documented success numbers | Mistake private matchmaking for a scam-free zone; families do get taken for a fee with no match delivered |

## Do's and Don'ts: Türkiye 🇹🇷

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect Istanbul and coastal cities to run a genuinely Western-style, mixed-gender social scene | Expect the same scene in much of Anatolia; the urban-secular/rural-conservative split is real and sharp |
| Accept the tea invitation fully — declining it is itself a social negotiation with consequences | Push for a direct "no" when you sense disapproval; withdrawal, not confrontation, is how it's usually signalled |
| Notice the asymmetry: Turkish men dating foreign women draws little comment; the reverse often does | Say you're "just using the apps ironically"; 60% of 18-50s do, and the culture still side-eyes it anyway |

Pakistan runs its romantic economy almost entirely outside the apps it banned, and the numbers on how matches actually happen are almost shockingly old-fashioned: a nationally representative Gallup Pakistan survey found 85% of people met their spouse through family arrangement, with only 5% citing a love marriage and a mere 1% a professional matchmaker. The "rishta aunty" — an informal or semi-professional female matchmaker — remains a named, functioning cultural institution, operating alongside biodata documents: CV-style marriage résumés listing caste, complexion, salary, and family background, submitted and compared like job applications, because in a meaningful sense that's exactly what they are. The 2021 ban on Tinder, Grindr, Tagged, Skout, and SayHi for "immoral and indecent content" didn't eliminate demand so much as redirect it; private Facebook marriage groups filled the vacuum almost immediately, with one — "Two Rings" — amassing over 228,000 members and reportedly facilitating more than 355 marriages. The tension underneath all of it is demographic: nearly two-thirds of Pakistan's population is under thirty, a generation negotiating the aunty system and the biodata spreadsheet against a backdrop of apps that technically don't exist there anymore but absolutely still get used through VPNs by people who will deny it to your face at a dinner party.

Türkiye's contradiction runs in the opposite direction: wide adoption, narrow admission. A Turkish academic study of 546 respondents aged 18 to 50 found 60% actively use dating apps, mostly Tinder, with Instagram and Twitter doubling as informal social-discovery tools — and yet only 30% rated the apps as better than meeting offline, with the broader cultural attitude toward online dating staying stubbornly negative despite the usage numbers. Geography does most of the explaining: Istanbul and the coastal cities run a genuinely cosmopolitan social scene — Beyoğlu rooftop bars, Bosphorus dinner cruises, meyhane tavern culture built around raki, mezze, and live fasıl music — while large stretches of inland Anatolia retain a far more segregated, family-chaperoned social structure. There's a documented, repeatedly-noted asymmetry worth flagging bluntly: Turkish men dating foreign women draws little social comment, while the reverse — Turkish women dating foreign men — attracts noticeably more, a blend one expat forum poster described as "European culture with conservative Middle Eastern attitudes" layered on top of each other rather than resolved. Hospitality itself becomes a social test here: a tea invitation is rarely a single polite gesture, and declining it is "the opening bid in a long negotiation," per one expat blogger's account, with withdrawal rather than direct confrontation being the standard way disapproval of any kind — romantic or otherwise — gets communicated.

One more layer worth dwelling on, because it complicates the easy "conservative Pakistan" framing: nearly two-thirds of the population being under thirty hasn't produced a quiet, uniform acceptance of the aunty-and-biodata system. Private matchmakers charging families up to $500 a match sit in open tension with free Facebook alternatives, and the forum chatter around this — matchmakers who "take money from the families and never turn up," per one Khaleej Times source — suggests a generation increasingly willing to route around institutions it no longer fully trusts, even while technically operating within them. On the Turkish side, the geographic split cuts in a specific, named direction worth repeating: Istanbul, Izmir, and Bodrum run a Western-style mixed-gender scene, while inland Anatolia remains considerably more chaperoned and family-structured — meaning "Türkiye's dating culture" isn't one culture at all, any more than "Pakistan's dating culture" is, once you actually map where the under-thirty cohort is living and who's watching.

Put the two side by side and the real divergence isn't conservative-versus-liberal; it's institutional-versus-informal. Pakistan has built actual infrastructure around arranged introduction — aunties, biodata, vetted Facebook groups with success-rate bragging rights — that operates openly, confidently, and largely without embarrassment. Türkiye has the apps, the usage stats, and the cosmopolitan Istanbul nightlife to match any European capital, but seems collectively unable to say any of it out loud without a small apologetic grimace.

## The Verdict

If you want a dating culture that's at least honest about its own machinery — biodata and all — Pakistan's system, however alien it feels to outsiders, operates with a confidence Türkiye's app-using-but-app-denying population can't quite muster. If you want genuine, accessible, Western-style nightlife and social mixing, Istanbul delivers it better than most European capitals I've lived in. I'm giving this one to Türkiye on raw experience alone — a Bosphorus dinner cruise beats a biodata spreadsheet every single time, and I will not be relitigating this with the rishta aunties who've already tried to set me up twice this year.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Khaleej Times, Ayesha Sohail, paraphrased: the real obstacle for Pakistani singles abroad often isn't culture but logistics — "the major reason is the lack of social circle here" — prompting her to start a matchmaking Facebook group that drew around 300 enquiries in its first week alone.*

> *Expat.com, Ankara forum, "Xeeschan," paraphrased: the treatment of women in social settings is often "very less than desirable," and the poster was careful to note this isn't limited to Türkiye or the wider Middle East specifically.*

> *Yabangee.com, paraphrased: a two-week acquaintance in Türkiye can escalate into a full social circle overnight, and early personal questions about family or homesickness should be read as genuine care, not nosiness.*

## Conclusion

Pick your battle: a culture that will formally vet your family background before introducing you to anyone, or one that will hand you a glass of tea and quietly judge your next three decisions based on how you handle it. Both will, eventually, ask you a question you weren't prepared to answer honestly. My only real advice, earned the hard way across both countries: answer the tea question first. The biodata question can wait until you've actually decided you like the person asking it.
