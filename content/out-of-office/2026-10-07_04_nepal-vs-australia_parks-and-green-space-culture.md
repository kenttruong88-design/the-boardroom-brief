# A Country Where the Park Is a Mountain Range and a Country Where the Park Comes with a Free Barbecue

🇳🇵 Nepal vs 🇦🇺 Australia — Parks and Green Space Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nepal does not really have "parks" in the gentle, manicured, picnic-blanket sense most expats arrive expecting. It has the actual Himalayas, several protected ecosystems containing rhinos and the occasional snow leopard, and a relationship with green space that treats "nature" as something you earn through genuine physical effort, not something you stroll into after brunch. Australia, meanwhile, has built an entire civic identity around the public park as a free, communal, barbecue-equipped extension of everyone's backyard, and will defend this with real passion.

I have sat, slightly breathless, in a teahouse at four thousand metres in the Annapurna region, and I have also sat, considerably less breathless, on a rug in a Melbourne park while someone's uninvited dog attempted to steal my sandwich. Both experiences count as "green space culture." Only one of them requires a packing list.

## Do's & Don'ts: Nepal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrange proper permits and, for serious trekking regions, a licensed guide — this is non-negotiable, not a suggestion | Assume a "national park" here means a gentle afternoon walk; many require real altitude preparation |
| Respect local shrines and prayer flags you encounter on trails — these are living religious sites, not photo props | Underestimate altitude sickness; it affects fit, young, overconfident people just as often as anyone else |
| Support teahouse and local guide economies directly — tourism revenue here genuinely reaches local communities | Litter on trails; the "leave no trace" expectation is taken extremely seriously by trekking communities |
| Visit Chitwan or Bardia for lower-altitude, wildlife-focused park experiences if high trekking isn't your thing | Expect Kathmandu Valley's urban green space to resemble what's outside the city; the contrast is stark |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book a free electric barbecue at a major park on a weekend — they fill up fast and the culture around them is real | Forget sun protection; Australian UV levels catch newcomers out badly, even on overcast days |
| Join a local parkrun — the free weekly 5km culture is a genuinely excellent way to meet people | Assume every green space is risk-free; check for local wildlife advisories, especially near water |
| Explore state and national parks beyond your city — the variety from rainforest to desert is enormous | Ignore fire danger ratings in summer; total fire ban days genuinely restrict what you can do outdoors |
| Bring your own gear to a public park and just use it; nobody will think it's strange, that's the entire point | Expect off-leash dog areas to be fenced everywhere; always check the specific park's rules first |

## Nepal: Green Space That Demands Something Back From You

Nepal's relationship with nature is not passive. The Department of National Parks and Wildlife Conservation oversees some genuinely extraordinary protected ecosystems — Sagarmatha, home to the world's highest peak, Chitwan's lowland jungle with its rhino and tiger populations, Annapurna's staggering range of altitude and ecology within a single trekking circuit — and every single one of them expects you to show up having actually prepared. Permits are mandatory, often non-negotiable, and for good reason: altitude here is not a scenic backdrop, it is an active physiological threat that doesn't care how many marathons you've run at sea level.

What makes Nepal's outdoor culture genuinely special, and genuinely different from the manicured-park model most expats default to expecting, is how embedded the local economy and spirituality are in the landscape itself. Teahouses along major trekking routes are family-run, multi-generational operations, prayer flags and mani walls mark the trail as sacred ground rather than mere path furniture, and your trekking fees and guide fees flow directly into communities that depend on exactly this kind of respectful, prepared tourism. Chitwan and Bardia offer a gentler, lower-altitude alternative for anyone who wants genuine wildlife-rich green space without the trekking commitment — jeep safaris and guided walks that put you disconcertingly close to one-horned rhinos. Kathmandu Valley's own urban green space, by contrast, is comparatively sparse and chaotic, which makes the first proper hike out of the city feel like stepping through a portal into an entirely different country. Nepal has never been part of Hofstede's original cultural research sample and carries no individually published score on his framework — worth noting honestly rather than inventing a number that doesn't exist.

## Australia: The Park as a Civic Right, Barbecue Included

Australia's parks operate on a completely different social contract: green space here is treated as an entitlement so basic that free electric barbecues, built into public parks as standard civic infrastructure, are considered entirely unremarkable. Turn up to any decent park on a Saturday and you'll find multigenerational family gatherings, cricket games assembled from whoever happens to be standing nearby, and an off-leash dog culture so enthusiastic it borders on a parallel social scene of its own. Parks Australia and the state park services maintain an enormous range of accessible green space, from inner-city botanical gardens to vast national parks a short drive from every major city, and the expectation that you'll actually use them — properly, regularly, without needing a special occasion — is baked deep into the culture.

The parkrun phenomenon has taken on a life of its own here: free, timed, community-organised 5km runs happen most Saturday mornings in parks across the country, and they function as much as a social institution as a fitness one. What the brochures don't lead with is how genuinely dangerous the outdoors can be if you treat it too casually — UV exposure here is severe enough to cause serious sunburn in twenty minutes even on a cloudy day, total fire ban days in summer carry real legal weight and real consequences for ignoring them, and water-adjacent green spaces often carry specific wildlife advisories that are not decorative signage. The Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water publishes fire danger ratings that locals check with the same reflexive regularity other cultures check the weather, because here, in summer, the fire rating effectively is the weather.

## The Verdict

If you want green space that asks nothing of you beyond showing up with a sausage and an esky, Australia built an entire civic culture specifically to deliver that, generously and repeatedly. If you want green space that asks everything of you — preparation, permits, respect, genuine physical commitment — and rewards it with some of the most staggering landscapes on the planet, Nepal will not let you leave unchanged.

I'm giving this one to Nepal, purely because nothing in Australia's admittedly excellent park culture has ever once made me cry from sheer scale and altitude-induced delirium at four thousand metres. Australia wins on accessibility and civic generosity. Nepal wins on actually meaning something.

## Conclusion

Nepal and Australia have both built entire identities around their relationship with the outdoors, and both would be horrified to be compared to the other, which is exactly why the comparison is worth making. One treats green space as something sacred, difficult, and earned. The other treats it as something free, communal, and faintly competitive once the barbecue tongs come out. Prepare properly for one. Bring sunscreen and low expectations about your cricket skills to the other. Either way, go outside. That's apparently the one thing both countries can agree on.
