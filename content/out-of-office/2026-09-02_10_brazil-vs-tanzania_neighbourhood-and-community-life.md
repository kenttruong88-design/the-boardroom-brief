# One Country Built Community on a Hillside Out of Necessity, the Other Tried to Build It by Government Decree

🇧🇷 Brazil vs 🇹🇿 Tanzania — neighbourhood and community life

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Rio's favelas built some of the most tightly woven neighbourhood networks on the planet without a single planning permit, purely because nobody else was going to provide the infrastructure, so residents organised it themselves. Tanzania tried the opposite approach entirely — a president literally relocating citizens by policy into collective villages under the banner of ujamaa, familyhood engineered from the top down. One community grew from the ground up out of necessity. The other was handed down from a government office and only partially took root. Guess which one still functions.

I've sat in a Rio favela courtyard where three generations of one family and half the surrounding block wandered in and out over a single afternoon, no invitation required. I've also read enough about ujamaa's collapse to know that community, decreed rather than grown, tends to curdle the moment the decree loses its enforcement teeth.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇧🇷 Brazil**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Engage with street-level life — favela sociability happens in alleys, narrow roads, and small squares, not behind closed doors | Assume favelas are defined solely by hardship; they're culturally vibrant, economically diverse communities with deep organising traditions |
| Respect existing community organising structures — many favelas run their own public health and social initiatives independent of the state | Treat favela tourism casually; these are functioning residential neighbourhoods, not attractions built for outside consumption |
| Recognise the outsized cultural influence — favelas shape Rio's broader music, art, and street culture far beyond their physical footprint | Underestimate the social diversity within a single favela; class and economic variation exist there just as anywhere else |

**🇹🇿 Tanzania**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand the historical weight of ujamaa — the word itself, "familyhood," still carries real cultural resonance beyond its policy failures | Assume the 1967–1985 villagization campaign achieved its stated communal goals; outcomes in peri-urban Dar es Salaam often ran opposite to plan |
| Recognise Dar es Salaam's organic urban culture, shaped partly by migrants who built city life despite, not because of, official policy | Reduce Tanzanian community life to the ujamaa policy era; daily neighbourhood life today is its own distinct, evolved thing |
| Approach neighbourhood integration with patience — trust and inclusion build through consistent presence, not policy or proximity alone | Expect instant communal integration just by living nearby; relationships in Tanzanian neighbourhoods are earned over time, same as anywhere |

## Brazil: Community as Self-Built Infrastructure

The thing outsiders get wrong about favelas, almost universally, is treating them as the absence of community rather than one of its most concentrated expressions. Long-standing inequality and historic state neglect are real and well documented, but the response to that neglect wasn't social collapse — it was self-organisation, dense and remarkably effective, built directly into the physical fabric of the neighbourhood. Narrow alleys, small squares, and tightly packed roads aren't just an aesthetic quirk of hillside geography; they're the literal architecture of sociability, spaces engineered by necessity to put people in constant, unavoidable contact with each other.

That density produces something genuinely rare: favelas function as Brazil's cultural incubators, vibrant spaces whose influence radiates outward into the wider city's music, art, and street culture, punching enormously above their formal economic weight. And crucially, the organising isn't incidental — it has deep roots. Community networks in favelas have historically run their own public health initiatives, built their own resource-sharing systems, and established genuine grassroots political structures, precisely because waiting on the state to provide those things was never a reliable option. What emerged wasn't chaos filling a vacuum; it was a parallel, self-sustaining civic infrastructure, built and maintained by residents themselves.

None of this erases the real hardship many favela residents face — but flattening these communities into a single story of poverty misses the actual social technology on display: walkable, mixed-use neighbourhoods where retail and residential life blend, where gatherings happen outdoors as a matter of course, and where economic diversity exists within a single community far more than most outsiders assume. It is, in a very real sense, community that had to earn its own resilience, and did.

## Tanzania: When Community Gets Ordered From Above

Tanzania's modern relationship with neighbourhood life carries the long shadow of a genuinely audacious social experiment. From 1967 to 1985, President Julius Nyerere pursued ujamaa — Swahili for "familyhood" — as the organising principle of African socialism, relocating citizens out of cities like Dar es Salaam and into newly created collective rural villages, built around traditional communal values reimagined as state policy. The intention was genuinely idealistic: egalitarian, family-structured community life, engineered at national scale.

The results, particularly in the peri-urban areas ringing Dar es Salaam, tended to run counter to the plan. Rather than neatly redistributing population into tidy collective villages, ujamaa villagization in districts like Kinondoni produced outcomes that, counterintuitively, helped fuel the city's sprawling expansion into its own hinterland — the opposite of the containment the policy imagined. Meanwhile, urban life in Dar es Salaam kept evolving on its own terms regardless of official doctrine: youth migrating in from across East Africa built a genuinely distinct, culturally rich urban subjectivity throughout the socialist era, one that owed as much to informal networks, literary production, and street-level cultural innovation as to anything ujamaa policy actually engineered.

What this history leaves modern Tanzanian neighbourhood life with is a complicated but resonant inheritance: the word ujamaa itself still carries real cultural weight, evoking familyhood and communal obligation even though the specific policy apparatus behind it collapsed decades ago. Today's neighbourhood culture in Dar es Salaam is its own organic thing, shaped by that history without being defined by it — closer, in practice, to Brazil's ground-up model than the top-down one the country once officially pursued, because that's ultimately what actually endured.

## The Verdict

Here's the uncomfortable symmetry: both countries' most resilient neighbourhood cultures ended up being the ones nobody planned from an office. Brazil's favelas built genuine, dense community because the state largely stayed out of the way and residents filled the gap themselves. Tanzania's most authentic urban community life outlasted, rather than resulted from, a government's explicit attempt to engineer it. If there's a lesson here it's an unflattering one for policymakers everywhere: community, it turns out, is something people build when they need each other, not something a decree can install like plumbing. Favour the alley over the five-year plan, every time.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — "What is community life actually like inside a Rio favela?" — paraphrased: a resident describes neighbours dropping by unannounced as completely normal, and says the closest Western equivalent — a scheduled visit — would actually feel rude by local standards.*

> *Expat.com Rio forum — paraphrased: a foreign resident living adjacent to a favela community says it took months of consistent, low-key presence — greeting the same shopkeepers, showing up to the same corner — before genuine trust started to form.*

> *TheLocal-style expat blog, Dar es Salaam — paraphrased: a new arrival notes that neighbourhood integration happened almost entirely through small, repeated daily interactions rather than any single formal introduction, and that patience mattered more than any grand gesture.*

## Conclusion

Strip away the very different histories — one shaped by neglect and self-reliance, the other by an ambitious and ultimately unrealised policy experiment — and both countries land in roughly the same place: real neighbourhood community isn't handed to you, planned for you, or decreed into existence around you. It's built, slowly, through proximity, repetition, and a willingness to actually show up. Brazil's favelas prove it can flourish under pressure. Tanzania's post-ujamaa urban life proves it can outlast even a government's best intentions to manufacture it. Wherever you land, skip the five-year plan and start with the neighbour on your actual corner.
