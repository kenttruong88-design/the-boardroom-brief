# One Country Air-Conditions the Outdoors. The Other Just Lets the Grass Do Whatever It Wants.

🇶🇦 Qatar 🇭🇺 Hungary
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Doha has, in the space of two decades, built parks so manicured, so irrigated, so aggressively green against a desert backdrop that they read less like public space and more like an act of defiance against the climate itself. Budapest, meanwhile, has parks that have simply existed for a century and a half, doing what parks did before anyone thought to optimise them — going a bit wild in August, filling with chess players and day-drinkers, occasionally hosting a folk festival nobody outside the neighbourhood has heard of. Both, in their own way, are a triumph. One triumphs over nature. The other triumphs by leaving it mostly alone.

I've picnicked in both, sweated through one and shivered through the other, and I can report that the difference between engineered green space and inherited green space says more about a country's relationship with control than almost anything else I've encountered. Qatar treats its parks as infrastructure — funded, irrigated, patrolled, immaculate. Hungary treats its parks as inheritance — a bit shaggy round the edges, occasionally home to a stray dog with more social standing than most tourists, and entirely, gloriously free of anyone's five-year plan.

## Qatar: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit parks early morning or after sunset — midday heat is genuinely dangerous | Expect much use of Corniche parks between June and September; nobody is out there |
| Respect modest dress norms even in outdoor leisure spaces | Bring alcohol; public parks are dry, and it's enforced |
| Try the Aspire Park or Al Bidda Park running tracks in winter — genuinely excellent | Assume every green patch is public — some landscaped areas belong to private developments |

## Hungary: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your own drinks and a blanket to Városliget or Margaret Island — totally normal | Expect manicured perfection; Hungarian parks are used hard and look it |
| Join a pickup football or chess game if invited — it happens constantly | Assume parks close or restrict access; most are open and unpoliced late into the evening |
| Visit Margaret Island's thermal-adjacent gardens for a genuinely surreal green space in the Danube | Litter — locals notice, and increasingly, so do fines |

## Qatar: Green Space as a Feat of Engineering, Not a Given

Nothing about a lush, walkable park in Doha is natural, and Qatar has never pretended otherwise — the entire concept requires desalinated or treated water piped to irrigation systems keeping grass alive in a climate that would otherwise support essentially nothing green at this scale. Aspire Park, the Corniche's landscaped stretches, and the newer developments around Lusail represent a genuine national investment in the idea that a city built on oil wealth and desert sand should still offer its residents somewhere to jog, picnic, and let children run around on actual grass rather than sand or concrete. It's an impressive act of civic will, and the parks themselves, when you catch them at the right hour, are genuinely lovely — manicured, safe, clean to a standard most European cities would envy, with running tracks lit for the evening crowds who only emerge once the temperature drops below a level that would otherwise hospitalise them.

The catch is seasonal and severe: for roughly five months of the year, from May through September, using these parks in daylight hours ranges from unpleasant to actively dangerous, with temperatures regularly exceeding 40°C and humidity along the coast making it feel considerably worse. Qatari and expat park culture has adapted accordingly — evening and early-morning use spikes, families picnic under floodlights at 9pm, and the parks essentially operate on an inverted clock for nearly half the year. There's also a formality to Qatari public space that doesn't exist in Budapest — alcohol is prohibited outright, dress expectations remain modest even in leisure settings, and the parks, however beautiful, carry a sense of being maintained rather than lived-in in the loose, chaotic way Hungarian green space is lived-in. It's a park culture built by design, for a population that has to actively choose the hours when nature briefly becomes hospitable again.

## Hungary: A City That Lets Its Parks Age Like Everything Else

Budapest's relationship with its green spaces is the product of a much longer, less deliberate history — Városliget (City Park) has existed in some form since the late 1700s, Margaret Island has been a public leisure destination since the 19th century, and neither has ever been subjected to the kind of top-down design overhaul that would make them feel new. What you get instead is texture: parks that show their age, that fill unevenly with different crowds depending on the hour and the season, that host everything from formal thermal bath complexes to entirely unofficial pickup football games with equal indifference. Hungarians use their parks the way people use furniture they've had for decades — without ceremony, without asking permission, and without much concern for whether it looks pristine for visitors.

This produces a genuinely different emotional register than Doha's engineered green space. Nobody in Budapest experiences a park as an achievement to be marvelled at; it's simply where you go, on a warm evening, with a bottle of wine and a blanket, because that's what the space has always been for. Municipal investment does happen — Városliget has undergone a significant renewal programme in recent years, adding new museums and cultural buildings around its green core — but even the renewed sections retain a kind of unforced, lived-in Hungarian looseness that resists the manicured feel of a masterplan. Winters strip the parks down to something genuinely bleak and grey, a stark contrast to Doha's temperate winter peak season, which means Budapest's parks operate on the opposite seasonal rhythm entirely: glorious and crowded in summer, largely abandoned in deep winter except by the truly committed dog walkers.

## The Verdict

Qatar wins on sheer engineering audacity — building genuine green oases in a desert through nothing but investment and willpower is legitimately impressive, and the parks, at the right hour, are stunning. But Hungary wins on soul, because a park that's been lived in for two centuries carries a texture, a looseness, an unbothered humanity that no amount of desalinated irrigation can replicate in twenty years. I'll take a slightly overgrown Budapest evening over a pristine Doha one every time — though I'll grant Doha this: at least you can sit on the grass at midday in January without your teeth chattering, which is more than Budapest in February can offer anyone.

## Conclusion

There's something to be said for both approaches — the sheer will required to grow grass in a desert, and the quiet confidence of a country that's never once worried about whether its parks look impressive because they've simply always been there. Go to Doha for the spectacle of green space that shouldn't exist. Go to Budapest for a bottle of wine on a blanket and nobody asking you to leave by any particular hour. Just check the thermometer before you commit to either plan, and if you're picnicking in Qatar in July, I genuinely cannot help you.
