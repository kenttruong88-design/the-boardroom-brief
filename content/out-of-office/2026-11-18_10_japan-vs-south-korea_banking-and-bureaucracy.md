# Japan Wants Your Seal and Korea Wants Your Phone Contract, and Both Want You to Come Back Tomorrow

🇯🇵 Japan vs 🇰🇷 South Korea

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Bureaucracy is a nation's love language. Japan expresses it with stamps, forms and exquisite politeness, a warm ceremony in which you are refused with a bow. South Korea expresses it with apps, certificates and a loud insistence that your phone number must match your residence card to the last digit. Both want your paperwork. Neither is going to tell you what is missing until you have finished queuing.

For the corporate expat, banking is the first real test. You can arrive with a contract, a title and an employer who sends a driver, and still be unable to pay your rent because a bank has decided you are insufficiently resident. Welcome to East Asia, where the cash is real and the account is a rumour.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Japan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register your address at the ward office within 14 days | Wait until the bank asks for proof |
| Carry your residence card, which is legally required | Leave it at home |
| Bring a registered seal if the bank asks for one | Assume a signature always works |
| Check how long your visa has left before applying | Apply with only a few months remaining |

**South Korea**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for your Alien Registration Card promptly | Leave it past the 90-day window |
| Keep your phone contract in the same name as your card | Use a friend's or a prepaid number |
| Book your immigration appointment in advance | Turn up on the day and hope |
| Expect a limited account at first | Assume a transfer limit is a mistake |

## Japan

The Japanese process begins at the ward office. A third-party guide to the first two weeks says mid- to long-term residents must notify their municipality within fourteen days of establishing a main residence, bringing a residence card or passport, and that residents aged sixteen or over must carry the card. My Number is assigned on registration, though the physical card is optional. National Health Insurance enrolment is not automatic, working-age patients pay thirty per cent and residents aged twenty to fifty-nine generally enter the National Pension.

Then comes the bank. A 2026 guide says most banks require a registered seal, a *hanko*, which costs around ¥500 to 1,000, and that a residence card showing your current address is non-negotiable. Another guide says it depends on the procedure, and a forum user says Japan Post Bank does not require one, so this is contested and you should ask. The same guides say banks typically reject applicants with under six months left on their visa, while Japan Post Bank will not open an account if the stay expires within three months. A certificate of residence, the *juminhyo*, costs about ¥300.

The banks themselves have personalities. Shinsei is described as foreigner-friendly with an English platform, Japan Post Bank has more than 24,000 ATMs but limited English, SMBC Prestia reportedly needs around ¥500,000 to avoid fees and Sony Bank requires a Japanese phone number. Cash cards arrive by post in one to two weeks.

Underneath this is a cultural logic of its own. Japan's system prizes accountability to a local community: a seal, an address, a ward. The bank wants to know that you are the sort of person who will still be there next year, and a short visa tells it you are not. That is not xenophobia so much as an actuarial prejudice with good manners. It does not make a refusal any more pleasant, but it explains why waiting out the six months, or finding a bank with a wider view, is the standard advice.

The human cost is in the stories. Tim Odagiri, an American in Yokohama employed by his US headquarters, wrote that several banks refused him, and that Yucho, Japan Post Bank, opened an account only after he visited three times and a branch manager called headquarters. He argues banks treat anyone with under six months in Japan as a non-resident. A JREF forum poster describes applying by printed and mailed forms, with a cash card arriving about a month later, and a two-hour branch visit to fix an ATM block after renewing his residence card. He later moved to Prestia and found it smooth, and others reported good experiences at MUFG, Shinsei, Mizuho and Resona. The pattern is clear: Japan can be done, but it must be done in person, with patience and a branch that has decided to like you.

## South Korea

Korea's centre of gravity is the Alien Registration Card. A third-party guide says to apply within ninety days of arrival, via HiKorea appointments, and says processing takes three to five weeks, though another source says two to four. The fees conflict between sources, so I will not quote them. Address changes must be reported within fourteen days.

New accounts are generally "limit accounts". The guides disagree on the daily caps, and one says the limit was raised in May 2024, so treat any figure as unreliable; the principle is that proof of purpose, such as an employment contract or salary deposits, lifts the cap. The big five banks are KB Kookmin, Shinhan, Hana, Woori and NongHyup, with internet banks Kakao, K Bank and Toss, and Toss Bank is reported to accept an ARC for remote signup. Banks typically close at four in the afternoon and on weekends, and your phone number must be linked to your ARC. One guide says that from March 2025 foreign residents can open accounts at six banks using a mobile foreigner residence card, though I could not check it against an official page.

Then comes digital identity. A joint certificate lasts one year and a financial certificate three, and simple authentication through Kakao, Naver or PASS needs your phone contract name to match your ARC exactly. Tax runs through Hometax, with year-end settlement through the employer in January and February and freelancer filing in May, and a flat 19 per cent option for foreigners in their first twenty years is reported by one third-party guide. I could not verify how the ARC number substitutes for a Korean resident registration number, so I will say only that you will be asked for one.

There is a deeper cultural logic behind the Korean rules, and it is trust through identity. The system wants a verified person at the end of every transaction, tied to a phone, a card and a certificate, which is why a mismatch between a phone contract and an ARC can lock you out of nearly everything. For a newcomer it feels like being locked in a very efficient room. The only way out is to line up your documents so that they say the same thing in the same name, and then everything works at once.

The editorial voice is dry. MoveAndStay's piece on the silent stress of living in Seoul says immigration paperwork runs for hours, that one missed form can reset the process and that you cannot open a proper bank account without sorted legal status. It is an editorial, not a first-hand quote, and I could not find a verified Korean ARC or Hometax story. The "ppalli-ppalli" reputation for speed applies to delivery apps, not to the immigration office.

## The Verdict

Korea wins on technology, and Japan wins on humanity, which is a polite way of saying I would rather be refused by a person than by an app. A Japanese branch manager, shown enough persistence, will sometimes pick up the phone. A Korean authentication loop, shown enough persistence, will merely ask for the same code again.

But once you are inside the Korean system, it is astonishingly smooth. Internet banks, instant transfers and one-tap authentication are a pleasure, and the hard part is a single door. Japan's difficulty is spread more evenly across the process, with seals, forms, branches and a cash card in the post.

If I were choosing, I would bank in Korea and register in Japan. Nobody makes it that easy, so pick the country, then pick your patience.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Into Japan Substack, July 2022 — an American in Yokohama says several banks refused him and Japan Post Bank opened an account only after three visits and a branch manager's call to headquarters; he argues banks treat people under six months in Japan as non-residents.*

> *JREF forum — a poster who applied for a Japan Post Bank account by mailed forms waited about a month for the cash card, spent roughly two hours at a branch fixing an ATM block after a residence-card renewal, and later found Prestia smooth.*

> *MoveAndStay editorial on Seoul — immigration paperwork can run for hours, one missed form can reset the process and a proper bank account is out of reach until your legal status is sorted.*

## Conclusion

Banking abroad is a lesson in humility. Japan asks you to prove you are staying. Korea asks you to prove you are you. In both cases the answer is a document you do not have yet.

Bring your passport, your contract, a passport photo, a second copy of everything and a very long afternoon. Be polite to the person behind the glass and cheerful to the person behind the app. And never, under any circumstances, say "but it works at home".
