# The Shared Taxi Doesn't Leave Until It's Ready, and Neither Should You Expect It To

🇬🇦 Gabon and 🇨🇬 Congo

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Libreville and Brazzaville rarely make it into any conversation about African cities that isn't immediately followed by an oil-and-instability caveat, which is a shame, because both cities have developed genuinely inventive, thoroughly chaotic commuting cultures built almost entirely around the shared taxi — a vehicle that operates on a logic no Western transit app has ever successfully modelled, because the logic is: we leave when the car is full, and not one moment sooner.

Gabon, oil-wealthy on paper and startlingly underdeveloped in public transit infrastructure given that wealth, and Congo-Brazzaville, smaller and scrappier but no less committed to the shared-taxi philosophy, both make a strong case that the actual transport system of a city has very little to do with what its government built and everything to do with what its drivers improvised.

## Do's & Don'ts: Gabon

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the informal shared-taxi routes around Libreville by riding with a local first | Expect posted schedules, route maps, or numbered stops; none of that exists formally |
| Negotiate or confirm the fare before getting in, every single time | Assume a fare agreed yesterday still applies today; prices shift with fuel and traffic |
| Keep small change ready — drivers rarely have it | Hand over a large bill and expect prompt, exact change |
| Accept that "full" is a flexible concept that always has room for one more passenger | Expect personal space once the taxi declares itself "full" |

## Do's & Don'ts: Congo

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use "taxi-brousse" or shared city taxis for cross-town trips rather than expecting a bus network | Wait around for a fixed-schedule public bus in Brazzaville; frequency is informal at best |
| Confirm whether you're paying for a shared ride or chartering the whole vehicle ("dépôt") | Assume the fare structure without asking; shared and private rates differ significantly |
| Be ready to switch taxis mid-route as drivers consolidate passengers going the same direction | Expect a single taxi to take you door to door without any transfers |
| Travel with cash in small denominations for river crossings and ferry transport | Assume river transport between Brazzaville and Kinshasa runs on a fixed timetable |

## Gabon: Wealth Without a Metro

Gabon's peculiar economic profile — genuine oil wealth, one of the highest GDP-per-capita figures in sub-Saharan Africa, and yet a public transport system that never received the investment that wealth might suggest — makes Libreville's commuting culture a study in improvisation filling a vacuum the state never got around to addressing. There is no metro, no comprehensive city bus network with the kind of route reliability you'd take for granted almost anywhere else. What exists instead is a dense, informal web of shared taxis, mostly ageing sedans, that circulate along understood but entirely unofficial routes, picking up and dropping off passengers with a fluidity that would baffle a transit planner and function perfectly well for everyone who's grown up navigating it.

The core rule, learned quickly or learned expensively, is that a shared taxi departs when it's full, not on any schedule, and "full" in Libreville has a considerably more generous definition than most passengers arrive expecting. Confirming the fare before boarding is essential — prices fluctuate with fuel costs and aren't posted anywhere, and a foreigner who doesn't ask first will reliably be quoted more than a local would pay for the identical seat. Drivers rarely carry change for large notes, and turning up with nothing smaller than a large bill is a fast way to either overpay considerably or hold up the entire vehicle while someone runs to find change from a nearby vendor.

What foreigners consistently misjudge is how socially functional this apparent chaos actually is. Regular routes develop regular riders, regular riders develop familiarity with specific drivers, and that familiarity translates into small kindnesses — a driver waiting an extra minute for a known passenger, a slight fare break for someone who rides daily — that no formal transit system, however well-funded, reliably offers its most loyal customers.

## Congo: The Taxi-Brousse and the River as Highway

Brazzaville's commuting culture runs on a similarly informal shared-taxi system, though the local vocabulary and specific customs differ enough from Gabon's that assuming the two cities work identically is its own small mistake. The crucial distinction visitors need to grasp immediately is the difference between a shared ride, where you're one of several passengers paying a set per-seat rate, and a "dépôt" or chartered ride, where you're paying for the entire vehicle and its exclusive use — conflating the two leads to some genuinely confusing fare disputes that a moment's clarification upfront would have avoided entirely.

Mid-route consolidation is a defining feature of Congolese shared-taxi culture — drivers will actively reorganise passengers between vehicles heading the same general direction, meaning your straightforward cross-town trip might involve an unplanned transfer to a different car entirely, negotiated between drivers in a rapid exchange that happens over your head, quite literally, while you sit in the back seat wondering what just happened. This isn't a scam; it's route optimisation, Congolese style, and once you understand it, it's actually a rather elegant informal solution to underutilised capacity.

Congo-Brazzaville's relationship with Kinshasa, its sister capital across the Congo River and the closest pair of capital cities in the world, adds a transport dimension found almost nowhere else — the river crossing itself functions as a genuine commuting route for some residents, informal and formal ferry services alike operating on timetables that bend considerably around demand, weather, and the availability of the boat itself, rather than any fixed schedule a visitor could reliably plan around.

## The Verdict

Gabon wins on sheer improvisational density — an oil-wealthy nation that somehow built one of the continent's most thoroughly informal, thoroughly functional shared-taxi ecosystems purely because the state never filled the gap itself. Congo wins on sheer audacity of scale — a cross-river international commute between two capital cities, conducted with the same shrugging informality as a cross-town taxi ride.

Neither system will hand you a schedule, a map, or a guarantee. Both will get you where you're going, eventually, provided you've confirmed the fare, kept small change ready, and accepted that "full" and "on time" are considerably more elastic concepts here than anywhere your transit app has ever prepared you for.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a Libreville-based NGO worker paraphrased: I once waited forty minutes for a shared taxi to fill up, only to watch it somehow accept a sixth passenger for a five-seat car right as I'd finally accepted defeat and started walking.*

> *expat.com forum — a Brazzaville resident noted that being mid-route transferred between two taxis by drivers negotiating over their heads was disorienting the first time and completely unremarkable by the third.*

> *Internations Central Africa forum — a longtime expat paraphrased: the river crossing between Brazzaville and Kinshasa runs on a logic entirely its own, budget for delays that have nothing to do with distance and everything to do with the boat simply not being ready yet.*

## Conclusion

Libreville and Brazzaville prove that a functioning transport system doesn't require a metro map or a government transit authority with a nice logo — it requires drivers, passengers, and an unspoken shared agreement about what "full" and "ready" actually mean. Confirm your fare, keep small notes handy, and let go of any expectation that this system owes you a timetable. It was never going to give you one, and it works considerably better than you'd expect regardless.
