# Asunción Will Hand You Antibiotics With No Questions Asked. Ottawa Will Hand You a Waitlist.

🇵🇾 Paraguay vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Everyone assumes the healthcare conversation is a simple one: rich country, good system; poorer country, worse system. Spend any real time as an expat navigating both a Paraguayan farmacia and the Canadian primary-care waitlist, and that assumption collapses within about a week, usually right around the moment you realise one of these countries will sell you anything over the counter and the other won't let you see a doctor at all.

I've needed urgent, unglamorous medical attention in both countries — nothing dramatic, just the ordinary indignities of being a body that travels too much — and the contrast taught me more about what "healthcare" actually means than either government's own messaging ever did.

## Do's and Don'ts: Paraguay 🇵🇾

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use a private hospital in Villa Morra or Las Carmelitas for anything beyond routine care; it's genuinely affordable | Expect public MSPBS hospitals to supply your basics; bring your own gauze, saline, even bedding where needed |
| Keep cash or card ready for private care; it's pay-as-you-go, not a running tab | Assume IPS social-security coverage extends to you automatically; it's tied to formal employment and a cédula |
| Treat the farmacia as a genuine first stop; antibiotics and most medicines are sold over the counter with ease | Ignore dengue and chikungunya risk; they're live, practical concerns the UK government itself flags for travellers |

## Do's and Don'ts: Canada 🇨🇦

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for a family doctor the day you arrive, not when you first feel ill | Expect speed; the national median wait from referral to treatment is 28.6 weeks — longer than a pregnancy |
| Get employer supplemental insurance sorted for drugs, dental, and vision; the public system doesn't cover those | Assume every province is equal; Ontario's wait (19.2 weeks) is a third of New Brunswick's (60.9 weeks) |
| Use your pharmacist for minor ailments; expanded prescribing powers now exist in several provinces | Walk into a pharmacy expecting an antibiotic without a prescription; the system is tightly, deliberately regulated |

Paraguay's system runs on a blunt but workable logic: the public network, under MSPBS, is free "in principle" and chronically underfunded in practice, which in real terms means patients often bring their own basic supplies to a public hospital visit. Formal employees get IPS, the mandatory social-security health scheme, funded by a combined 25.5% contribution split between employee and employer — but the self-employed, retirees, and plenty of expats simply can't buy into it, and family coverage for a worker's dependents often doesn't kick in until that worker holds a Paraguayan cédula, a bureaucratic snag that catches newcomers constantly. Where Paraguay quietly wins is private care: GP visits in Asunción run $15 to $50, specialists $50 to $150, an MRI $150 to $400, and even major procedures land at 50 to 70% below equivalent US pricing. Pharmacies are everywhere and sell most medicines, including antibiotics, over the counter with minimal friction — a genuine point of contrast with the tightly dispensed Canadian system, and one that regularly stuns newly arrived expats the first time they walk out of a farmacia with something they'd have needed three appointments to get at home. The system's strangest and most telling detail, documented in local reporting this year: more than 2,500 Argentines have reportedly been crossing the border to use Paraguay's free public hospitals, overwhelming the regional hospital in Encarnación — a nation widely assumed to have the "weaker" healthcare system somehow running a reverse medical-tourism operation on its wealthier neighbour.

Canada's system has the opposite problem entirely: universality without velocity. The Fraser Institute's 2025 wait-time study put the national median wait from GP referral to actual treatment at 28.6 weeks — down slightly from the prior year, but still more than triple the 9.3-week median recorded in 1993. Break it down by province and the "universal" part gets complicated fast: Ontario sits at a relatively brisk 19.2 weeks while New Brunswick stretches to 60.9 — nearly a three-times gap within a single country's single public system. By specialty, neurosurgery waits average 49.9 weeks and orthopaedic surgery 48.6, while radiation oncology, mercifully, moves in about 4.2. Imaging has its own queue: an MRI averages an 18.1-week wait. None of this is abstract to the people living it — CTV News reported over 23,000 Canadians died on medical wait lists in a single recent year — and a 2025 Canadian Medical Association survey found 5.9 million adults, roughly the population of a mid-sized province, still lack reliable access to a regular family doctor at all, leaving walk-in clinics and emergency rooms to absorb primary care that was never supposed to be their job. Pharmacy culture reflects the same careful regulation: antibiotics require a prescription full stop, though several provinces have recently expanded pharmacists' prescribing powers for minor ailments specifically as a workaround for the doctor shortage, which is a tidy admission of the underlying problem dressed up as a policy win.

A practical detail worth flagging before anyone packs a bag for Asunción: dengue and chikungunya are live, ongoing risks there, flagged explicitly by the UK government's own travel advisory, in a way that simply has no Canadian equivalent — the calculus of "which country is riskier" depends enormously on what kind of risk you're weighing, acute infectious disease versus chronic systemic delay. And on the Canadian side, the regulatory tightness around pharmacy dispensing isn't pure bureaucratic caution; it's a direct response to the same doctor-shortage pressure driving the 5.9-million-person access gap, with provinces expanding what a pharmacist can prescribe specifically because the family-doctor pipeline can't keep up with demand on its own.

The honest summary: Paraguay will let you walk in and get treated, fast, cheaply, privately, assuming you can pay, with a public safety net that's threadbare but real enough to attract cross-border patients from a wealthier neighbour. Canada will eventually treat you excellently, for free, assuming you survive the queue, which by specialty and by province is not a guarantee anyone in government seems prepared to make out loud.

One final data point worth sitting with: Paraguay's current health expenditure runs at roughly 7.17% of GDP, a modest figure next to Canada's far larger public outlay, and yet the private system it funds manages appendectomies at $2,000 to $5,000 total and specialist consultations under $150 — numbers that make the Canadian comparison less about "rich country versus poor country" and more about where each system chose to spend its money: Canada on universal coverage with a queue attached, Paraguay on speed and price for whoever can pay, with a threadbare public net underneath that a neighbouring country is apparently finding more reliable than its own.

## The Verdict

If speed and affordability matter more than the word "free" printed on a pamphlet, Paraguay's private system will outperform Canada's public one on almost every practical metric that affects an actual sick person on an actual Tuesday. If you want a system that will never bankrupt you and simply asks you to be patient — extremely, structurally, multi-season patient — Canada remains a remarkable long-term bet. I'm calling this one for Paraguay, because a system an entire neighbouring country is quietly smuggling itself into for free care has clearly got something right that a 28.6-week median wait has not.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *ExpatExchange, Paraguay healthcare thread, paraphrased: the public system is "largely underfunded and understaffed, leading to long wait times," while private care is "often expensive and out of reach for many" — a split verdict depending entirely on your budget, not your symptoms.*

> *CTV News, paraphrased: more than 23,000 Canadians died on medical wait lists in a single recent year — a toll rarely mentioned in the same breath as the country's "universal healthcare" reputation.*

> *La Política Online, paraphrased: Encarnación's regional public hospital has been overwhelmed by Argentine patients crossing the border specifically to access Paraguay's free care — a reverse medical-tourism flow nobody predicted a decade ago.*

## Conclusion

Bring your own gauze to Paraguay and your own patience to Canada, and you'll survive either system intact. The deeper lesson, which took me an embarrassing number of relocations to actually absorb: "universal" and "fast" are not the same promise, and "free" and "accessible" aren't either. Pick the farmacia or pick the queue. Just don't pretend either country's brochure told you the whole truth about which one you were getting.
