# The Only Place You'll Be Punished for Learning the Local Language Is the One That Speaks It Best

🇩🇰 Denmark vs 🇳🇱 Netherlands — Language Barrier Experiences
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is the cruel joke at the centre of Northern European relocation: both the Danes and the Dutch speak better English than most native English speakers, which means your carefully rehearsed local-language sentence will be met, within roughly two syllables, by a fluent English reply and a look that says "why are we doing this." I have watched grown adults — capable professionals who negotiate contracts for a living — reduced to stammering wrecks by a Copenhagen barista's instant code-switch. It is not cruelty. It is efficiency. But it feels like cruelty, and someone needs to say so.

The two countries arrive at the same maddening result — you will almost never be forced to speak the local language — via completely different cultural logic, and understanding which logic you're up against will save you months of unnecessary shame.

## Do's & Don'ts: Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn Danish anyway, for residency and genuine belonging, not for daily necessity | Expect praise for trying — a mangled "tak" gets a polite nod, not encouragement |
| Accept that "hyggelig" small talk happens in English the moment you hesitate | Take the switch to English personally; it's usually impatience with the pace, not judgment of you |
| Use workplace English as your social safety net — it's near-universal in Copenhagen | Assume rural Jutland works the same way English fluency drops fast outside the capital |

## Do's & Don'ts: Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Prepare for blunt correction if you do attempt Dutch — it's meant helpfully | Mistake the correction for hostility; Dutch directness is a feature, not an insult |
| Lean on English confidently in Amsterdam, Rotterdam, and most corporate settings | Assume the same holds in smaller towns, where Dutch pride in the language runs stronger |
| Try Duolingo Dutch for basics — supermarket and neighbour interactions still reward minimal effort | Get discouraged when a five-minute conversation flips to English after one wrong verb conjugation |

## Denmark: Fluency So Total It Erases the Point of Trying

Denmark's English proficiency isn't just high, it's structurally embedded — English-medium instruction starts early, Danish television airs subtitled rather than dubbed foreign content, and the workplace, especially in Copenhagen's tech and pharma sectors, often runs on English by default because half the team is international anyway. The upshot is that learning Danish for practical survival is almost pointless in the capital, which creates a strange paradox: Denmark requires language proficiency for permanent residency and citizenship, essentially mandating you learn a language you will structurally never need to use.

Danes aren't rude about the switch to English, they're just uninterested in performing patience for your benefit. Small talk is not a beloved national sport here the way it is in, say, the American Midwest — Danes are famously reserved with strangers, and adding a language barrier on top of that reserve just gives them one more reason to keep the interaction short. If you fumble a sentence at the bakery, you will get your rundstykker and a swift pivot to English, not a warm "din danske er så god!" There is no reward for effort here, only efficiency, and expats who arrive expecting gold stars for trying leave feeling oddly deflated.

The real language wall in Denmark isn't linguistic, it's social — Danish social circles, the tight childhood-friend clusters Danes maintain into their forties, are far harder to break into than the language itself. You could speak flawless Danish and still spend two years on the outside of every dinner party. Learn the language for the bureaucracy and your own dignity, not because it will meaningfully change how quickly you're let in.

## Netherlands: Blunt, Confident, Weirdly Encouraging Once You Push Through

The Dutch approach the same underlying reality — near-universal, genuinely excellent English — with an entirely different social temperature. Where Danish reserve makes the English-switch feel like polite withdrawal, Dutch directness makes it feel like an active correction: you will be told, immediately and without cushioning, that your sentence was wrong, that the word order is backwards, that you should have used "de" not "het." This is not malice. This is the single most misunderstood trait in Dutch culture — directness is considered a form of respect, an assumption that you're capable of hearing the truth and improving, rather than someone who needs to be managed.

Once you push past the initial sting, Dutch directness is oddly generous. Dutch colleagues who switch to English mid-sentence aren't dismissing your effort, they're solving a communication problem at maximum speed, the same instinct that makes Dutch meetings shockingly efficient and Dutch feedback culture legendarily blunt. If you keep trying, Dutch people will keep correcting you, which is, in its own graceless way, a sign they've decided you're worth correcting.

The regional variation matters more here than in Denmark. Amsterdam and the Randstad run on English so thoroughly that some expats live there for years, functionally monolingual, without consequence. Venture into smaller towns in Friesland, Limburg, or Zeeland and the calculus shifts — Dutch (or, in Friesland's case, Frisian) pride runs deeper, English proficiency drops, and the effort you put in actually gets noticed and rewarded, occasionally with a beer.

## The Verdict

Denmark will let you disappear into English indefinitely and quietly judge you for it in ways you'll never quite see. The Netherlands will correct every mistake you make to your face and somehow mean it kindly. If you need your effort acknowledged, however roughly, the Dutch win — at least you know where you stand. If you'd rather never be corrected and never be included, Denmark offers a smoother, colder version of the same outcome. Neither country is going to make you struggle in the local tongue, which, if you actually wanted the immersive language experience your relocation brochure promised, means you picked the wrong region of Europe entirely.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/Denmark — paraphrased: a poster said they'd spent eight months trying to get shopkeepers to let them practise Danish, and eventually gave up and just accepted the English default as a mercy.*

> *Internations Amsterdam — paraphrased: a member described being corrected on gendered articles mid-sentence by a stranger at a language exchange, and eventually realising it was the closest thing to friendliness on offer.*

## Conclusion

Both countries will hand you an English-speaking off-ramp before you've finished your sentence, and both will make you feel, in entirely different ways, that the attempt was slightly beside the point. The Dutch will at least tell you why. The Danes will just let you wonder. If your ego needs the language struggle to feel earned, go somewhere that actually requires the local tongue for survival — Northern Europe long ago made that struggle optional, and optional struggles, in my experience, are the ones nobody actually finishes.
