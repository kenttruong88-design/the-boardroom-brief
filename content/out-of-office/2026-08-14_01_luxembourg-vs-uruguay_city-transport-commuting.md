# Free Trains Won't Save You From a Fifteen-Kilometre Traffic Jam

🇱🇺 Luxembourg 🇺🇾 Uruguay
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Luxembourg made every single bus, tram, and train free in 2020, and somehow still managed to build a country where three out of four commuters sit in a car every morning anyway. I find that magnificent. It's the transport equivalent of installing a gym in the office and watching everyone still take the lift. Uruguay, meanwhile, never bothered pretending it had a plan — Montevideo's buses run when they run, the STM card works until it doesn't, and everybody just accepts that getting anywhere involves a small negotiation with fate.

I've commuted in both. One made me feel like a spoiled toddler being chauffeured for free through a country the size of a decent shopping mall. The other made me late for a job interview because the bus driver stopped to argue with his cousin. Neither system respects your time, but only one of them will admit it.

## Do's & Don'ts: Luxembourg

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get the free mKaart even if you rarely ride — it's genuinely free, no catch | Assume "free" means uncrowded — cross-border trains from France are sardine cans by 8am |
| Cycle if you live in the city centre; the bike lanes are better than the roads | Drive into Luxembourg City during rush hour expecting the "small country" myth to save you |
| Use the train for cross-border commutes rather than the motorway | Forget that "free" public transport was partly a political fix for horrific road congestion, not a transport miracle |

## Do's & Don'ts: Uruguay

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Load your STM card with more credit than you think you need | Expect a printed timetable to mean anything at all |
| Use remises (private car services) for anything time-sensitive | Hail a random taxi off the street without confirming the meter works |
| Learn the informal hand signals locals use to flag buses | Assume Montevideo's compact size means short commute times — the buses take the scenic route, always |

## Luxembourg: A Free System Nobody Needed to Fix the Real Problem

Let's be honest about what happened here. Luxembourg didn't wake up one day overflowing with civic generosity. It became the country with the highest number of cars per capita in the EU, its motorways turned into daily car parks fed by tens of thousands of cross-border workers pouring in from France, Belgium, and Germany, and the government did the maths on toll booths versus political embarrassment. Free transport won. Genuinely free — no ticket machines, no barriers, no awkward fumbling for change on a tram in Kirchberg. You just get on.

And it is, on paper, wonderful. The trains are clean, air-conditioned, and run with the sort of punctuality that makes you suspicious. The trams gliding down Avenue de la Liberté are the closest thing to elegant public transport I've experienced outside Zurich. But here's the sting: free didn't fix congestion, because free transport doesn't fix geography. Half of Luxembourg's workforce lives across a border and still drives, because the trains from Thionville or Trier, however cheap, still take an hour and involve a walk at both ends. The government essentially gifted the country a beautiful, empty-ish train network while the actual problem — an economy that imports 200,000 workers a day into a city built for a fraction of that — sits there, untouched, in gridlock on the A3.

What nobody tells newcomers is that "free" also means underfunded ambition. There's no metro. Luxembourg City is still, structurally, a town that got rich too fast to redesign its own streets. You'll ride a genuinely lovely tram for four stops and then discover the rest of your journey is a fifteen-minute walk uphill because the network simply stops. Free is nice. Comprehensive would have been better.

## Uruguay: Function Over Formality, and Somehow It Works

Montevideo's transport system has no interest in impressing you, and I respect that. There is no glossy free-fare announcement, no minister posing next to a tram. There is a bus network that has existed in more or less the same tangled form for decades, an STM smart card that half the population still doesn't fully trust, and drivers who treat the timetable as a loose suggestion rather than a contract.

And yet. It works, in the maddening, analog way things work in places that never had the money to over-engineer them. Buses are frequent even if they're unpredictable — wait fifteen minutes for one, and three arrive in convoy, nose to tail, like buses that have been talking about you behind your back. Fares are cheap. The STM card, once you load it correctly, gets you almost anywhere in the city for less than the price of a coffee. And because Montevideo is flat, walkable, and refuses to sprawl the way Latin American capitals ten times its size do, most locals simply don't commute that far to begin with.

What surprises every expat is the informality layered on top of the formal system: remises, unlicensed but broadly trusted private cars booked by phone, fill every gap the buses leave. Uruguayans don't see this as a workaround; it's just how the city has always moved. The government hasn't modernised the fleet in any dramatic way, hasn't gone free, hasn't built anything resembling a metro, and somehow the whole thing has more warmth to it than Luxembourg's spotless trams. You learn the driver on your regular route. You learn which stop actually gets serviced on Sundays and which one is decorative. It's chaotic, but it's chaotic on a human scale.

## The Verdict

Luxembourg wins on paper, on cleanliness, on the sheer audacity of making an entire national transport system free while still charging you psychologically for the traffic jam you'll sit in anyway. Uruguay wins on honesty — it never promised you a revolution, so you're never disappointed when the 405 doesn't show. If I had to actually live and commute somewhere long-term, I'd take Montevideo's buses over Luxembourg's motorway, because at least in Montevideo nobody's pretending the system is perfect. Luxembourg gave its commuters a gift and then quietly kept the traffic jam as a souvenir. That's not generosity. That's marketing.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/Luxembourg — someone pointed out that "free" transport still means a 90-minute round trip from Metz, and asked why nobody mentions that in the tourism brochures.*

> *Internations Montevideo — an expat vented that she waited 40 minutes for a bus that supposedly ran every 10, then three showed up together, and the driver of the third one just shrugged.*

> *expat.com Uruguay — a longtime resident said the STM card system logged her out of credit twice in one month with zero explanation, and customer service simply told her to "try again."*

## Conclusion

Both countries have decided, in their own way, that the perfect commute doesn't exist, so why bother pretending. Luxembourg spent a fortune making the pretence disappear and left the traffic behind as evidence of the lie. Uruguay never spent the fortune and never told the lie. If you want efficiency dressed up as generosity, book your flat near a Luxembourg tram stop and enjoy the free ride to a jammed motorway on-ramp. If you want a system that's honest about its own limitations, get comfortable with Montevideo's bus convoys and the quiet dignity of a driver who knows your face. Either way, budget more time than Google Maps tells you. It always lies. Just less politely in Luxembourg.
