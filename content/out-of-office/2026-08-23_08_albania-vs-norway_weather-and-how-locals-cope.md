# One Country Melts for Three Months, the Other Loses the Sun Entirely and Calls It Cosy

🇦🇱 Albania vs 🇳🇴 Norway
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Weather is the one topic every culture insists is small talk, and every culture is lying, because how a place handles its worst season tells you more about its character than its cuisine ever will. Albania spends its summer somewhere between 34 and 40 degrees Celsius, coastal humidity included, and simply adjusts its entire daily rhythm around not dying of heatstroke. Norway spends its winter losing the sun outright — actual months of polar night in the north — and has built a whole philosophy of contentment specifically to avoid going mad about it.

I have sweated through an Albanian August and shivered through a Norwegian January within the same twelve-month stretch, and I can report with confidence that both nations think the other's weather sounds like a personal insult.

## Do's & Don'ts: Albania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Adjust your schedule around the worst midday heat, like everyone else does | Plan strenuous activity for 1pm to 5pm in July or August — nobody sensible does |
| Head inland or to the mountains for genuine relief from coastal humidity | Assume a coastal sea breeze will be enough to make peak heat comfortable |
| Expect sudden, dramatic thunderstorms to break a heatwave within a day | Panic when a 40°C day is followed by a ten-degree overnight drop — it's normal here |
| Pack for genuinely hot, dry, sun-heavy conditions from June through August | Underestimate coastal humidity just because inland temperatures read lower |

## Do's & Don'ts: Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get outside daily regardless of darkness or cold — it's central to local wellbeing | Treat winter as a season to hibernate through indoors |
| Invest in genuinely good cold-weather clothing — locals mean it when they say there's no bad weather | Complain about the cold without first checking whether you're actually dressed for it |
| Embrace small daily rituals — a walk, a ski trip, coffee outside — through the dark months | Expect the far north's winter darkness to feel the same as the milder south's |
| Join local winter festivals, especially in cities like Tromsø that lean into the dark | Assume the polar night is uniformly depressing — many locals describe genuinely liking parts of it |

## Albania: A Country That Simply Reorganises the Day Around the Sun's Worst Behaviour

Albania's Mediterranean climate delivers exactly the postcard summer you'd expect — hot, dry, relentlessly sunny — right up until you're standing in it at 2pm in Durrës with coastal humidity turning every step into a small negotiation with your own body. Average summer temperatures along the coast run between 25°C and 35°C, and heatwaves push that comfortably past 40°C, with the country logging well over 180 hours of sunshine in a typical summer month. That is, to be clear, a genuinely brutal amount of sun exposure, and Albanians have responded the way most Mediterranean cultures eventually do: by simply refusing to fight it head-on.

The midday hours get quietly abandoned. Shops shift their rhythm, outdoor activity clusters at the edges of the day, and anyone with the option retreats either toward the coast for what breeze there is, or inland and upward, toward the mountains, where the temperature drop is real and immediate rather than psychological. What nobody quite prepares you for is the volatility underneath the heat — Albanian summer weather isn't a flat, predictable scorch; heatwaves break suddenly and dramatically, often via heavy rain and thunderstorms violent enough to drop the daily temperature by ten degrees or more within hours, turning an unbearable afternoon into a genuinely pleasant evening almost without warning.

This volatility shapes the coping mechanism as much as the heat itself does. Locals don't just endure the temperature; they read it, anticipate the swing, and plan around a climate that punishes you for assuming today's forecast tells you anything reliable about tonight's. It's less resignation than fluency — an entire population that has learned to live inside a climate that changes its mind constantly, and simply changes its own plans right alongside it.

## Norway: A Country That Turned Losing the Sun Into a National Personality Trait

Norway's winter problem isn't cold, exactly — plenty of countries get cold — it's darkness, and specifically the sheer scale of it. In the far north, the sun doesn't rise at all for a genuine stretch of the polar night, while even the milder south loses the bulk of its daylight hours for months at a time. This is not a minor seasonal inconvenience; it's a structural absence that would, in most cultures, be treated as something to survive rather than something to embrace.

Norway chose embrace, and the concept has a name: friluftsliv, "open-air life," a cultural instinct that treats being outdoors, year-round, including in the depths of winter darkness, as simply non-negotiable rather than optional. This isn't extreme wilderness expeditions as a niche hobby — it's a lunchtime walk, a weekend ski trip to a mountain cabin, or standing outside for a coffee in the snow, folded into ordinary daily life rather than treated as a seasonal event requiring special motivation. The oft-repeated national refrain — "there's no bad weather, only bad clothing" — isn't a throwaway platitude; it's a genuinely load-bearing piece of the culture's psychological infrastructure.

What surprised researchers studying wintertime mindset in northern Norway, and what surprised me when I actually asked locals directly rather than assuming, is how many describe liking specific elements of the dark season rather than merely tolerating it — the quality of soft winter light, the particular silence after snowfall, a slower, more deliberate pace of life the darkness seems to permit. Cities like Tromsø lean into this rather than fighting it, staging winter festivals and lighting up entire streets specifically to make the darkest months a season worth looking forward to, not just surviving. It is, frankly, one of the more impressive collective psychological achievements I've witnessed on this beat — an entire nation deciding, apparently by consensus, that the thing that should be depressing them simply isn't going to.

## The Verdict

Albania copes with its climate through practical rescheduling — a sensible, almost administrative adjustment to a predictable seasonal extreme. Norway copes with its climate through a full philosophical reframe, convincing itself, largely successfully, that the absence of the sun is an aesthetic experience rather than a deprivation. One is logistics. The other is closer to alchemy.

Norway wins, and it isn't close. Rescheduling your day around the heat is common sense. Convincing an entire nation to genuinely enjoy months without daylight is closer to cultural sorcery, and I say that with real, if slightly begrudging, admiration.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — "How do Albanians deal with the summer heat without much air conditioning?" — paraphrased: several answers note that AC is far less universal than visitors expect, and that thick-walled older buildings, combined with a shifted daily schedule, do more of the practical work than appliances.*

## Conclusion

Both countries face down a climate that would break plenty of other cultures' spirit entirely, and both have found their own answer — one procedural, one philosophical. Albania simply moves its life around the sun's worst hours and lets the volatility sort itself out. Norway has decided, with startling collective discipline, to like the dark. Pack accordingly for either, and don't, under any circumstances, tell a Norwegian the weather is bad. They will correct you, cheerfully, and they will be right to.
