# Guatemala Will Hand You a Language Map With Twenty-Four Branches and Watch You Panic. Botswana Will Just Speak Perfect English and Let You Feel Briefly, Falsely, Relaxed.

🇬🇹 Guatemala 🇧🇼 Botswana
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I arrived in Guatemala fairly confident in my Spanish and left, several months later, having learned that "Spanish" was doing an enormous amount of quiet, misleading work in that sentence. Depending on which highland town you're standing in, the actual first language on the street might be K'iche', Q'eqchi', Mam, Kaqchikel, or one of nineteen other Mayan languages, and Spanish itself might be a distant, formally-taught second tongue spoken with real reluctance. Botswana, by contrast, greeted me in flawless English within minutes of landing — official language, taught from primary school, spoken fluently by essentially everyone in Gaborone — and I spent the first week feeling smug about how easy this was going to be, right up until I realised the actual social and emotional life of the country was happening in Setswana, around me, politely, in a language I wasn't remotely equipped to follow.

Both countries taught me the same lesson from opposite directions: official language policy and lived linguistic reality are two entirely different maps, and only one of them is printed on any government brochure.

## Guatemala: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn at least basic greetings in the Mayan language spoken locally, not just Spanish | Assume your capital-city Spanish will translate smoothly to a rural highland market |
| Ask before photographing traditional dress (traje) — it carries deep community and linguistic identity | Treat Indigenous languages as "dialects" of Spanish; they are entirely distinct language families |
| Use simple, patient Spanish in rural areas — many speakers use it as a functional second language | Assume everyone in a tourist town speaks fluent English just because the menu is translated |

## Botswana: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Setswana greetings (Dumela, "hello") even though English will get you everywhere formally | Mistake fluent English for full inclusion in every conversation — plenty happens in Setswana around you |
| Use English confidently in business, government, and most urban service settings | Assume Setswana is the only other language present — Kalanga, Herero, and San languages exist too |
| Notice the code-switching — locals move between English and Setswana constantly, often mid-sentence | Interrupt a Setswana conversation assuming it will smoothly switch to English on your account |

## Guatemala: A Country Where "Spanish" Is Only Ever Part of the Answer

Guatemala's linguistic landscape is one of the most complex in the Americas — the government officially recognises twenty-three Mayan languages alongside Spanish, Xinka, and Garífuna, and in large stretches of the western highlands, Spanish is genuinely a second language, taught in schools but not the language of home, market, or prayer. Travel through the Ixil region and you'll hear Ixil. Head into the Q'eqchi'-speaking areas around Cobán and your careful, classroom Spanish will occasionally be met with the same patient, functional Spanish spoken back to you by someone who is also translating, mentally, from their actual first language, and the gap between you closes not because either of you is fluent but because you're both doing effortful work to meet in the middle.

This creates a specific and humbling texture to expat life that's different from the usual "learn the local language" advice — because there often isn't one single local language to learn, there's a hierarchy of them, geographically distributed, some more institutionally supported than others, and a foreigner who assumes competent Spanish equals cultural fluency in rural Guatemala is making a category error. The flip side is a real, ongoing cultural pride: Indigenous language revitalisation is an active, visible movement, from bilingual education programmes to Mayan-language radio, and foreigners who show even modest respect for that — learning a K'iche' greeting rather than defaulting to loud, slow Spanish — tend to get warmth back disproportionate to the tiny effort involved.

## Botswana: Fluent English as the Formal Layer Over an Entirely Separate Social World

Botswana's relationship with English is almost the structural inverse of Guatemala's relationship with Spanish. English is the official language of government, courts, and most higher education, spoken with genuine fluency by the vast majority of the urban population, which makes Gaborone one of the easiest African capitals for an Anglophone expat to operate in on paper — contracts, banking, hospital visits, all conducted smoothly in English with barely a hitch. Setswana, meanwhile, is the national language and the actual first language of daily life for most Batswana, spoken at home, among friends, in markets, and — this is the part newcomers consistently underestimate — woven constantly into English conversation through fluid, unconscious code-switching that can leave an English-only speaker suddenly, politely, lost mid-sentence.

The result is a strange kind of illusion of ease. You can function entirely in English in Botswana and never technically hit a wall — but you'll also never be fully inside the actual social current of the place, which moves in and out of Setswana constantly, especially once formality drops and people are genuinely relaxed with each other. Learning even basic Setswana — the greeting rituals alone are elaborate and genuinely important, Batswana take a proper "Dumela, o kae?" exchange seriously even in brief interactions — signals something English fluency alone can't: that you're trying to meet the culture on its own terms rather than assuming the accommodation should only run one way.

## The Verdict

Guatemala confronts you with the language barrier immediately and honestly — you know from day one you're navigating something layered and complex, and the effort required is visible. Botswana disguises the barrier behind fluent, welcoming English, which is generous but also, subtly, a trap for the complacent — you can go months believing you've integrated when you've actually just been operating in the formal, outer layer the whole time. I'd rather be honestly lost in Guatemala than comfortably, invisibly excluded in Gaborone. Guatemala wins on candour. Botswana wins on convenience, and loses a little on how easy that convenience makes it to never actually try.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/guatemala — paraphrased: spent a year confident in conversational Spanish, moved to a highland town for work, realized within a week that half the market transactions happening around were entirely in K'iche' and Spanish was being used specifically, and only, for outsiders.*

> *Internations Gaborone — paraphrased: worked in an office for two years assuming full inclusion because every meeting was in English, only later realized the real decisions and relationship-building happened in Setswana asides before and after the formal meeting.*

> *Quora — paraphrased response on living in Guatemala without fluent Spanish: you can survive in Antigua or Guatemala City on English and tourist Spanish, but "survive" and "actually live there" turned out to be very different verbs.*

## Conclusion

Somewhere between a Q'eqchi' greeting you'll mispronounce for months and a Setswana joke you'll only catch the tail end of, you'll learn that fluency and belonging are not the same achievement. Guatemala makes you earn the language, layer by layer, and rewards the earning generously. Botswana hands you English at the door and quietly dares you to notice everything happening past it. Learn a greeting in both places, properly, and watch how much faster the actual door opens — because in neither country does anyone owe you access just because you can technically be understood.
