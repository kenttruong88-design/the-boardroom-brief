# Nobody Warned Denmark About Rain, and Nobody Warned You About the Harmattan

🇨🇮 Ivory Coast vs 🇩🇰 Denmark
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Everyone packing for Abidjan asks about malaria pills and nobody asks about the weather, which is a mistake, because the weather in Ivory Coast will restructure your personality before the mosquitoes get a chance. Everyone packing for Copenhagen assumes Scandinavia means postcard snow and Danish hygge candles, and then discovers that Denmark's actual climate is a grey, horizontal drizzle that never quite commits to being a storm, delivered at a temperature best described as "personally insulting."

Both places have built entire cultures around coping with weather that outsiders underestimate. Ivorians have made peace with heat and humidity so total it feels like breathing through a hot flannel; Danes have engineered an entire national psychology, several government subsidy schemes, and a design industry around the fact that the sun sets at 3:47pm in December and nobody can do a thing about it. One country copes by slowing down. The other copes by turning on very expensive lighting and insisting, through gritted teeth, that this is fine.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇨🇮 Ivory Coast

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Schedule anything outdoorsy for before 10am or after 4pm | Book a walking tour at high noon in March and act surprised when you nearly faint |
| Carry a second shirt during harmattan-to-rainy-season transitions | Wear your one good linen shirt and expect it to survive Abidjan's humidity past breakfast |
| Respect the midday siesta rhythm many businesses quietly keep | Assume every office runs on a rigid 9-to-5 regardless of the heat index |
| Stock up on lotion and lip balm during harmattan (Dec–Feb) | Ignore the dry, dusty harmattan winds because "Africa is hot, right?" |

### 🇩🇰 Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy proper waterproof outerwear, not a "fashion" raincoat | Trust a Danish weather forecast further than the next two hours |
| Lean into hygge — candles, blankets, indoor rituals — from October on | Schedule your entire trip around "seeing the sun" in November |
| Use the bike lanes; locals cycle through actual sleet without complaint | Assume Danish winter is all snow; mostly it's relentless grey wet cold |
| Get a lamp with a daylight bulb if you're staying through winter | Underestimate seasonal affective disorder — Danes take vitamin D seriously for a reason |

## Ivory Coast: Heat as a Way of Life

Let's dispense with the fantasy immediately: Ivory Coast does not have "seasons" in the way a Copenhagen calendar understands the word. It has a wet season and a dry season, and the dry season further splits into "regular hot" and "harmattan," which is when Saharan dust rolls down from the north and turns the sky the colour of a dirty teacup, coats every surface in fine grit, and cracks the lips of anyone who forgot to pack lip balm. Temperatures in Abidjan hover in the high 20s to low 30s Celsius essentially year-round, but it's the humidity — regularly above 80% — that does the real damage. This isn't heat you tolerate. It's heat you negotiate with.

Locals negotiate brilliantly, mostly by refusing to fight it. The day's rhythm bends around the sun: markets are loudest at dawn, hard physical work happens early, and the early afternoon quiet that visitors mistake for laziness is actually survival intelligence. Ceiling fans outnumber air conditioners outside wealthier districts, and vernacular architecture — high ceilings, shaded verandas, walls angled to catch any breeze — has handled cross-ventilation for a century longer than imported AC units have existed.

Then there's the rain. In the two rainy seasons (roughly April–July and October–November), it doesn't drizzle politely — it does tropical downpour, turning a street into a river in four minutes and stopping just as fast, leaving everything steaming. Ivorians carry an attitude more than an umbrella: shelter under the nearest awning with six strangers, wait it out, don't panic. What actually catches visitors out is the harmattan haze cutting visibility and drying sinuses to the point of nosebleeds — a detail no travel blog mentions because it isn't "exotic," it's just Tuesday in January.

## Denmark: The Art of Being Fine About It

Denmark's weather is not extreme. That's precisely the problem. Nothing about a Danish winter will kill you quickly; it will simply grind you down across four months of low grey cloud, average daytime temperatures hovering around freezing, and a sun that, in December, gives up around 3:30 in the afternoon like it has somewhere better to be. Summers are mild and genuinely lovely — 20s Celsius, long light evenings that stretch past 10pm — which is exactly the cruelty of it: you get roughly ten weeks of proof that daylight exists before it's confiscated again.

The Danish response has been to build an entire cultural infrastructure around not letting the weather win. Hygge, reduced abroad to candles and cosy socks, is at root a coping mechanism for meteorological bleakness — scheduled cosiness as psychological maintenance. Public health messaging pushes vitamin D supplements from October to April as routine, not wellness fad, and SAD lamps sit on ordinary office desks, not just therapists' waiting rooms. Critically, nobody stays inside: Danes cycle to work in genuine sleet in proper technical rainwear, because waiting for good weather means never leaving the house from October to March.

Visitors consistently underestimate two things. First, how wet Danish winter actually is — it's rarely the snow-globe scene from the tourist board, more a persistent damp chill that gets into everything. Second, how much the locals have simply stopped discussing it. Complaining about weather is a foreigner's pastime; Danes just dress for it, layer up, light another candle, and get on the bike.

## The Verdict

If you want weather with drama, Ivory Coast wins outright — thunderous rains, dust storms, heat you can lean against. If you want weather that will quietly erode your will to live over a Tuesday afternoon in February, Denmark takes the trophy, gold-plated and slightly damp. But here's the sting: Ivorians have built a culture that actively enjoys its climate's rhythms, shade, siesta, storm-watching from a veranda, while an alarming number of Danes have simply agreed, as a nation, to pretend four months of darkness is charming rather than admit it's genuinely rough. Hygge is magnificent marketing for what is, at its core, collective coping. Ivory Coast copes by living with the weather. Denmark copes by aestheticising the fact that it's miserable. Both work. Only one of them is honest about it.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/expats — someone who moved to Abidjan for work says nobody mentioned the harmattan dust would get into every electronic device they owned, laptop included, within the first dry season.*

> *Internations Copenhagen — a longtime expat member notes that the hardest adjustment wasn't the cold, it was realising by January that they hadn't felt real sunlight on their face in six weeks and hadn't noticed until a visiting friend pointed it out.*

> *expat.com — a contributor living in Abidjan warns newcomers that "rainy season" undersells it completely; drainage in parts of the city can't keep up, and a commute that takes twenty minutes on a dry day can take two hours after a downpour.*

## Conclusion

Ask a Copenhagen local about the weather and they'll shrug and mention their SAD lamp like it's a kettle. Ask an Abidjan local and they'll tell you, matter-of-factly, which hour of the afternoon you're a fool for being outside in. Neither country apologises for its climate, and neither should have to; the apologising is entirely a tourist's habit, born of arriving with the wrong shoes and the wrong assumptions. Pack wellingtons and a candle habit for Denmark. Pack lip balm and a willingness to nap for Ivory Coast. And retire, permanently, the word "exotic" — there is nothing exotic about a country that has simply figured out how to live somewhere properly, on its own terms, while you stand there sweating or shivering, entirely unprepared.
