# Silence Is Free in Tripoli. In Sliema, It Costs a Fortune.

🇱🇾 Libya vs 🇲🇹 Malta

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ask anyone what they did last weekend and you learn more about their government than their personality. In Libya, the honest answer is something like: "we drove to the beach on Friday afternoon and left before it got dark, because after dark is a different country." In Malta, the honest answer is: "there were fireworks, a man dressed as a sardine, and I have no memory of Sunday." Both answers are infinitely more interesting than "brunch," and both tell you everything about how two small Mediterranean nations, forty-five minutes apart by jet, have organised the one thing every government eventually has to let its people have: time off.

Libya didn't so much design a weekend as inherit one from a dictator, lose it in a revolution, and never quite get it back in working order. Malta, by contrast, has spent decades perfecting the weekend as a product line — bottled, branded, and sold back to three million tourists a year who outnumber the population six to one. One country has no nightclubs, no pubs, no cinemas, and precisely one shopping mall worth mentioning. The other has an entire nightlife district that its own mayor reportedly tells people to avoid. Neither of these is a flaw. Both are simply what happens when leisure meets its local reality and refuses to apologise for it.

**Do's & Don'ts: Libya**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Head to the coast on Friday afternoon, like every other family in Tripoli | Don't expect a bar, a pint, or a wine list — alcohol is illegal, no exceptions |
| Bring your own chairs, your own snacks, and zero expectations of infrastructure | Don't linger past sunset; locals pack up and leave before dark for a reason |
| Treat an invitation to a family picnic as the only VIP pass that matters | Don't assume a "scene" is quietly forming somewhere — it isn't, and hasn't for years |
| Join an informal expat network — quiz nights, birding trips, walking groups — for sanity | Don't photograph checkpoints, ports, or anything with a flag on it |

**Do's & Don'ts: Malta**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Let a village festa happen to you at least once, fireworks and all | Don't book a "quiet weekend" in Paceville on purpose — it does not exist |
| Make peace with the entire island knowing your business within a fortnight | Don't expect privacy; this is a rock, not a metropolis |
| Eat Sunday lunch like it's an Olympic event, because the Maltese do | Don't assume Sunday is universally sacred — half the island is at work serving you brunch |
| Hop to Gozo if you want live music without the Paceville cover charge | Don't go to Marsaxlokk expecting an undiscovered fishing village; everyone already discovered it |

## Libya: Where the Weekend Is a Rumour With Occasional Sand

In August 2012, Libya's new government announced, with the slightly exhausted optimism of people rebuilding a state from scratch, that civil servants would now get two rest days instead of one — Friday and Saturday, not just the Friday that Gaddafi had decreed decades earlier. It was a nice gesture. Whether anyone's weekend actually doubled in substance is a different question entirely, because the single rest day Gaddafi mandated had already "stopped being complied with once he was ousted," according to reporting at the time, and institutional Libya has been improvising its schedule ever since.

What fills the time instead is refreshingly unpretentious. Expats on Tripoli forums are blunt about what's missing: no cinemas, no clubs, no pubs, no casinos, one shopping mall, and "a load of souks." One expat summarised the entire leisure landscape as liking "old ruins and lots of sand." Alcohol is flatly illegal, so the famously resourceful foreign community has taken to brewing its own beer, joining the invite-only Oasis Club (members only, "awash with alcohol," by one account), or simply watching the city's actual national pastime: young men doing wheelies and donuts down Gargaresh Road on a Friday night, which one long-term resident called his favourite form of entertainment, unironically.

Libyans themselves have it simpler and more sincere. Friday afternoon means the Tripoli coast — families at plastic tables, kids building sandcastles, jet skis and dirt bikes churning up the shoreline, and everyone gone before sunset because nobody trusts the roads after dark. A former Gaddafi-era military academy for his all-female "Amazons" bodyguard unit has, since the revolution, become exactly the kind of public park Libya actually needed: benches, a playground, an ice cream van, and — tellingly — a place where women can exercise without a chaperone, which a decade ago would have been unthinkable. "Libyans need these kinds of spaces to release the pressure of daily life," one local mayor said, and he wasn't wrong. It's not a leisure industry. It's a population teaching itself to rest in the gaps a broken state left behind.

## Malta: The Island That Monetised Its Own Patron Saints

Malta's weekend looks, on paper, like a normal European one: Saturday, Sunday, done. In practice, an island this small and this dependent on tourism never really clocks off — "many people work on Sundays, including in tourism and public transport," as even its own guides admit, which rather undercuts the postcard. What Malta has instead of a day of rest is a rolling, almost theatrical performance of leisure that starts in late May and doesn't stop until September: the village festa.

There is, more or less, a festa happening somewhere on the island every single weekend of summer — a different town each time, each one convinced its patron saint deserves the loudest fireworks. Streets get hung with coloured festoons, households repaint their facades weeks in advance, papier-mâché saints are hoisted onto wooden columns, and the whole affair detonates into aerial fireworks and petards after dark. UNESCO now recognises Maltese festas as Intangible Cultural Heritage, which is a very elegant way of saying "this country has turned Catholic devotion into the best party on the Mediterranean," and I mean that as a compliment.

The rest of the week's leisure is just as concentrated and just as loud. Nightlife is crammed almost entirely into Paceville and St Julian's — so dense and so rowdy that one expat noted even the area's own officials advise steering clear of it, while everything that isn't clubbing (dinner, live music, "actual culture") happens everywhere else. Gozo, mercifully, offers the antidote: free live music most nights, "next to no crime," and a pace that makes St Julian's feel like a fever dream. And Sunday itself? There's officially no single ritual left — church, a family lunch, the Marsaxlokk fish market, or simply going to work, take your pick. Malta didn't lose its Sunday. It just monetised every version of it simultaneously.

## The Verdict

If you want leisure that still means something — unforced, undocumented, utterly indifferent to being Instagrammed — Libya wins, and it isn't close. A Friday on a Tripoli beach, gone by dusk because the roads aren't safe after dark, is more honest than anything happening in Paceville on a Saturday night, because nobody in Tripoli is pretending it's for the tourists. There are no tourists. That's rather the point.

But "more honest" and "more enjoyable" are not the same sentence, and if you'd like your weekend to include a drink, a nightclub, or the physical option of staying out past 9pm, Malta wins by default, because it's the only one of the two offering a contest at all. Malta has simply industrialised its charm to a degree Libya's circumstances won't currently allow — fireworks on a schedule, saints on a rota, Sunday lunch as competitive sport.

So: Libya for authenticity you can't buy. Malta for a good time you absolutely can, provided you don't mind the entire island watching you have it.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com (Tripoli forum) — "Don't expect much of a social life here — no cinemas, no clubs, no pubs, just one shopping mall and a lot of sand. We've gotten good at making our own entertainment, which mostly means watching the local boy racers do wheelies and donuts on Gargaresh Road on a Friday night."*

> *Expat.com (Malta forum) — "The clubs are basically all crammed into one strip, Paceville, and even the local mayor tells people to avoid it. Everything else — dinner, live music, anything you'd actually call culture — happens everywhere else on the island, which is where you want to be."*

> *Expat.com (Malta forum) — "Remember, this is a small country. You came for the sunny, laid-back lifestyle, and suddenly that's not enough for you? Everyone will know your business within a fortnight — that's the trade-off for the view."*

## Conclusion

Here is the thing nobody tells you before you book either flight: a weekend is never really about the beach, the bar, or the fireworks. It's about what a country has decided to let its people believe they deserve. Libya, stripped of nightlife by law and of safety by circumstance, has quietly decided that a beach, a family, and a few hours before dark is enough — and built something unexpectedly tender out of very little. Malta, drowning in tourists and patron saints, has decided that enough is never enough, and turned its entire calendar into a performance with a cover charge.

Neither is wrong. But only one of them is lying to you about why it's fun. I'll let you work out which, Malta, you magnificent, overexposed little rock.
