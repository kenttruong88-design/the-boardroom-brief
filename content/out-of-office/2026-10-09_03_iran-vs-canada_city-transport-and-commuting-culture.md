# One City's Commute Is a Negotiation With Traffic. The Other's Is a Negotiation With Winter.

🇮🇷 Iran 🇨🇦 Canada
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nobody emigrates for the commute, and yet the commute is the single most reliable daily proof of whether you actually understand the place you've moved to. Tehran and the major cities of Canada have built two entirely different relationships with the daily grind of getting to work, and both will test a newcomer's patience in ways no amount of pre-reading can prepare you for.

Tehran's commute is a test of nerve — a dense, loud, gloriously chaotic negotiation between cars, motorbikes threading impossible gaps, and a metro system that is simultaneously excellent and gender-segregated in ways that confuse every first-time rider. Canada's commute is a test of endurance — long distances, long winters, and a public transit patchwork that ranges from genuinely world-class to "you'll need a car, sorry" depending entirely on which city you've landed in. One will make you a more aggressive pedestrian. The other will make you own a parka you never thought you'd need.

## Do's & Don'ts: Iran

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the women-only carriage system on the Tehran Metro immediately — it's strictly enforced and genuinely useful | Expect lanes, signals, or right-of-way to function as suggestions rather than live negotiations |
| Download a local ride-hailing app (Snapp or similar) rather than relying on street hails | Be shocked by the odd-even licence-plate traffic restrictions in central Tehran; check before you drive in |
| Treat motorbike and pedestrian traffic as part of the road, not an obstacle to it | Underestimate how bad the air quality gets on high-traffic winter days — check it before planning outdoor commutes |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether your city actually has usable transit before committing to a car-free lifestyle — it varies wildly | Assume "Canada" means one commute culture; Toronto's GO Train and Vancouver's SkyTrain have nothing in common with rural Alberta's car dependence |
| Budget real time for winter tyre changes, snow-day delays, and the occasional transit shutdown | Leave your winter boots in storage until the first snowfall actually happens; by then it's too late to find a good pair |
| Get used to genuinely long commute times in the Greater Toronto Area — they rank among the worst in North America | Expect small talk on transit; Canadians are famously, almost aggressively, polite about leaving each other alone |

## Iran: Chaos With a Surprisingly Good Metro Underneath It

The thing that catches most newcomers off guard about Tehran traffic is not that it's bad — everyone's been warned it's bad — it's that the chaos has its own internal logic once you've lived inside it for a few months. Lanes are a polite fiction. Motorbikes will use whatever space exists, including space that technically doesn't exist, and pedestrians cross multi-lane roads through moving traffic with a confidence that looks suicidal until you realise it's actually a skill, practised daily, that drivers expect and accommodate. The city's odd-even licence plate scheme restricts central-zone driving on alternating days specifically to manage this congestion and the pollution it generates, and Tehran's Air Quality Control Company regularly flags high-traffic winter days when the smog genuinely limits how much time you want to spend outdoors at all.

And yet underneath all that chaos sits a metro system that is, honestly, better than its reputation abroad suggests — extensive, cheap, heavily used, and expanding. The detail that trips up almost every foreigner is the women-only carriage, usually at the front or rear of the train: a strictly observed, legally codified separation that exists specifically to give women a harassment-free commuting option, and newcomers who don't clock this before boarding tend to find out the hard way, via a very direct correction from fellow passengers. Once you've learned the system — which carriage, which app for ride-hailing, which hours to simply avoid driving entirely — Tehran's commute stops being a horror story and starts being what it actually is: a dense, loud, remarkably functional city figuring out how to move twelve million people through geography that was never built for this many cars.

## Canada: A Beautiful Transit System, If You Happen to Live Near One

Canada's commuting culture suffers from a problem of scale that Iran's doesn't: this is an enormous country, and "Canadian commuting" genuinely means wildly different things depending on your postal code. Toronto and its GO Train network, Montreal's metro, Vancouver's SkyTrain — these are legitimate, well-run systems that can carry you comfortably car-free. Step outside those specific urban cores, though, and you're very quickly back to car dependence as the only realistic option, something that catches a lot of newcomers who assumed "developed country" automatically meant "comprehensive transit."

Even where the transit is good, it's fighting geography and climate that Tehran never has to think about. Winter doesn't just make commuting uncomfortable here — it actively restructures it: snow days genuinely close roads and delay trains, black ice turns a routine drive into a real hazard, and Toronto's own commute-time rankings are consistently among the longest in North America, a fact locals will grumble about with a specific, resigned pride that suggests they've made peace with losing ninety minutes a day to it. The social texture of the commute is its own adjustment too — Canadians famously do not make small talk on transit, maintaining a studied, polite distance that can read as coldness to anyone arriving from a more conversational commuting culture, when really it's just a very Canadian form of consideration: giving you the space to have your terrible commute in peace.

## The Verdict

I'm giving this one to Iran, somewhat to my own surprise. Canada's transit, at its best, is genuinely world-class — but "at its best" is the operative phrase, and too much of the country doesn't get it, leaving newcomers at the mercy of a car and a windshield scraper for half the year. Tehran's commute looks terrifying on paper and, in fairness, occasionally is terrifying in practice. But it's consistent: the chaos is everywhere, the metro is everywhere, the system — such as it is — applies uniformly across the city rather than depending entirely on which postal code you happened to land in. There's something almost comforting about a transport culture that's equally, predictably intense for everyone, versus one where your entire daily experience is a lottery based on municipal transit investment decisions made decades before you arrived.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Iran — a newcomer admitted accidentally boarding a women-only carriage and being redirected, firmly but kindly, within seconds, calling it "the fastest cultural lesson I've ever received."*

> *Reddit r/Toronto — a long-running thread on GO Train delays included someone calculating they'd spent the equivalent of eleven full days of their life waiting on a single platform over three years.*

> *Internations Toronto — a new arrival from a warmer climate described their first real snow-day transit shutdown as "the exact moment I understood why everyone kept asking if I'd bought real boots yet."*

## Conclusion

Commuting culture is, in the end, just a city being honest with you about its own priorities, whether it means to be or not. Tehran tells you, loudly and immediately, that this is a place negotiating scarcity — of road space, of clean air, of calm — with improvisation and a metro system doing more work than it probably should have to. Canada tells you something quieter and in some ways more unsettling: that even a wealthy, well-governed country can simply decide not to build transit everywhere, and leave millions of its own citizens to solve that problem alone, in a car, in the snow. Pick your commute. Just don't pick it based on the brochure.
