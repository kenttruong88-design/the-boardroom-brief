🇮🇷 Iran 🇦🇺 Australia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Tehran will sell you a carpet through a process involving tea, a cousin, a counter-offer theatrically rejected twice, and a final price that was, transparently, decided within the first ninety seconds — the rest is ceremony, and the ceremony is the point. Australia will sell you the same carpet for a number printed on a tag, scanned by a bored teenager, with the only negotiation available being whether you want the extended warranty, which you don't, and which they will ask about anyway, twice.

I have shopped for the same category of object in a thousand-year-old bazaar and a beige suburban Westfield, and the experience could not be more different if one of them were conducted entirely underwater. Iran treats shopping as a relationship and a performance. Australia treats it as a logistics problem that's already been solved, thank you, please move along, there's a queue.

## Do's & Don'ts: Iran

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bargain at the bazaar — it's expected, respected, and often genuinely enjoyed by both sides | Pay the first price quoted at a traditional bazaar stall; it marks you as someone to be gently fleeced |
| Accept tea or small hospitality offered mid-negotiation; declining can read as cold | Rush the process; a good negotiation is unhurried and almost performative by design |
| Visit Tehran's newer malls (like Palladium or Iran Mall) for fixed-price, Western-style retail | Expect bazaar rules to apply in a modern shopping mall; prices there are generally fixed, same as anywhere else |
| Carry cash for smaller bazaar vendors; card acceptance is inconsistent outside modern retail | Assume international cards will work broadly; sanctions-related banking restrictions complicate this significantly |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Trust the price tag; it's the actual price, full stop, no theatre required | Attempt to haggle at a chain retailer; staff genuinely won't know how to respond and may just look alarmed |
| Use click-and-collect and online ordering freely; retail is heavily digitised | Expect late-night shopping everywhere; trading hours still vary significantly by state and are more limited than many expats assume |
| Check return policies carefully; Australian consumer law gives strong statutory guarantees | Assume "no returns" signs override your legal rights; statutory consumer guarantees often apply regardless |
| Expect large suburban shopping centres to function as genuine social hubs, not just retail | Dismiss the mall as purely commercial; in car-dependent suburbs, it's often the de facto town square |

## Iran: the bazaar as negotiated theatre

Shopping in Iran, especially in Tehran's Grand Bazaar or any provincial equivalent, is built on *chaane zani* — haggling — a practice so embedded that skipping it can actually offend a vendor, who may feel cheated of the social ritual rather than pleased by a quick sale. The opening price is understood by both parties to be a starting position, not a figure, and the negotiation that follows — often accompanied by tea, questions about your family, and a vendor's theatrical protestations that he's practically losing money on this — is genuinely enjoyed as a social exchange, not merely tolerated as a cost of doing business.

Iran's retail landscape has also been rapidly bifurcating: alongside the centuries-old bazaar tradition, a wave of large, glossy shopping malls — Iran Mall in Tehran among the largest in the world by some measures — has emerged, serving a younger, urban demographic that treats mall-going as both retail and recreation, closer in spirit to a Gulf shopping-mall culture than to the bazaar. These malls run on fixed prices, international-style retail norms, and a noticeably different social code; haggling there gets you nothing but a confused shop assistant.

International sanctions complicate the practical side considerably — foreign cards are largely unusable, international retailers are scarce, and cash remains dominant for a huge share of daily transactions, which makes Iran's shopping culture feel, to a newly arrived expat, like two different economies operating in the same city: one ancient and relational, one modern and sanctioned into a strange, cash-heavy isolation.

## Australia: the mall as suburb-in-a-box

Australian shopping culture is organised almost entirely around fixed pricing, consumer protection law, and the suburban shopping centre as a social and logistical anchor point. Haggling essentially doesn't exist outside of markets, car dealerships, and the odd garage sale — try it at a department store and you'll get a polite, slightly bemused "sorry, that's just the price," which is both the final word and, to an Iranian newcomer, a genuinely strange absence of ritual.

Australia's car-dependent suburban geography has turned large shopping centres (Westfield, DFO, and dozens of regional equivalents) into something closer to civic infrastructure than pure retail — they house medical clinics, cinemas, food courts, and libraries alongside shops, and in many outer-suburban areas they function as the only real indoor public gathering space for kilometres. This is less a cultural choice than an urban-planning consequence, but it shapes shopping behaviour profoundly: a trip to "the shops" is often a half-day social outing involving multiple generations of a family, not a quick errand.

Australian consumer law is also unusually robust and worth knowing explicitly — statutory consumer guarantees under the Australian Consumer Law often override a retailer's posted "no refunds" signage, something many newcomers (and plenty of long-term residents) don't realise they can actually invoke.

Gold shopping deserves a category of its own in Iran, because it isn't really "shopping" in the retail sense at all — it's investment, inheritance planning, and inflation hedging, conducted at a jeweller's counter. With the rial's value eroded by decades of sanctions and inflation, many Iranians treat gold coins and jewellery as a far more trustworthy store of value than cash savings, and a visit to Tehran's gold bazaar is as much a financial consultation as a purchase, complete with real-time price checks against the day's gold rate and negotiation over purity and weight rather than any fixed "price." Australia's closest cultural equivalent — the frenzied Boxing Day sales, where shoppers queue overnight for discounted electronics and homewares — is a completely different kind of ritual: pure consumer theatre around getting a bargain on something you wanted anyway, with zero thought given to it as a store of value. An Iranian newcomer watching footage of Australians trampling each other for a discounted television can be forgiven for finding the whole spectacle a little bewildering, given what shopping has had to mean, financially, back home.

## The Verdict

Iran wins on texture — nowhere else will buying a rug involve this much tea, theatre, and genuine human connection, and there's a real loss in trading that for a bar-coded sticker. Australia wins on certainty — you will never once wonder if you paid more than the person behind you, because you both paid the same printed number, and the law has your back if something goes wrong. If you like shopping as performance, Iran is unmatched. If you like shopping as a solved problem you never have to think about again, Australia will never once make you negotiate for anything, which some days is its own kind of relief.

## Conclusion

What's really being traded in each system isn't just goods — it's time and relationship, priced differently. Iran's bazaar spends time generously to build a connection around a transaction; Australia's retail model spends almost none of it, trusting the law and the price tag to do the work instead. Neither approach is wrong, but they produce fundamentally different relationships to the simple act of buying something. Go to Tehran expecting Australian efficiency and you'll feel impatient and rude. Go to Sydney expecting Tehran's warmth and you'll feel, politely, like you're the only one who showed up for a conversation nobody else agreed to have.
