# Six-Pack Diplomacy: One Nation Prays Before It Lifts, the Other Gets Reimbursed For It

🇵🇰 Pakistan 🇸🇪 Sweden

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country has a relationship with its own body, and most of them are toxic in ways that are at least interesting. Pakistan's is a slow-motion awakening happening behind frosted glass and curtained windows, somewhere between a treadmill and a family argument. Sweden's is a spreadsheet. A government-approved, receipt-required, tax-capped spreadsheet, in which the state has decided that your quadriceps are a line item worth subsidising, provided you don't try to expense a pair of running shoes.

I have lived in both flavours of fitness culture, and I have drawn exactly one conclusion: neither nation has the faintest idea how smug it looks to the other. Pakistan thinks Sweden's gym culture sounds suspiciously bureaucratic for something that's supposed to be about sweat. Sweden thinks Pakistan's is obviously about something other than cardio. They're both right, and they're both missing the much better story underneath.

## Do's & Don'ts: Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask whether a gym is ladies-only before assuming a mixed one will feel comfortable | Assume "mixed gender" means "co-ed energy" — it rarely does in practice |
| Expect your trainer to have opinions about your diet, your marriage timeline, and your cardio form, in that order | Expect a quick fix — trainers here are now actively warning clients off crash diets, not selling them |
| Try an embassy or hotel gym (the Serena in Islamabad, for instance) if you want reliable equipment and a pool | Expect small-town or rural facilities to match what you find in Lahore or Karachi |
| Support the women-only studios springing up in smaller cities — they're doing genuinely disruptive work | Treat a 60-member waiting list at a local ladies' gym as a quirky anecdote instead of a real cultural shift |

## Do's & Don'ts: Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask HR about your friskvårdsbidrag on day one — it's free money you're otherwise leaving on the table | Expect it to cover running shoes, physiotherapy, or a spa day — the Tax Agency has opinions about "beauty care" |
| Embrace the outdoor gyms — they're free, scattered through every park, and used earnestly, year-round | Mistake the famous Nordic reserve for coldness at the gym — it's just a very committed form of minding your business |
| Keep every single receipt for your wellness spend | Be shocked by casual nudity in saunas and changing rooms — it is normal, unsexual, and non-negotiable |
| Try Fitness 24/7 if you want a no-frills, 24-hour membership that won't wreck your budget | Expect gym small talk — Swedes go to train, not to network |

## Pakistan: The Quiet Revolution Behind the Curtain

Pakistan's relationship with the gym used to be simple: men went, women didn't, and anyone suggesting otherwise was treated like they'd proposed something scandalous involving the neighbour's rooftop. That's changing, and it's changing fast enough that the old narrative about Pakistan being "behind" on fitness culture is already out of date, even if the infrastructure hasn't quite caught up with the appetite.

Islamabad alone had roughly 225 gyms by early 2026, a jump of more than seven percent since 2023, and the growth isn't coming from men adding a second weekly session. It's coming from women. Farzana Amin's Ladies Fitness Club in Shaheen Town went from a steady 10 to 15 regulars to around 60 to 65 members within a few months, not because of a marketing campaign but because women finally had a room where nobody was watching them exist. That's the real engine here: segregation, so often framed by outsiders as a restriction, is functioning as the enabling condition. Remove the male gaze from the equation and suddenly aunties, students, and working professionals who'd never have set foot in a mixed facility are deadlifting.

Of course, it's not uncomplicated. Social media has imported the same unrealistic body standards that plague every other fitness culture on earth, and Pakistani trainers are now fighting a very specific, very local battle against the "lose 10 kilos before the wedding" industrial complex. Nutritionists are publicly debunking gym claims and warning against sub-1,200-calorie diets, which tells you two things simultaneously: the demand for rapid transformation is intense, and the professional class pushing back against it is, encouragingly, winning column space. Meanwhile, half of Pakistani women are classified as overweight by WHO's regional office, while others face outright nutritional deficiency — a contradiction that makes "fitness culture" here less a lifestyle trend than a public health reckoning that happens to have good lighting on Instagram. Startups have noticed: AimFit, a female-focused fitness platform, raised a million dollars specifically to serve women who wanted structured training without the social cost. Capital follows demand, and the demand, unmistakably, is to be left alone to sweat in peace. None of this is confined to the big cities, either — the same appetite is showing up in semi-urban towns, which is the detail that should actually make you sit up, because it means this isn't a Lahori or Islamabadi elite fad. It's a national mood shift, arriving on foot, in a borrowed car, or on the back of a motorbike, one newly confident woman at a time.

## Sweden: Government-Subsidised Smugness

Sweden did not stumble into its fitness culture; it legislated an adjacent corner of it. The friskvårdsbidrag — a wellness allowance employers can offer tax-free up to 5,000 kronor a year — isn't a perk so much as a cultural confession: Sweden has decided, at a policy level, that your personal fitness is partly the state's business, provided you keep your receipts and don't try to expense a chiropractor. The approved list reads like a Scandinavian fever dream — skiing, climbing, sauna, cold plunges, golf, meditation — while physiotherapy, spa days, and running shoes are explicitly excluded, because nothing says "wellness bureaucracy" like a government deciding a sauna counts as medicine but new trainers don't.

What this produces, in practice, is a population that treats movement as background noise rather than achievement. Outdoor gyms — free, weatherproofed, scattered through every park — see heavy use from spring through autumn, and plenty of people simply switch to an indoor membership for the coldest months and switch back out again, treating the whole arrangement as seasonal logistics rather than a commitment they need to announce on social media. Chains like Fitness 24/7 and Nordic Wellness have made memberships genuinely accessible, though newcomers from cheaper economies still find Swedish gym prices a shock relative to what they're used to.

The culture itself is almost aggressively unperformative. Saunas and changing rooms involve matter-of-fact nudity that unsettles exactly nobody who's lived there more than a month. Nobody makes eye contact, nobody offers unsolicited advice, and the silence is not hostility — it's just the national operating system. The irony is that a country this administratively obsessed with fitness has produced a gym floor that feels almost monastic. You lift, you leave, the receipt goes in a folder, and somewhere a tax form quietly thanks you for your cardiovascular health. Newcomers from cheaper economies will gripe, correctly, about the price tags, right up until the moment they realise the allowance exists precisely to absorb that complaint — Sweden isn't apologising for the cost, it's reimbursing it, which is a very Swedish way of making a grievance disappear without ever actually addressing it.

## The Verdict

If you want fitness as liberation — a genuinely new social space carved out against real resistance, with all the mess and momentum that implies — Pakistan is doing the more interesting thing right now. A gym membership in Shaheen Town is a small act of rebellion with a waiting list. That's not a culture catching up to the world; that's a culture having its own reckoning on its own terms, which is considerably more compelling than anything happening on a treadmill in Stockholm.

But if you want fitness that simply works, unglamorously, forever, without needing a cultural revolution to sustain it — Sweden wins by default, the way bureaucracies always eventually win. It has made movement structurally unavoidable: free outdoor gyms, subsidised memberships, saunas as civic furniture. Nobody's inspired. Nobody needs to be. The system just runs.

So: Pakistan for the story, Sweden for the outcome. Suki's actual winner is whichever one stops asking me to admire its "gym culture" and just lets me lift in silence — which, infuriatingly, is Sweden, smug tax forms and all.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *TalesMag, Real Post Report (Islamabad) — paraphrased: the embassy gym gets the job done for day-to-day training, but plenty of diplomatic staff quietly spring for a Serena Hotel day pass when they want a proper pool and sauna experience, treating it less as exercise than as a mini escape from the compound.*

> *Watching the Swedes (expat blog) — paraphrased: a foreigner expecting hushed, orderly Scandinavian gym etiquette can be blindsided by the opposite — loud families, an undressed "Mr Svensson" working the step machine in swim trunks, and a toddler crawling across the floor while nobody else in the room seems to register it as unusual.*

## Conclusion

Here's the thing nobody tells you before you relocate for either of these cultures: the gym was never really about the gym. In Pakistan, it's about who gets to take up space, and the answer is finally, slowly, expanding. In Sweden, it's about a state so committed to your wellbeing that it has turned sweating into a reimbursable expense, which is either deeply caring or deeply unsettling depending on how you feel about governments knowing your sauna schedule. Neither country needs your astonishment, and both have stopped waiting for it. Pack modest activewear for one, a folder for receipts in the other, and leave your assumptions at whichever border you're least attached to.
