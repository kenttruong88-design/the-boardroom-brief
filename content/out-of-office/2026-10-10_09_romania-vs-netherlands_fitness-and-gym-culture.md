# One Country Built A Gym Culture To Rival Anywhere On Earth. The Other Just Never Stopped Cycling To Begin With.

🇷🇴 Romania 🇳🇱 Netherlands
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Fitness, as a cultural category, reveals a strange truth once you've lived in enough places: some countries treat it as a deliberate, structured pursuit requiring a gym membership, a program, and visible effort. Others have simply built exercise into the architecture of daily life so thoroughly that nobody there would even describe cycling to the shops as "fitness" at all.

Romania, and Bucharest in particular, has developed one of the more intense, aesthetics-forward gym cultures I've encountered in Europe — serious equipment, serious physiques, a genuine subculture around personal training and bodybuilding aesthetics. The Netherlands, meanwhile, has largely opted out of the gym-as-destination model altogether, because when your entire country is flat, bike-laned, and organised around cycling as basic transport, going to a separate building to deliberately move your body starts to look slightly redundant.

## Do's & Don'ts: Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect gyms in Bucharest and Cluj to be serious, well-equipped, and busy at all hours | Assume a casual, low-effort gym culture — the aesthetic bar is set genuinely high |
| Consider a personal trainer early; it's common, relatively affordable, and widely used | Hog equipment without awareness — etiquette around supersets and machine-sharing is real |
| Try a local strength-sport or bodybuilding gym at least once for the full subculture experience | Show up in gear that signals you're not taking the environment seriously; it's noticed |
| Learn basic gym vocabulary in Romanian — not every trainer or front desk operates in English | Expect off-peak hours to be quiet; dedicated lifters train on a schedule, not a whim |

## Do's & Don'ts: Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a bike almost immediately — it is genuinely the default mode of transport, not a lifestyle choice | Assume a car-first commuting habit will survive contact with Dutch urban planning |
| Treat cycling itself as your baseline fitness, rather than searching for a gym to replace it | Expect gym culture to carry the same social weight it does in more gym-centric countries |
| Learn basic cycling etiquette and hand signals fast — locals cycle at real commuting speed | Wobble, stop suddenly, or cycle against the flow in a dedicated bike lane; it causes real anger |
| Explore sport clubs (verenigingen) if you want structured fitness — they're a major social outlet | Underestimate headwinds and rain as genuine obstacles; gear up properly, year-round |

## Romania: Gym As Serious Craft, Not Casual Habit

Bucharest's gym scene surprised me more than almost anywhere else I've trained, precisely because the stereotype of post-communist Eastern Europe as fitness-indifferent is badly out of date. The National Institute of Statistics' own sport-participation data shows steadily rising gym and structured-exercise engagement over the past decade, and walking into any serious Bucharest gym confirms the number instantly — packed floors, genuinely advanced lifters, an aesthetic culture that treats visible physical development as a point of real personal pride rather than a slightly embarrassing vanity project.

Personal training is far more normalised and far more affordable, relative to local income, than in most Western European markets, and the Romanian Ministry of Sport's own facility-regulation framework has supported a genuine proliferation of well-equipped private gyms across Bucharest and Cluj in particular. What this produces, on the ground, is a gym culture with real depth — specialised strength gyms, a visible bodybuilding subculture, trainers who take client programming seriously rather than treating it as a basic upsell.

The etiquette bar reflects the seriousness. Equipment-sharing, rest-period awareness, and general gym floor conduct are taken genuinely seriously by regulars, and showing up with a casual, half-committed attitude toward the space reads, subtly but unmistakably, as out of step with everyone else in the room. It took me a few sessions to adjust from a more relaxed Western gym culture to Bucharest's noticeably higher collective intensity, and once adjusted, it became one of the more motivating gym environments I've trained in anywhere.

## Netherlands: Fitness Built Into The Infrastructure, Not Bolted On Afterward

The Dutch relationship with physical activity operates on an entirely different premise: that deliberate, gym-based fitness is largely unnecessary when the national infrastructure has already built movement into every single day. CBS's own daily-activity data confirms what a week of living in any Dutch city makes obvious within hours — cycling isn't exercise, framed that way, to most Dutch people. It's just how you get to work, and the fact that it happens to involve twenty minutes of genuine cardiovascular effort, several times a day, every day, rarely registers as "fitness" at all.

The Dutch Ministry of Health's own national exercise-guideline reporting reflects this split clearly: a population that, on paper, meets recommended activity levels substantially through incidental movement — cycling, walking, standing — rather than through structured gym attendance at anything close to the rate seen in Romania or, for that matter, most of Southern and Eastern Europe. Gyms exist, certainly, and plenty of Dutch people use them, but the cultural weight and identity wrapped up in gym attendance elsewhere simply isn't replicated here in the same way.

What gyms lose out on, sport clubs — verenigingen — more than make up for. Structured amateur sport, from football to field hockey to rowing, carries enormous social weight in Dutch life, often functioning as a primary social network rather than a side activity, in a way that reframes "fitness" yet again as something closer to community infrastructure than individual self-improvement. The cycling itself, meanwhile, demands its own real competence: Dutch commuter cyclists move at a pace and a confidence that catches newcomers off guard completely, and the etiquette — hand signals, lane discipline, not wobbling in front of someone doing forty kilometres an hour on their way to a meeting — is a genuinely steep, occasionally humiliating learning curve.

## The Verdict

Romania has built a deliberate, structured, aesthetically serious gym culture that rewards real commitment and real craft. The Netherlands has simply made exercise unavoidable by building an entire country around the bicycle, sidestepping the gym question almost entirely for a huge share of the population. If you want a gym culture that will push you toward genuine physical development with real community around it, Romania delivers something excellent. If you want fitness that requires zero willpower because it's simply how you get a coffee, the Dutch model wins on sheer elegance. I'm handing this one to the Netherlands — any system that makes fitness a byproduct of just living your life, rather than a chore you have to motivate yourself into, has solved something the rest of us are still working on.

## Conclusion

Neither country set out to win a "best fitness culture" contest, and that's precisely what makes both approaches so instructive. Romania decided fitness deserved dedicated craft, serious spaces, and real structured effort. The Netherlands decided fitness didn't need a separate category at all if the rest of daily life was designed properly around it. Arrive in either expecting the other's model, and you'll either wonder why nobody at your new Bucharest gym is taking it casually, or wonder, in Amsterdam, why nobody seems to go to the gym at all. Both have quietly figured something out. They just figured out opposite things.
