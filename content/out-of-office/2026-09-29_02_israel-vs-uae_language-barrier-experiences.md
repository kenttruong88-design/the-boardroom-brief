# In Tel Aviv You'll Be Shouted At in Hebrew. In Dubai You'll Never Need Arabic — and That's the Problem.

🇮🇱 Israel vs 🇦🇪 UAE

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Language barriers come in two flavours. There is the kind that slaps you in the face — a bureaucrat, a bus driver and a pharmacist all speaking at you, fast, in a language with no vowels written down — and there is the kind that is so perfectly padded you don't realise it's there until you've lived somewhere four years and cannot order a coffee in the national language. Israel is the first kind. The United Arab Emirates is the second. Guess which one is worse for you.

Israel will make you learn Hebrew by sheer irritation. The UAE will make sure you never have to learn Arabic at all, because roughly nine in ten of the people you meet in Dubai were born somewhere else, and the lingua franca is a globalised, transactional English spoken with a Keralan, Tagalog, Russian or Glaswegian accent. One country is a language immersion course with the kindness setting turned off. The other is an airport lounge with a coastline.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇮🇱 Israel**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Enrol in an ulpan (intensive Hebrew course) in your first months | Assume "everyone speaks English" extends to the Ministry of Interior |
| Learn "slicha" (sorry/excuse me), "rega" (wait) and "yalla" before you land | Take bluntness personally — directness is grammar, not rudeness |
| Keep a Hebrew-speaking friend on speed dial for government letters | Try to read a handwritten sign without learning the script first |
| Try Hebrew in the shuk — vendors love it and will correct you loudly | Expect the Hebrew word order to make any sense in week one |

**🇦🇪 UAE**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn Arabic greetings — "marhaba", "shukran", "inshallah" — and use them | Assume English is the only thing that matters because it is the only thing you hear |
| Pick up a few words of Hindi, Urdu or Tagalog; they're the real street languages | Speak loudly and slowly in English at service staff. It's rude in any language |
| Get official documents properly translated by a certified legal translator | Assume the English version of a contract is the legally binding one — check |
| Ask Emirati colleagues about dialect; Gulf Arabic differs from textbook Arabic | Mock accents. Half the city is speaking its third or fourth language |

## Israel: A Language That Was Dead, and Now Yells at You

Modern Hebrew is a revived language — a liturgical tongue dragged back into daily use roughly a century ago — and Israelis speak it as though making up for lost time. It is fast, clipped, full of slang borrowed from Arabic, Yiddish, Russian and English, and delivered with a directness that makes a New Yorker look like a Japanese diplomat. You will ask a question. You will receive an answer, a counter-question and some unsolicited advice about your life choices, all before you've finished the word "excuse".

The good news is that English is widely spoken in Tel Aviv, among younger people and anyone in tech. The bad news is that the moment you step outside the startup-and-beach bubble, it evaporates. The Ministry of Interior, the health fund (kupat holim) phone lines, your landlord's elderly father, the electricity company, the man at the local makolet: Hebrew. Letters arrive in Hebrew. Parking tickets arrive in Hebrew. Your bank's app may have English, but the fine print does not.

The script is a significant hurdle. Written Hebrew mostly omits vowels, so you're effectively reading a sentence with half the letters missing and filling in the gaps from context you don't yet have. It's like doing a crossword where every clue is also a crossword.

And yet Israel has built something remarkable to handle this: the ulpan, a state-supported intensive Hebrew school system originally designed for waves of immigrants arriving from dozens of countries at once. Sit in an ulpan class and you'll be learning next to Ethiopian grandmothers, French doctors and Ukrainian engineers, all equally lost and equally shouted at by a teacher with the patience of a saint and the volume of a football coach. It works. Because the society around you demands it, you will learn — out of necessity, spite, or both.

## The UAE: Where Arabic Is Official and Almost Optional

Arabic is the official language of the Emirates. Road signs are bilingual, government documents are issued in Arabic, and the call to prayer frames the day. But walk through Dubai Marina, Business Bay or any mall in Abu Dhabi and you can go weeks without a single sentence of Arabic addressed to you. The waiter is from Nepal, the taxi driver from Pakistan, the cashier from the Philippines, your manager from Lebanon who switches to English the moment you appear, and your neighbour is British and has lived there eleven years without learning to count to five.

This is the real language barrier in the UAE, and it's invisible. It isn't between you and the country — it's between you and the country's own citizens, who make up a small minority of the population. Emiratis speak Gulf Arabic with each other, English with foreigners, and there is rarely a context that forces the two worlds to meet. Expats end up living in a parallel, English-speaking city with no particular incentive to leave it.

The second, subtler barrier is between expats themselves. Dubai's English is a working dialect, polished in offices and simplified everywhere else. Instructions get lost between an Indian contractor, a Filipino property manager and a French tenant, each confident they've been understood. Misunderstandings are common and usually resolved with a WhatsApp voice note, a thumbs-up emoji and a great deal of assumed goodwill.

Where it bites is legal. Contracts, court proceedings and some official documents operate in Arabic, and if there's a dispute, the Arabic version can carry the weight. Plenty of expats discover this at the worst possible moment.

## The Verdict

Israel wins, and I'll defend it to anyone. A language barrier that forces you to learn is a gift disguised as an assault. You will be frustrated, corrected in public and hung up on by a government helpline, and six months later you'll be haggling in the Carmel Market in bad Hebrew and feeling, for the first time, like you live somewhere rather than simply reside there.

The UAE's frictionless English is pleasant, convenient and quietly corrosive. It lets you inhabit a country for a decade as a guest who never once spoke the host's language. Many residents say they intended to learn Arabic. Very few did. And the country, busy and cosmopolitan and entirely comfortable with this arrangement, will never punish them for it — which is precisely why so few ever bother.

Comfort is not an achievement. Being shouted at in Hebrew is at least a relationship.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Israel — English got me through Tel Aviv for a year. Then I had to deal with the Ministry of Interior and my health fund in the same week and signed up for ulpan the following Monday.*

> *Reddit r/dubai — Lived here eight years and the only Arabic I use is "yalla" and "khalas". My Filipina colleague speaks better Arabic than I do because she actually bothered.*

> *Quora — Hebrew speakers aren't being rude, the language is just built for getting to the point. Once I understood that, every conversation felt less like an argument.*

## Conclusion

Every language barrier is a choice about what kind of foreigner you want to be. Israel doesn't give you much of a choice: learn, or spend your life at the mercy of a Hebrew envelope. The UAE hands you the choice on a silver tray and quietly assumes you'll choose convenience. Most people do.

I've lived in places where I spoke the language beautifully and places where I spoke it abysmally, and I can tell you which I remember more fondly. It's the ones where I was mocked, corrected and eventually understood.

So learn the Hebrew. Learn some Arabic too, even if nobody asks you to. The people who never learn are the people who, after ten years abroad, still describe the country they live in as "a great base for travelling". That's not living abroad. That's a long layover.
