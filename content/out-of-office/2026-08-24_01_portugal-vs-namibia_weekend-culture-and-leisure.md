# One Country Naps Through Its Weekend Over a Three-Hour Lunch, the Other Drives Six Hundred Kilometres to Find Silence

🇵🇹 Portugal vs 🇳🇦 Namibia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Fourteen moves in, I've learned that "weekend culture" is really just a country confessing what it thinks leisure is for. Portugal thinks leisure is for eating slowly, at length, with your entire extended family, until the light changes outside the window. Namibia thinks leisure is for putting several hundred kilometres of gravel road between yourself and the nearest other human being. Both positions are defensible. Only one of them requires you to own a roof tent.

I've spent enough weekends in both countries to say with total confidence that neither is "relaxed" in the way glossy travel copy likes to claim. Portugal's relaxation is a performance with strict choreography. Namibia's relaxation requires a spare tyre, two jerry cans, and the acceptance that your phone will not work for two days. Neither is exotic. Both are, in their own ways, absolutely serious about how they spend a Saturday.

## Do's & Don'ts: Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clear your entire Sunday afternoon for lunch — it is not a meal, it is an occupation | Book anything before 4pm on a Sunday; nobody you know will be available |
| Join the evening praia-adjacent walk (the passeio) even if you'd rather sit down | Rush a café coffee — a bica is meant to be drunk standing, but never fast |
| Expect shops to genuinely close on Sundays outside major tourist zones | Assume Lisbon's weekend pace applies to smaller towns — it's slower still |
| Learn to enjoy silence at the table — not every lunch needs constant conversation | Interpret a slow weekend as a lazy one; Portuguese leisure has real structure |

## Do's & Don'ts: Namibia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a proper 4x4 and learn to check your own tyre pressure before a self-drive weekend | Expect fuel stations, phone signal, or company for long stretches between towns |
| Book campsites and lodges well ahead — weekend capacity outside Windhoek is genuinely limited | Underestimate distances; "just down the road" can mean four hours |
| Embrace the braai as the actual centrepiece of the weekend, not a side event | Show up to someone's braai without contributing meat, drink, or firewood |
| Pack for total self-sufficiency — water, spares, a real first-aid kit | Assume weekend "getaways" are short; Namibians think in multi-day trips |

## Portugal: A Nation That Has Turned Doing Nothing Into an Art Form With Rules

Portugal's weekend doesn't start on Saturday morning so much as it announces itself gradually across Friday evening, when café terraces fill with people who have, by all appearances, nowhere urgent to be and no intention of pretending otherwise. The country's own time-use data backs up what any visitor notices within a day: leisure here skews heavily toward eating, sitting, and being near family, rather than the frantic activity-stacking that passes for a weekend in places that pride themselves on productivity even at rest.

The genuinely defining institution is Sunday lunch — almoço de domingo — which is less a meal than a scheduled multi-hour commitment involving grandparents, second cousins, and a bacalhau dish somebody's mother has strong, litigable opinions about. It routinely runs three hours. Nobody apologises for this. Trying to schedule something at 2pm on a Sunday marks you as either a tourist or someone with no functioning family, and Portuguese friends have told me this with a straight face more than once.

Beyond the table, the weekend leisure economy leans coastal and communal — the evening passeio along the seafront or riverside, café culture that treats a single bica as a legitimate reason to occupy a terrace chair for forty minutes, and a domestic tourism pattern, per Turismo de Portugal's own figures, heavily weighted toward short breaks to the Algarve or Douro rather than anything requiring real planning. It is, structurally, a weekend built entirely around proximity to other people. The idea of deliberately driving somewhere to be alone would strike most Portuguese people I know as faintly tragic.

What foreigners consistently misjudge is the rigidity underneath the apparent looseness. The pace is slow, but the schedule — lunch at 1, coffee after, evening walk before dinner — is closer to liturgy than improvisation. Portugal isn't lazy on weekends. It's disciplined about leisure in a way that looks, from the outside, exactly like doing nothing.

## Namibia: A Country That Treats the Weekend as a Logistics Exercise With a Braai at the End

Namibia's weekend culture starts from a premise Portugal would find baffling: that the point of a day off is distance. This is, to be fair, a country roughly the size of France and Germany combined with a population smaller than greater Lisbon, so "nip out for the weekend" here means something closer to an expedition than an outing. The Namibia Tourism Board's own visitor data confirms what every Windhoek resident already knows — self-drive travel isn't a niche tourist activity, it's the default domestic weekend format, and locals do it as enthusiastically as anyone with a rental Hilux and a Lonely Planet.

The braai is non-negotiable and functions as the actual social contract of the weekend — less "barbecue" than a structured gathering with unspoken rules about who brings what, who tends the fire, and how long everyone pretends the meat isn't ready yet. I have been quietly but firmly corrected on braai etiquette by three separate Namibian hosts, and I respect every single correction.

What's genuinely striking, coming from smaller, denser countries, is how casually Namibians discuss distance. A "quick weekend trip" to the coast at Swakopmund is a five-hour drive each way, and nobody blinks. Camping and self-catering lodges dominate over hotels for weekend leisure, not from thrift but from preference — the whole point is self-sufficiency, silence, and a night sky that makes most other countries' stargazing claims look like a joke. Namibia Statistics Agency data on household leisure spending consistently shows travel and outdoor recreation eating a disproportionate share of discretionary budgets relative to comparable economies, which tells you where the actual national priorities sit.

Where Portugal's weekend pulls people closer together at a table, Namibia's pushes them further apart into landscape, and treats the getting-there as half the point rather than an inconvenience to be minimised.

## The Verdict

Portugal offers a weekend you can join within a week of arriving, provided you can sit still for three hours and develop opinions about salt cod. Namibia offers a weekend that requires planning, equipment, and a tolerance for your own company that some visitors discover, somewhere around hour four of gravel road, they do not actually have.

I'm giving this one to Namibia, and I say that as someone who genuinely loves a long Portuguese lunch. There's something about a country that builds its entire leisure culture around vastness and self-reliance, rather than convenience, that feels more honest about what a weekend is actually for. Portugal relaxes you. Namibia earns it.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — "What do Portuguese people actually do on weekends outside Lisbon and Porto?" — paraphrased: several answers stress that in smaller towns, Sunday genuinely shuts down — no shops, no errands, just family lunch and a walk, and newcomers who try to "get things done" find themselves alone in an empty street.*

> *expat.com Lisbon forum — paraphrased: a recurring thread describes the shock of realising Sunday lunch invitations are not optional social suggestions but semi-mandatory family fixtures, with posters warning against booking anything else that day for the first year or two.*

> *expat.com Windhoek forum — paraphrased: posters repeatedly warn newcomers not to underestimate distances on a "quick weekend trip," with more than one account of running low on fuel or water after misjudging how far towns actually are from each other.*

## Conclusion

Portugal and Namibia have arrived at opposite answers to the same question — what is a weekend actually for — and both answers are, infuriatingly, correct on their own terms. One country has perfected sitting still with people you love until the light goes gold. The other has perfected disappearing so completely that the loudest sound for two days is your own tyres on gravel. Try both, if you can. Just don't bring Namibia's packing list to a Portuguese Sunday lunch, and don't bring Portugal's three-hour patience for a starter course to a Namibian braai — you'll be waiting considerably longer than that for the meat.
