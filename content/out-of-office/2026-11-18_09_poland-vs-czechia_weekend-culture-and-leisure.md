# Poland Shuts the Shops on Sunday and Czechia Shuts the City Because Everyone Has Left for the Cottage

🇵🇱 Poland vs 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Poland and Czechia are neighbours, share a long border, a fondness for dumplings and a history of being rearranged by other people's maps. On the weekend, they diverge. Poland has legislated its Sunday into a state of rest, and woe betide the expat who wants a bag of flour at four in the afternoon. Czechia leaves its shops open and its citizens vanish anyway, to the chata, the garden, the forest and the nearest outdoor tap.

One country has a law about leisure. The other has a habit. As a person who has lived in fourteen countries and never successfully bought milk on a Sunday in the first month, I know which of these I find more infuriating, and I intend to explain.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Poland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the trading-Sunday calendar before you plan a shop | Assume Sunday means open |
| Know where the Żabka, petrol station and pharmacy are | Panic when the supermarket is dark |
| Shop on Saturday, and expect crowds | Leave the big shop to the last minute |
| Try a family allotment garden if you are invited | Comment on someone's tomatoes |

**Czechia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the eight public-holiday closing days for large shops | Assume every holiday is a normal trading day |
| Join a pub evening, outdoors if possible | Treat the pub as just a place to eat |
| Plan a weekend trip to the mountains or a castle | Spend every weekend in the city |
| Greet everyone at the cottage settlement | Skip the small talk |

## Poland

Poland's Sunday trading ban, introduced in 2018 and phased in over time, is not total. Cleanwhale's guide lists the 2026 trading Sundays as 25 January, 29 March, 26 April, 28 June, 30 August and 6, 13 and 20 December. Bakeries, petrol stations, pharmacies, airport and station shops, hotels, online shops and owner-run convenience stores are exempt. The most famous loophole belongs to Żabka: franchisees registered as postal agents to keep trading. The Krakow Post reported in 2018 that Solidarity objected while a Żabka spokesperson said related court cases had been rejected, and Notes from Poland reported in 2019 that the law might be amended so only full post offices would count.

The economics are contested. A 2021 Notes from Poland opinion piece, from a think-tank author, said about 6,500 shops disappeared after the ban, with outlets falling from about 106,000 to 99,400, while 2,000 new online shops opened in the first quarter of 2021 and Żabka grew past 7,200 stores. That is a single viewpoint, but it matches the rhythm of the thing: the ban did not remove Sunday shopping, it moved it online and into the convenience store.

For the expat, the experience is a rearrangement of life. A UK-experienced commenter on the Krakow Post said Sunday trading at home came with higher Sunday pay and staff rotation, and that Poland should have let workers choose. Another said Saturdays in Kraków are now crowded. Others praised Sunday as a day for family and rest. The commenters are anonymous and their nationality is unverified, but the three positions are the whole debate, and you will hear all of them at a Polish dinner.

What the ban changed, culturally, is the shape of Saturday. A Polish Saturday is now a logistics exercise: the big shop, the queue, the car park, the sense that an entire nation has been given one day to buy its groceries and has decided to do it simultaneously. Sunday, by contrast, has recovered some of its older character, with long lunches, church for those who go and a stroll in the park for those who do not. You can dislike the law and still see that it has handed back a day. That is a rare thing in a European economy, and it is why the argument refuses to die.

The other Polish weekend institution is the *działka*, the family allotment garden. A 2018 paper in Sustainability reports 4,667 gardens with 917,445 plots on 40,863 hectares, with plots capped at 500 square metres under the 2013 act, and Kraków alone has 87 gardens over more than 470 hectares, about 1.5 per cent of the city. It is a vast network of small ambitions. A Polish weekend is not a rest from work so much as a different kind of work, with better vegetables.

## Czechia

Czechia has kept its shops open on Sundays. The restriction is on public holidays: since 28 October 2016, shops larger than 200 square metres close on eight days, namely New Year's Day, Easter Monday, 8 May, 28 September, 28 October, the afternoon of 24 December and 25 and 26 December, while smaller shops may stay open. I have not checked the rule's current status after a 2019 Constitutional Court ruling, so confirm it before you plan Christmas.

The weekend itself is something else. Expat.com's leisure guide says beer typically costs 40 to 70 crowns, pubs and beer gardens are central to social life, gyms run 800 to 1,500 crowns a month, and weekend trips go to Bohemian Switzerland, the Krkonoše mountains and Karlštejn castle. In other words, the Czech weekend is a pub on Friday, a mountain on Saturday and a nap on Sunday.

But the weekend's true centre is the cottage. Radio Prague says there are more than 500,000 chata-style second homes, about a fifth of domestic buildings. Expats.cz describes the Saturday outdoor-pub evening in a cottage settlement, with owners taking turns on the beer tap, and the history goes back to the 1920s tramping movement along the Vltava, Berounka and Sázava rivers. An expat writer's tongue-in-cheek survival guide for a Czech family cottage includes praising the air, staying visibly busy and bringing earplugs. Other expats in an Expats.cz round-up mention mushrooming, hiking and cycling as their weekend habits.

Compare this with Poland's version. A Polish weekend leans toward family, the table and the plot of land, with shopping squeezed into a Saturday. A Czech weekend leans toward the pub, the trail and the cottage, with shopping an afterthought, because the shops are open and so nothing is urgent. The difference is the pressure. A Czech can decide on Sunday afternoon to buy a loaf, whereas a Pole must have decided on Saturday, and that small gap shapes the mood of an entire week.

Which leads to the peculiar thing about Czech leisure: it is mostly outdoors and entirely participatory. You do not watch the weekend; you take part in it. The Czech leisure industry is a bag of mushrooms, a mountain bike and a neighbour who insists you try his plum brandy.

## The Verdict

Czechia wins on freedom, and Poland wins on rest. If you want to buy a pair of socks at eleven on a Sunday morning, Czechia is the answer, and a trip to the countryside will not be a hardship either. If you want a society in which a whole country agrees to stop, Poland offers that, with a loophole the size of a corner shop.

I find the Polish model oddly admirable and practically irritating. A forced day of rest is a good idea until you need a plug adapter. The Czech model trusts adults to decide for themselves, and then watches them all choose the same mountain.

For an expat, my advice is to lean into the local rhythm: in Poland, plan Saturday and cook on Sunday; in Czechia, leave town on Friday and come back with mushrooms.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expats.cz, 'How to Survive A Czech Summer Cottage Stay' — an expat writer's weekends at her husband's family cottage come with rules: praise the air, stay visibly busy, bring earplugs, greet every neighbour.*

> *Expats.cz, 'Expat voices' — respondents mention mushroom picking, hiking and cycling at weekends, and one says he cycles and hikes on weekends.*

## Conclusion

A weekend reveals what a country believes about time. Poland believes in a shared stop, enforced by law and softened by a small shop with a post-office licence. Czechia believes in a shared escape, enforced by custom and softened by a beer tap.

Pick your poison: a country that tells you when to rest or a country that tells you where to go. Either way, buy your bread on Saturday, bring a jumper to the cottage and learn the word for "round of beer". You will need it in both.
