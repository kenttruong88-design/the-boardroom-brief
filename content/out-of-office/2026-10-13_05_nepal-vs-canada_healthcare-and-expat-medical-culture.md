# In Nepal, You Pay the Pharmacist Directly and Walk Out Cured. In Canada, You Wait Three Weeks to Be Told to Wait Longer.

🇳🇵 Nepal vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ask a Nepali friend about going to the doctor and they'll describe something close to a transaction: you go, you pay, someone competent looks at you, you leave with medicine, often on the same day. Ask a Canadian friend about going to the doctor and watch their face do something complicated — a mixture of national pride about "free" healthcare and quiet despair about the eleven-month wait for a specialist referral that the pride somehow never quite covers.

Nepal's healthcare system is a patchwork of private clinics, pharmacy self-service, and family-driven decision-making that moves fast precisely because so much of it runs on direct payment and personal relationships. Canada's system is a single-payer point of pride that moves slowly precisely because "free at the point of use" has never meant "fast," and an entire national identity has been built around not noticing the gap between those two words.

## Nepal: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go directly to a pharmacist for minor ailments — they'll often diagnose and dispense on the spot, no appointment needed | Don't expect a formal referral system for specialists; you typically just go to the specialist directly and pay |
| Involve family in major medical decisions — it's expected, not intrusive, and often genuinely useful for navigating options | Don't assume all facilities are equal; quality varies hugely between Kathmandu's better private hospitals and rural government posts |
| Keep cash or a card ready; private care moves fast specifically because payment happens immediately | Don't rely solely on public hospitals for anything beyond the basics if you can afford private alternatives |
| Research hospitals in advance for serious conditions — reputation varies enormously and word of mouth matters more than rankings | Don't expect comprehensive insurance coverage to be the norm; many pay out of pocket as a matter of course |

## Canada: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for your provincial health card the moment you arrive — some provinces impose a waiting period before coverage kicks in | Don't expect same-week access to a family doctor; many Canadians don't have one and rely on walk-in clinics instead |
| Use a walk-in clinic or telehealth line for anything non-urgent rather than the emergency room | Don't go to the ER for a non-emergency; the wait can stretch past twelve hours and you'll be triaged behind genuine emergencies |
| Get supplemental private insurance if your employer offers it — dental and prescription drugs usually aren't covered by the public system | Don't assume "free healthcare" means comprehensive; plenty of essential care sits outside what the public system actually pays for |
| Be patient, and be your own advocate, when chasing a specialist referral | Don't expect urgency to speed up a routine referral; wait times are measured in months as standard, not exception |

### Nepal: Fast, Direct, and Quietly Unequal

Healthcare in Nepal runs on an entirely different logic than the insurance-and-referral systems that dominate most wealthy countries: you largely pay as you go, you can often see a specialist directly without a gatekeeping family doctor, and pharmacists function as a genuine first line of medical advice rather than simply filling prescriptions written by someone else. For common ailments, this produces a system that's often faster and more direct than its wealthier counterparts — walk into a pharmacy, describe your symptoms, and walk out minutes later with medication and advice, no appointment, no referral, no three-week wait required.

The tradeoff is enormous variation in quality and access. Kathmandu's better private hospitals can deliver care that rivals international standards, staffed by doctors trained abroad and equipped with modern facilities, provided you can pay for it. Outside the capital, and particularly in rural areas, government health posts can be under-resourced, understaffed, and genuinely difficult to reach, meaning the "fast and direct" reputation of Nepali healthcare applies far more reliably to those with money and urban access than to the country as a whole. Family involvement in medical decisions is the norm rather than the exception here — showing up to a hospital alone for anything serious tends to surprise staff as much as it would surprise relatives, since major health decisions are treated as a family matter requiring consultation, not a private affair conducted solo.

Insurance coverage remains the exception rather than the rule for much of the population, meaning direct payment isn't just common, it's structurally central to how fast the system can move — and expats who assume international travel insurance will smooth over every gap often discover, usually at the worst possible moment, that the private hospital wants payment up front regardless of what's written in their policy documents back home.

### Canada: Free, Slow, and Quietly Incomplete

Canada's publicly funded healthcare system is, without question, a genuine point of national identity, built on the principle that medically necessary hospital and physician services should be available regardless of ability to pay. What this principle doesn't guarantee, and what catches almost every newcomer off guard, is speed. A referral to a specialist, flagged as "routine" rather than urgent, can take months — sometimes most of a year — to actually produce an appointment, during which the patient is simply expected to wait, manage symptoms as best they can, and periodically call to confirm they haven't been forgotten.

Finding a family doctor in the first place has become its own ordeal in much of the country; many Canadians, including long-term citizens, simply don't have one, relying instead on walk-in clinics or telehealth lines for anything that comes up. New arrivals face an additional wrinkle: several provinces impose a waiting period, often around three months, before provincial health coverage actually kicks in, leaving a gap that private travel insurance is specifically designed, and specifically priced, to cover.

The emergency room absorbs a lot of the system's overflow as a result, and the folklore around ER wait times — genuinely substantiated by hospital data in many cities — has become a dark running joke among residents, who swap stories of twelve-hour waits the way other cultures swap restaurant recommendations. And "free" healthcare, it turns out, has a fairly narrow legal definition: dental care, prescription drugs, physiotherapy, and vision care generally sit outside what the public system covers, which is why a healthy chunk of the Canadian workforce quietly depends on employer-provided supplemental insurance to access care that most assume, from the outside, is already included.

## The Verdict

Nepal moves fast for those who can pay and slow, sometimes dangerously so, for those who can't. Canada moves at roughly the same deliberate pace for almost everyone, regardless of income, which is either a profound equaliser or a universally shared frustration, depending on how urgently you currently need to see someone about that referral.

If I had to bet my own health on speed alone, I'd take a well-chosen private hospital in Kathmandu over a Canadian specialist waitlist any day of the week. But if I had to bet on not being bankrupted by a sudden diagnosis, Canada wins without much argument. Nepal optimises for access when you can pay. Canada optimises for fairness when you can wait. Neither country has quite solved for both.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat Exchange reader comments on Nepal's health system — paraphrased: several contributors note that private hospitals in Kathmandu offer genuinely fast, high-quality care, but caution that travel insurance claims can be slow to reimburse, so having cash or a card ready at the time of treatment matters more than the policy documents suggest.*

> *expat.com forums, Canada — paraphrased: newcomers repeatedly describe being surprised by the provincial health card waiting period, with several recommending private interim insurance as essential rather than optional for the first few months after arrival.*

> *Internations Vancouver community notes — paraphrased: a recurring theme is the difficulty of finding a family doctor accepting new patients, with multiple members describing months-long searches and eventually settling for walk-in clinics as their default source of ongoing care.*

## Conclusion

What both systems quietly reveal is a tradeoff every country makes but rarely says out loud: speed, fairness, and cost can't all be maximised at once, so each culture picks two and lives with the consequences on the third. Nepal picked speed and affordability for those who can pay privately, at the cost of consistent access for everyone else. Canada picked fairness and comprehensiveness of principle, at the cost of speed for nearly everyone.

Neither country would describe their system as broken, exactly — just as the result of choices made decades ago that nobody has fully revisited since. I'd just gently suggest that if you're choosing where to get sick, you ask first whether you're optimising for a fast diagnosis or a fair bill. Rarely, in either country, do you get both.
