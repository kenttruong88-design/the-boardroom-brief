# One Country Gives You Eight Days Off a Year. The Other Gives You Fourteen and a Marching Band.

🇬🇧 United Kingdom 🇲🇹 Malta
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

If you want to know how seriously a country takes collective joy, count its public holidays and then watch what actually happens on them. The UK and Malta sit at genuinely opposite ends of the European holiday-generosity table, and the gap is bigger than most people realise until they've actually lived on both sides of it.

England and Wales get eight bank holidays a year — one of the lowest counts anywhere in Europe, a fact British people will bring up unprompted, with real bitterness, at the slightest provocation. Malta, a country roughly the size of a large British town, gets fourteen, several of them tied to the extraordinary village festa season, where every town takes its turn flooding its own streets with fireworks, brass bands, and a patron saint statue carried through crowds that would make a British council health-and-safety officer faint. One country rations its collective joy. The other has built an entire summer around giving it away, parish by parish, week after week.

## Do's & Don'ts: United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat a bank holiday Monday as sacred — plans get made around it months in advance precisely because there are so few of them | Expect every region to share the same calendar; Scotland and Northern Ireland both observe different bank holidays from England and Wales |
| Lean into the unofficial festival calendar — Notting Hill Carnival, Glastonbury, Bonfire Night — since the official holidays won't satisfy the appetite for celebration alone | Assume a bank holiday guarantees good weather plans will survive; British outdoor festivities are a triumph of optimism over forecast |
| Join the national complaining ritual about having fewer public holidays than almost anywhere else in Europe; it's a genuine, shared grievance | Mistake quietness on a bank holiday high street for indifference; much of the celebration has simply moved to private gardens and pub beer gardens |

## Do's & Don'ts: Malta

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the local festa calendar before planning anything involving driving through a town centre in summer — roads close, genuinely, for days | Underestimate the fireworks; Malta's festa pyrotechnics displays are serious, loud, and a genuine source of local pride and rivalry between villages |
| Join in a village festa at least once, even as an outsider — they're public, joyful, and visitors are welcomed warmly | Confuse a village's patron saint feast with a minor local event; for that town, it's the single biggest date on the entire calendar |
| Plan around the sheer density of public holidays, including several tied to historical and religious dates specific to Malta | Assume the celebration is purely religious; many festa elements — the band marches, the fireworks competitions — are as much civic pride as devotion |

## United Kingdom: Scarcity Breeds a Strange Devotion

The UK's relationship with public holidays is defined almost entirely by their scarcity. Eight bank holidays in England and Wales — Scotland and Northern Ireland get slightly different, equally modest, calendars — puts the country near the bottom of any European ranking, and the British have responded to this, predictably, not by lobbying for more but by developing an almost ritualistic devotion to the few they have. A bank holiday Monday gets treated with genuine reverence: barbecues planned regardless of forecast, DIY projects scheduled months out, entire extended families committing to a trip that absolutely will involve queueing on a motorway at some point. Reddit threads complaining about the UK's comparatively meagre holiday count are a recurring, almost seasonal genre of their own, and the complaint is entirely fair — most EU countries comfortably beat Britain on this metric.

What the official calendar doesn't capture, though, is how much of Britain's actual festival energy has migrated into events that aren't public holidays at all. Glastonbury, Notting Hill Carnival, Bonfire Night, the endless procession of village fetes and Christmas markets that pop up with real local devotion despite requiring everyone to still turn up for work the next day — Britain's festival instinct is real, it's just been rerouted around an official calendar that refuses to accommodate it. You get the sense, living here, of a culture that wants to celebrate considerably more than its own government has officially sanctioned, and has simply decided to do it anyway, unpaid leave be damned.

## Malta: Every Village Gets Its Turn

Malta takes the opposite approach entirely, and does it on an island you could drive across in under an hour. Fourteen official public holidays — nearly double the UK's count — covering a mix of national days (Independence Day, Freedom Day, Republic Day, Victory Day) and religious feasts, and that's before you even get to the real engine of Maltese festival culture: the village festa season, running roughly from late spring through September, during which each town takes its turn throwing an enormous, multi-day celebration for its own patron saint. Streets fill with elaborately decorated church facades, brass bands march in formal competition with neighbouring villages, and the fireworks — genuinely, seriously competitive between towns, a point of fierce local pride — light up the sky with a scale that would require a six-month council application process in Britain.

What's easy to miss as an outsider is how deeply civic, rather than purely religious, this all is. Yes, the festa marks a patron saint, and yes, the statue procession through the crowd is a real devotional act for many. But the rivalry between village band clubs, the pride in whose fireworks display was louder or more elaborate this year, the sheer scale of a town's entire population turning out for days — this is identity and belonging as much as faith, and newcomers who show up expecting a solemn religious observance are usually surprised by how loud, joyful, and genuinely fun the whole thing turns out to be. The road closures are real, the noise is real, and nobody local seems to mind either.

## The Verdict

Malta, obviously, and it's not remotely close. The UK's bank-holiday scarcity has produced a culture that celebrates in spite of its calendar rather than because of it, and there's a certain resilience to that, sure, but resilience isn't the same as abundance. Malta simply gives more — more official days off, more festival density, more of its own civic identity wrapped up in the next village's turn to light the sky. Eight days of grudging British sunshine-optimism against fourteen days plus an entire summer of competitive pyrotechnics isn't really a contest. It's just a country that decided joy should be scheduled generously, and built its whole calendar accordingly.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/unitedkingdom — a recurring thread comparing bank-holiday counts across Europe routinely surfaces the same frustration: "we work more days a year than almost anyone on the continent and somehow still complain less than we should."*

> *Reddit r/malta — a newcomer asked why a normally ten-minute drive had taken ninety minutes, and the top reply was simply: "festa season, check the calendar before you drive anywhere in summer, this isn't optional information."*

> *Internations Malta — a longtime resident described their first village festa as "somewhere between a religious procession, a rock concert, and a village feud settled entirely through fireworks," and meant it as the highest possible compliment.*

## Conclusion

What the UK and Malta really disagree about is whether celebration should be rationed carefully or distributed generously, parish by parish, saint by saint. Britain, true to form, has chosen scarcity and compensated with sheer stubborn improvisation — festivals built entirely outside the official calendar because the official calendar simply wasn't going to provide enough. Malta chose abundance, baked an entire summer's rhythm around it, and let every single village have its own night to be the loudest place on the island. If you're choosing where to spend your public holidays, choose generously. Britain will give you grudging bank-holiday sunshine. Malta will give you someone else's patron saint, a brass band, and fireworks you'll feel in your chest.
