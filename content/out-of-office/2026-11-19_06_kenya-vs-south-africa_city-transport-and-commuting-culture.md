# Nairobi Commutes by Matatu and Faith, Johannesburg Commutes by Car and Anxiety

🇰🇪 Kenya · 🇿🇦 South Africa

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A city's transport tells you who it was built for, and Nairobi and Johannesburg were built for two entirely different people. Johannesburg was built for the motorist, the mining-era grid of highways and security gates, where public transport is something that happens to other people. Nairobi was built by improvisation, a city that never quite agreed on a plan and instead evolved the matatu, a decorated minibus with a sound system and a philosophy of personal space that I can only describe as optional.

Both cities are loud, congested and wonderful, and both will have you doing arithmetic about risk before breakfast. The difference is in the arithmetic. In Nairobi, you ask whether the ride will be crowded. In Johannesburg, you ask whether it will be safe. I have done both commutes, and one of them leaves you with a story and the other leaves you with a locked car door.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇰🇪 Kenya**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use Uber, Bolt or Little for peace of mind; expats mostly do | Assume every matatu is safe at rush hour |
| Agree the fare before getting into an unmetered taxi | Hop in and discuss the price afterwards |
| Apply for a Kenyan licence through the NTSA portal after the first three months | Drive on a foreign licence indefinitely |
| Stick to drivers you know and trust for boda bodas | Treat motorbike taxis as a harmless shortcut |

**🇿🇦 South Africa**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take the Gautrain between Sandton, OR Tambo, Midrand and Pretoria | Assume the Gautrain network covers the whole city |
| Use Uber or Bolt if you are not driving | Walk in the inner city at night |
| Keep car doors locked and windows closed, and use an anti-hijack alarm | Leave valuables visible in the car |
| Try Rea Vaya for the Soweto line | Rely on Metrorail at peak without watching for pickpockets |

## 🇰🇪 Kenya: The Matatu Philosophy

The matatu is the beating heart of Nairobi's public transport, a shared minibus, commonly described as a 14-seater, that is often packed beyond capacity and driven with what the polite guides call aggression. Operators must belong to registered Saccos, which sounds orderly, and which is the sort of regulation that exists in the same city as the vehicle it regulates. Most expats avoid matatus. Not all do: an expat adviser on the Nairobi forum calls them the safest cheap daytime option, advising you to avoid rush hour, never walk after dark, and skip motorbike taxis and cycling due to accident risk. Another poster notes that a student at Strathmore finds them cheap, widely used and safe for getting to campus.

The consensus among expats without cars is that you use taxis and boda bodas, but only drivers you know and trust. A decade of ride-hailing has made this simpler: Uber, Bolt and the Kenyan-built Little all operate, and the apps also offer boda bodas, which are cheaper and quicker in traffic and far riskier. Unmetered taxis need the fare agreed beforehand. A Talesmag post report puts commutes at anywhere from five to 45-55 minutes depending on neighbourhood, deems street-hailed taxis and matatus unsafe, calls Uber safe, and recommends booking Uber Comfort for a working seatbelt and a door that locks. That sentence tells you more about the market than any statistic.

There are other options for the intrepid. Kenya Railways commuter trains link the centre with Ruiru, Kikuyu, Embakasi and Syokimau, though reliability is patchy and services have been suspended on certain holidays and in heavy rain. The Madaraka Express SGR to Mombasa takes about five hours and is the one piece of Kenyan transport that feels like the future. Domestic flights to the coast are popular. If you are driving, you hold a foreign licence or international permit for the first three months and then apply for a Kenyan licence via the NTSA portal on eCitizen. Kenyans drive on the left, pedestrians can be fined for crossing outside footbridges, and the National Road Safety Action Plan 2024 to 2028 aims to halve road deaths by 2030.

An old forum post I want to flag as dated quoted 2009 WHO figures for Kenyan road deaths and a twenty-shilling matatu fare, and I would not rely on either. What I will rely on is the city's character: relentless traffic, patchy signage, a GPS you should trust more than your instincts, and a transport culture that is improvised, loud and, in its way, more honest than the polished alternatives.

## 🇿🇦 South Africa: The Gated Commute

Johannesburg's relationship with public transport is a story of ambition meeting reality. The Gautrain is the pride of the system, an 80-kilometre rail line completed in June 2012 that links Johannesburg's CBD, Sandton, OR Tambo Airport, Midrand, Centurion and Pretoria, running every ten minutes at weekday peaks and every twenty off-peak. It is clean, quick and safe, and it covers precisely the places a foreigner is likely to go. I could not find fare figures on the pages I checked, so I will not guess.

Beyond that, the options are less comforting. Minibus taxis are the most common public transport in the city, run on informal routes, hailed by hand signals and described as loud, overcrowded and notorious for ignoring the rules of the road. Expats are unlikely to use them. The Rea Vaya bus rapid transit system began in 2009, expanded in phases, includes the Johannesburg to Soweto line and has weathered strike stoppages, and is described as one of the safest and most efficient options. Metrorail has peak-time pickpocketing problems. It is a transport system with three speeds and no connecting line.

So Johannesburg expats drive, or Uber. One interviewee calls Uber generally the best and safest option if you are not driving. The standard safety advice reads like a pre-flight checklist: an anti-hijack alarm, locked doors, closed windows, no walking in the inner city at night, and the northern suburbs mostly safe. Anxiety is part of the commute, and nobody pretends otherwise. Hijacking and smash-and-grab are real enough to be written into the guidebooks.

What this produces is a city of private cocoons. You move from gated home to office park to shopping centre in a sealed vehicle, and the public realm is a thing you observe through glass. It is efficient and sad, and I say that as someone who spent three months doing it and loved my car more than most of my relatives.

## The Verdict

Nairobi wins for texture, Johannesburg wins for infrastructure, and I am giving the overall verdict to Nairobi for one stubborn reason: the matatu may be reckless, but it is a shared experience. A commute in Nairobi puts you in contact with the city, and a commute in Johannesburg largely protects you from it. I would rather arrive a little frazzled and a lot informed than serene and sealed in.

That said, the Gautrain is the single best-run piece of public transport in either country, and if your life is Sandton to the airport, it is spotless. The problem is that a city is more than two nodes. Johannesburg has a world-class line and no culture of using it, while Nairobi has a world-class culture of moving and no tidy infrastructure to support it.

My sting: one city has built the train and forgotten the passengers, and the other has built the passengers and forgotten the train. Choose which gap you can live with.

## Conclusion

I have commuted by train, taxi, tuk-tuk, bicycle and, once, a donkey, and the lesson is that a city's transport is a confession. Nairobi confesses that it improvised. Johannesburg confesses that it fears itself. Both are honest, in their ways.

If you are visiting, ride the Gautrain and book an Uber in both. If you are staying, learn the matatu, then learn when to stop using it. And in either city, agree the fare before you get in and buckle up if the belt works. The belt, as a Nairobi post report reminds us, is a feature not every ride includes.
