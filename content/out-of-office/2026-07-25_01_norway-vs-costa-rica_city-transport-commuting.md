# Punctuality Is a Personality Trait in Oslo, and a Suggestion in San José

🇳🇴 Norway vs 🇨🇷 Costa Rica

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have stood at a bus stop in Oslo at minus twelve degrees, wearing every layer I own, and watched the digital sign count down to the second while a Norwegian beside me checked his watch against it — not out of anxiety, just to confirm the universe was still behaving correctly. Three months later I stood at a parada in San José with no sign, no schedule, and no discernible system, and a man selling mangoes from a wheelbarrow told me the bus "comes when it comes, mae." Both systems work. Neither system apologises for what it is.

That's the whole story of commuting in these two countries, really. One nation has engineered spontaneity out of daily life entirely; the other has never seen the appeal. If you think punctuality is a moral virtue, move to Norway. If you think punctuality is a symptom of a joyless society, San José will validate every suspicion you've ever had.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a Ruter app pass before boarding | Try to pay cash on the bus — most drivers won't take it |
| Stand on the right on escalators | Make small talk with strangers on the T-bane |
| Cycle even in light snow — everyone does | Assume the last train waits for anyone |
| Validate your ticket even if no one checks | Eat a smelly lunch on public transport |

### 🇨🇷 Costa Rica

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry small colones for bus fare | Expect a fixed timetable to mean anything precise |
| Ask locals which bus goes where — signage is scarce | Assume Waze or Google Maps has the full picture |
| Befriend your regular driver; it pays off | Rely on buses after dark outside San José |
| Budget extra time, always | Get annoyed when "15 minutes" becomes 45 |

## Norway: The Republic of the Perfectly Timed Departure

Oslo's public transport doesn't inspire affection so much as quiet, grudging respect, the way you respect a Swiss watch or a tax auditor. The T-bane arrives when the app says it will, to the point where missing it by ninety seconds feels like a personal failing rather than bad luck. Norwegians don't run for trains. Running for a train implies you didn't plan properly, and planning properly is basically the national religion.

What nobody tells you before you move here is how much the system assumes competence from you. There's no announcer chirping reminders, no friendly signage explaining zones — you're expected to have downloaded Ruter, loaded a card, and internalised the network map before you so much as approach a platform. Fail to do this and you'll get the specific Norwegian silence that communicates disappointment more effectively than any lecture could.

The electrified bus fleet and near-total absence of diesel fumes make the whole experience oddly serene, which is unsettling if you're used to commuting as an assault on the senses. Cycling infrastructure is taken just as seriously; Norwegians will bike through weather that would have most nations issuing a state of emergency, and they'll do it in full technical gear that costs more than your monthly transit pass. Own a car in central Oslo and you'll pay road tolls that function as a not-so-subtle hint to reconsider your choices.

The trade-off for all this efficiency is a total absence of charm. Nobody chats on the metro. Nobody offers you a seat with a wink. The system works because everyone agrees, silently and permanently, not to disrupt it with personality. Efficient, yes. Warm, absolutely not — and Norwegians would tell you that's rather the point.

## Costa Rica: Buses Run on Vibes, and Somehow That's Fine

Costa Rican bus culture operates on a completely different physics. Timetables exist in the way horoscopes exist — as a loose gesture toward structure that nobody actually expects to hold. Ask a local when the next bus comes and you'll get a shrug, a number pulled from somewhere near their soul, and a reassurance that it'll be fine. It usually is, eventually.

What makes this bearable, even charming, is that the unpredictability comes wrapped in genuine warmth. Drivers know their regulars by name. Passengers will flag down a bus for a stranger running late. Someone will always tell you which stop is yours if you look lost enough, and in San José you will look lost fairly often, because street signage was apparently deemed optional at some point in urban planning history and nobody's revisited the decision since.

Traffic in the Central Valley is its own creature — the road network wasn't built for the volume it now carries, and rush hour turns a twenty-minute journey into ninety without warning or apology. Locals have made peace with this in a way that borders on spiritual; pura vida isn't just a tourist slogan, it's a coping mechanism deployed daily at bus stops nationwide. Uber operates in a legal grey zone that everyone uses anyway, which tells you everything about how much faith anyone places in the official taxi system.

If you need control, structure, and certainty, Costa Rican transit will break something in you within a month. If you can surrender to the chaos, you'll find a system that's slower, messier, and somehow kinder than anything Scandinavia has engineered.

## The Verdict

Norway wins on every metric you'd put in a spreadsheet — punctuality, cleanliness, electrification, safety. If your standard for a good commute is "predictable," Oslo will never once disappoint you, and it will never once surprise you either, which is its own quiet tragedy.

Costa Rica wins on the metric nobody puts in a spreadsheet: does commuting make you hate humanity a little less by the time you arrive? Norway's system is a machine you slot yourself into. Costa Rica's is a negotiation you have with your fellow passengers, your driver, and occasionally your own patience — and negotiations, unlike machines, sometimes end in a genuine smile from a stranger.

I'd take Oslo's efficiency for getting to a meeting on time. I'd take San José's chaos for actually enjoying the twenty extra minutes I didn't plan for. Suki's verdict: Costa Rica wins on humanity, Norway wins on everything an actuary would care about — and I have never once trusted an actuary's idea of a good time.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Norway — someone missed a T-bane by four seconds and described feeling "genuine shame," which checks out entirely.*

> *Reddit r/costarica — a new arrival asked for the "bus schedule app" and was told, with real affection, that the schedule is a feeling, not a document.*

> *Internations San José — a longtime expat warned that Uber drivers in the Central Valley know the traffic patterns better than any app, and to always ask the driver's opinion before trusting Waze.*

## Conclusion

Two commuting cultures, two entirely different definitions of what "on time" even means. Norway has built a machine so precise it borders on unsettling; Costa Rica has built a network so loose it borders on improvisational theatre. Neither is wrong. Both are, in their own way, an honest reflection of the country around them — one obsessed with order, one allergic to it.

If you're moving to Oslo, buy the app, learn the map, and never, ever be the person who holds up the escalator. If you're moving to San José, buy patience in bulk, befriend your bus driver, and accept that "soon" is a legitimate unit of time. Just don't try to bring Norwegian punctuality to a Costa Rican bus stop. Nobody there is impressed by your watch.
