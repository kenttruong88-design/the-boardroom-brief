# One Nation Measures Happiness by Committee. The Other Just Wants You to Relax, Chill Out, Stop Asking Questions

🇧🇹 Bhutan vs Costa Rica 🇨🇷

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Bhutan invented an entire government metric to formalise the pursuit of joy, complete with surveys, indices, and presumably a civil servant whose job title is literally "Minister of Happiness." Costa Rica took one look at that bureaucratic apparatus, shrugged, said "pura vida," and went back to the beach. Both nations have somehow convinced the rest of the world they've cracked the code on weekends. Neither has, but they've cracked it more convincingly than the country that invented the TGIF mug.

I have spent enough weekends in both to report back with the required lack of diplomacy: one country's leisure is a spiritual discipline dressed as a hobby, and the other's is a shrug elevated to a national identity. Pack your own patience for both, because neither place is going to hurry up on your behalf.

## Do's & Don'ts: Bhutan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to a village archery match and actually watch — the singing and dancing between rounds is the real event | Assume archery is a quiet, meditative sport. It is loud, competitive, and frequently fuelled by ara (rice spirit) |
| Accept an invitation to a weekend picnic (bonchu) without expecting a fixed start time | Expect anything resembling a "quick" weekend outing — everything unfolds at its own unhurried pace |
| Dress modestly even for casual weekend outings, especially near monasteries and dzongs | Photograph religious ceremonies without asking — weekend festivals are not backdrops for your feed |

## Do's & Don'ts: Costa Rica

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say "pura vida" and mean it — it functions as hello, goodbye, thank you, and "it's fine, let it go" | Show up expecting punctuality. "Tico time" is real and it is not apologising to you |
| Learn to love plans that change three times before Saturday actually arrives | Confuse the tourist beach towns with how most Costa Ricans actually spend their weekends — inland and low-key |
| Bring cash and patience to smaller beach towns where card machines are more theoretical than functional | Assume every beach is swimmable — some Pacific breaks will happily rearrange your skeleton |

## Bhutan: Happiness by Appointment

Let's address the elephant — or rather, the takin, Bhutan's absurd national animal — in the room. Gross National Happiness is not a vibe. It is an actual policy framework with nine domains and thirty-three indicators, and the government surveys its own citizens on their psychological wellbeing with the same rigour most countries reserve for inflation data. It is, genuinely, one of the more interesting governance experiments on the planet. It is also not why weekends in Bhutan are good. Weekends in Bhutan are good because of archery.

Every Saturday and Sunday, village archery ranges — dochey — fill with men in ghos shooting bamboo arrows at targets roughly the size of a large dinner plate, from a distance that should be illegal. The matches themselves are almost beside the point. What you're actually watching is a full social institution: teams taunting each other with improvised songs when the opposing archer misses, women performing celebratory dances when their team scores, and enough ara circulating to explain why accuracy tends to decline as the afternoon progresses. This is not a quiet pastime. It is closer to a football match crossed with a wedding, and outsiders who wander in expecting Zen-garden tranquillity are in for a rude, delightful awakening.

Beyond archery, the Bhutanese weekend runs on family obligation and religious rhythm rather than leisure as the West understands it. Sunday isn't a day off so much as a day redirected — toward the dzong for a blessing, toward an elderly relative's house, toward whatever agricultural task the season demands. There is no real concept of the "treat yourself" weekend that dominates Western leisure culture, largely because the entire national philosophy is built around collective wellbeing rather than individual indulgence. You will not find a spa culture here in the Western sense, though you will find hot stone baths (dotsho) heated by literal river stones, which is either more authentic or less convenient than a jacuzzi, depending on how much you enjoy hauling rocks.

The tension for any visitor is that Bhutan does not want you moving fast. The country caps tourist numbers deliberately and charges a Sustainable Development Fee precisely to keep the pace glacial and the crowds thin. Your weekend, like everyone else's, will be shaped by altitude, distance, and a national unwillingness to rush anything for your convenience.

## Costa Rica: The Beach Doesn't Care About Your Itinerary

Costa Rica's approach to weekends is, on paper, the opposite of Bhutan's institutional wellbeing project — there's no ministry measuring anyone's joy here. And yet "pura vida" functions as its own kind of national indicator, deployed so constantly and sincerely that it has become genuinely impossible to be stressed near a Tico for more than about eleven minutes before someone tells you to let it go. This is either the healthiest cultural habit I've encountered or a extremely effective form of collective denial about traffic. Possibly both.

Weekends here revolve, unsurprisingly, around the coasts — Pacific for surf towns like Tamarindo and Santa Teresa, Caribbean for the slower, reggae-inflected rhythm of Puerto Viejo. But the tourist beach towns are not where most Costa Ricans actually spend their Saturdays. The real national weekend happens inland: family asados, football matches watched with genuine violence of feeling, and long lunches at sodas (the local answer to diners) that stretch well past the point any productivity-obsessed visitor would consider reasonable. Ticos treat the weekend as sacred family time first and recreation second, which means your invitation to "come by Sunday" might mean 11am, might mean 3pm, and will absolutely not be clarified further no matter how many times you ask.

Here is where I lose patience with people who call this country "exotic" while sipping a smoothie in an infinity pool built by a European hotel chain: Costa Rica's real leisure culture is not the resort. It's the ability to genuinely, structurally not care about being late, and to treat that as a form of wellness rather than a character flaw. The national parks — over a quarter of the country is protected land, which is more ecological ambition than most G7 nations manage — mean an actual weekend activity is "go look at a sloth," which remains, against all odds, never not delightful.

## The Verdict

Bhutan wins on originality and depth — nowhere else on Earth builds an entire government metric around collective joy and then backs it up with an actual national sport that doubles as a village-wide party. But it demands surrender: to altitude, to slowness, to a Sustainable Development Fee that makes spontaneity expensive. Costa Rica wins on accessibility and sheer, contagious ease — pura vida asks nothing of you except to stop checking your watch. If you want a weekend that changes how you think about happiness as a governed concept, book Bhutan. If you want a weekend that changes how much you care whether the plan happens on time, Costa Rica will cure you of caring within about 48 hours. Ticos already know they've won this argument. They're too relaxed to gloat about it, which is somehow the most annoying part.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Bhutan — paraphrased: a visitor was surprised that a "quiet archery match" turned into a three-hour event with singing, taunting, and enough rice wine to make the final rounds noticeably less accurate.*

> *Expat.com Costa Rica — paraphrased: newcomers repeatedly note that "Tico time" isn't rudeness, it's a genuine cultural operating system, and fighting it just produces stress no one else in the room is sharing.*

> *Internations Costa Rica — paraphrased: a longtime resident observed that the beach towns marketed to tourists bear almost no resemblance to how most Costa Rican families actually spend a Sunday, which is usually inland, loud, and centred on food.*

## Conclusion

Two small countries, two entirely different theories of the weekend, both somehow correct. Bhutan treats leisure as a communal ritual wrapped in archery and altitude; Costa Rica treats it as a philosophical stance you adopt the moment you land and can't ever fully abandon. Neither will optimise your Saturday. Neither cares that you wanted them to. If you're the sort of person who needs a firm itinerary to feel relaxed, do everyone a favour and stay home — these two nations have already decided, with or without your input, exactly how fast your weekend is going to move. Pura vida, and good luck with the archery.
