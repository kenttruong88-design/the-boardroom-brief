# Singapore Is a Sauna With a Receipt. Hong Kong Is Four Seasons and a Typhoon Waiting Room

🇸🇬 Singapore vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Singapore has one season. It is called "damp", and it lasts all year. The temperature sits around thirty degrees, the humidity hangs somewhere between unpleasant and personal, and the only reliable plan is to move from one air-conditioned box to another with the speed of someone fleeing a crime scene.

Hong Kong at least pretends to have variety. Four seasons, a lovely autumn, a winter that may require a coat and then, in summer, a hot and sticky spell with a typhoon waiting at the end of it like a punchline. Both cities are within a short flight of each other, share a time zone and a taste for tower blocks, and yet manage to suffer in completely different ways.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Singapore**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry an umbrella, for rain and for sun | Trust a clear sky at 2pm |
| Wear light, loose fabrics | Wear leather or wool and call it professional |
| Reapply SPF 30 or higher | Believe cloud cover is protection |
| Use malls as corridors between destinations | Walk the whole way outdoors in a good shirt |

**Hong Kong**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pack a coat for winter, because it can be windy | Assume Asia means permanent summer |
| Take typhoon warnings seriously and leave early | Treat a signal as an invitation to go sightseeing |
| Tape your windows when a big storm approaches | Leave the glass bare |
| Save the hiking for autumn | Climb a hill in August |

## Singapore

Singapore's climate is a policy problem that nobody can vote on. According to Expat Arrivals, the temperature hovers near 30°C all year, humidity usually runs 70 to 90 per cent, and that humidity, rather than the heat, is the main source of discomfort. Rain falls year-round, and the monsoon months at the turn of the year bring frequent showers. The UV can be extreme, so sun cream is not a holiday habit but a daily one.

The locals' survival kit is practical. A Singapore Expats guide written in 2017 says people spend a lot of time in shopping centres to escape the heat, and that offices are kept very cold. It also claims Singapore gets more rainfall than London, so an umbrella is standard issue. Its advice on dressing is brutal and sensible: light, loose clothing, not leather or wool. Business dress is usually a button-down shirt, with a suit a rare creature. The guide also warns that make-up may melt within a five-minute walk and that perfume fades faster in the heat, which is the sort of detail that tells you the writer has actually been outside.

What I admire is the infrastructure of cooling. Covered walkways, air-conditioned malls, connected stations: the city has been engineered so you can cross large stretches of it without ever feeling the weather on your skin. That is not a luxury. It is a coping mechanism, built in concrete. The sweat you lose when you do go outdoors is real. The guide says a body exercising can lose more than a litre of sweat, which is why people take to the water for sport and cool-down instead of going for a heroic jog at noon.

The catch is that you never really acclimatise. You just get used to being slightly damp, and to carrying a spare shirt as if you were an unusually fastidious plumber. The weather here does not change. It simply continues.

Singapore's other coping method is the body itself. The same guide notes that fruit such as melons, berries and apples are about 80 per cent water, so a cold fruit plate is not an indulgence but an engineering decision, and it suggests water sports such as canoeing, windsurfing and paddle boarding as ways of staying cool while exercising. That is the Singaporean philosophy of weather in a single sentence: if you cannot escape the heat, go and sit in the water it comes from. It sounds like a travel brochure and, for once, it is also advice.

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong gives you the opposite problem: a climate with a range, and therefore with surprises. Expat Arrivals lists spring from March to May, a summer from May to September with peaks around 33°C and plenty of humidity, an autumn from October to early December that it calls arguably the best time, and a cloudy, dry winter of around 15°C. Then there are typhoons. The season runs from May to November, and the page warns of multi-day rain, flooding and landslides, with warnings issued on a ranked scale through news and radio.

A seven-year resident posting on an Expat.com thread, under the name Hansson, gave a pleasingly practical rundown. Typhoons disrupt ferries and transport, employers usually let staff leave early in advance, and people tape their windows in a criss-cross pattern in case the glass goes. Summers are hot and humid, so a summer suit should be light wool, and malls are well air-conditioned for walking. In winter you want a coat, because it can be windy. This is the coping style of a city that has done this before: no drama, some masking tape, home by three.

A British expat quoted on the same site calls the outdoor life and the good weather a real improvement on the UK, which tells you more about the UK than about Hong Kong. But it is also true. Autumn in Hong Kong, with the dry air and the hills behind the harbour, is one of the best things the region does. The mistake is thinking you can hike in the middle of August.

The weather here has a plot. It builds, it threatens, it arrives, it lifts, and everyone goes back to the office on Monday as if nothing happened.

One more thing, for the pedants. A removals company's comparison of the two cities classes Hong Kong as humid subtropical, with hot summers and a typhoon season, and Singapore as tropical, hot and humid all year with frequent rain, and notes that both sit in the same GMT+8 time zone. It gives no figures, which is exactly the problem with most comparisons of these cities: the numbers matter less than the texture. Hansson worked near the Landmark in Central and took the ferry home to Discovery Bay, which is a commute that gives you a very intimate relationship with the sea state. In Singapore the commute is a corridor. In Hong Kong it can be a weather event.

## The Verdict

Hong Kong wins on climate, narrowly and with caveats. A place that gives you an actual autumn, a bearable winter and a reason to wear a jacket is better for the soul than one that offers a permanent warm flannel over the face. Typhoon season is annoying, but it is an event with a start and an end. Singapore's weather is just a condition.

I do respect how thoroughly Singapore has built a city around its own weather. If your priority is being able to get anywhere without sunstroke, it is hard to beat. If your priority is variety, light, hills and the occasional cool evening, it is not even a contest.

Choose Singapore if you like predictability and a rigorous relationship with air conditioning. Choose Hong Kong if you want weather that has opinions, as long as you also like tape.

## Conclusion

Weather shapes a city's character more than any government does. Singapore's is a long exhale of warm air. Hong Kong's is a mood swing with a forecast.

Pack for both: an umbrella, a light shirt, a coat you will use twice a year and a roll of tape you will hope never to need. And whichever you pick, remember the rule that every local in both places has learned the hard way.

Never underestimate the sky. It is always planning something.
