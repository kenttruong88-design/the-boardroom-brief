# One Country Will Judge Your German. The Other Will Just Switch to Hand Gestures and Get On With It.

🇦🇹 Austria vs 🇵🇪 Peru — Language Barrier Experiences

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Austria has a curious relationship with language learners: it expects you to speak German, but the German you learned in a classroom bears only a passing resemblance to actual Viennese dialect, which swaps vocabulary, softens consonants, and generally behaves as though standard German were merely a polite suggestion. Peru, meanwhile, operates across Spanish, Quechua, Aymara, and dozens of Indigenous Amazonian languages depending entirely on which region you're standing in, and has developed, out of sheer practical necessity, one of the most patient, gesture-friendly, improvisational approaches to cross-language communication I've encountered anywhere.

I once confidently ordered what I thought was a coffee in textbook German at a Vienna café and was met with a blank stare, because the word I needed was Viennese, not German, and nobody had mentioned this distinction existed. In the Peruvian Andes, meanwhile, I once successfully negotiated directions, a fair taxi price, and a genuinely warm conversation entirely through hand gestures, pointing, and the eight words of Spanish I had at the time, because my failure to communicate was met with patience rather than judgment. Austria expects fluency and quietly disapproves of its absence. Peru expects nothing and rewards any effort at all, generously.

## Do's & Don'ts: Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn some Austrian dialect vocabulary beyond standard German | Assume textbook German will get you through every conversation |
| Use formal "Sie" address with strangers, officials, and elders | Switch to informal "du" prematurely — it can read as disrespectful |
| Attempt German first, even badly, before switching to English | Expect government offices to default to English out of convenience |
| Apologise briefly for language mistakes — it's appreciated, not required | Get discouraged by dialect confusion — even Germans struggle with it |

## Do's & Don'ts: Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Spanish greetings — effort is warmly rewarded everywhere | Assume Spanish alone works in every rural or Andean region |
| Use gestures and pointing freely — it's a normal part of communication | Get frustrated if a local speaks Quechua as their first language, not Spanish |
| Carry a translation app for remote areas with limited Spanish overlap | Expect uniform accents or vocabulary between Lima and the highlands |
| Smile and stay patient — warmth communicates as much as words | Assume city-taught Spanish phrases translate directly to indigenous communities |

## Austria: Precision, Dialect, and Quiet Judgment

Austrian German presents a genuinely disorienting trap for anyone who studied "standard" German in school, expecting it to function identically in Vienna, Salzburg, or Graz. It doesn't. Austrian German — and Viennese dialect especially — swaps out core vocabulary (a tomato becomes a *Paradeiser*, not a *Tomate*; a potato becomes an *Erdapfel*, not a *Kartoffel*), softens certain sounds, and layers in enough regional idiom that even fluent German speakers from Germany sometimes find themselves quietly lost in a Viennese conversation. Learners who arrive having diligently studied Hochdeutsch often discover, deflatingly, that their textbook fluency doesn't translate cleanly to ordering lunch.

Compounding this is a broader Austrian cultural expectation that German, in some form, is simply the baseline requirement for functioning in daily life — bureaucratic offices, in particular, can be notoriously unwilling to conduct business in English even when staff are clearly capable of it, treating the expectation of English service as a presumption that inconveniences the system rather than a reasonable accommodation. There's also a real formality embedded in the language itself: the distinction between formal *Sie* and informal *du* carries genuine social weight, and switching to informal address prematurely with an official, an elder, or even a new colleague can land as presumptuous or disrespectful.

The upside, once you push through the dialect confusion, is a culture that genuinely respects visible effort. Attempting German first, even with an accent thick enough to cut glass, before defaulting to English is noticed and appreciated, and Austrians will often meet a sincere, halting attempt with real warmth, even if the conversation eventually needs to continue in English to actually get anything done.

## Peru: Improvisation Across Many Tongues

Peru's linguistic landscape is genuinely more complex than most visitors expect, shaped by the enduring presence of Quechua — spoken by millions, particularly across the Andean highlands, and holding official status alongside Spanish — plus Aymara around Lake Titicaca and dozens of distinct Indigenous languages spoken across Amazonian regions. Spanish functions as the practical lingua franca almost everywhere, but assuming it's every Peruvian's first or most comfortable language, particularly in rural highland communities, is a mistake that visitors make constantly and locals have long since learned to work around with grace.

This linguistic diversity has produced a genuinely warm, low-pressure culture around cross-language communication. Where Austria can feel like it's quietly grading your grammar, Peru — especially outside Lima's more formal business environments — treats communication as a collaborative, improvisational effort involving whatever combination of broken Spanish, hand gestures, pointing at menu items, and patient repetition gets the job done. I've watched market vendors in Cusco switch fluidly between Quechua with other vendors, Spanish with Peruvian customers, and a genuinely inventive pidgin of gestures and simple words with tourists, without ever seeming remotely bothered by the constant code-switching required of them.

Regional accents add another layer: Lima Spanish, coastal Spanish, and Andean Spanish (often influenced by Quechua phonetics and rhythm) can sound meaningfully different from one another, and travellers who've only practiced with a Lima-based tutor or app sometimes find themselves briefly recalibrating their ear on arrival in Arequipa or Puno. None of this seems to bother Peruvians much; there's a broad cultural acceptance that communication across this many languages and accents requires flexibility from everyone involved, not just visitors.

## The Verdict

Peru wins on warmth and flexibility — nowhere else have I felt so unjudged for stumbling through basic phrases while a stranger patiently helped me get there through pointing and goodwill alone. Austria wins on the reward for genuine effort — push past the dialect confusion and formal address rules, and you'll find a culture that respects precision and diligence in equal measure. If you want a place where imperfect communication is met with total patience and creativity, Peru will never make you feel small for trying. If you want a place that will politely, quietly expect you to eventually get it right, Austria's exacting standards are waiting, alongside a slightly confusing menu of regional vocabulary nobody warned you about.

## Conclusion

Austria and Peru sit at opposite ends of the language-barrier experience — one exacting and quietly formal, the other warm and endlessly improvisational — and both will teach you something true about communication that no phrasebook can. Austria will make you a more precise, more careful speaker, purely out of self-preservation. Peru will make you a more creative, more confident communicator, purely because gestures and goodwill turn out to carry you further than you'd expect. Learn the Viennese word for tomato before you order lunch. Learn to point cheerfully and smile in the Andes. Both skills will serve you long after you've left.
