# Two Kingdoms in the Sky and on the Plain, Both Run on Borrowed Sugar

🇸🇿 Eswatini and 🇱🇸 Lesotho

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Africa's two remaining absolute-ish monarchies sit within a few hundred kilometres of each other, and both get flattened in Western coverage into a single condescending category: "landlocked, poor, forgotten." What that framing conveniently skips is that neighbourhood life in Eswatini and Lesotho runs on a form of social infrastructure most Western suburbs quietly dismantled decades ago in favour of privacy fences and doorbell cameras.

Eswatini, the last absolute monarchy in Africa and still occasionally called Swaziland by people who haven't updated their vocabulary since 2018, organises community life around the chieftaincy system and communal land. Lesotho, entirely surrounded by South Africa and sitting almost entirely above 1,000 metres, builds its village life around the mountain, the flock, and an interdependence that altitude and isolation made non-negotiable centuries ago. Let's get into what that actually looks like day to day.

## Do's & Don'ts: Eswatini

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet the local chief or headman when settling in a rural or peri-urban community | Assume you can simply move into communal land without proper introduction through the chieftaincy structure |
| Participate in umcwasho or community labour days if invited — refusal is noticed | Treat community work obligations as optional extras rather than genuine social expectations |
| Share food generously when neighbours visit unannounced, which they will | Treat an unannounced neighbour visit as an imposition rather than the norm |
| Attend Incwala or Umhlanga ceremonies respectfully as a guest if welcomed | Wander into restricted ceremonial areas without explicit invitation |

## Do's & Don'ts: Lesotho

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a Basotho blanket in cold months — it's practical, not costume | Treat the blanket as a tourist souvenir rather than genuine daily attire |
| Greet everyone you pass on a mountain path, even strangers | Walk past someone on a rural path without a greeting; it reads as deeply rude |
| Accept that herding boys and shepherds hold real social standing in village hierarchy | Dismiss herding as a lesser occupation; it's a respected rite of passage |
| Join in communal work parties (matsema) when your village organises one | Opt out of communal obligations without a genuinely accepted reason |

## Eswatini: The Chieftaincy as Neighbourhood Infrastructure

Community life in Eswatini, particularly outside the small urban centres of Mbabane and Manzini, still runs substantially through the chieftaincy system, where local chiefs (indvuna and umphakatsi structures) hold real authority over communal land allocation and dispute resolution. This isn't ceremonial window-dressing left over from a bygone era; it's an active, functioning system that shapes who lives where, how land disputes get settled, and how newcomers, including foreigners, are formally welcomed into a community.

What surprises outsiders most is how genuinely porous the boundary between "neighbour" and "family" becomes in practice. Unannounced visits are the norm rather than the exception, and a neighbour showing up at your door expecting to be fed isn't presumption, it's simply how reciprocal obligation works here — you'll be on the receiving end of that same hospitality soon enough, and keeping strict tabs on who owes whom a meal would be considered faintly bizarre. Communal labour days, sometimes still organised through structures with roots in traditional umcwasho customs, bring neighbours together for shared tasks — clearing land, building repairs — and treating these as skippable is a genuine social misstep that gets remembered.

The Incwala kingship ceremony and the Umhlanga reed dance, both major national events, ripple down to neighbourhood level in ways visitors rarely anticipate — entire communities reorganise their week around participation, transport, and preparation, and foreigners who've built genuine local relationships often find themselves informally included, provided they approach the invitation with real respect rather than treating it as an exotic photo opportunity, which, again, it very much is not.

## Lesotho: The Mountain Kingdom's Interdependence

Lesotho's nickname, the Mountain Kingdom, isn't just scenic branding — it's the actual organising fact of community life. With most of the country sitting above 1,000 metres and significant portions only reachable by horse, pony, or serious hiking, villages here developed an interdependence that isn't romantic sentiment, it's survival logic. The Basotho blanket, thick, patterned, genuinely essential in the punishing mountain winters, is worn daily by people of every age, and treating it as a quaint cultural costume rather than the practical, near-universal cold-weather garment it is marks you instantly as someone who hasn't spent a July night at altitude here.

Greeting culture in Lesotho runs deep even in the most remote settings — passing a stranger on a mountain path without a greeting isn't just impolite, it can read as genuinely alarming, since in a landscape this sparsely populated, every person you encounter is potentially someone you'll need to rely on. Herding remains a central social institution, and the herding boys who spend months at a time in remote mountain posts tending livestock occupy a respected position in village hierarchy, a rite of passage that shapes standing well into adulthood — dismissing it as menial labour, as some outside observers carelessly do, badly misreads its social weight.

Communal work parties, known as matsema, still mobilise villages for shared agricultural or construction tasks, coordinated informally but with real social consequence for those who opt out without accepted reason. What foreigners repeatedly underestimate is how quickly this interdependence extends to them, too, once they're genuinely settled — a mountain village doesn't have the population density to sustain strangers who keep entirely to themselves, and isolation here isn't really an option anyone's offering you.

## The Verdict

Eswatini wins on ceremonial richness — the chieftaincy system and national ceremonies like Incwala give neighbourhood life a formal, structured texture that's genuinely distinctive on the continent. Lesotho wins on sheer interdependence forged by geography — an altitude and isolation that made community obligation not a cultural nicety but a basic condition of survival, and that history still visibly shapes how neighbours treat each other today.

Both kingdoms will fold you into their social fabric whether or not you were prepared for it. Resist that at your peril; embrace it and you'll find a version of neighbourliness most Western suburbs privatised out of existence generations ago.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a Mbabane-based volunteer paraphrased: I was mildly startled the first time a neighbour walked in unannounced expecting dinner, and mortified months later when I realised how normal my own unannounced visits had become to them in return.*

> *expat.com forum — a Maseru-based teacher noted that skipping a greeting to a stranger on a mountain path got genuinely concerned follow-up questions from a companion, since in that setting silence reads as something being wrong.*

> *Internations Southern Africa forum — a longtime resident paraphrased: buying a Basotho blanket as a souvenir felt silly until the first proper mountain winter, after which it became the single most-used item in the entire wardrobe.*

## Conclusion

Neither Eswatini nor Lesotho organises neighbourhood life around the polite, arm's-length distance most Western communities have settled for. Both still run on genuine mutual obligation, formal and informal alike, and both will absorb you into that obligation the moment you stop resisting it. Bring food to share before you're asked. Greet the stranger on the path. You didn't move somewhere "forgotten" — you moved somewhere that never outsourced its sense of community to an app in the first place.
