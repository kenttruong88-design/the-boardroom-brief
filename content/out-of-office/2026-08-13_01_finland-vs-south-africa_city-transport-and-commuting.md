# One Country's Trains Apologise for Being 40 Seconds Late. The Other's Roads Are a Philosophical Debate.

🇫🇮 🇿🇦 Finland vs South Africa
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Finland runs its transport system the way it runs everything else: with the quiet, faintly unsettling competence of a nation that has never once needed to raise its voice. Trains arrive to the minute. Buses depart to the minute. If the 8:14 to Espoo is ninety seconds late, the driver looks personally wounded, and somewhere a Finn is already drafting a strongly worded but extremely polite email. South Africa, meanwhile, treats the timetable as a charming foreign custom, something to be admired from a respectful distance, the way you'd admire abstract art you don't quite understand and don't intend to buy.

I have lived in both. I have stood on a Helsinki platform at minus fifteen, boots welded to the concrete, watching a train materialise at the exact second the app promised. And I have stood on a Johannesburg pavement watching a minibus taxi reverse down a highway on-ramp because the off-ramp "felt long today." Both experiences taught me something about commuting. Only one taught me to update my will first.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇫🇮 Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Validate your ticket before boarding — inspectors are silent and merciless | Make small talk on the tram; Finns treat pre-9am chat as a hostile act |
| Learn the HSL app; it is the only oracle you need | Expect anyone to hold a door, ever |
| Cycle through winter — studded tyres exist for a reason | Be shocked when the bus leaves exactly on time, even as you sprint toward it |
| Stand right on escalators, without exception | Sit next to a stranger if any other seat exists |

### 🇿🇦 South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use Uber or Bolt rather than freelancing your own route | Hail a minibus taxi without knowing the hand signals |
| Keep doors locked and windows up at robots (traffic lights) | Drive after dark through unfamiliar neighbourhoods |
| Budget double whatever time you think a journey needs | Assume a green light is a binding promise — check both ways, always |
| Befriend someone with a reliable car; the Gautrain only goes so far | Expect load-shedding to consult the train schedule — it will win |

## Finland: Precision as a Personality Trait

Finnish commuting is less a transport system than a national character reference. The trains run because Finns have collectively decided that chaos is embarrassing, and Finns would rather die than be embarrassed in public. HSL's app tells you, to the minute, when your tram will arrive, and it is right so consistently that using it feels less like checking a schedule and more like consulting a very calm oracle who has never once lied to you.

Helsinki's metro is clean enough to eat off, which no one does, because eating on public transport here is treated with the mild horror usually reserved for eating during a funeral. Cycling infrastructure is so good that Finns bike through blizzards that would have most nations declaring a state of emergency, wrapped in reflective gear because visibility matters more than vanity at 3pm in December when the sun has already clocked out for the day.

What nobody warns you about is the silence. A Finnish bus at rush hour is quieter than most libraries. Eye contact is minimal, conversation is rarer, and the unwritten rule is that your commute is your own private sanctuary, not a networking opportunity. Try to chat with a stranger about the weather and you will be met with the particular Finnish stare that says, politely but unmistakably, why are you doing this to me. Once you stop taking it personally, it's oddly restful — nobody wants anything from you, the train is never late, and you can spend twenty-five minutes staring at a lake in peace. It's commuting as meditation, and I have never once missed a connection here in three years, which after a childhood of European rail chaos felt almost suspicious.

## South Africa: Negotiated Chaos, Occasionally Beautiful

South African commuting operates on an entirely different physics, one where the timetable is aspirational rather than binding. The minibus taxi industry — informal, vast, and moving roughly 15 million commuters a day — runs on a language of hand signals through the windscreen, honks, and a driver's personal relationship with risk. It is simultaneously the most efficient way to get almost anywhere and the single most terrifying transport experience I've had outside of a war zone press trip, and I include that comparison with complete sincerity.

The Gautrain, by contrast, is a genuinely lovely piece of infrastructure — fast, clean, air-conditioned, running between Johannesburg, Pretoria, and the airport with an efficiency that feels imported from another country entirely, because functionally it was. But it covers a tiny fraction of the metro area, so most people are back to cars, minibuses, or the extraordinary patience required to wait for a bus that may or may not exist today.

Then there's load-shedding — scheduled power cuts that turn every traffic light into a four-way stop negotiated purely through eye contact and nerve. South African drivers have developed, out of sheer necessity, a traffic etiquette at dead robots that is more sophisticated and more genuinely considerate than anything I've seen in a country with working infrastructure: everyone takes turns, nobody honks first, and it somehow works better than you'd expect. It's chaos with a code of honour, which is, in its own way, more human than Finland's silent precision. You will arrive late. You will also, more often than not, arrive having had an actual conversation with a stranger, which in Helsinki would require a court order.

## The Verdict

If you want your life to run like a Swiss watch built by someone even more uptight than the Swiss, Finland wins outright — nowhere else will a country's entire commuting culture reorganise itself around your ability to trust a printed timetable completely. But if you want commuting that occasionally produces a genuine human moment amid the mayhem — a taxi driver who waits for you, a stranger who negotiates a dead traffic light with you like an old dance partner — South Africa's disorder has a warmth Finland's efficiency will never bother to fake.

Neither is objectively correct. One will get you to your meeting on time and completely alone in your own head. The other might get you there twenty minutes late with a new phone number in your contacts and a story you'll tell for a decade. Choose your relationship with time and risk accordingly, and for God's sake, learn the hand signals before you get in that taxi.

## Conclusion

I won't pretend one system is "better," because better depends entirely on what you're optimising for — sanity or serendipity, silence or spectacle. What I will say, having lived the reality rather than the brochure version of both, is that Finland will make you calmer and South Africa will make you more resilient, and only one of those skills is transferable to literally every other country you'll ever live in. Learn the hand signals. Learn to sit in silence. Do not, under any circumstances, confuse the two systems' rules — a Finn will not appreciate your improvised hand signal for "going straight," and a South African taxi will not wait ninety seconds for your apology.
