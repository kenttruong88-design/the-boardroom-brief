# The Dutch Cycle to Work Through Rain They Consider Decorative. The Belgians Sit in a Company Car and Call It Fringe Benefits

🇳🇱 Netherlands vs 🇧🇪 Belgium

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two small countries share a border, a railway and an unfortunate rivalry. They also share a commute problem and have solved it in opposite directions. The Dutch took the car out of the equation and built an entire civilisation around the bicycle. The Belgians looked at the car and said, "yes, but what if the employer pays for it".

If you are being posted between Amsterdam and Antwerp you will discover that the 160 kilometres separating them is the widest cultural gap in Western Europe. One side thinks the other is smug, one side thinks the other is stuck in traffic, and both are right.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Netherlands**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a second-hand bike and a very good lock | Leave a bike unlocked, even for a minute |
| Fit front and rear lights at night | Cycle in the dark without them and hope |
| Check out with your card at the end of a trip | Forget to check out and pay the maximum fare |
| Avoid the train with a bike at rush hour | Wheel a bike onto the 8 a.m. NS train |

**Belgium**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your employer about a company car | Assume you must buy a vehicle |
| Budget extra time for the Antwerp and Brussels rings | Promise to be there in half an hour |
| Buy a De Lijn MOBIB card for the bus and tram | Rely on late-night buses |
| Use shared bikes for the last mile | Leave a bike unattended in Leuven |

## Netherlands

The Netherlands has made cycling so ordinary that nobody mentions it. The IamExpat cycling guide, updated January 2026, reports more than 23 million bikes for about 18 million people, roughly 37,000 kilometres of cycle paths, and an average of around 1,094 kilometres cycled per person per year. Utrecht's station bike park holds 12,500 bikes. About five percent of bikes are reported stolen each year, and police rarely do much about it. A second-hand bike costs between 50 and 250 euros, a new one from about 250. You must have front and rear lights at night or risk a fine, and helmets are not mandatory.

The trains play along. Expat.com's transport guide says that more than a quarter of Dutch journeys are made by bike. The OV-chipkaart costs 7.50 euros, with anonymous cards being phased out as contactless bank cards take over, and forgetting to check out charges you the maximum fare, a lesson every newcomer learns once. A Dal Voordeel subscription costs about 5.50 euros a month for 40 percent off-peak discounts. Children aged four to eleven ride free on Kids Vrij. And bikes are banned on NS trains from 6.30 to 9.00 and 16.00 to 18.30, which means that the sacred national vehicle must be left outside the sacred national railway at the exact hour you need it.

The system is hard to fault, with one exception. It assumes you live and work in the Randstad. Car-free life is much harder outside it, and with small children it is a negotiation, not a lifestyle. A forum poster who had been commuting about sixty kilometres in Belgium in thirty to forty minutes, in a company car, worried that the Dutch offer covered only fuel and left him reliant on bike and public transport. A user labelled a UK-and-Netherlands expert replied that company cars are uncommon in the Netherlands outside sales, and joked that commuting from anywhere in Belgium to Amersfoort would be unbearable.

That exchange is the whole comparison in miniature. The Dutch consider the car a tool. The Belgians consider it a benefit.

## Belgium

Belgium is a commuting champion for the wrong reasons. A September 2026 piece on newmobility.news, summarising an SD Worx survey, says Belgians commute an average of 61 minutes a day, the longest of sixteen European countries (the international average is 48). Thirteen percent of Belgian employees commute two hours or more every day, against eight percent internationally. Only about one in four finds public transport convenient and reliable. Roughly two-thirds of commuters mainly drive, more than 80 percent in Wallonia, and about half of Brussels workers live outside the region.

At the heart of it sits the company car. The Brussels Times, in an October 2026 piece, reports that about 15 percent of Belgian employees have one, that the tax formula undervalues the benefit, and that fuel cards can cover personal and foreign trips. Abolishing company cars would save an estimated five billion euros, according to the Federal Planning Bureau. A Vrije Universiteit Brussel economist says it is far more advantageous to have a company car than to own your own. A politician quoted in the same article says he would rather swallow a lightbulb than abolish them. Nothing about that sentence is surprising.

Public transport is real but patchy. In Leuven, the Expat.com guide lists De Lijn fares of three euros a single and 37 euros a month, with Friday and Saturday night buses running about every ninety minutes, a Blue-bike scheme at 62 locations for twelve euros a year, and a warning that bike theft is a genuine risk. A train to Brussels costs around nine euros one way.

The cross-border commuters on Reddit tell the grim version. One said he lived in Hoogstraten and worked in north Rotterdam and found the days "long exhausting". Others said that NS international trains are the first to be cancelled in any disruption, and that the Antwerp ring road and the Haringvliet bridges are chronic bottlenecks.

Consider the money. The Expat.com guide to Leuven lists parking at 2.50 euros for the first hour, rising to five euros an hour from the fourth, and a train from Brussels Airport that takes about sixteen minutes with a 6.90 euro supplement. A Swapfiets rental bike starts at around 21 euros a month. In the Netherlands, the first 24 hours of parking a bike at an NS station are free. The Dutch have made the cheap option the easy option. The Belgians have made the expensive option the subsidised one. Policy is never neutral, and neither is the commute it produces.

## The Verdict

The Netherlands wins, and it is not close, unless you value sitting in a heated leather seat while a spreadsheet ruins your afternoon. The Dutch commute is cheaper, healthier and more pleasant. The Belgian commute is longer, more expensive in time, and propped up by a tax subsidy that distorts the whole market.

But fairness requires me to note the Belgian argument. A company car is a genuine benefit. The roads, when empty, are fine. And if you live in a Flemish village and work in Brussels, a bicycle is a hobby, not a solution.

My verdict: the Netherlands for anyone who can live in a city and wants a life that includes daylight. Belgium for anyone whose employer is handing out fuel cards and who has made peace with the ring road. And for anyone who must commute across the border, my deepest sympathy and a flask of something strong.

## Conclusion

Your commute is the first and last thing that happens to you every day. It shapes your mood more than your salary does. In the Netherlands it is a ride through the rain with a smug expression and a good lock. In Belgium it is a slow conversation with a satnav.

Choose the one that suits your temperament. If you like wind in your face, go Dutch. If you like heated seats and a fuel card, go Belgian.

Just do not try to do both in the same week. The border crossing will not forgive you.
