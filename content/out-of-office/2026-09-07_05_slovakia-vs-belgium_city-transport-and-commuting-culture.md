# One City's Trams Run on Time. The Other's Ring Road Is a Cautionary Tale.

🇸🇰 Slovakia · 🇧🇪 Belgium
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Bratislava runs a small, unglamorous, and genuinely excellent public transport network that nobody outside Central Europe ever talks about, largely because there's nothing dramatic to say about a tram that simply shows up when it's supposed to. Brussels, the self-appointed capital of the European Union, runs a transport system so congested that its own ring road has become a running joke among the diplomats and bureaucrats forced to sit in it daily, commuting to meetings about, among other things, sustainable urban mobility. One of these cities solved commuting by not overcomplicating it. The other is still negotiating with itself.

I have caught a Bratislava tram with thirty seconds to spare and had it arrive exactly on schedule, and I have sat in Brussels traffic long enough to finish a podcast episode, start another, and begin questioning my life choices. Only one of these experiences left me impressed.

## Do's & Don'ts: Slovakia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy tram and bus tickets before boarding via the app or machine — inspectors do check, and fines are steep | Expect much English signage outside central Bratislava; learn the basic route-number system instead |
| Take advantage of free intercity rail if you're a student or senior — a genuinely underrated national policy | Assume Bratislava's small size means poor coverage; the tram network is dense and reliable for the city's footprint |
| Validate your ticket the moment you board, every time, without exception | Jaywalk near tram tracks; Slovak trams do not stop quickly and drivers will not be sympathetic |

## Do's & Don'ts: Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the metro and tram (STIB/MIVB) rather than driving anywhere near the city centre during peak hours | Attempt to drive the Brussels ring road at rush hour unless you enjoy existential reflection |
| Get a MOBIB card for seamless travel across Brussels' integrated bus, tram, and metro system | Assume Belgian efficiency applies to traffic planning the way it applies to, say, bureaucracy or beer regulation |
| Cycle if you can — protected bike lanes have expanded significantly and cut commute stress dramatically | Underestimate how bad it gets during a transit strike; Belgium has a well-earned reputation for these |

## Slovakia: Unglamorous, Unbothered, and It Just Works

Bratislava does not have the transport reputation of Vienna, an hour up the Danube, or Prague, a few hours north, and this is entirely undeserved. The city runs a genuinely well-integrated network of trams, trolleybuses, and buses under the Dopravný podnik Bratislava that covers a compact urban footprint with a frequency and reliability that would be the envy of cities three times its size. Trams here don't announce themselves with fanfare; they simply arrive, roughly when the schedule says they will, which after enough time in less disciplined transit systems starts to feel like a small daily miracle rather than a baseline expectation.

The ticketing system rewards a bit of local knowledge: single tickets, time-based tickets, and the increasingly popular mobile app all work across the entire network, and the fine for riding without a validated ticket is steep enough that even budget travelers quickly learn to comply. What genuinely surprises visitors, though, is Slovakia's national policy of free intercity rail travel for students and seniors — a quietly generous piece of social infrastructure that gets almost no international attention despite meaningfully shaping how younger and older Slovaks move around their own country.

The trade-off, if there is one, is charm rather than function: Bratislava's trams and buses are practical rather than picturesque, and the network's small scale means there's no sprawling metro to marvel at, no architectural showpiece stations. It's a system built entirely for the people who actually live there rather than for anyone's Instagram feed, and once you've adjusted your expectations accordingly, it becomes very hard to find a genuine complaint. It simply works, quietly, every day, which is more than most European capitals can say with a straight face.

## Belgium: Where the Ring Road Becomes a Personality Trait

Brussels presents a genuinely strange paradox: a city that hosts the EU's central institutions, is stuffed with capable transport planners, and still can't shake a reputation for some of the worst urban traffic in Western Europe. The Brussels ring road (R0) has achieved a kind of dark celebrity status among anyone who has ever had to use it during a weekday commute — congestion here isn't an occasional bad day, it's a structural, near-daily condition that has shaped how an entire generation of Bruxellois think about their own city's usability.

The public transport that does exist — STIB/MIVB's integrated metro, tram, and bus network — is, credit where due, genuinely solid within the city itself, with a unified MOBIB card that makes hopping between modes frictionless once you're inside it. The problem isn't the transit network's design; it's that Brussels' sprawling commuter catchment, drawing workers in from Flanders and Wallonia alike, has never been matched by regional rail or transit capacity ambitious enough to actually pull people out of cars, leaving the ring road to absorb a volume of daily traffic it was never built to handle gracefully.

Belgium's cycling infrastructure has improved substantially and represents the most genuinely hopeful trend in the city's transport story — protected lanes have expanded rapidly, and a visible, growing population of commuter cyclists has emerged as the group most likely to arrive at work calm rather than furious. But the country's well-earned reputation for transit strikes, a recurring feature of Belgian labour relations, means even committed public-transport users learn to keep a backup plan handy. Nobody who's lived through a Belgian transit strike season schedules anything time-critical without a contingency.

## The Verdict

Slovakia wins this comparison by simply not creating the problem Belgium has spent decades failing to solve. Bratislava's transport isn't glamorous, isn't internationally celebrated, and isn't trying to be — it's a modest, well-run system serving a modest, well-organized city, and the absence of drama is itself the achievement. Brussels, for all its institutional weight and genuine improvements in cycling and transit integration, remains held hostage by a ring road that has become a running punchline even among the people whose job it is to fix these things for a living.

If your metric for a good transport system is "did I get where I needed to go without incident," Bratislava wins comfortably. If your metric is "did I get a good story about suffering for it," Brussels remains undefeated.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Bratislava forum — a German transplant wrote that he was almost offended by how boring his daily tram commute became, having come from a city where transit delays were a near-daily conversation topic.*

> *Internations Brussels — one member described her first Brussels rush hour as "the moment I understood why everyone here has such strong opinions about which side street bypasses the ring road."*

> *Quora — a Brussels-based EU staffer answering "why is Brussels traffic so bad" argued the core issue isn't capacity but institutional fragmentation — three regions, multiple transit authorities, and no single body with the mandate to fix the ring road holistically.*

## Conclusion

Bratislava will keep quietly running its trams on schedule, entirely uninterested in international recognition it hasn't sought and doesn't need. Brussels will keep hosting summits about sustainable mobility while its own ring road remains a case study in exactly the problem those summits are meant to solve. If you want a transport system that simply does its job, go where nobody's talking about it. The silence is the endorsement.
