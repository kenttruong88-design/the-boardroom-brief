# Canadians Apologise to Lampposts, and Moldovans Will Not Let You Refuse the Wine

🇲🇩 Moldova vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Canada runs on a single, load-bearing syllable. "Sorry" is what you say when you bump into someone, when someone bumps into you, when a door arrives before you do and when the weather has been unreasonable. It is not an apology so much as a lubricant, and I have watched a grown man apologise to a bollard in Toronto with real feeling. The country has built an entire civilisation on the principle that everyone must be seen to be trying.

Moldova runs on a different sentence: "Have you eaten?" It is a country where a guest is a small act of God, where a toast is a contract and where refusing a glass without a decent excuse is taken as a statement about your character. Both places are polite. They just disagree violently about what politeness is for. In Canada it keeps strangers comfortably apart. In Moldova it pulls them to the table whether they like it or not.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇲🇩 Moldova ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn a few Romanian or Russian greetings | Expect everyone to switch to English for you |
| Bring flowers or wine when invited home | Arrive empty-handed and expect to be forgiven |
| Take your shoes off at the door | Stride across the carpet in your boots |
| Wait for the host to begin eating | Dive in because the table looks ready |

| 🇨🇦 Canada ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say sorry, and mean it in a purely structural way | Treat an apology as an admission of guilt |
| Hold the door and thank whoever holds one for you | Let a door swing back on the person behind |
| Tip 15 to 20 percent and expect the screen to suggest more | Walk out of a restaurant without tipping |
| Text if you will be late | Turn up late without a word |

## 🇲🇩 Moldova

I will be straight about the evidence. The best English-language guide I could reach is a ten-point list from Expat Exchange, which carries no named author voice and no figures, and a couple of its points read like generic advice that could be stapled to any country. I have taken only what is plausible and consistent, and I would treat the rest as a sketch. What it does say is revealing. Learn greetings in Romanian or Russian. Bring flowers or wine when you are invited into a home. Take off your shoes on entry. Dress conservatively, especially in rural areas and around churches and monasteries. Be punctual. Wait for your host to start eating.

Then there is the toasting. The guide says toasts are common and that turning one down is seen as impolite, with the advice that a non-drinker should explain why. This is the part Western Europeans underestimate. In Moldova the toast is not a flourish before the meal, it is the meal's grammar. A refusal without a reason leaves a hole in the sentence, and the table will notice it. If you do not drink, say so early, warmly and with a story. If you do drink, pace yourself, because the host's duty to refill you is stronger than your duty to stay vertical.

Other norms are quieter. In Orthodox churches women cover their heads and men remove hats. Politics, especially anything touching on Romania and Russia, is a minefield best left to people with deeper roots in it than you have. On 1 March there is Mărțișor, the custom of exchanging small red-and-white trinkets, which is the closest thing the country has to a mass act of gentle public sentiment. Friends greet with a kiss on the cheek, which is a long way from the handshake and arm's-length posture of the north Atlantic.

What strikes me is how much of Moldovan public behaviour is relational rather than procedural. You are not following rules about space and queues so much as reading the people in front of you. That is lovely when you are welcome and exhausting when you are a stranger who has not yet been assigned a role. Learn the three or four gestures that matter, show real curiosity about the place and you will be forgiven most of what you get wrong.

## 🇨🇦 Canada

Canada, by contrast, has rules, and they are on the whole written down in articles aimed at newcomers. Remitly's guide to Canadian politeness lists the greatest hits: a handshake with direct eye contact, a hug or a cheek kiss among close friends and in French-Canadian regions, arm's-length personal space, a quick "sorry" after contact of any kind, orderly queues, punctuality or a text if you are going to be late, and tipping of around 15 to 20 percent in restaurants. Conversation begins with the weather and sports and moves on to personal matters slowly. Prying questions and divisive jokes are poor form, and a debate should be polite enough that everyone leaves with their dignity.

For the lived version I turn to Alona Vasylieva, a Ukrainian-born writer in Ontario who had spent about four years in Canada when she wrote for Narcity this month. Her observations are the ones that never make the official leaflets. The payment terminal prompts for around 25 percent, even for a coffee you poured yourself, and she feels the pressure to tip everywhere. Pedestrians do not reliably get priority at unlit crossings, and indicators are often skipped. The office holiday event is typically a midday Christmas lunch, not an all-night party. Customers wait to be seated, water arrives automatically, even iced in winter, and on weekends the queue may form outside in the cold.

That is the real Canadian paradox. The country is famous for courtesy, yet the courtesy is largely ritual: an unhurried choreography of door-holding, apologising and tipping that keeps friction low and intimacy lower. Remitly itself concedes that the politeness stereotype is too simple, and not every Canadian behaves alike. I agree. The ritual is a sort of public-service announcement that nobody intends to cause you harm, and it works marvellously until you want to actually befriend someone, at which point you discover the warmth takes a winter or two.

If you are an expat, the upside is predictability. You will always know what a Canadian is going to do in a queue. You will not always know what they actually think, and you will wait a good while to find out.

## The Verdict

Canada wins on public behaviour if what you want is a quiet commute and no surprises, and I say that as someone who has been shouldered off pavements in four continents. The rules are published, followed and cheerfully enforced by sheer social pressure. You can land on a Monday and be competent by Thursday.

Moldova wins on warmth, and loses on legibility. It asks more of you: language, gifts, stamina, a willingness to be fed against your will. The reward is a welcome that feels like belonging, not an arrangement. If I had to be dropped somewhere with no phone and no plan, I would take Moldova, because somebody would feed me. If I had to be dropped somewhere to conduct a spreadsheet-driven relocation, I would take Canada, because somebody would apologise.

## Conclusion

Canada will apologise to you until you feel guilty about taking up the pavement. Moldova will hand you a glass and wait until you feel guilty about not drinking it. One is a nation of people being careful not to impose, and the other is a nation of people insisting that you impose a little. Pack your sorrys and your flowers, learn both greetings, and keep a decent excuse in reserve. You will need it in only one of them, and you will not be told which.
