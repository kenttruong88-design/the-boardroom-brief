# In Norway, Nobody Needs You to Speak Norwegian. In New Zealand, Everyone Will Quietly Judge You for Not Learning a Few Words of Māori.

🇳🇴 Norway 🇳🇿 New Zealand
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Norway is one of the least language-barrier-y countries on Earth for an English speaker, and that turns out to be its own peculiar trap — when literally everyone under seventy speaks flawless, faintly accented English, there's almost no functional incentive to learn Norwegian, and you can live an entire comfortable expat life in Oslo without ever needing to conjugate a verb. New Zealand presents the opposite puzzle: English is the dominant language everywhere, official and total, and yet an entirely different linguistic expectation has quietly taken hold — te reo Māori words have woven themselves into everyday English so thoroughly that not knowing basic terms now reads as a small social failure, a gap in cultural fluency rather than a literal communication barrier.

I've lived the "everyone speaks better English than me" problem in Oslo and the "wait, what does that word mean" problem in Wellington, and they are genuinely different species of foreignness. One is the absence of a barrier so complete it becomes its own kind of isolation. The other is a barrier built not from necessity but from respect — a language you technically don't need but increasingly, culturally, should want.

## Norway: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn Norwegian anyway, even though you technically won't need to — it changes how deeply you're accepted | Assume perfect English means you don't need Norwegian for work — many employers still expect it eventually |
| Take free or subsidised Norwegian classes offered to residents — a genuinely good system | Switch to English the moment someone hesitates in Norwegian — let them practice too |
| Expect Norwegians to switch effortlessly to English in almost any situation | Mistake fluent English for an invitation to skip integration — locals notice who's actually trying |

## New Zealand: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic te reo Māori greetings and place-name pronunciations — genuinely expected now | Mispronounce Māori place names repeatedly without attempting correction — it's noticed and it matters |
| Use kia ora, whānau, and other common integrated terms naturally once comfortable | Treat te reo as decorative rather than a living, official language with real speakers |
| Attend a pōwhiri or cultural event if invited, and follow local guidance respectfully | Assume all New Zealanders are equally fluent — usage varies significantly by region and generation |

## Norway: The Country Where You'll Never Struggle to Communicate, and That's Sort of the Problem

Norway consistently ranks among the very highest countries globally for English proficiency among non-native speakers, a product of decades of English-language media consumption, an education system that introduces English early and seriously, and a small population that has long understood the practical necessity of speaking a global language fluently. For an English-speaking expat, this means the language barrier that defines so much of relocation abroad simply... doesn't exist in any functional sense. Order coffee, negotiate a lease, argue with a phone company — all entirely possible in English, delivered back to you in English so precise and idiomatically fluent it can genuinely startle you.

The catch, and Norwegians will tell you this themselves if you ask directly, is that fluent English creates a subtle social ceiling. Norwegian workplaces, however international-feeling on the surface, still conduct their real cultural and social life in Norwegian — the jokes, the office gossip, the deeper friendships — and expats who never bother learning the language often find themselves professionally functional but socially peripheral for years, sometimes permanently. The government has responded with genuinely excellent, often free or subsidised Norwegian language courses for residents, a recognition that integration requires more than functional communication. The expats who thrive long-term in Norway are almost universally the ones who learned Norwegian anyway, despite never strictly needing to — not for necessity, but because the language is where the actual culture, warmth, and belonging live, hidden just behind a fluent English facade that never quite lets you all the way in.

## New Zealand: A Language Barrier Built From Respect, Not Necessity

New Zealand presents no traditional language barrier whatsoever for English speakers — English is the dominant, functionally universal language of daily life, government, and business. But over the past two decades, and accelerating significantly in the last ten years, te reo Māori has undergone a genuine cultural and linguistic revival, moving from a language under serious threat of decline to one actively woven back into mainstream New Zealand English. Government departments now commonly use Māori names alongside or instead of English ones, media broadcasts incorporate te reo terms as standard vocabulary, and everyday greetings and phrases — kia ora, whānau, kai, aroha — have become so normalised in general usage that not recognising them marks you, gently but unmistakably, as someone who hasn't been paying attention.

This creates a genuinely unusual dynamic for expats: there's no functional communication barrier to overcome, but there is a cultural fluency expectation that's grown substantially and continues to grow, particularly among younger New Zealanders and in public institutions actively championing the revival. Getting Māori place names right — actually attempting the correct pronunciation of Whakatāne or Whanganui rather than flattening them into English phonetics — has become a small but real marker of respect, and locals notice, warmly, when newcomers make the effort, and notice, less warmly, when they persistently don't. It's a language barrier not of necessity but of cultural citizenship — nobody needs you to speak te reo to survive in New Zealand, but a growing number of New Zealanders quietly expect you to try, at least a little, as basic acknowledgment of whose land you're actually living on.

## The Verdict

Norway wins on pure convenience — you will never once struggle to be understood, and that ease is genuinely remarkable. But New Zealand wins on meaning, because the linguistic effort it quietly asks of you isn't functional, it's relational — an invitation to understand a culture more fully rather than a hurdle to clear. I have more respect, honestly, for a barrier built on cultural reverence than for the absence of one built on convenience. Learn Norwegian in Norway because the culture deserves it even when the logistics don't demand it. Learn a bit of te reo in New Zealand because, increasingly, everyone else already has, and being the person who hasn't bothered is a look nobody should want.

## Conclusion

Ease and meaning aren't the same thing, and these two countries prove it in opposite directions — one removes the barrier entirely and dares you to notice what you're missing by not building your own bridge anyway, the other never had a functional barrier at all and built a cultural one out of pure intention. Learn the language in both places. In Norway, nobody will make you. In New Zealand, increasingly, everyone expects you to have started.
