# One Country Makes You Guess Which Language, The Other Won't Let You Practise Its Own

🇱🇰 Sri Lanka 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two distinct flavours of language-barrier despair, and I have personally experienced both on the same continent-hopping year, which felt less like variety and more like the universe having a laugh. Sri Lanka gives you three languages running in parallel — Sinhala, Tamil, and English, all constitutionally recognised, all genuinely in active use depending on region, generation, and who's in the room — and the puzzle is simply working out which one applies where. The Netherlands gives you the opposite problem entirely: a beautiful, guttural, deeply idiosyncratic language that the Dutch will not, under almost any circumstances, let you actually practise, because the second you stumble over a sentence, they'll switch to flawless English out of what they insist is politeness and what is, in practice, a mild act of linguistic sabotage against your own learning.

Sri Lanka's trilingual setup isn't a colonial hangover quietly tolerated; it's constitutionally formalised, with Sinhala and Tamil as official languages and English holding "link language" status, a designation that exists precisely because the country needed a neutral bridge between its two largest linguistic communities after decades of tension around language policy. Road signs, official documents, and government communication are meant to appear trilingually, though the reality on the ground is patchier and more regional than the policy suggests — Colombo leans English-fluent among the professional class, the north and east lean Tamil, and Sinhala dominates much of the rest, with genuine pockets where none of your assumptions will hold. The Netherlands has no such three-way split; it has one dominant language and a population so aggressively, internationally fluent in English — consistently ranking at or near the top of global English proficiency indices for non-native speakers — that trying to have a conversation in your carefully rehearsed beginner Dutch often gets gently, efficiently overridden within the first sentence.

## Do's & Don'ts: Sri Lanka

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn a handful of basic Sinhala and Tamil greetings before arriving — even imperfect effort in both is noticed and appreciated | Assume English will work everywhere just because it's widely spoken in Colombo — rural areas and older generations may not share that fluency |
| Pay attention to regional context — Tamil is dominant in the north and east, Sinhala elsewhere, and reading the room matters | Default loudly to English and assume it's neutral — language carries real historical weight here, so approach with awareness, not entitlement |
| Use trilingual road signs and signage as a genuine navigation aid — they're there specifically because policy requires it | Be surprised if official trilingual policy doesn't match ground-level reality everywhere — implementation is uneven outside major cities |

## Do's & Don'ts: Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Insist, politely but firmly, on continuing in Dutch if you're trying to learn — say "mag ik het in het Nederlands proberen?" upfront | Take offence when someone switches to English — it's near-universal reflex, not a judgement on your accent |
| Use Dutch for the basics (ordering, greetings, small transactions) even if the rest of the conversation drifts to English | Expect daily immersion to build fluency passively — you will need to actively fight for Dutch-language practice, not stumble into it |
| Join a language course or "taalcafé" specifically designed around the switch-to-English problem — locals there expect and support the struggle | Assume high English proficiency means Dutch identity or language matters less — it's a point of real pride, just not one that gets in the way of efficiency |

## Sri Lanka: Three Languages, One Constant Calculation

Sri Lanka's language landscape is less a barrier than a live, ongoing negotiation, shaped by a history in which language policy itself was a source of serious national tension — the decades-long push and pull between Sinhala-only and multilingual approaches left scars that show up even now in how carefully official communication tries to stay trilingual. For a newcomer, this translates into a constant, low-grade calculation: which language does this shopkeeper likely speak, does this form need to be read in Tamil or Sinhala, will English actually land here or just produce a polite blank stare. Colombo, as the commercial and diplomatic capital, skews comfortably English-fluent among professionals, educated younger people, and anyone working tourism or business — but step outside that bubble, into a rural market town or a government office in a smaller city, and the calculation changes fast. Road signage is meant to appear in all three languages, a small, visible daily reminder of the policy's intent, though the honest experience of many newcomers is that the trilingual promise is more consistent in Colombo than in the provinces. What makes Sri Lanka's version of the language barrier oddly generous, compared to some countries, is that attempting any of the three languages, however badly, is read as effort rather than presumption — there's no single "correct default" you're expected to defer to, which paradoxically makes the whole guessing game less intimidating once you understand it's a guessing game everyone, including locals crossing regions, occasionally has to play.

## Netherlands: Fluent Enough To Ruin Your Practice

The Dutch language-switch phenomenon is so consistent, so widely reported by learners, that it's become something of a national in-joke even among the Dutch themselves — ask a beginner-level question in tentative Dutch and watch the response arrive, immediately, in better English than you speak. It's not rudeness; by every account it's intended and received as courtesy, an instinct to minimise friction and get the actual information across efficiently, consistent with a culture that prizes directness and getting to the point over drawn-out social performance. But for anyone actually trying to learn Dutch, it becomes a genuine structural obstacle: the passive immersion that works in most countries — absorbing a language simply by being surrounded by it daily — collapses here, because the moment your Dutch falters even slightly, the room adjusts to you rather than the reverse. Learners who've cracked this describe having to be almost confrontationally insistent, explicitly asking to continue in Dutch, sometimes multiple times in the same conversation, before locals stop reflexively rescuing them with English. The upside, and it is genuine, is that once your Dutch is functional rather than fluent, the switching mostly stops — it's specifically triggered by visible struggle, not by having a foreign accent per se, which means the barrier is more about the learning curve's early, humiliating phase than a permanent ceiling.

## The Verdict

Sri Lanka's language barrier is geographic and situational — solvable with preparation, regional awareness, and genuine effort across three languages rather than one, and forgiving of imperfection because everyone, natives included, is navigating a multilingual landscape. The Netherlands' barrier is almost the inverse problem: not too little English, but too much of it, deployed so efficiently and so kindly that it actively undermines your ability to learn the local language unless you fight for the friction. If you want a challenge that rewards broad effort across multiple languages, Sri Lanka will meet you halfway in three directions. If you want to actually learn Dutch, prepare to be more stubborn than the entire country's instinct to help you.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — paraphrased: a traveller asked whether to learn Sinhala, Tamil or English before a Sri Lanka trip and was told, essentially, "all three, a little, depending exactly where you're going," which was less helpful than hoped but entirely accurate.*

> *Reddit r/Netherlands — paraphrased: a longtime learner described ordering coffee in deliberately halting Dutch specifically to practise, only for the barista to reply in English, prompting the now-famous learner complaint: "I will never get good at this if you keep being nice to me."*

> *Expat.com Amsterdam forum — paraphrased: a member said the only reliable way they found to get Dutch replies was explicitly telling people, up front, "ik leer Nederlands, mag ik het proberen" — and even then, some slipped back to English within a minute.*

## Conclusion

Sri Lanka asks you to read the room, three times over, and rewards imperfect effort with genuine warmth. The Netherlands asks you to actively resist the country's own overwhelming competence at your native tongue, which is a stranger, more ironic kind of barrier than most people expect going in. Pack patience for Sri Lanka's regional puzzle. Pack stubbornness for the Netherlands' relentless, well-meaning English. Only one of these countries is trying to make it easier for you, and infuriatingly, that's the one that'll leave you fluent in nothing at all.
