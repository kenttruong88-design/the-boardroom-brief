# Jordan Makes You Meet the Family First. Australia Just Wants to Know If You're Shouting the Next Round.

🇯🇴 Jordan vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nowhere does "getting to know someone" mean two more wildly different things than in Amman and Sydney. In one, you are vetted by a committee before you've finished your coffee. In the other, you are vetted by nothing at all, possibly including your own judgment, three VBs deep at a beer garden in Surry Hills.

I've relocated fourteen times and watched grown, competent adults turn into nervous teenagers on both continents — the Jordanian professional terrified of being seen in a car alone with a woman who isn't his sister, and the thirty-eight-year-old Australian man who still communicates romantic interest exclusively through the medium of "shall we get another round." Both systems work, after a fashion. Neither is designed with foreigners in mind, and both will happily let you make a fool of yourself first.

## Do's & Don'ts: Jordan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Let a relationship develop through family, friends, or a shared social circle | Hold hands, hug, or kiss a partner in public, even if you're married |
| Wear a (real or convincingly fake) wedding or engagement ring if travelling with a partner | Assume "we're just friends" clears up anything — it usually raises more questions |
| Treat an invitation to meet someone's family as the relationship becoming official | Ask a Jordanian man about his wife, by name or otherwise |
| Expect your social life to run largely through the embassy or expat circuit if you're single | Expect the local dating "scene" to resemble anything you'd call a scene |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to split the bill, or at least offer — chivalry here is optional, not owed | Assume a few drinks together is a date; it might just be Tuesday |
| Meet people through sport, the pub, or a dating app — rarely anything more formal | Expect a text back within any particular timeframe; "ghosting" is a national pastime |
| Say directly if you're not drinking or not interested — subtlety gets lost in the noise | Mistake friendliness for flirtation; Australians are warm with everyone, strategically |
| Bring a healthy tolerance for ordinary, unglamorous first dates — coffee, a walk, the pub | Expect "dinner and a movie" to impress anyone; it reads as try-hard, not romantic |

## Jordan: Love By Committee

Jordan's dating culture doesn't really have an apps-and-bars layer sitting on top of it the way most Western cities do; it has a family layer, and that layer does most of the actual work. Rough Guides' own etiquette briefing is blunt about the baseline: unmarried or unrelated men and women simply don't touch in public, not even a guiding hand at the elbow, and a wedding ring — genuine or not — functions as a kind of social passport that makes everyone around you relax. This isn't a quirky local custom to photograph. It's the operating system.

For foreigners, the practical result is a dating pool that runs almost entirely through who you already know. Expat forum threads on the subject are consistent on this point to the point of repetition: relationships with Jordanians, when they happen, tend to be family-mediated from early on, introductions arranged rather than stumbled into at a bar, and premarital intimacy treated as something to protect rather than broadcast. One long-running Expat.com thread about a foreign woman dating a Jordanian man surfaces the uncomfortable truth under all the sympathetic replies: even in Amman, a lot of marriages are still substantially arranged, and a foreign partner is often the plot twist the family didn't order.

Singles who land here without a local network, meanwhile, tend to default to each other. The compiled Post Report answers for Amman gathered by TalesMag — real responses from foreign service staff and spouses posted over more than a decade — describe a dating pool for outsiders that is "fairly restricted," with more than one contributor noting flatly that you'll likely end up finding someone within the embassy or expat bubble rather than outside it. That's not a failure of charm. It's geometry: the local scene isn't built to let you in casually, and it was never trying to be.

None of this makes Jordan a romantic dead zone — younger, more liberal Jordanians in Amman do date, discreetly, and Western women are often socially filed under "honorary men" and welcomed into circles that would otherwise be closed to them. But arrive expecting Tinder energy and you'll be reaching for a ring, real or not, within a fortnight just to make your life easier.

It's also worth saying plainly that this asymmetry cuts harder for women than for men, foreign or local. Single men, by most accounts, navigate Amman's scene with relative ease; single women report a noticeably steeper climb, fielding more scrutiny, more unsolicited opinions, and more assumptions about what their presence at a bar or a dinner table actually means. That's not a footnote. It's the single most useful piece of intelligence anyone relocating here for romance, rather than merely tolerance of the idea, needs to hear before they land.

## Australia: Love By Attrition

Australia's dating culture runs on a completely different fuel: proximity, beer, and a cultivated allergy to seeming too keen. Expat.com's own guide to dating in Australia describes the national style as "casual, forthright, and sociable," with splitting the bill treated as default and a few drinks on a date considered standard enough that non-drinkers are advised to flag it upfront on their profile, lest they spend the evening explaining themselves. Insider Guides' breakdown of the sex-and-dating scene is even more relaxed, noting there are "no formal rules for who can approach whom" and that early-stage non-exclusivity is simply assumed rather than scandalous.

What this produces, in practice, is a social scene that is enormously easy to enter and surprisingly hard to get anything serious out of. Everyone is friendly. Almost no one is unambiguous. The apps (Tinder, Hinge, Bumble, with niche options like Salaam Swipe for Muslim singles) have mostly replaced the pub as the first point of contact, but the pub remains the default second and third date, largely because Australians seem constitutionally unable to sit still indoors without a drink in hand.

The sting is structural, not anecdotal. Relationships Australia's 2024 national survey found 24% of Australians reporting loneliness and over a third socially lonely, even as 92% say they feel loved by someone in their life — a split that suggests the problem isn't a lack of people, it's a lack of anyone making the first unambiguous move. Singledom here is increasingly a lifestyle rather than a waiting room, which is either liberating or faintly tragic depending on your mood and your rent.

Newcomers tend to read the friendliness as an opening and the vagueness as a glitch, when in fact the vagueness is the entire point. Directness is reserved for complaints about the weather, the government, and the price of a flat white — romantic intentions get the polite, non-committal treatment right up until someone, eventually, decides they can't be bothered keeping it casual anymore. That decision can take anywhere from one weekend to several years.

## The Verdict

Jordan asks more of you upfront — a ring, a reputation, a family's approval — and in exchange tells you exactly where you stand, because everyone involved has made their expectations explicit from the start. Australia asks nothing of you upfront, which feels like freedom right up until you realise nobody else is asking anything of you either, including the person you've been seeing for four months who still introduces you as "my mate."

If you want a system with rules, however restrictive, go to Amman. If you want a system with no rules and correspondingly no guarantees, go to Sydney. I'd rather be told no by someone's mother than ghosted by someone's nothing. Jordan wins on honesty. Australia wins on volume. Pick your poison, and bring a ring either way — in one country it buys you peace, in the other it just confuses everyone at the pub.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com, "Dating a Jordanian Man II" forum thread — paraphrased: a long-time contributor warns that even in the capital, marriages are still largely arranged and that nearly everyone in the smaller towns marries within the community, so an outside relationship is the exception the family didn't plan for.*

## Conclusion

Here's the part nobody wants printed on a tourism brochure: neither of these countries is optimising for your love life, and both will let you believe otherwise. Jordan will let you mistake politeness for permission until a family quietly closes a door you didn't know existed. Australia will let you mistake a Saturday afternoon of Fosters and flirtation for a relationship until you realise it was always just a Saturday afternoon. The difference is that one culture tells you the rules and expects you to follow them, and the other has no rules and expects you to be fine with that. I am, for the record, fine with neither. But if you're forcing me to choose a bar to cry in afterwards, make it the one in Sydney — at least there, the drinks are stronger and nobody's mother is watching.
