# In America You Buy Health Insurance. In Australia You Just Get Ill, Like a Civilised Person

🇺🇸 USA vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

An American in Australia will, within about a fortnight, experience something that feels like a glitch in the simulation: a doctor who sees you, treats you, and then does not hand you a bill shaped like a small mortgage. One US traveller, Coleman Geiger from Indiana, reported paying roughly AU$483 a month for his insurance at home, skipping a renewal while travelling, catching a nasty infection, and then getting a same-day telehealth consultation with a Sydney GP, prescription included, for about AU$48. He said it felt like cheating. That is the correct reaction. It is also the reaction of a person who has spent his entire life being financially punished for the crime of having organs.

Australia, to be clear, is not a socialist paradise where nobody ever thinks about money. It is a country with a levy, a rebate, a gap, a waiting period, a lifetime loading and a government advert shouting about all of them. But the system underneath has a floor, and the floor is called Medicare. America has no floor, only a series of trapdoors wearing the logos of insurers. If you are an American executive relocating to Sydney, your first lesson is that the two countries disagree on a fundamental question: whether being sick is a personal failing or an administrative inconvenience.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**USA**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep your insurance card in your wallet, because every visit begins with a financial interrogation | Assume the price will be on a menu anywhere you can see it before the procedure |
| Check whether the provider is in-network before, not after, you are on the table | Trust the first number you are quoted over the phone |
| Budget for the premium, the deductible, the copay and the surprise | Skip renewing your cover for a month because you are travelling |
| Ask for the itemised bill and read every line | Treat the emergency room as a bargain bin for minor ailments |

**Australia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Find a GP who bulk-bills, or ask what the gap will be before you sit down | Assume Medicare covers you automatically if you arrive on a US passport without a reciprocal agreement |
| Buy private hospital cover early, because waiting periods run long | Wait until you are ill to shop for cover |
| Use telehealth for the small stuff; it is fast and cheap | Expect private extras like dental to be included in the basic plan |
| Check your visa conditions, which often require some health insurance | Confuse the Medicare levy with a bill for care; it is a tax |

## United States: The Only Country That Sells Healthcare Like a Timeshare

The American system has the great merit of being world-class when you can get into it, and the great defect that getting into it is a sport. One of the Australia-based Americans writing for Democrats Abroad cited a US family premium figure of $25,826 a year against a median family income of $56,516. That figure is a few years old, but the point survives: it is a number a fully functioning adult would take for a ransom demand. A visit to a US doctor without insurance, according to the same Indiana traveller, might run between AU$483 and AU$967, with antibiotics adding another AU$67 to AU$356 on top. Those are one traveller's reported figures, not a government price list, but nobody who has stood at a US pharmacy counter will find them implausible.

What an expat executive needs to understand is that the real cost of the American model is not just money; it is mental bandwidth. You spend your working life carrying a mental spreadsheet of deductibles, networks, prior authorisations and the exact status of your employer's open enrolment window. Every medical event is a small project. People talk about "job lock", the habit of staying in a role you hate because it holds your cover, and it is not a quirk; it is the system working as designed. If you move abroad, that spreadsheet goes with you, and half the comedy of an American's first month in Australia is watching them try to find the form.

None of this means American care is bad. It means American care is a luxury good wearing the costume of a public service. If you have excellent employer cover, you will get fast specialist access and a car park that looks like a spaceport. If you do not, you will learn the phrase "out of pocket" more intimately than you ever wished. The sting for expats is that you are used to it. You have been conditioned to treat paying hundreds of dollars for a ten-minute consultation as normal, which is why an AU$48 telehealth appointment feels like theft in reverse.

## Australia: A Safety Net With Fine Print

Australia's model is a hybrid: public Medicare, funded in part by a levy of 2% of taxable income with relief below an income threshold, plus a thriving private insurance sector for those who want choice of doctor, shorter queues for elective work, and extras like dental and physio. Julia Bryan, an American writing from Australia, says she and her husband pay about $4,000 a year for private cover, and that they never lie awake worrying about losing it. That is the whole sales pitch in one sentence: not that the care is free, but that the fear has been removed.

The fine print is where expats get caught. Medicare is generally limited to citizens, permanent residents and visitors from countries with a reciprocal agreement, and the United States is not on the list. The countries that are, according to the Your Life Choices piece, are Belgium, Finland, Italy, Malta, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Ireland, Slovenia, Sweden and the UK, and even those agreements are not a substitute for travel insurance. So the average American arriving on a work visa is not automatically in the glorious public system at all; they are expected to arrange private cover, and many visas require it. Then come the waiting periods: BrightTax, a commercial tax adviser and therefore not entirely neutral, notes that these are often twelve months for pre-existing conditions and for maternity. Basic hospital cover, it says, tends to start around AUD $100 to $150 a month. The twelve-month wait for the thing you actually need is a lesson in patience that no American has been trained for.

Still, once you are inside the system, the day-to-day experience is startlingly calm. Telehealth works. GPs are everywhere. The question "can I afford to see a doctor?" is replaced by "which doctor, and how soon?", which is a much nicer question. Australians will complain endlessly about gap fees and the state of the hospitals, and they are not wrong to, because a system that works is exactly the kind of thing people feel entitled to complain about.

## The Verdict

Australia wins, and not narrowly. The American system produces extraordinary medicine and extraordinary anxiety in equal measure, and the anxiety is the thing you carry with you for life. Australia offers a plain bargain: pay a levy, accept some queues, buy private cover if you want to skip them, and sleep. The one honest warning for the Americans reading this is that you will not benefit from Medicare automatically, and you must not treat the cover question as something to sort out later. Sort it out first. Then enjoy the disorienting sensation of walking out of a doctor's office feeling merely better, rather than also poorer.

If you are from Indiana and you need a person to tell you it is okay to enjoy that sensation, consider it said. You have been very brave about all this.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Coleman Geiger, American traveller, via Your Life Choices — let his insurance lapse while travelling, got a severe infection, and was amazed that a same-day telehealth GP appointment with a filled prescription cost about AU$48. He laughed because it felt like cheating.*

> *Julia Bryan, American expat in Australia, via Democrats Abroad — says she and her husband never worry about losing their cover, and calls the US private insurance model crazy and inhumane in comparison to the Australian arrangement.*

> *BrightTax expat guide — warns Americans that Medicare is generally limited to citizens, permanent residents and reciprocal-agreement visitors, and that private cover often comes with twelve-month waiting periods for pre-existing conditions.*

## Conclusion

Healthcare is the one subject where your passport decides how you are allowed to feel. In America you feel vigilant, permanently, like a person watching a pot that might be a bomb. In Australia you feel, for the most part, looked after, and mildly irritated about the parking. Neither country is perfect. One of them simply has the decency to let you be ill without also being bankrupt, and I would suggest that if you are the sort of person who relocates for a living, you start valuing that sooner than later.

Pack your documents, buy your cover before you board, and for the love of all that is holy, keep the receipts. The rest of us will be over here, using the word "gap" with the weary familiarity of people who have escaped something much worse.
