# One Country Dates Through a Phone Screen at a Night Market, the Other Still Expects You to Just Walk Up and Say Something

🇹🇼 Taiwan vs 🇧🇬 Bulgaria
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Taiwan's dating scene runs heavily through apps, group outings, and a genuinely careful, indirect social choreography where night markets and bubble tea shops function as low-pressure meeting grounds precisely because they let everyone avoid the terror of direct one-on-one confrontation for as long as possible. Bulgaria, by contrast, retains a considerably more direct, old-fashioned social culture, where approaching someone in a cafe or at a gathering is still a completely normal thing to just do, and where romantic bluntness is treated as honesty rather than an ambush.

One country has built an elaborate technological and social buffer around the terrifying business of meeting people. The other still just walks over and says hello.

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|

| Suggest group outings or night market visits early on; they lower pressure and are culturally well-understood | Push for exclusivity or serious labels too quickly; pacing here tends to be considerably slower and more cautious |
| Be patient with indirect communication; hints and context often carry more weight than explicit statements | Take initial reserve as disinterest; genuine warmth frequently develops gradually rather than upfront |
| Respect family involvement in serious relationships; parental opinion still carries real weight for many | Assume Western dating timelines apply; milestones like meeting family happen on a noticeably different schedule |

## Taiwan: A Dating Culture Built Around Elaborate, Considerate Ways to Avoid Direct Confrontation

Taiwan's approach to dating and its broader social scene is defined by a real cultural preference for indirectness, and the Taiwan Ministry of the Interior's demographic data on dating app usage confirms just how thoroughly apps have become the default entry point for meeting romantic partners, particularly in dense urban environments like Taipei where organic public approaches simply aren't the cultural norm the way they might be elsewhere.

What's genuinely charming about this, once you understand it, is how much thoughtful social infrastructure has grown up around making the whole process gentler. Night markets, extensively documented by the Taipei City Government as genuine social and tourism assets, function as ideal low-pressure dating environments: crowded enough to feel casual, varied enough to give conversation constant fresh material, and structured enough that a first date can feel more like a shared activity than an interrogation across a restaurant table. Group outings before pairing off into actual couples are common and culturally well understood, easing the transition from acquaintance to romantic interest without anyone needing to have an awkward, explicit conversation about intentions.

The pacing that results from all this can genuinely test the patience of visitors used to more direct dating cultures. Family involvement remains a real factor for serious relationships, hints and context frequently carry more communicative weight than explicit statements, and rushing any of this tends to backfire rather than accelerate things. It's a considerate, carefully calibrated system, and once you stop expecting it to move at a Western pace, it's genuinely easier to appreciate for what it actually is.

## Bulgaria: A Dating Culture That Never Saw the Point of All That Indirection

Bulgaria's social and dating scene operates from a completely different set of assumptions, and the directness can be genuinely refreshing after a country like Taiwan's careful choreography. The National Statistical Institute of Bulgaria's demographic trends show relationships and marriages still forming at a noticeably younger age than much of Western Europe, and the on-the-ground social culture reflects a comparable directness: approaching someone in a cafe, at a gathering, or on the street remains a completely normal, unremarkable thing to do, without the elaborate app-mediated buffering common elsewhere.

Sofia Municipality's documentation of the city's cafe culture captures something genuinely central to how Bulgarians actually meet and get to know each other. Cafes function as the real social infrastructure here, spaces for lingering conversation, direct flirtation, and a level of romantic bluntness that would read as forward in plenty of other cultures but lands here as straightforward honesty. Compliments are given plainly, interest is expressed clearly, and the whole process tends to move considerably faster than Taiwan's more cautious pacing once two people have actually connected.

What's worth flagging honestly is the regional variation. Sofia's dating scene, increasingly influenced by EU integration and a younger, more internationally minded population, has picked up some of the app-based habits common elsewhere, while smaller Bulgarian towns retain a considerably more traditional, community-and-family-mediated approach to romance. Family involvement, once a relationship turns serious, remains genuinely significant across the country, directness in the early stages notwithstanding.

## The Verdict

Bulgaria wins on sheer efficiency — if you actually want to meet someone without downloading three apps and enduring weeks of carefully calibrated group outings first, walking into a Sofia cafe will get you there considerably faster. But Taiwan's system, once properly understood, has a genuine thoughtfulness to it that Bulgaria's bluntness doesn't really attempt to replicate, and the night-market-first-date is honestly one of the more charming dating customs I've encountered anywhere.

Download the apps and be patient in Taiwan. Walk into a Sofia cafe and just say something in Bulgaria. Both approaches work, evidently, given that both countries continue to produce new generations of people. They just could not have arrived at their methods more differently if they'd tried.

## Conclusion

Taiwan and Bulgaria have built dating cultures that reflect two genuinely opposite instincts — one layering careful, considerate indirection over every early interaction, the other cutting straight to the point and trusting bluntness over subtlety. Be patient and app-savvy in Taiwan. Be direct and unbothered in Bulgaria. Neither country is doing it wrong; they've simply decided, independently and with total conviction, that the other country's approach would be completely exhausting.
