# One Country Has a Literal Cloud Forest Mountain Inside Its Capital. The Other Fought a Political Battle Over a Single Small Park.

🇻🇪 Venezuela 🇹🇷 Turkey
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Caracas has one of the most absurd geographic blessings of any capital city on earth: Ávila National Park, a genuine cloud forest mountain, rising directly behind the metropolis, accessible by cable car or a serious hike, offering a wilderness experience most capitals would need a two-hour drive to approximate. Istanbul, by contrast, is a dense, historic megacity of sixteen million people where green space is so precious, so contested, that a single modest park — Gezi — became the flashpoint for one of the defining political moments of modern Turkish history.

I have ridden the Ávila cable car out of Caracas and watched the entire sprawling city shrink beneath actual cloud cover within fifteen minutes. I have also sat in Gezi Park itself, a patch of green barely large enough for a proper picnic, and understood within about ten minutes exactly why people were willing to clash with riot police over it. Scale isn't the point. Scarcity and meaning are.

## Do's & Don'ts: Venezuela 🇻🇪

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take the Ávila cable car (Teleférico) at least once — it's one of the single best urban-adjacent wilderness experiences anywhere | Hike Ávila's more remote trails solo or without checking current local safety guidance — conditions and security vary and change |
| Visit with a local or a known group if possible — shared local knowledge genuinely improves both safety and the experience | Expect the same park maintenance standards Venezuela had decades ago — economic crisis has visibly affected upkeep in many public spaces |
| Explore beyond Caracas — Canaima National Park's tepuis and waterfalls are a genuinely different, extraordinary green-space category entirely | Assume all of Venezuela's park infrastructure is equally accessible right now — travel logistics and costs have shifted substantially |

## Do's & Don'ts: Turkey 🇹🇷

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit Gezi Park and Taksim Square with the political history in mind — understanding the 2013 protests changes what you're actually looking at | Treat it as "just a small, unremarkable park" in conversation with Istanbullus — the symbolism runs far deeper than its modest size suggests |
| Seek out Istanbul's larger green escapes — Belgrad Forest, the Princes' Islands, Emirgan Park's tulip season — for genuine breathing room | Assume Istanbul lacks green space entirely because its historic core feels dense — the periphery and islands offer real relief |
| Respect posted park rules around political gatherings or demonstrations — enforcement and sensitivity around these spaces remains real | Bring up Gezi Park politics casually as small talk with strangers — it remains a genuinely sensitive topic for many Turkish citizens |

## Venezuela: A Cloud Forest as a Civic Backyard

Ávila National Park (officially Waraira Repano) is, geographically speaking, one of the most ludicrous strokes of urban luck on the planet: a genuine, UNESCO-recognised cloud forest mountain range running directly along Caracas's northern edge, separating the capital from the Caribbean coast, rising to over 2,700 metres and hosting cloud forest ecosystems within what amounts to city-adjacent hiking distance. The Teleférico cable car, when operational, delivers you from urban density into genuine wilderness in roughly twenty minutes, a transition most capital cities would need to drive you out of town for hours to achieve.

For generations of Caraqueños, Ávila functioned as the city's default green lung and weekend escape valve — hiking, running, family picnics at its lower trailheads, a genuine civic relationship with nature baked into daily life rather than treated as a special occasion. The economic and political crisis of the past decade has visibly strained this relationship: maintenance of trails and facilities has been inconsistent, the cable car's operational reliability has fluctuated, and safety considerations around certain trails and times have shifted in ways that require real local knowledge to navigate well. The mountain itself hasn't changed; the infrastructure and context around accessing it safely and consistently has.

Beyond Caracas, Venezuela's broader park landscape is genuinely extraordinary and criminally underappreciated internationally — Canaima National Park, home to Angel Falls (the world's tallest uninterrupted waterfall) and the otherworldly tabletop tepui mountains that reportedly inspired the landscapes in Arthur Conan Doyle's "The Lost World," represents a category of green space most countries simply don't have available at any price. Access has become logistically and financially more complicated amid the broader crisis, but the parks themselves remain among the most visually singular wilderness landscapes on the continent, waiting for a more stable chapter to be properly rediscovered.

## Turkey: Fighting Over Every Square Metre

Istanbul's relationship with green space is defined almost entirely by scarcity and density. A megacity of roughly sixteen million people, built across a historic peninsula and sprawling hillsides with centuries of dense urban layering, Istanbul simply doesn't have the raw geographic luxury of a Caracas-style mountain backyard. Green space here is precious, contested, and consequently invested with a political and emotional weight that a more spacious city rarely attaches to its parks.

That weight exploded into global headlines in 2013, when a government redevelopment plan for Gezi Park — a small, unassuming green patch adjacent to Taksim Square, one of the last significant green spaces in central Istanbul — triggered nationwide protests over urban development, government overreach, and civic voice, resulting in a defining moment in modern Turkish political history. Visit Gezi Park today and you'll find, physically, an unremarkable small park. Mention it to an Istanbullu and you'll find, immediately, that the physical modesty of the space has nothing to do with what it represents.

Istanbul does offer real green relief beyond its dense historic core, for those willing to travel slightly: Belgrad Forest on the European side offers genuine woodland hiking within city limits, the car-free Princes' Islands provide an entirely different pace just a ferry ride from the chaos, and Emirgan Park's spectacular tulip festival each spring draws crowds specifically for its horticultural display, a reminder that Ottoman garden culture ran deep even if modern density has squeezed much of it out of the city's core. The pattern across Turkish green space culture generally is this: where Venezuela's nature feels abundant and occasionally under-resourced, Turkey's feels scarce and consequently fiercely, sometimes politically, defended.

## The Verdict

On raw natural spectacle, Venezuela wins without any real contest — a cloud forest mountain and a continent-defining waterfall system aren't things Istanbul's park committee could manufacture with any budget. But Turkey's story is the more instructive one about what green space actually means to a population: when nature is scarce, people fight for it, organise around it, and invest a single small park with civic meaning that Ávila's abundance never required Venezuelans to develop in the same way. Venezuela has the better backyard. Turkey has the more hard-won relationship with the concept of a park at all.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Venezuela — paraphrased: a Caracas native says Ávila still "saves everyone's sanity" despite reduced maintenance, and that locals have simply adapted their expectations rather than giving up on it.*

## Conclusion

Venezuela's green space tells a story of natural abundance meeting institutional strain — a cloud forest mountain that deserves better infrastructure than crisis has allowed it to keep. Turkey's tells a story of scarcity sharpening civic attachment until a modest park becomes a genuine flashpoint of national identity. Neither country needs the other's lecture on how to value a park. One has too much nature to maintain properly; the other has too little to ever take for granted again.
