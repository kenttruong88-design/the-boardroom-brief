# Germany Will Apologise For Being Late, Korea Will Just Not Be

🇩🇪 Germany vs 🇰🇷 South Korea — City Transport and Commuting Culture
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Germany's reputation for engineering precision has done its public transport system no favours, because the gap between the myth and the actual, lived experience of the U-Bahn arriving four minutes late with a shrugging announcer is one of the great disappointments of modern expat life. Seoul's subway, by contrast, is one of the most genuinely reliable, spotless, technologically sophisticated systems on the planet, and nobody warns you how quietly it will ruin every other transit system you use afterward.

Both cities move enormous numbers of people daily with real sophistication, but the actual on-the-ground commuting culture — the punctuality, the etiquette, the ticketing honesty system — could not be more differently calibrated, and getting it wrong in either place marks you instantly as new in town.

## Do's & Don'ts: Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Validate your ticket every single time, even on the honour system | Ride without a validated ticket assuming inspectors won't show up |
| Stand right, walk left on escalators, without exception | Block the escalator while standing still on the left side |
| Build in buffer time — delays are common, not rare | Assume German efficiency means the U-Bahn will actually be on time |
| Keep conversation quiet on trains, especially quiet carriages | Take a loud phone call in a designated Ruhe (quiet) car |

## Do's & Don'ts: South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a T-money card on day one — it works on everything | Buy single paper tickets repeatedly; it's slower and pricier |
| Give up priority seats even if they look empty | Sit in pink priority seats reserved for pregnant passengers |
| Queue in the marked floor lines before doors open | Push forward before alighting passengers have fully exited |
| Keep your phone on silent and voice low in the carriage | Eat strong-smelling food on the subway — it's a genuine faux pas |

## Germany: The Precision Myth Meets Chronic Delay

The single most disillusioning experience for newcomers to Germany is discovering, usually within the first two weeks, that Deutsche Bahn's reputation for punctuality is closer to folklore than current fact. Long-distance and increasingly regional and urban services have struggled with chronic delays for years now, driven by aging infrastructure and underinvestment that even Germans openly, bitterly joke about — "German efficiency" has become something of a national punchline specifically because of the trains, not despite them. Arriving expats who plan tight connections based on the country's reputation rather than its actual current performance data learn this lesson the hard way, usually while standing on a platform watching a delay announcement tick upward in five-minute increments.

What does hold up, impressively, is the etiquette infrastructure around the imperfect service. The honour-based ticketing system — validate your own ticket, no gates, no barriers, just the threat of a plainclothes inspector and a genuinely punishing fine — runs almost entirely on social trust, and Germans overwhelmingly comply with it, not out of fear exactly, but because the system's integrity is treated as a shared civic responsibility. Escalator etiquette (stand right, walk left) is enforced with an intensity that borders on the ideological, and violating it, even briefly, tends to produce audible sighs from behind you within about four seconds.

The quiet-car culture, too, is real and rigorously self-policed — a loud phone call in a Ruhe zone will get you shushed by multiple strangers in quick succession, a level of collective enforcement that newcomers from more permissive transit cultures find genuinely startling. The overall German commuting experience, then, is a strange hybrid: infrastructure that's meaningfully less reliable than its reputation suggests, paired with a rider culture that's more rigorously self-disciplined than almost anywhere else in Europe. Build in delay buffers, validate every ticket, and never, ever stand on the left.

## South Korea: Engineering That Actually Delivers

Seoul's subway system is, without much competition, one of the best in the world — extensive, immaculately clean, air-conditioned, blanketed in free WiFi, and running with a punctuality that makes German commuters visiting for the first time genuinely emotional. The T-money card system unifies buses, subways, and even some taxis into a single tap-in, tap-out network with proportional transfer discounts, a level of integration that newcomers used to fragmented Western ticketing systems find almost suspiciously convenient.

What nobody explains clearly before you arrive is how much unwritten etiquette sits underneath the technological polish. Priority seating, marked in pink, yellow, or blue depending on the designated group — pregnant passengers, elderly riders, passengers with disabilities — is treated as genuinely sacred, and sitting in one even when it appears empty, particularly as a visibly able-bodied foreigner, draws real, if silent, disapproval. Floor markings indicate exactly where to queue before doors open, and Seoul commuters follow them with a discipline that makes free-for-all boarding in other major cities look, in retrospect, faintly chaotic.

Food and phone etiquette carry weight too: eating anything with a noticeable smell on the subway is a quiet but real social violation, and phone calls, when unavoidable, are conducted at a near-whisper with a hand cupped over the mouth, a norm so consistently observed that a foreigner speaking at normal volume on a call stands out immediately. The overall culture prizes collective consideration over individual convenience, expressed through small, constant, almost invisible acts of restraint that add up to a commuting experience remarkably free of the friction, delay, and mild chaos that define transit in most major world cities, Germany very much included.

## The Verdict

Germany's transit culture is a fascinating contradiction — a rider base that behaves with near-total civic discipline atop infrastructure that increasingly fails to reward that discipline with reliability. Seoul delivers what Germany merely promises: genuine punctuality, immaculate cleanliness, and a commuting culture so quietly considerate it makes almost every other major city's subway feel a little feral by comparison. This one isn't close. Seoul wins, decisively, and Germany's famous reputation for engineering precision would do well to spend a week riding the Seoul subway and taking some very pointed notes.

## Conclusion

Germany teaches you to respect a system even when the system doesn't always respect you back, a strangely admirable kind of civic faith that survives despite genuinely disappointing service. Korea teaches you what happens when engineering excellence and collective etiquette actually reinforce each other instead of compensating for each other's shortcomings. Learn to validate your ticket religiously in Berlin and learn to whisper on the phone in Seoul, and you'll get by fine in both. But only one of these cities will actually get you there on time, and it isn't the one with the reputation for it.
