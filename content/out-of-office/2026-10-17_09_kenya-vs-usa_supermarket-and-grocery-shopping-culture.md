# Bagging Rights: How Kenya and America Turned Buying Dinner Into Rival Religions

🇰🇪 Kenya 🇺🇸 USA

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country thinks its way of buying onions is simply "normal" and everyone else's is a quaint eccentricity. Kenya and the United States have taken this delusion and built entire civilisations on top of it. One nation treats a trip for vegetables as a negotiation, a social event and occasionally a small act of theatre. The other has engineered grocery shopping into a loyalty-card-gated data-harvesting operation so vast it could run a small government.

I have pushed a trolley through Naivas in Nairobi and wheeled one through a Kroger in Ohio with the same bewildered expression both times, for entirely different reasons. One place wanted to know if I could haggle. The other wanted my phone number before it would sell me yoghurt at the advertised price. Neither experience, I should say up front, is "exotic." They are simply two very different answers to the same boring question: how does a society decide who eats what, and for how much.

Everyone arrives with assumptions baked in from home, and both countries will cheerfully dismantle them within a single shopping trip. The person who thinks "supermarket" automatically means "cheapest option" will be corrected fast in Nairobi. The person who thinks a price tag is a final, binding legal document will be corrected even faster in Nairobi's markets, and then quietly fleeced in an entirely different way back in an American checkout line, once tax and a declined loyalty card have done their work.

## Do's & Don'ts: Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to haggle gently at open-air markets — it's expected, not rude | Assume a fixed sticker price at a market stall is final |
| Carry small bills and coins; change for large notes can be scarce | Expect every supermarket to take card without a backup cash plan |
| Shop at a greengrocer or butcher for better quality and lower prices than supermarkets | Assume the supermarket is automatically cheaper — often it's the opposite |
| Try local staples like sukuma wiki, ugali maize flour and fresh tilapia | Bring imported peanut butter expectations to the local jar — ask first |

## Do's & Don'ts: USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Sign up for the store loyalty card immediately — the "regular" price is a trap for the cardless | Expect the shelf price to include tax; it's added at checkout, every time |
| Buy produce in season, or prepare to pay triple for strawberries in February | Assume one supermarket chain is the same experience nationwide — Whole Foods and Walmart are different planets |
| Check a weekly flyer or app like Flipp before you go if you actually want a deal | Try to bargain with a cashier — it will not go well, and security may be summoned |
| Visit an international or ethnic grocer for staples that are marked up badly in the "ethnic aisle" | Buy a cart's worth of bulk goods at a warehouse club without a membership — they will stop you at the door |

## Kenya: The Art of the Negotiated Tomato

Kenyan grocery shopping is not one experience, it's three, running in parallel and judging each other constantly. There's the open-air market, where tomatoes, sukuma wiki and onions are sold by women who have been assessing the gullibility of customers since before you were born, and where the "price" is an opening bid, not a fact. There's the kiosk or duka on your street corner, the genuinely essential infrastructure of Kenyan daily life, selling everything from cooking oil to phone credit to that one egg you need at 9 p.m. And then there's the modern supermarket — Naivas, Carrefour, Quickmart, Chandarana — gleaming, fixed-price, air-conditioned, and, crucially, not actually where the savings live.

This is the detail that wrecks every newly arrived expat's budget math: the supermarket, the place that looks like home, is frequently the expensive option. Naivas has become the chain locals point to as relatively fair, and it has expanded aggressively — past the 110-store mark, with its parent company IBL Ltd. posting a 43% jump in net profit and East Africa now accounting for 37% of group revenue. Carrefour, run by Majid Al Futtaim, has deliberately pivoted away from mall flagships toward community stores, sourcing 99% of its products locally and opening eight new branches in 2025 alone. Kenya's supermarket sector is not some sleepy backwater; it's one of the fastest-expanding retail stories on the continent. And yet the butcher down the road, the greengrocer, and the Parklands vegetable market will still beat it on price and, frequently, on quality, every single time.

Meanwhile, Kenya's official statistics bureau tracks grocery inflation with an intensity that would make a Fed economist blush — two kilos of unga, a kilo of sukuma wiki, cooking gas refills, all logged monthly because, unlike in wealthier markets, a few shillings' movement on maize flour is a genuine political event, not a rounding error. Shopping here is not leisure. It's arithmetic performed in public, with dignity, and often with excellent produce.

## USA: The Loyalty-Card Industrial Complex

America did not invent grocery shopping, but it industrialised the hell out of it, then wrapped the whole operation in a surveillance programme and called it "rewards." Walk into any American supermarket without a loyalty card and you are, functionally, shopping at a different and worse store than the person next to you, paying a different and worse price for the identical tub of hummus, because you declined to hand over your shopping history in exchange for forty cents off.

The American grocery landscape is a caste system disguised as convenience: Whole Foods for the aspirational, Trader Joe's for people who want to feel clever about snacks, Walmart and the dollar-store tier for everyone doing real arithmetic, and warehouse clubs like Costco and Sam's Club selling mustard in containers the size of a car tyre to people who insist this is "saving money." According to the government's own Consumer Expenditure Survey, the average American household now spends over six thousand dollars a year just on groceries — and that's before the strawberries-in-winter tax, when out-of-season produce can triple in price overnight, a fact that routinely blindsides newcomers who assumed "supermarket" meant "year-round abundance at stable prices."

Then there's the sales tax ambush — nothing on the shelf includes it, so your mental maths is wrong at every single checkout unless you've lived there long enough to pad every total by a reflexive eight or nine percent. And if you actually wanted the ingredients from home, the "international aisle" will cheerfully charge you double for a jar of something your grandmother bought for pennies, which is why every sensible immigrant community quietly built its own parallel grocery economy of H-Marts, Patel Brothers and unmarked mercados that the loyalty-card algorithm will never fully colonise.

## The Verdict

If the test is "which country makes buying dinner feel like a basic human activity rather than a loyalty-program onboarding flow," Kenya wins, comfortably, and without needing an app. There is something almost moving about a system where the price is a conversation, the vegetables are actually fresh because they were picked that morning, and nobody is harvesting your shopping data to sell you ads for the exact cereal you already bought.

America's version is efficient in the way a spreadsheet is efficient — impressive, scalable, utterly without soul. It has turned the weekly grocery run into a loyalty-scheme obstacle course where the "real" price is a secret password only cardholders know, and where seasonal produce pricing can swing so violently that budgeting becomes a form of gambling. Kenya's supermarkets, for their part, are expanding fast and getting slicker, racking up store counts and profit margins that would make a Wall Street analyst sit up, but they haven't yet managed to out-charm the woman at the Parklands market who will sell you better tomatoes for less, and insult your haggling technique while she does it. That's not inefficiency. That's a country that still remembers shopping is supposed to involve another human being, not an app clipping you a digital coupon for a discount you were owed in the first place.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Nairobi forum, poster Usiniharakishe — paraphrased: don't expect fair pricing as a newcomer at local markets; expats routinely get quoted inflated "mzungu prices," so go with a local, bargain properly, and expect to be tested before you're taken seriously.*

> *expat.com Nairobi forum, poster Longonot62 — paraphrased: Nairobi is a genuinely expensive city despite assumptions otherwise, and even "cheap" delivery services for organic vegetables rarely beat market prices once you account for transport.*

> *Remitly Blog, 'A Grocery Guide for Immigrants in the US' — paraphrased: many sale prices in American supermarkets are only available to loyalty-card holders, and the "ethnic aisle" markup on imported staples is steep enough that most immigrant households quietly shift their core shopping to dedicated international grocers instead.*

## Conclusion

Neither country is going to apologise for how it shops, and neither should. Kenya's grocery culture is a living negotiation — chaotic, personal, occasionally exhausting, and fundamentally honest about the fact that food costs what it costs and you're welcome to argue about it. America's is a masterclass in disguising a pricing system as a rewards programme, convincing an entire population that a discount it was always entitled to is actually a gift from a benevolent supermarket chain. Visit either one expecting nostalgia for home and you'll be robbed, metaphorically in Ohio, literally in Westlands if you don't learn to haggle. Go in paying attention instead, and you'll learn more about a country from forty minutes in its cereal aisle than from any guidebook. Just don't call either of them exotic. They're just hungry, in their own particular ways.
