# Nepal Didn't Rate Its Own Best Hotels Until 2021. India Rates Hotels That Don't Even Apply.

🇳🇵🇮🇳 Nepal and India, reviewed by a woman who has slept in everything from a Kathmandu teahouse dining room to a hotel that insisted on checking her marriage certificate

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Until 2021, Nepal's government had no formal star-rating category at all for boutique and heritage hotels — the country's most charming properties simply existed outside the classification system entirely, which is either delightfully unbothered or faintly insane for an economy that runs substantially on tourism. India, meanwhile, has a full, detailed national star-rating system — room-size minimums, mandatory cuisine counts, trained-staff ratios, the works — and it's entirely voluntary. A hotel can call itself five-star in every advertisement it runs and never once apply for the government's actual five-star rating. Two different philosophies of bureaucratic honesty, both arriving at roughly the same place: trust nothing on the sign out front.

## Do's & Don'ts: Nepal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book through a trekking agency or guide for teahouse lodging — there's no central booking system, and that's by design, not an oversight | Expect a private bathroom in Thamel's budget guesthouses — shared facilities are standard, and hot water often costs extra |
| Learn the names of the people running your guesthouse — the vernacular model, especially around Pokhara, is personal and family-run, not corporate | Assume a "full" teahouse means no room at all — owners typically find you a spot in the dining room rather than turn you away, so don't panic |

## Nepal: Family-Run by Necessity, Regulated by Afterthought

Nepal's hotel sector has a genuinely strange regulatory history: before 2021, only large conventional hotels carried any official star rating at all — boutique and "deluxe" heritage properties, some of the country's most sought-after stays, simply had no formal classification, a gap the Department of Tourism only closed by introducing automatic three-to-five-star tiers for boutique hotels and resorts, plus a separate symbol system (a "TS" for Tourist Standard, a circled "M" for motels, a circled "I" for tourist inns, red "B&B" lettering for bed and breakfasts) that properties must now physically display at the gate. As recently as 2019, the entire country had just twelve five-star hotels — and two of those, Hotel Everest and Fulbari Resort, weren't actually operating, one shut since the 2015 earthquake pending retrofit — meaning the real figure was closer to ten. Until shortly before that count, the whole Kathmandu Valley had only two four-star hotels, total.

The structural heart of Nepali accommodation, though, isn't the star-rated hotel at all — it's the trekking-route teahouse system, which operates nothing like a conventional hospitality industry. Properties are individually, locally owned, with no central booking platform; you book through a trekking agency or guide, full stop. Prices are vivid and altitude-dependent: 600 to 1,300 rupees a night at lower elevations, climbing past $15 higher up, with device charging and hot showers frequently costing extra once you're high enough that everything has to be hauled or generator-powered. Toilets shift from Western-style at lower, Everest-region lodges to manual bucket-flush squat toilets at altitude. And there's a quietly lovely social contract built into the system: even in peak season, if a teahouse is technically full, the owner typically finds you a spot in the dining room rather than turning you away outright — nobody sleeps outside. In Pokhara specifically, the informal guesthouse model is sometimes genuinely just a bed in a room with a hand-painted board hung in front of the family home — clean, cheap, and entirely without atmosphere, which real Fodor's forum posters confirm vividly, naming specific owners by first name: Siddharthi at the Prince Hotel, Roy and Esha at the Tibet Peace Guest House, described, respectively, as simply being the manager and as "delightful."

Foreign ownership of this ecosystem carries a real structural barrier: historically, a $50,000 minimum investment, pre-registered with the Department of Industry, keeping much of the trekking-lodge economy family-run rather than internationally chained — a documented Expat.com thread on opening a Nepali guesthouse confirms this directly, alongside the blunt observation that a backup generator is basically a prerequisite given regular power cuts. And dual pricing — foreigners paying more at heritage sites, temples and on domestic flights — remains a live, locally argued controversy rather than settled policy; Nepali commentary itself points out the obvious inconsistency, that a foreign-passport holder of ethnic-Nepali descent can get the local rate at sites like Patan Durbar Square, proving the pricing logic runs on passport and appearance rather than actual wealth.

## India: A Voluntary Rating System for an Involuntarily Bureaucratic Industry

India's star-rating system, run by HRACC under the Ministry of Tourism through the NIDHI+ portal, is genuinely detailed — minimum room sizes literally double from 120 square feet at 1-star to 200 square feet at 5-star, air conditioning becomes mandatory only from 4-star up, trained-staff ratios climb from roughly 20% at the bottom to 60-80% at the top, and classifications lapse after exactly five years, requiring renewal. The catch, and it's a genuine structural absurdity: the entire system is voluntary. A hotel can legally brand itself "five-star" in every piece of marketing it produces and never apply for the government's actual rating, which means the sign out front and the government's own classification database are, for a meaningful share of Indian hotels, two completely unrelated documents.

The regulatory layer gets considerably more serious, and more surprising to Western visitors, the moment a private home enters the picture. Under Section 7 of India's Foreigners Act, anyone providing paid accommodation to a foreign national — "even for a few hours," per the law's own language — must file Form C with the local Foreigners Regional Registration Office within 24 hours of check-in, using the guest's passport and visa details, and must separately maintain a handwritten Form B guest register, covering both Indian and foreign guests, available for inspection at any time. Skipping this carries fines and up to five years' imprisonment. This isn't theoretical: in August 2024, Gurugram police booked 52 ordinary flat owners in a single housing society after foreign nationals were found staying without the required paperwork. If you've ever rented out a spare room without thinking twice about it, India would like a word.

And then there's the genuinely unresolved cultural tension playing out in real time through OYO, India's dominant budget-hotel aggregator, scaled from a 2012 startup to over 43,000 properties and a million-plus rooms across 80 countries. OYO launched a "Relationship Mode" in 2016 specifically to let unmarried couples book couple-friendly hotels using just local ID, claiming 60% of its inventory once qualified across 100 cities — then partially reversed course in Meerut in January 2025, now requiring proof of marriage and handing individual hotels discretion to refuse unmarried couples, explicitly citing "local social sensibilities" and signalling the policy could spread to more cities. This is not a settled rule you can simply memorise and move on from; it's a live, two-sided negotiation between a tech platform's growth logic and local conservative pushback, still being fought out hotel by hotel, city by city. It sits uneasily alongside "Atithi Devo Bhava," the guest-is-God slogan that is literally the Ministry of Tourism's own official branding — a slogan that gets mocked in India's own press as "an exercise in hypocrisy" whenever tourists get overcharged, scammed by drivers allegedly working with crooked cops, or treated rudely by officials. The gap between the branding and the lived complaint is, itself, the most honest thing anyone's told you about Indian hospitality.

## The Verdict

Nepal wins on sincerity: a teahouse owner who finds you a spot in the dining room when "full" is practicing hospitality as an actual value, not a marketing slogan, and the country only got around to formally rating its loveliest hotels once it realised the gap looked bad on paper, which is a very relatable kind of bureaucratic lateness. India wins on sheer narrative density — a voluntary star system, a Form C requirement that can land an ordinary homeowner in genuine legal jeopardy, and an unmarried-couples policy actively being renegotiated city by city, all happening simultaneously, loudly, and mostly unresolved. If I have to choose where I'd rather be confused, I'll take Nepal's quiet inconsistency over India's extremely well-documented one. But I wouldn't skip either booking.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Fodor's Travel Talk Forum — paraphrased: real travellers name actual owners by first name — Roy and Esha at the Tibet Peace Guest House called "delightful," manager Siddharthi at the Prince Hotel greeted by name — a texture of personal familiarity nowhere near how most countries' hotel reviews read.*

> *Expat.com, Nepal forum — paraphrased: one poster describes the vernacular Pokhara guesthouse model bluntly as "a bed in a room and a board hung in front of your house" — clean and cheap, but with zero atmosphere, and recommends buying and renovating an existing property instead.*

> *myRepublica — paraphrased: the piece opens with a real incident, a foreign resident's Kathmandu sublet notice headlined "Attn: Foreigners ONLY," mocked by the local expat community — then argues Nepalis often face more discrimination from other Nepalis than foreigners do, citing a Nepali-American woman refused a rental viewing once her accent revealed her nationality.*

## Conclusion

Nepal will house you with more warmth than its own paperwork suggests it's capable of, and the dining-room-floor compromise when a teahouse is "full" tells you more about the country's values than any star rating ever could. India will house you inside a system so thoroughly documented, voluntary, and occasionally punitive that you'll leave with a genuinely funny story about Form C whether you wanted one or not. Learn the names of whoever's running your guesthouse in Nepal. Keep your passport handy in India, just in case someone needs to compare it to your signature, your marriage certificate, or both.
