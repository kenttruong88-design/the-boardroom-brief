# France Makes You Prove You Exist Before It Lets You Bank, Colombia Makes You Prove It Twice, In Person

🇫🇷 France 🇨🇴 Colombia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nothing clarifies a country's actual relationship with trust quite like watching it process a stranger who wants to open a bank account. France will interrogate you politely, in writing, across several appointments, demanding documents that each require another document to obtain, in a closed loop so elegant it's almost admirable — a bureaucratic ouroboros designed, apparently on purpose, by people who genuinely enjoy paperwork as an art form. Colombia will send you, in person, to a notaría to get a document stamped by a human being who must physically witness your signature, because a PDF, however official-looking, is not French enough — sorry, not Colombian enough — to be trusted on its own.

Having navigated both systems to the point of opening functioning accounts, I can tell you the underlying philosophies are almost opposites: France distrusts you until a government-adjacent document vouches for you in writing; Colombia distrusts the document until a specific human being vouches for it in person.

## Do's & Don'ts: France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your "justificatif de domicile" (proof of address) sorted before anything else | Expect to open an account without a French address already secured — it's the first domino |
| Request your RIB (bank details slip) the moment the account exists; you'll need it constantly | Assume any single document works everywhere; each institution quietly prefers its own list |
| Book appointments well ahead — French bank branches run on scheduled meetings, not walk-ins | Lose your patience at the counter; measured, slightly weary politeness gets you further |

## Do's & Don'ts: Colombia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your cédula de extranjería (foreign ID) sorted first — almost nothing works without it | Try to open an account on a tourist visa alone; most banks will simply decline |
| Expect to visit a notaría for document authentication at some point in the process | Assume a scanned or digital copy will be accepted in place of a notarised original |
| Build in buffer time — in-person visits and signatures mean the process rarely moves fast | Get frustrated at the teller; the layers exist structurally and are rarely their personal choice |

## France: The Paperwork Ouroboros

Opening a French bank account as a newcomer is a rite of passage so notorious it has its own expat-forum mythology, and the mythology is, if anything, understated. The core absurdity is this: most French banks require proof of address to open an account, but many landlords and utility providers require a French bank account to set up the very utilities that generate that proof of address. You are handed a closed loop and expected to solve it through sheer persistence, a sympathetic HR department, or, increasingly, one of the newer digital banks that have built entire businesses around quietly ignoring this particular French tradition.

Once you're through that door, the actual bureaucracy settles into something closer to ritual than obstacle. Everything in French financial and administrative life runs on specific documents with specific names, each one a tiny trophy: the RIB, a slip with your account details that you will be asked to produce for your employer, your gym, your phone provider, and apparently your own reflection; the avis d'imposition, your tax notice, treated as a kind of civic identity card that proves not just income but belonging; the justificatif de domicile, proof you live where you say you live, renewed and re-demanded with startling frequency given how rarely most people actually move.

What softens all this, eventually, is that the system is consistent — maddeningly bureaucratic, yes, but not arbitrary. Learn the documents, learn the appointment-booking etiquette, and the machine, slowly, starts working for you rather than against you. The French bank branch itself remains a deeply pre-digital institution at heart: appointments matter, a relationship with your specific advisor matters, and showing up without one, expecting to simply be served, is treated as a minor act of social chaos.

## Colombia: Trust Lives In The Notaría

Colombia's bureaucratic logic runs on a different fuel entirely: the notaría, a kind of civic institution that exists almost nowhere in this exact form in the Anglophone world, where a notary doesn't just witness a signature but functions as a genuine authenticator of fact, empowered to certify that a document, a signature, or a declared circumstance is real. Nothing of consequence — opening certain accounts, registering a lease, authenticating a power of attorney — happens without, at some point, a physical visit to one of these offices, often with a queue, often requiring an appointment you didn't know you needed to book three days in advance.

The foundational document for almost everything is the cédula de extranjería, the foreign resident ID card, and until you have it, most of Colombia's financial and administrative machinery simply will not engage with you at all — not grudgingly, not with extra paperwork, just a flat no. Getting it requires its own gauntlet of migración appointments, biometrics, and patience, and only once it exists does the rest of the bureaucratic apparatus begin, however slowly, to open up.

Banking itself layers formality on formality: a simple account opening can require your cédula, proof of income or employment, a notarised address declaration, and — depending on the bank's mood that particular week — an in-branch interview that functions less like a transaction and more like a mild character reference. Digital banking and neobanks have made inroads in recent years, loosening some of this, but anything involving larger sums, mortgages, or business accounts still tends to route back through a human being, a stamp, and a queue. The system isn't slow because it's broken. It's slow because, culturally, a signature witnessed in person by a specific accountable human still outranks a digital trail in the hierarchy of trust.

## The Verdict

France will frustrate you with an elegant, self-referential paper loop that eventually resolves if you're patient and willing to play by its oddly specific rules. Colombia will frustrate you with physical queues, notarial stamps, and a cédula you can't get fast and can't function without, but once you have it, the system treats you with real warmth rather than bureaucratic indifference. France's bureaucracy is cold and procedural; Colombia's is slow but personal, built around humans vouching for humans rather than documents vouching for documents. If you must suffer, suffer in Colombia — at least someone looks you in the eye while stamping your fate.

## Conclusion

Bureaucracy is never really about paperwork, it's about what a society has decided counts as proof that you're real, trustworthy, and worth serving. France wants a document chain, impeccably sequenced, that eventually vouches for you on paper. Colombia wants a human being, physically present, to vouch for your document in person. Both will test your patience in the first ninety days in ways no relocation guide fully prepares you for, and both will, eventually, let you in — just don't expect either one to apologise for how long it took, or how many stamps it demanded along the way.
