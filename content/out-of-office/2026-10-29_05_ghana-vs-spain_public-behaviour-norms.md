# One Of These Countries Queues Like It's A Religious Rite. The Other Treats Your Personal Space As A Rude Suggestion.

🇬🇭 Ghana 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ghanaians will queue for four hours at a bank with the serene patience of people who've accepted that this is simply what Tuesday is, and they'll do it without raising their voice or giving you a disapproving glance for your lateness — because you greeted everyone in the room first, right hand only, eldest person first, and all is forgiven. Spaniards will form something that resembles a queue only in the sense that a flock of starlings resembles a flight formation, a cluster held together by one sacred question — "¿quién es el último?" — and then undo all that democratic order the second a 78-year-old abuela decides the bus is boarding and she, personally, is not waiting for anyone.

Both countries will tell you, with total sincerity, that the other one's way of doing this is the chaotic one. Both are correct about the other and wrong about themselves. I've stood in both lines. I've had my forearm gripped mid-sentence by a Spaniard who was not flirting with me, merely making a point, and I've been quietly, devastatingly out-greeted by a Ghanaian toddler who knew the correct handshake order better than I did after six months in the country. Let's get into it.

## Do's & Don'ts: Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet every single person in the room before you say anything else, right hand first, eldest person before anyone else | Lead with business talk — skipping the pleasantries reads as an act of open hostility |
| Accept that "Africa time" means events start roughly an hour after the stated time, and plan your own arrival accordingly | Show up aggressively on time and expect anyone to be impressed — you'll just be the first one standing around alone |
| Use only your right hand to give, receive, eat, and pay — the left is for the bathroom and nothing else | Hand someone money, a business card, or food with your left hand, even by accident, even once |
| Accept the queue at the bank, the hospital, or the registry office as a fixed cost of living here, and bring patience and a book | Try to "efficiency hack" your way past a government queue through visible impatience — it buys you nothing and costs you everything socially |

## Do's & Don'ts: Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask "¿quién es el último?" the second you arrive anywhere resembling a line, and remember who answered | Assume a visible queue shape means anything — the real queue is a mental ledger, and you are not in it until you've asked |
| Let the double-cheek kiss happen on first social introductions once you're past the handshake-only business stage | Flinch, recoil, or turn the wrong cheek first — start right, always, or you'll headbutt someone |
| Expect dinner conversations, bars, and trotro-adjacent plazas to run loud, overlapping, and gloriously undisciplined | Mistake volume for aggression — raised voices over dinner are a sign the evening is going well, not badly |
| Get comfortable with people standing closer than you're used to and touching your arm mid-conversation | Back away to "restore your personal space" — to a Spaniard, that's you being cold, not you being polite |

## Ghana

Here's the thing nobody tells you about queuing in Ghana: it isn't a failure of the system, it's practically the system's personality. GhanaWeb ran a piece calling the country "the Queuing Republic," pointing out that lines form for everything — churches, funeral grounds, hospitals, banks, National Service registration — and that private banks have started digitising their way out of it while the public sector has, with magnificent consistency, not bothered. The complaint wasn't that Ghanaians queue badly. It's that they queue too well, too patiently, for processes with no earthly reason to take four hours. That patience is the tell. This is a culture that has decided dignity under duress matters more than efficiency, and if you show up with your Western urgency intact, you'll be the only person in the room who looks unhinged.

What will actually get you in trouble is the greeting order, not the wait. Ghanaian social conduct runs on a right-to-left, eldest-first greeting sequence that functions as a small, constant exam you're always taking. Skip someone in the room — a child, a visiting grandmother, the person topping up your water — and you haven't just been rude, you've implied they don't exist. The right hand does everything: eating, paying, shaking hands, exchanging gifts. The left hand is, bluntly, the toilet hand, and handing someone anything with it reads as a small insult whether or not you meant it that way. Business conversations open with family and pleasantries before a single commercial word gets spoken; announce your agenda cold, and you've told the room their time is a formality.

Noise runs on entirely different rules than the hushed-queue stereotype suggests. Trotro minibuses blast music loud enough to double as the vehicle's personality, church and mosque loudspeakers compete for Sunday-morning airspace, and a GhanaWeb culture column complained — not unreasonably — that unplanned mixed-use neighbourhoods mean markets, transport terminals, and homes all bleed noise into each other with zero municipal interest in fixing it. Public affection, meanwhile, goes the other way entirely: expat forums are blunt that hand-holding is risky and kissing in public is a genuine faux pas, which makes Ghana a country simultaneously sonically chaotic and physically buttoned-up. Contradictions are the whole point.

## Spain

Spain's queue is a philosophical trap disguised as a logistics problem. There's frequently no visible line at all — just a loose cluster near a counter — and the entire social contract rests on one sentence: "¿quién es el último?" You ask, someone answers, you silently log your position, and the system holds, mostly, through pure collective memory. The Local's own columnist called queue-jumping "a national trait" and singled out older women at bus stops as the most ruthless violators of a system everyone claims to respect and nobody enforces. It works brilliantly until it doesn't, and when it doesn't, you'll watch a pensioner physically outmanoeuvre six people for a bus seat and respect her for it.

Personal space is where Spain dismantles the northern-European instinct to keep a three-foot moat around yourself at all times. Standing close during conversation reads as engagement, not aggression; a hand on your forearm mid-sentence is punctuation, not a pass. Step back to protect your bubble and you've just told the other person you find them unpleasant — the opposite of what you intended. Add in the dos besos, the cheek-kiss greeting that starts right and escalates from handshake to abrazo as a relationship warms, and you have a culture that treats physical distance as something to be closed, not defended.

Then there's the noise, which is less a bug than a civic value. Dinners get louder as they go well, several conversations run at once at full volume, bars leave the television on as ambient wallpaper over the chatter, and the sobremesa — talking at the table long after the plates are cleared — can eat an entire evening. One TalesMag respondent in Madrid flatly noted that staring at strangers isn't considered rude there, so don't take it personally when it happens to you. Dinner at ten, noise until midnight, and an afternoon lull between two and five when the streets empty out — Spain runs on a clock built for people at peace with being tired and loud in roughly equal measure.

## The Verdict

If you want a culture that respects your time in the queue but will quietly judge your soul for skipping a greeting, pick Ghana. If you want a culture that will stand closer to you than your own mother and call it friendliness, pick Spain. Neither one is doing you the courtesy of matching your home country's instincts, and that's rather the point of leaving it.

My verdict, delivered with the smugness of someone who has queued badly in both: Spain wins on sheer honesty. Ghana's queue discipline is admirable but it's discipline in service of systems that shouldn't need it — four hours at a bank is not a cultural value, it's a shortfall wearing cultural clothing. Spain's chaos, by contrast, is chaos by design. Nobody pretends the "¿quién es el último?" system is efficient. They just agree it's theirs, shrug, and get on with being loud about something more interesting than admin. A culture that's honest about its disorder beats one that's quietly suffering through its order with impeccable manners. Ghana, you have the better etiquette. Spain, you have the better excuse.

## Conclusion

Neither country is going to apologise for how it behaves in public, and that's precisely why both are worth the culture shock. Ghana will make you a more patient, more socially literate human being whether you like it or not — you cannot skip the greeting, cheat the queue, or eat with your left hand and expect to get away with it twice. Spain will make you a louder, closer-standing human being, one who's stopped flinching when someone grabs your arm to make a point over dinner at eleven at night. The common thread, if you're looking for one, is that both places have decided efficiency is negotiable and dignity is not — they've just defined dignity in opposite directions. Go in assuming your own country's public manners are the neutral, correct default, and both Accra and Madrid will spend your first six months quietly, thoroughly correcting you. Go in assuming nothing, and you might actually enjoy the correction.
