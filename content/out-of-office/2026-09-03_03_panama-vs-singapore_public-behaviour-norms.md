# One Country Fines You for Chewing Gum, the Other Just Wants You to Enjoy the Noise

🇵🇦 Panama 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

If public behaviour norms were a spectrum, Panama City and Singapore would be occupying opposite ends of it while somehow both claiming to be the more "civilised" option. Panama runs on volume, proximity, and a cheerful disregard for personal space that would send a Singaporean straight into a small private panic. Singapore runs on order so total it feels less like a city and more like a very well-funded simulation, where jaywalking earns you a fine and littering earns you a lecture from strangers who've internalised the rulebook so thoroughly they've become unpaid enforcement officers.

Having lived amid Panama's glorious sensory overload and Singapore's spotless, hyper-regulated calm, I can say with confidence that both cities have simply optimised for different things. Panama optimised for life happening loudly, in public, all the time. Singapore optimised for nothing ever going wrong, ever, under any circumstances, and enforced that with fines specific enough to make a lawyer blush.

## Do's & Don'ts: Panama

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Embrace loud street conversation, salsa spilling from a car stereo, and vendors shouting prices | Expect a quiet commute, silence in public is treated as slightly suspicious |
| Greet people warmly and take time for small talk, rushing through interactions reads as rude | Cut in line without acknowledging anyone, Panamanians notice and will say something |
| Accept that personal space is a flexible concept, especially on public buses | Be surprised by impromptu music or dancing breaking out in a plaza on a weekday evening |

## Do's & Don'ts: Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stand on the left of the escalator and queue in an orderly single file, always | Bring durian onto public transport, it's an actual banned-item, not a joke |
| Dispose of litter properly, fines are real and enforcement is not symbolic | Jaywalk near a main road, cameras and fines make this a genuinely bad idea |
| Keep your voice down on the MRT, loud phone calls draw visible disapproval | Eat or drink on the train, a small thing that will get you side-eyed instantly |

## Panama: Life, Loudly, on Every Corner

Panama City does not do quiet. Walk through Casco Viejo or El Cangrejo at almost any hour and you'll hear reggaeton from a parked car, a vendor calling out fruit prices, someone's uncle greeting a neighbour across the street at a volume better suited to a stadium. This isn't disorder, it's simply a public culture that treats the street as an extension of the living room, and expects you to participate rather than pass through silently with headphones in.

Personal space bends accordingly. On a Diablo Rojo bus, the old, gloriously decorated school buses that still run some routes, you will be pressed against strangers, and nobody finds this remarkable. Physical closeness in conversation is standard, not an invasion, and Panamanians will lean in, touch your arm to make a point, and consider a transaction incomplete without at least thirty seconds of unrelated pleasantries first. Trying to rush a cashier or skip the "buenas" greeting on entering a shop marks you instantly as an outsider who hasn't learned the actual rules, which have nothing to do with efficiency and everything to do with warmth.

What took me longest to adjust to wasn't the noise, it was the unpredictability of public joy. A plaza can go from empty to a full impromptu dance party because someone brought a speaker and the mood struck. Nobody organised it. Nobody needed to. This is, genuinely, one of the more life-affirming things about Panamanian public life, once you stop instinctively looking around for the authority figure who's supposed to shut it down.

## Singapore: Order as a Civic Religion

Singapore, meanwhile, has turned public behaviour into something closer to a national engineering project. The famous fines, for littering, for jaywalking, for chewing gum in public, for failing to flush a public toilet, are real, specific, and genuinely enforced, and the result is a city so clean and orderly it can feel almost eerie to anyone arriving from somewhere less controlled. Panamanians would find the MRT platform queues, precisely aligned to the doors, waiting in total silence, borderline unsettling.

What's more interesting than the fines themselves is how deeply the norms have been internalised beyond the letter of the law. Singaporeans don't just avoid littering because they fear a fine, they've absorbed cleanliness as a point of collective pride, and will genuinely tut at a tourist who drops a wrapper, no government intervention required. The same goes for queuing: nobody's checking, people simply queue, in a single orderly line, for a bus, a hawker stall, an escalator, because the alternative feels almost physically wrong to them.

The trade-off, and there is one, is spontaneity. Public life here is comfortable, safe, and predictable, but it rarely erupts into anything unplanned. A street performance requires a permit. A gathering above a certain size requires notification. It's a city that has decided collective order is worth more than collective chaos, and largely, its residents agree, though newer generations increasingly grumble, quietly, in designated grumbling zones, presumably.

## The Verdict

Panama wins if you want a public life that feels alive, unscripted, and generous with human warmth, at the cost of your personal space and, occasionally, your eardrums. Singapore wins if you want a city that functions like clockwork and never once makes you nervous about walking home at night, at the cost of ever seeing a stranger break into spontaneous song on public transport. I love Panama for making me feel like part of something. I respect Singapore for never once making me late. Pick your poison, or in Singapore's case, pick your carefully regulated, non-poisonous, fully-permitted alternative.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — "Is Singapore's fine culture as strict as people say?" paraphrased: a longtime resident said the fines are real but rarely the actual deterrent, the deterrent is the collective disapproving stare you get from every commuter around you.*

> *expat.com Panama City forum, paraphrased: a new arrival described being startled the first week by how loud daily life was, then two months later realising they missed the noise entirely on a quiet trip home.*

> *InterNations Singapore, paraphrased: an expat recounted being firmly, politely corrected by a stranger for standing on the wrong side of the escalator, and said it happened within their first 48 hours in the country.*

## Conclusion

Public behaviour norms tell you exactly what a society has decided to prioritise, and Panama and Singapore have made almost opposite bets. One bet on spontaneity and human warmth spilling into every street corner. The other bet on order so complete it borders on architecture. Neither is wrong, but only one of them will fine you for a piece of gum, and only one of them will hand you a beer at a stranger's impromptu street party on a Tuesday. Choose your civilisation accordingly.
