# One Country Will Just Switch to English, the Other Expects You to Bow First and Ask Questions Later

🇭🇷 Croatia vs 🇯🇵 Japan

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Order a coffee wrong in Split and the waiter will simply switch to near-perfect English, roll their eyes affectionately at your attempt, and move on with the day. Order a coffee wrong in a small Tokyo café and you'll get a genuinely patient, slightly bewildered silence while the barista tries to parse what combination of gestures and mangled Japanese you've just produced. Croatia meets you halfway without being asked. Japan expects effort, rewards it generously when it appears, and remains politely unmoved when it doesn't.

I've fumbled through both languages badly, and the two failures felt completely different. In Croatia, botching the language barely registers as a social event. In Japan, it's a small, quiet performance that everyone involved handles with extraordinary grace — grace that, frankly, I hadn't earned.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇭🇷 Croatia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Try a few Croatian phrases — it's appreciated, not expected | Assume everyone over 60 speaks fluent English |
| Lean on English confidently in coastal tourist areas | Expect the same fluency levels inland |
| Learn "hvala" and "molim" at minimum | Speak loudly and slowly, assuming that helps |
| Use translation apps freely — nobody minds | Get frustrated by regional dialect differences |

### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic phrases — the effort is genuinely valued | Assume English will get you through bureaucracy |
| Use pointing, gestures, and translation apps without shame | Speak too fast or use idioms in English |
| Carry a phone with a translation app for menus and signs | Expect casual small talk in English from strangers |
| Bow slightly when thanking someone — it smooths everything | Be visibly frustrated — it makes the other person uncomfortable |

## Croatia: Meeting You Where You Are, Mostly

Croatia's relationship with English is refreshingly pragmatic, particularly along the Dalmatian coast, where decades of tourism have made fluency less a skill and more a survival requirement. Younger Croatians in Zagreb, Split, and Dubrovnik often speak English impressively well, and there's little of the self-consciousness about switching languages mid-conversation that you find in more linguistically protective cultures. Botch a Croatian sentence and you'll likely just get a smile and a smooth transition into English, no drama attached.

That ease thins out the further inland and further from tourist infrastructure you travel. Smaller towns and older generations lean far more heavily on Croatian alone, and expecting the coastal fluency rate to hold everywhere is a rookie mistake that leaves plenty of newcomers stranded at rural post offices with nothing but hand gestures.

Croatian itself is a genuinely difficult language for English speakers — seven grammatical cases will humble anyone — but the culture doesn't demand mastery before extending patience. A few mangled attempts at "dobar dan" or "hvala" earn real warmth, even if your grammar collapses immediately afterward. There's no expectation that you'll get fluent quickly, and no judgment for leaning on English as a crutch indefinitely.

What's most striking is the total absence of performative frustration on either side. Croatians don't sigh dramatically when a foreigner struggles, and foreigners rarely feel embarrassed enough to avoid trying. It's a low-stakes linguistic environment, forgiving by both convenience and temperament — which, frankly, makes it one of the easier places in Europe to be linguistically incompetent without consequence.

## Japan: Effort Is Rewarded, Fluency Is Not Assumed

Japan operates on almost the opposite social contract. English proficiency, outside major international hubs and younger urban populations, is genuinely limited — years of grammar-focused, exam-oriented English education haven't produced the conversational fluency you'd expect from the scores. This isn't reluctance or protectiveness the way it can feel in more linguistically confident nations; it's often genuine anxiety about getting it wrong in front of a native speaker.

What Japan does instead is reward visible effort with disproportionate warmth. Attempt even a clumsy sentence in Japanese, get the grammar wrong, mispronounce half the words, and you'll often be met with genuine delight rather than correction. The culture values the attempt itself as a sign of respect, and that attempt unlocks a kindness that fluency alone sometimes doesn't.

Bureaucracy is where the language barrier bites hardest. City hall registrations, banking, healthcare paperwork — these processes assume Japanese fluency almost entirely, and navigating them without it, or without a translator, ranges from slow to genuinely paralysing. Translation apps have become an unofficial third language for foreign residents, held up at counters as a substitute for conversation nobody's embarrassed to use anymore.

Gestures, pointing, and the universal language of an apologetic smile do a surprising amount of heavy lifting day to day. Japanese communication culture already relies heavily on non-verbal cues and contextual reading even between native speakers, which means foreigners fumbling through gestures aren't as out of place as they might feel. The politeness cushioning every interaction means frustration rarely surfaces visibly, even when both parties are quietly lost.

## The Verdict

Croatia's linguistic hospitality means you can coast on English indefinitely, particularly near the coast, and rarely feel the friction. Japan's linguistic culture demands more from you but gives back proportionally — the effort you put in is met, warmly and specifically, every single time.

I found Croatia easier and Japan more rewarding, which is perhaps the least surprising sentence I've ever written about language learning. Suki's verdict: if you want convenience without guilt, Croatia will let you off the hook entirely. If you want a language barrier that actually teaches you something about humility and effort, Japan will make you earn every scrap of connection — and somehow that makes the connection mean more.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/croatia — someone admitted living three years on the coast without learning Croatian beyond "hvala," and nobody in the thread seemed particularly scandalised.*

> *Reddit r/japanlife — a longtime resident described an entire successful apartment rental negotiated through pointing, a translation app, and mutual bowing, calling it "the most Japanese thing that's ever happened to me."*

> *expat.com Tokyo — a newcomer warned that city hall staff rarely speak English, and recommended bringing a Japanese-speaking friend to any bureaucratic appointment, without exception.*

## Conclusion

Croatia lets the language barrier dissolve quietly, almost apologetically, the moment you need it to. Japan holds the barrier firmly in place, but hands you genuine warmth and respect for every honest attempt to cross it. Move to Split and you can get by comfortably on English and goodwill. Move to Tokyo and invest in a decent translation app, a handful of memorised phrases, and a willingness to be gently, endlessly patient with yourself. Both will humble you eventually. Only one will make you bow while it does.
