# One Country Shuts Down for a Month, the Other Rearranges Time Itself

🇫🇷 France vs 🇲🇦 Morocco — Festival and Public Holiday Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

France doesn't have public holidays so much as it has a series of scheduled national sulks, each one an excuse to close the boulangerie and remind you that leisure is a right, not a privilege you earn. Morocco, meanwhile, treats its holidays — religious, royal, agricultural — as full-body events that reorganise entire cities around them, sometimes with only a lunar calendar's worth of warning. Both countries know how to stop working. Only one of them tells you in advance.

I have been locked out of an entire village in Provence in August, and I have arrived in Marrakech the day Ramadan began without knowing it, wondering why every restaurant had gone dark by sundown. Guess which one embarrassed me more.

## Do's & Don'ts: France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan around the "pont" — when a holiday lands near a weekend, France takes the whole bridge off | Try to get any admin done in the first three weeks of August |
| Join a Fête de la Musique street crowd on 21 June — it's free and genuinely joyful | Expect shops in small towns to reopen quickly after a holiday Monday |
| Book restaurants well ahead of Bastille Day fireworks nights | Assume "closed for the holiday" means just the one day |
| Learn the difference between a jour férié and an actual day off — they're not the same thing | Schedule a delivery, repair, or bureaucratic appointment for the week of 15 August |

## Do's & Don'ts: Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the lunar calendar close to the date — Eid dates shift and get confirmed late | Eat, drink, or smoke visibly in public during Ramadan daylight hours |
| Adjust your own schedule around Ramadan working hours if you're staying long-term | Expect restaurants to run normal hours during Ramadan — many flip entirely to night service |
| Say "Eid Mubarak" and mean it — locals notice and appreciate the effort | Book Eid al-Adha weekend transport without reserving weeks ahead |
| Experience a moussem (regional festival) if you're near one — they're spectacular and underrated | Assume the holiday calendar is fixed a year out — the Islamic calendar moves annually |

## France: The Art of the Scheduled Disappearance

France treats its public holidays less as days off and more as legally protected acts of collective disappearance. Eleven jours fériés a year, and the French have turned the spaces between them into an art form — the "pont" (bridge), where if a holiday falls on a Tuesday, you simply don't bother coming in on Monday either, and nobody questions it. Then there's August, which isn't technically a public holiday at all but functions as one anyway: shops shutter with hand-written signs reading "fermeture annuelle," entire towns empty toward the coast, and if your boiler breaks on the 5th of August, it is staying broken until September, and no amount of foreign indignation will change that.

What foreigners misread as laziness is, in fact, a deeply held philosophical position: work exists to fund life, not the other way around, and a country that will strike over pension reform is not going to quietly surrender its right to a proper lunch, let alone a proper summer. Bastille Day, 14 July, is the one holiday that turns outward rather than inward — fireworks over every city, fire brigades throwing open-invite balls, actual joy in the streets rather than the shuttered calm of most French holidays. Fête de la Musique in June is better still: free, spontaneous, every street corner suddenly a stage, the one day the French seem happy to let strangers into the party.

The trap for newcomers is assuming French holidays are single-day events. They rarely are. A holiday Thursday quietly annexes Friday. A holiday near a weekend annexes the whole bridge. Plan your admin, your deliveries, your entire bureaucratic life around this, or don't plan it at all in the two weeks either side of any given jour férié.

## Morocco: Time Bends Around Faith and the Palace

Morocco's calendar runs on two systems simultaneously — the fixed Gregorian dates for royal and national holidays, and the lunar Islamic calendar for Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha, and Ramadan, which shift roughly eleven days earlier every year and aren't confirmed until religious authorities sight the moon, sometimes mere days before the holiday itself. This drives planning-obsessed foreigners to genuine despair, and it is, frankly, glorious that a country simply refuses to pretend the moon operates on a spreadsheet.

Ramadan reorganises the entire country's daily rhythm for a full month: daytime hours slow to a near-halt, many businesses shorten their day, and eating or drinking visibly in public during daylight is a real social transgression, not a quaint suggestion — then at sunset, the iftar cannon fires (literally, in some cities) and the streets erupt into a nightlife that runs until dawn. It is, without exaggeration, one of the most dramatic shifts in national tempo you will witness anywhere on earth, and treating it as an inconvenience rather than a privilege to witness says more about you than about Morocco.

Beyond the religious calendar, Morocco's regional moussems — festivals honouring local saints, harvests, or crafts, from the Imilchil marriage festival to the Rose Festival in the Dadès Valley — remain criminally underrated by outsiders who fly straight to Marrakech's medina and miss the country entirely. Throw in Throne Day, honouring the king, and Independence Day, and you have a calendar that blends faith, monarchy, and harvest in a way France, for all its secular pride, never quite manages.

## The Verdict

France closes on schedule; Morocco closes on faith, and faith doesn't consult your itinerary. If you value predictability, France wins by a mile — you can plan a decade of holidays around the Gregorian calendar and never be surprised. But if you want a public holiday that actually transforms how a society feels, Morocco's Ramadan and Eid leave France's fireworks looking like a corporate team-building exercise. My verdict, reluctantly, favours Morocco — not because it's easier, but because nothing in France's calendar comes close to the collective emotional weight of a sunset iftar cannon.

## Conclusion

France gives you a calendar you can print out and trust; Morocco gives you a calendar that trusts the moon more than it trusts you, which is, honestly, the more interesting way to live. Both countries know that a holiday worth having is one that actually stops the machine — France just prefers its stoppages scheduled to the minute, while Morocco prefers them sacred. Neither is wrong. Just don't book anything important in August in one, or the week before Eid in the other. You have been warned, generously, by someone who wasn't.
