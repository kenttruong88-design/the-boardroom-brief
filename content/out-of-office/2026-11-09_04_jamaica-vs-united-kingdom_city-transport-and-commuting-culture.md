# Nobody Queues for a Route Taxi, and Nobody Talks on the Tube — That's the Whole Difference

🇯🇲 Jamaica · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is the first thing you learn about moving between these two islands: Jamaica will put five people in the back seat of a Toyota that was engineered for three, and Britain will build you a six-platform cathedral of a train station and then cancel the 8:14 without an apology. Both systems get you to work. Neither will ever admit it has a problem.

I've commuted on both — crammed into a route taxi outside Half Way Tree with a stranger's elbow in my ribs, and standing in London Bridge station at rush hour pretending the delay board isn't lying to me — and I can tell you the real divide isn't infrastructure. It's honesty. Jamaica's transport chaos is loud, visible, and nobody pretends otherwise. Britain's chaos wears a blazer, speaks in a calm recorded voice, and calls itself "a good service with minor delays." One of these is more annoying. I'll let you guess which.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Jamaica**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the route taxi's numbered plate system and flag it like you mean it | Expect a fixed timetable — there isn't one, there's vibes |
| Agree the fare before you get in a charter taxi, or make sure the meter's running | Assume the bus is "too empty to bother" — some drivers will turn back if it is |
| Travel in daylight where possible and use registered taxis at night | Leave valuables loose — pickpocketing is the main real risk, not the driving |
| Keep small bills ready for route taxis and buses | Expect personal space — four or five to a back seat is standard, not a crisis |

**United Kingdom**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stand on the right on escalators, always, without exception | Block the doors, chat loudly, or make eye contact on the Tube |
| Tap in and out properly — if the barrier says "seek assistance," ask staff, don't just keep tapping | Shout "move down please!" into a carriage that is already full to the doors |
| Check live departure boards; "on time" is a provisional concept | Assume a delay means a refund is coming without checking compensation rules first |
| Offer your seat to anyone who looks like they need it more than you | Take your bulky bag off and leave it blocking the aisle during rush hour |

## Jamaica: Organised Chaos, Mostly Organised

Kingston's buses, run by the state-owned Jamaica Urban Transit Company, are the backbone that nobody fully trusts and everybody still uses, because they're cheap and the network genuinely covers the city. The real workhorse, though, is the route taxi — a shared car or minibus running a fixed route, flagged down anywhere along it, identifiable by red plates and a number painted somewhere you'll squint to find. They're faster than the bus, cost a bit more, and pack you in with a cheerfulness that would get a London bus driver reported to TfL. One expat forum veteran, writing on Expat.com under the handle LisaHR, put it plainly: route taxis "pack people in tho, so u might find 4 or 5 people in the back seat," with stragglers squeezed into the front too. She also noted the system's quieter hazard: "you have your crazy driver who will decide the bus is empty" and simply turns around rather than run a thin route — a logic that makes sense to precisely the driver making it.

The government has been trying, gamely, to formalise all this. In March 2025, Transport Minister Daryl Vaz announced that eleven hackney carriage routes in the Kingston Metropolitan Transport Region would convert to official route taxi licences — routes previously ring-fenced for the JUTC, which, remarkably, didn't object. It's the kind of bureaucratic housekeeping that sounds dull until you realise it's an attempt to legitimise a system that half the city already runs on informally.

Then there's the driving itself, which is its own cultural event. Road deaths actually fell from 425 in 2023 to 365 in 2024, according to data compiled for expats — real, meaningful progress — but Jamaica's fatality rate remains among the highest in the Caribbean, with speeding linked to 87 deaths in 2024 alone. Enforcement of electronic ticketing is, by official admission, patchy. An ExpatExchange contributor summed up the daily commuter reality with the kind of weary understatement only someone who's done the school run on a JUTC bus can manage: the regular bus service "is very crowded, you'd probably prefer to drive." Quite.

There's also the matter of the "robot taxi" — not an autonomous vehicle, confusingly, but local slang for an unlicensed private car working as an informal taxi, operating entirely outside the hackney and route-taxi licensing system. Everyone knows which cars are robots, nobody officially acknowledges it, and the arrangement persists because the formal network simply doesn't reach everywhere it needs to. It's the same instinct that built the route taxi system in the first place: when the official infrastructure has a gap, Kingston fills it itself and worries about the paperwork later, if at all.

## United Kingdom: The Theatre of Quiet Suffering

If Jamaica's transport culture is loud about its flaws, Britain's is a masterclass in suffering with your headphones in and your face arranged into polite blankness. The Tube — and by extension the entire British commuting psyche — runs on unwritten law. Stand right on escalators. Don't make eye contact. Let people off before you board. These aren't suggestions; breach them and you will feel the collective disapproval of an entire carriage without a single word being spoken, which is, frankly, more effective than shouting.

A Reddit thread that went properly viral turned into a crowdsourced etiquette rulebook, and it's more revealing about the British psyche than any sociology textbook. User Roy Badami's rule — "have a bulky bag? Take it off" — got widespread agreement, as did the demand that boarding passengers leave room for the people behind them, since "trains don't stop for long." But the one that stuck with me was John Luther's response to people who keep jabbing a card at a barrier that's already told them twice to seek assistance: "SEEK BLOODY ASSISTANCE." It's rage, but it's rage in service of orderly queueing, which is the most British sentence I will write this year.

Underneath the etiquette is a punctuality system that the Office of Rail and Road tracks with genuine rigour — quarterly reports on delays, causes, and compensation, published with civil-service solemnity. The data exists, the transparency exists, and none of it stops the 17:46 from Paddington being seven minutes late while a recorded voice thanks you for your patience. The infrastructure is extraordinary — more Tube lines, trains, and route density than Kingston could dream of — and the national mood around it is still, somehow, one of grim, shared martyrdom.

The Oyster card and its contactless successors deserve credit for one genuine miracle: daily and weekly fare capping, which quietly charges you the cheapest possible total for the journeys you've actually made rather than forcing you to buy the right ticket upfront. It's the single most user-friendly thing about the whole system, and it exists, naturally, alongside a fare structure so byzantine that entire articles are written each year just explaining how zones 1 through 9 actually work.

## The Verdict

Jamaica wins this one, and it's not close. Not because the system is more efficient — it manifestly isn't — but because it's honest about what it is. A route taxi driver who decides the bus is "too empty" and turns around is operating on a logic you can at least follow. A London train that's "delayed due to a signalling issue" at 8am on a Tuesday, every Tuesday, for the third year running, is operating on a logic that insults you while doing it. Jamaica's chaos has personality. Britain's dysfunction has a laminated noticeboard and an apologetic PA system, which somehow makes it worse. I'd rather share a back seat with four strangers and a crate of mangoes than stand in dignified silence pretending the 08:14 isn't a lie.

**What Nobody Warned You About**

> *LisaHR, Expat.com Jamaica forum — paraphrased: route taxis in Jamaica are fine, but be ready to find four or five people already wedged into the back seat before you even get in.*

> *ExpatExchange contributor, "Living in Kingston" — paraphrased: the regular bus service in Kingston gets so crowded that most people who can afford it end up choosing to drive instead.*

> *Reddit user John Luther, via a London Tube-etiquette thread — paraphrased: if the ticket barrier tells you twice to seek assistance, stop tapping and actually go find a member of staff.*

So there you have it: one country where the system is informal but the people are blunt about its limits, and one where the system is formalised within an inch of its life but everyone's too polite to say the quiet part out loud — that it still doesn't really work. Move to Kingston and you'll learn to read a driver's intentions before he's finished deciding them. Move to London and you'll learn to stand on the right, say nothing, and internalise your rage like a true local. Both are survivable. Only one will make you a more interesting dinner guest.
