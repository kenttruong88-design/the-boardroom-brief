# Germany Closes on Sundays Out of Principle, and Romania Keeps the Market Open Out of Necessity

🇷🇴 Romania · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two ways to run a grocery system. The first is to treat the weekly shop as a moral exercise, with rules, deposits, and a closed door on Sunday to remind you that you have a soul. The second is to treat food as a negotiation between you, a market stall and a woman who has opinions about your tomatoes. Germany chose the first with Teutonic sincerity. Romania, with a shrug and a plastic bag, chose the second.

Both countries have supermarkets, and both countries have strong views about what happens in them. But the German one comes with a coin for the trolley, a deposit on the bottle and a locked door on the seventh day. The Romanian one comes with a hypermarket, a farmers' market down the road and a feeling that anyone selling you beef might have blended it with pork. I have loved both and been furious at both, and the fury was always about food I could not buy at the hour I wanted it.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇷🇴 Romania**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit the outdoor market (piața) for produce; Obor is the largest in Bucharest | Assume the supermarket tomato is the best tomato available |
| Know the main chains: Mega Image, Carrefour, Lidl, Kaufland | Expect every chain to follow the same holiday schedule |
| Use warehouse stores like Metro or Selgros for bulk buying | Assume everything on the shelf is what the label implies, especially mixed beef |
| Check hours before Christmas and New Year; many hypermarkets shut on 25 December and 1 January | Assume the shop will be open because you are hungry |

**🇩🇪 Germany**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry a €1 coin for the trolley deposit | Expect free carrier bags |
| Shop by Saturday evening; most supermarkets are closed on Sundays | Plan a Sunday roast without having bought the joint |
| Return Pfand bottles at the machine and use the coupon at the till | Forget that Aldi and Lidl accept only single-use cans and plastic bottles |
| Check for a verkaufsoffener Sonntag in your state if you are desperate | Assume a Späti is always legal or always open |

## 🇷🇴 Romania: Chains Above, Markets Below

The Romanian grocery scene is an argument between the global and the local, and the local is still winning on points. The chain landscape is familiar to anyone who has stood in a Central European car park: Mega Image, which belongs to the Delhaize family, plus Carrefour, Lidl and Kaufland, with Metro and Selgros warehouse stores for the serious bulk buyer. A Bucharest post report lists a J&K Store for East Asian staples and the Obor market as the largest outdoor market in the city, open year-round. A 2015 column on one Bucharest suburb counted four Mega Image shops, two Carrefour Markets, a Penny, a Lidl and a lone 24/7 independent, and predicted a price war. I include it as a sketch of the era, not as a map of today.

The real differences are cultural. The same post report gives a picture of prices that depends on what you buy: raw ingredients cost a fraction of American prices, while prepackaged foods can run higher, and a June 2023 poster says prices are still affordable but "much higher post-COVID". One poster warns that beef in shops is often mixed with pork and that a genuinely 100 per cent beef steak comes from a local butcher. Another says fresh fish is hard to find. This is not a failure, it is an instruction: go to the person who knows.

Holidays are the other trap. A Romania Insider piece from the 2015 season reported that most hypermarkets and supermarkets shut on 25 December and 1 January, with a few opening for afternoon hours, and that Lidl was the only chain said to close on both 25 and 26 December. Dated, certainly, but the lesson hasn't aged: in Romania you buy Christmas dinner before Christmas, not on it.

What I love about Romanian shopping is that it has not yet been standardised into joylessness. The outdoor market is still the place where the produce is excellent and affordable, a Dec 2008 poster said, and the chains still have personality. What I do not love is the uncertainty. You never quite know whether the thing on the label is the thing in the package.

## 🇩🇪 Germany: The Republic of Rules and Receipts

Germany has built the most orderly supermarket system on earth, and it will not let you forget it. The numbers alone are staggering: Edeka is the largest chain with nearly 6,000 stores, Rewe has over 3,000, Kaufland about 600 hypermarkets, and the discounters, Aldi with over 4,000 stores, Netto Marken-Discount over 4,000, Lidl over 3,000, fight over every cent. For organics there are Denn's Biomarkt and Alnatura. The trolley needs a coin, usually €1. Bags cost money. Hours run roughly from 07:00 to 20:00, though many supermarkets stay open later, and the whole edifice closes on Sunday.

That Sunday closure has a history. The Ladenschlussgesetz of 1956 set the national hours; the long Thursday arrived in 1989, Saturday closing moved to 20:00 in 2003, and in 2006 the sixteen states took over the power to set hours, though Bavaria still uses the 2003 federal rules. Many states allow a handful of verkaufsoffene Sonntage per year, usually 13:00 to 20:00. Exceptions exist for train stations, airports, petrol-station mini-marts, bakeries, florists and pharmacies. Berlin's Späti operate in what the sources politely call a legal gray area. An Expat.com thread argued about the Wednesday-afternoon shop closures in suburban Stuttgart, with one veteran noting that opening hours have improved hugely, that most places now open until 22:00 Monday to Saturday, and that Sunday stays reserved for family with broad political support. I respect the principle and resent the practice.

And then there is Pfand. It is the deposit system that turns every German into a part-time recycling clerk. The amounts: 8 cents for beer bottles, 25 cents for cans and single-use plastic bottles, and about €1.50 for a crate. The machine prints a coupon for the till. Aldi and Lidl accept only single-use cans and plastic bottles, while Rewe and Edeka take most types. It is efficient and mildly humiliating, and it works, which is the German condition in a sentence.

## The Verdict

Germany wins on reliability and loses on soul. Romania wins on freshness and loses on predictability. If you want to know exactly what you are buying, how much it costs and when the shop will be open, Germany will deliver, and the deposit coin in your pocket will become a sort of rosary. If you want a plum that tastes like a plum, bought from a person who has views, Romania is the place.

I will give the point to Germany, reluctantly, for one reason: I can plan around a closed Sunday. I cannot plan around a label that says one thing and a butcher who says another. Order is a flavour, and I have grown fond of it, though I would trade the coin and the closed door for a Romanian market on any Saturday morning.

My sting: the German system treats you like an adult who needs a rule. The Romanian one treats you like an adult who needs a butcher. Only one of them actually makes dinner.

## Conclusion

I have shopped in sixty countries, and the supermarket is where you learn the real social contract. In Germany, the contract is written down, deposit included. In Romania, it is verbal and negotiable, and the market will forgive your accent if your money is good.

Take a coin to Germany and a bag to Romania. Buy your Sunday food on Saturday in one and your Christmas food before Christmas in the other. And in both, find the person who sells the thing you actually want, because the shelf will never love you back.
