# One City's Traffic Has No Rules, the Other's Has Too Many and Everyone Follows Them Anyway

🇳🇵 Nepal 🇨🇱 Chile
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Kathmandu traffic operates on a principle I can only describe as negotiated chaos: lanes are theoretical, horns function as a second language, and a motorbike will find a gap that shouldn't physically exist and use it with total confidence. Somehow, almost nobody crashes into anybody, which suggests either extraordinary collective skill or a citywide agreement to simply not discuss how close every commute comes to catastrophe.

Santiago, by brutal contrast, runs one of the most efficient metro systems in Latin America, a genuinely impressive feat of infrastructure that Chileans use with the calm, orderly resignation of people boarding a very punctual, very crowded train they've taken ten thousand times before. One city's commute is an adrenaline sport. The other's is a masterclass in queueing. Guess which one I'd rather do at 8am with a hangover.

## Do's & Don'ts: Nepal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate the taxi fare before getting in, always, no exceptions | Expect a meter to be used, honoured, or even present |
| Embrace the horn as communication, not aggression — it means "I'm here," not "I'm angry" | Try to walk in a straight line and expect traffic to yield; you weave, it flows |
| Use tempos (shared electric three-wheelers) for genuinely cheap, fast local trips | Get in a microbus expecting personal space; it will fill past reasonable capacity |
| Budget serious extra time during monsoon season for flooded, impassable streets | Assume road rules posted on paper reflect actual road behaviour |

## Do's & Don'ts: Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a bip! card immediately; cash isn't accepted on Metro or most buses | Stand on the left of the escalator — it's reserved for walking, enforced socially and firmly |
| Expect the Metro to be genuinely on time, and act accordingly | Eat or drink on the Metro; it's against the rules and locals will notice |
| Validate your card on both bus and Metro legs — the integrated fare requires it | Assume rush hour ends by 7pm; Santiago's peak commute runs late |
| Stand right, walk left, queue properly — Santiago's commuting culture is orderly | Block doors during boarding; Chileans form disciplined lines and expect the same |

## Nepal: Traffic as Improvised Choreography

Kathmandu's roads were never built for the volume of vehicles now using them, and the resulting system, if "system" is even the word, runs on a kind of collective improvisation that terrifies newcomers before it, eventually, impresses them. Lane markings exist mostly as suggestions, traffic lights are more decorative than functional at many intersections, and the actual right-of-way hierarchy is unwritten but universally understood: bigger vehicle wins, except when a determined pedestrian simply steps into the flow and dares the traffic to stop, which it usually, miraculously, does.

The horn is the real infrastructure here. Nepali drivers use it constantly, but not as an expression of road rage, rather as continuous positional broadcasting: I am here, I am passing, I am about to do something you should know about. Visitors who read the horn as hostility miss that it's actually a courtesy system, just one that sounds, to unfamiliar ears, like a citywide argument.

Motorbikes dominate, weaving between stalled buses and idling taxis with a fluency that looks reckless and is, in fact, a highly developed skill most Kathmandu residents acquire by their late teens. Tempos, the electric three-wheelers, offer a genuinely efficient way to move short distances for very little money, though "efficient" here means "fast," not "comfortable" — expect to be pressed against several strangers while your driver executes manoeuvres that would fail a Western driving test spectacularly.

Monsoon season adds an entirely separate layer of chaos: streets flood, potholes become small lakes, and commute times that are already unpredictable become genuinely impossible to estimate. Locals simply build enormous slack into any plan involving travel across the valley, treating "I'll be there in twenty minutes" as an aspiration rather than a commitment, which, once you adjust your own expectations to match, turns out to be the only sane way to live.

## Chile: Order as National Character, Applied to Public Transit

Santiago's Metro is, by any honest regional comparison, remarkable: clean, extensive, punctual, and genuinely well-run, connecting much of the sprawling city with a reliability that puts many wealthier nations' systems to shame. It's also a near-perfect physical manifestation of Chilean social character, which prizes order, queueing, and a kind of quiet civic discipline that stands out sharply in Latin America.

The bip! card, an integrated fare system covering both Metro and the extensive bus network (Red Metropolitana), is essentially mandatory; cash doesn't work on either, and visitors who arrive expecting to pay in coins are quickly, firmly redirected to buy a card. Once you have one, the system rewards the organised: transfers between bus and Metro within a set window don't incur additional charges, encouraging genuinely efficient multi-leg commutes.

What surprises newcomers most is the social enforcement of order. Santiaguinos form real queues, stand right and walk left on escalators without exception, and treat rule-breaking, standing on the wrong side, blocking train doors, eating a sandwich mid-commute, with the kind of collective disapproval that needs no words, just a look. This isn't imposed by signage alone, it's genuinely internalised civic behaviour, passed down through sheer repetition and social pressure rather than heavy policing.

Rush hour is intense and extends later than visitors expect, often well past 7pm, as Santiago's workday runs long by regional standards. Trains during peak hours reach a fullness that requires genuine physical negotiation to board, but even then, the culture of order holds: people wait their turn to squeeze in rather than shoving indiscriminately, a small but telling detail that separates Santiago's crush hour from the more free-for-all commuting cultures found elsewhere on the continent.

## The Verdict

Kathmandu's traffic will terrify you, then convert you into a reluctant admirer of a system that has no right to work as well as it does. Santiago's Metro will simply work, efficiently, predictably, almost boringly well, which after enough time abroad starts to feel like the greater luxury. If you want an experience that makes you understand chaos as its own form of order, choose Nepal. If you actually need to get somewhere on time for a meeting that matters, choose Chile, and thank the bip! card system when you arrive with minutes to spare.

## Conclusion

Nepal and Chile represent two entirely opposite philosophies of movement: one that thrives on flexible, negotiated chaos and somehow gets everyone home in one piece, and one that runs on rigid, near-Germanic order and gets everyone home exactly on schedule. Neither is wrong. Kathmandu will teach you to trust improvisation. Santiago will teach you to trust a system. What both will absolutely teach you, without exception, is that your home country's commuting complaints were embarrassingly small by comparison, and you will never again complain about a five-minute train delay with a straight face.
