# Belgium Will Let You Open Fourteen Committees Before It Lets You Open a Bank Account

🇵🇰 Pakistan — 🇧🇪 Belgium
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Let's get something out of the way: Belgium does not have a bureaucracy problem. Belgium *is* a bureaucracy that occasionally allows a country to happen around it. I have moved fourteen times across six continents, and the only place that has ever asked me for a notarised translation of a document I did not know existed was a commune office in Brussels, staffed by a civil servant who looked personally offended that I'd arrived with only three photocopies instead of four.

Pakistan, by contrast, runs on relationships, not stamps. If you know the right branch manager, your account opens over tea and a friendly argument about cricket. If you don't, you queue. Both systems are, in their own ways, designed to make you feel something — Belgium wants you to feel small, Pakistan wants you to feel like you owe somebody a favour. Neither wants you to feel efficient. Here's what actually happens when you try to bank and bureaucratise your way between the two.

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a relationship with one specific bank branch and one specific manager — Pakistani banking still runs on faces, not ticket numbers | Assume an app will fix everything; digital banking (Raast, mobile wallets) is improving fast but most account-opening and larger transfers still want a human in the room |
| Bring a local co-signer or sponsor for your first account — it collapses weeks of documentation into an afternoon | Walk in expecting Belgian-style silence and order; a Pakistani bank branch at 11am is a negotiation, not a queue |
| Carry physical copies of everything — CNIC, NICOP, utility bill, salary letter — in triplicate, always | Rely on one bank for everything; routing international transfers through a second, remittance-friendly bank (often UBL or HBL for overseas transfers) saves real money |
| Use registered couriers or in-person visits for anything involving property or inheritance paperwork | Trust an unregistered "facilitator" who promises to skip the queue for a fee — these are exactly as legal as they sound |

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇧🇪 Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register at your commune (gemeente/commune) within eight days of arrival — nothing else, including your bank account, fully works until this exists | Expect any Belgian institution to accept a document that isn't already translated, legalised, or apostilled exactly the way their checklist demands |
| Open your bank account the moment you have a registration certificate — ING, BNP Paribas Fortis, and KBC all run expat-friendly desks, but they still want paperwork | Assume the Flemish and French-speaking administrations run the same process, at the same speed, or even in the same mood |
| Keep a dedicated folder — physical, not digital — for every stamped form, because you will be asked to produce it again, at a different office, for an unrelated reason | Lose your patience audibly; Belgian civil servants have seen angrier people than you, and they have infinite shelf space for your file |
| Budget for the fact that "it's being processed" can mean genuinely anything from three days to three months | Try to rush a Belgian bureaucrat by explaining how things work "where you're from" — this has never once worked, for anyone, in recorded history |

## Pakistan: Where the System Is People

Pakistani banking has leapt forward in the last decade — Raast, the government's instant payment system, and a genuine mobile-wallet boom (JazzCash, Easypaisa) mean a huge amount of daily money-moving now happens on a phone screen, which is more than can be said for several European countries still printing paper giros. But the architecture underneath all that convenience is still fundamentally relational. Opening a proper current account, especially one that needs to receive international wires or handle property transactions, still generally wants a human: a branch manager, a reference, a conversation that starts with tea and ends, forty minutes later, with a form.

This is not inefficiency so much as a different theory of trust. Belgian bureaucracy trusts documents. Pakistani banking trusts people who can vouch for other people. Overseas Pakistanis sending remittances home — and Pakistan remains one of the largest remittance-receiving economies in the world — quickly learn that the State Bank of Pakistan's own guidance on legal transfer channels exists precisely because the informal alternative, hawala-style transfers, is faster, cheaper, and entirely outside anyone's oversight. The formal system is playing catch-up with a culture that has been moving money through trust networks for generations.

What foreigners — and returning overseas Pakistanis — consistently underestimate is the sheer flexibility available if you work the relationship correctly. A good branch manager can expedite, waive, and improvise in ways no Belgian commune clerk would ever be authorised to do. The flip side is that nothing is standardised: your experience depends entirely on which branch, which city, which manager, which day. Karachi is not Lahore is not a small-town branch in Punjab. The system bends for you, which is wonderful, right up until it bends the wrong way.

Bureaucracy proper — passports, property registration, court documents — remains the part everyone warns you about, and the warnings are earned. Multiple attestations, in-person visits, and a tolerance for ambiguity about timelines are simply priced into the experience. The honest advice from every long-term expat forum thread is identical: budget double the time you think you need, and never, ever pay a "facilitator" you met outside a government building.

## Belgium: Where the People Are the System's Hostages

Belgium's bureaucracy is not corrupt, not arbitrary, and not even particularly slow by global standards. It is, instead, something worse: internally consistent and completely unmoved by your feelings about it. The commune registration — your *inscription* — is the master key to everything else. Without it, you cannot get a bank account, a SIM contract, health insurance, or, in a delightful bit of circular logic, in some communes, an appointment to register. New arrivals on Brussels expat forums describe the same ritual with weary precision: the correct document, in the correct order, submitted to the correct window, by appointment, months in advance, or not at all.

Belgian banking itself is actually excellent once you're inside the system — ING, BNP Paribas Fortis, and KBC all run genuinely good digital banking apps, SEPA transfers across the Eurozone are same-day and free, and the regulatory environment is about as safe as money gets. The friction is entirely at the gate. You need an address to get registered, a registration to get a bank account, and — this is the part that makes people on expat forums write essays — in many cases a bank account to pay the deposit on an address. Belgium has built a bureaucratic ouroboros and seems quietly proud of it.

The country's famous linguistic split compounds things in ways outsiders never expect: a form, a process, or an official's mood can differ meaningfully depending on whether you're dealing with the Flemish or French-speaking administration, even within the same metropolitan area. None of this is explained anywhere helpfully. You simply learn it, the way you learn which bakery closes on Mondays — through repeated, low-grade disappointment.

What redeems it, eventually, is that the system genuinely doesn't care who you are, which is its own kind of fairness. A minister's child and a delivery driver queue at the same window for the same stamp. Belgium's bureaucracy is tedious to everyone equally. There's something almost comforting in that, once you stop expecting speed and start expecting consistency.

## The Verdict

If you want a system that bends for the right person, go to Pakistan — and pray you're that person. If you want a system that bends for no one, regardless of status, go to Belgium, and bring four photocopies of everything you own. Pakistan's banking sector is modernising faster than its bureaucracy; Belgium's bureaucracy has fossilised faster than its banking is improving. Both countries will, eventually, make your money move and your paperwork exist. Neither will apologise for how long it took. If I'm picking a winner for sheer *predictability* — the thing that actually saves you time once you understand it — it's Belgium, barely, and only because at least its chaos is the same chaos every single day.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Belgium forum — a poster describing eighteen months of commune back-and-forth over a single missing legalisation stamp, paraphrased: "it's not been easy, and nobody tells you it gets harder before it gets easier."*

> *ExpatExchange Belgium thread, "What do expats find most challenging" — multiple posters converge on the same complaint: the chicken-and-egg loop between address registration, bank account, and rental deposit, with one calling it "the Belgian triangle nobody warns you about."*

> *Internations Brussels bureaucracy meetup, paraphrased consensus: bring a Belgian-fluent friend to your first commune appointment even if your French or Dutch is decent — the vocabulary of bureaucracy is its own dialect.*

## Conclusion

Here is the thing nobody wants to hear: both of these systems work. They work for millions of people, every single day, in ways that are genuinely, structurally different and equally capable of ruining your Tuesday. Pakistan will teach you that paperwork is a conversation. Belgium will teach you that paperwork is a liturgy, performed exactly the same way every time, to a god who does not care if you're running late. Neither lesson is comfortable. Both are useful, assuming you survive long enough to learn them. Bring photocopies either way. Bring patience regardless. And if anyone tells you either country's system is "just more exotic" than the other's — they've confused bureaucracy with culture, and frankly, they should file that opinion in triplicate.
