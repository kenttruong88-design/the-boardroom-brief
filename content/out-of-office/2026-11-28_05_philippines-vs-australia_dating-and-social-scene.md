# The Philippines Courts for a Year, Australia Courts for a Weekend and Calls It Efficiency

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Dating is the one subject on which every culture believes it has the sensible approach and everyone else is mad. The Philippines treats romance as a long, public, family-supervised campaign, complete with friends, relatives and the occasional serenade. Australia treats it as an app, a pub and a casual shrug, delivered with the cheerfulness of a person who has never been rejected in public.

I have dated, been dated and been thoroughly matchmade across enough borders to know that the same sentence can mean four different things depending on the postcode. "I like you" in Manila is a commitment. "I like you" in Melbourne is a mild preference with an exit clause.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇭 Philippines ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Be polite and attentive to the family from the first meeting | Treat the parents as an obstacle to be avoided |
| Take your time, since courtship is expected to be gradual | Rush to define the relationship in the first month |
| Offer to pay and to be generous on dates | Split the bill to the cent and announce it |
| Be respectful online, since social media is part of the process | Post about a new partner before they are ready |

| 🇦🇺 Australia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Be direct about what you want, with humour | Hint for weeks and hope they decode it |
| Meet for a coffee or a drink in a public place first | Plan an elaborate first date that feels like a proposal |
| Accept that splitting the bill is normal | Take offence when someone offers to pay half |
| Keep the banter light and self deprecating | Boast, since modesty is the national style |

## 🇵🇭 Philippines

Courtship in the Philippines is a slow burn with a very visible audience. Friends and family are involved early, a new partner is often introduced within weeks, and a relationship is understood to join two households as much as two people. It is warm, sociable and occasionally overwhelming, and for a newcomer it can feel like being adopted by a committee.

Traditional gestures survive, and they are charming. A suitor may be expected to visit the home, to bring something small, to be polite to the elders and to show patience. Serenades are less common in the cities than the stories suggest, but the spirit remains: effort counts, and effort shown in public counts double. Respect and modesty are the currency, and a flashy approach tends to fall flat.

The modern layer is exactly what you would expect. Dating apps are widely used in the cities, messaging is constant, and long conversations over text can stretch for weeks before anyone meets in person. Social media is a major part of the process, and a relationship that has not been quietly noticed by friends is probably not yet a relationship.

Expats learn two things quickly. First, directness can be mistaken for bluntness, and a firm refusal may be softened so much that you do not realise it was a refusal. Second, a relationship with a Filipino partner usually involves their family, their obligations and sometimes their finances, and it is wise to understand that before you are part of it.

This is not a criticism. In a culture that values closeness, a dinner table full of cousins is how affection is measured, and nobody falls in love in a vacuum.

Expats should also note the role of faith and community. In the Philippines, many relationships are shaped by church and family networks, and a partner's religious background is often part of the early conversation. Australia is more secular, and people may be quite relaxed about faith, which can make a visitor from a devout household feel both free and slightly unmoored.

## 🇦🇺 Australia

Australian dating is brisk, friendly and slightly allergic to ceremony. People meet through friends, at the pub, at a barbecue, on the beach, through a sports club and, increasingly, on apps. The first date is a casual drink or coffee, and nobody expects a great performance. A sincere conversation and a joke at your own expense are worth more than a perfectly chosen restaurant.

The unspoken rule is that you are not supposed to try too hard. Enthusiasm is acceptable, but it must be delivered with irony, and anything that sounds like a grand gesture is likely to be met with a raised eyebrow. Australians are warm, open and quick to befriend a stranger, and they are also masters of the polite brush-off, which is delivered so gently that you may take it for encouragement.

Money is handled practically. Splitting the bill is common and rarely a sign of disinterest, and in many circles it is the expected form of respect. Expats from cultures where the man pays, or the host pays, sometimes read this as stinginess. It is not. It is egalitarianism, which in Australia is practically a religion with a barbecue.

The social scene is big on outdoor activity, weekend plans and friend groups. A relationship is typically introduced to friends before family, and meeting the parents is a later chapter. For newcomers, joining a club, a sports team or a volunteer group is the surest route to a social life, and many a romance has begun at a Saturday morning run.

The downside is that the same casual style can make early relationships feel unclear. Without a long courtship, nobody quite knows when two people become a couple, and "seeing each other" can mean anything from one coffee to an engagement.

Online dating in Australia carries its own dialect. Profiles are laconic, openers are jokes, and a first message that reads like a love letter is likely to be mistaken for a scam. The national style is to be warm but not intense, and to prove sincerity through plain action rather than poetry. If someone suggests a walk, a coffee or a market on Sunday, it is a real invitation, not a rehearsal.

## The Verdict

The Philippines wins on warmth and Australia wins on clarity, and the best relationship would borrow from both. If you want to be adored, welcomed and gradually folded into a great big family, Manila will do it better than anywhere. If you want to be left alone to work out your own feelings without a chaperone, Sydney is the place.

I have a soft spot for the Filipino approach, partly because it takes love seriously and partly because it takes food even more seriously. Australia's directness is refreshing, but a country where romance is described as "keen for a drink" has set the bar at ground level.

The sting is that both cultures claim to value honesty and neither wants to deliver it. In the Philippines, a rejection is softened until it disappears. In Australia, it is joked about until it is forgotten. In either case, you may spend a month wondering whether you have been turned down.

The practical lesson is to match the pace. Slow down in Manila and speed up in Sydney, and do not confuse the local custom with the other person's feelings. A person who does not text back is not always uninterested, and a person who texts back constantly is not always in love.

Cross-cultural couples between the two countries are common, and many of them say the sensible approach is to talk early about expectations: family, money, children, holidays and where to live. It is not romantic, but it is the conversation that prevents a decade of quiet resentment, and the best couples have it twice.

## Conclusion

Dating abroad is a translation exercise, and nobody warns you that the dictionary is wrong. A look, a message, a dinner invitation or a silence can mean entirely different things in Manila and in Brisbane, and the mistakes are usually made in good faith.

My advice is to be curious and kind, to ask rather than assume, and to pay attention to what is done as much as what is said. In the Philippines, that means noticing how people treat the family. In Australia, it means noticing how they treat the waiter.

Whichever way you go, remember that love does not need a passport. It does, however, need a decent sense of timing.
