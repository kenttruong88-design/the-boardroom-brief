# Everyone Here Speaks Three Languages and Somehow You Still Can't Order Lunch

🇱🇧 Lebanon vs 🇲🇾 Malaysia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There's a particular flavour of smugness that arrives the moment you realise a country is more multilingual than you'll ever be, and both Lebanon and Malaysia deliver it within about ninety seconds of landing. Lebanon will greet you in Arabic, pivot to French mid-sentence for emotional emphasis, then finish in English because someone's phone rang. Malaysia will do something similar across four language families simultaneously, in the same market stall, without anyone present finding this remarkable in the slightest.

I have been humbled in both countries, repeatedly, by children doing casual trilingual code-switching while I fumbled for the word for "receipt."

## Do's & Don'ts: Lebanon

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to English in Beirut business districts — it's the modern common tongue | Assume French will land the same way with everyone; it carries class and generational weight |
| Learn basic Lebanese Arabic greetings — the effort is noticed and rewarded warmly | Confuse Lebanese Arabic with Modern Standard Arabic taught in language apps |
| Let conversations code-switch naturally; interrupting to "pick a language" is odd | Rely purely on English outside Beirut and coastal cities — rural areas vary |
| Use French for older generations if you have it — it often opens doors English won't | Assume trilingualism means uniform fluency — comfort levels vary hugely by person |

## Do's & Don'ts: Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn a handful of Bahasa Malaysia phrases — it's the genuine bridge language | Assume English alone covers rural Malaysia the way it covers Kuala Lumpur |
| Expect English to work extensively in cities, business, and tourism | Assume all Malaysian Chinese or Tamil speakers are equally comfortable in Malay |
| Ask which language someone prefers rather than guessing from appearance | Treat Manglish (localised English) as "incorrect" — it's a legitimate dialect |
| Appreciate that switching languages mid-sentence is normal, not confusion | Get frustrated when a shopkeeper switches to a dialect with another customer mid-transaction |

## Lebanon: A Propeller With Many Blades, and You're Not Holding Any of Them

The Lebanese have a phrase for their own linguistic chaos — a propeller with many blades — and it's the most accurate self-description I've encountered from any nation, ever. Since a 1997 education policy formalised trilingual schooling, Arabic, French, and English have coexisted not as separate systems you switch between politely, but as a single fused vernacular where sentences routinely start in one and finish in another, sometimes for grammatical convenience, sometimes for pure emotional punctuation.

Here's what the guidebooks undersell: this isn't uniform. English has surged hard among younger, business-oriented Lebanese, effectively becoming the language of commerce and international communication. French retains real currency, but its use now skews toward older generations and carries genuine social signalling — historically tied to Christian communities and a certain aspirational class marker that persists whether anyone admits it aloud or not. Arabic, specifically Lebanese Arabic rather than the Modern Standard Arabic you'll find in any textbook or app, remains the emotional home language regardless of what else is layered on top.

For the visitor, this produces a strange inversion of the usual travel language anxiety. You will rarely be stuck — someone within earshot speaks serviceable English almost everywhere in Beirut and the coastal cities. What you will be is perpetually behind, watching conversations flow through three languages faster than you can track which one to respond in. The real trap, one every expat forum flags eventually, is that this ease becomes a crutch: English and French get you everywhere pleasant enough that you never actually learn Arabic, and five years in, you're still ordering coffee in the same three memorised phrases while your neighbours have long since concluded you simply prefer it that way.

## Malaysia: Four Language Families, One Country, Zero Fuss

Where Lebanon fuses three languages into a single conversational blend, Malaysia keeps its languages more distinct and simply switches between them at speed, depending entirely on who's in the room. Bahasa Malaysia is the national language and genuine bridge — spoken to some degree across all major ethnic communities — but Malay, Chinese, and Tamil populations each maintain robust use of their own languages and dialects, and English sits over the top of all of it as the language of business, higher education, and tourism, a colonial inheritance repurposed into practical utility.

What surprises newcomers is how functionally segregated the fluency can be. Plenty of Malaysian Chinese speakers move comfortably between Mandarin, a regional Chinese dialect, English, and passable Malay. Others, particularly older generations in tighter ethnic-Chinese communities, genuinely never developed strong Malay or English at all, having lived entire working lives within a single dialect community. This isn't a knowledge gap to be embarrassed about — it's simply how a genuinely plural society organises itself, in parallel linguistic lanes rather than one shared highway.

For the traveller, English is a remarkably reliable default in Kuala Lumpur, Penang, and most tourist infrastructure — arguably more consistently useful than in many European capitals that consider themselves more cosmopolitan. Step outside the cities, though, and that reliability thins fast. And even within English-fluent circles, you'll encounter Manglish — a distinctly Malaysian localisation of English, dropped grammar, borrowed particles, and all — which is not broken English to be corrected but a legitimate, proudly used dialect in its own right. Correcting it, as more than one mortified tourist has discovered on Reddit, does not go over well.

## The Verdict

Lebanon hands you a single blended conversation you're perpetually one beat behind, and rewards genuine Arabic effort disproportionately because so few visitors bother. Malaysia hands you a country of parallel language communities you're expected to read the room to navigate, with English as a reliable but incomplete safety net. If I had to choose which barrier is more forgiving for the average out-of-office wanderer, it's Malaysia — the English coverage in urban and tourist zones is simply more extensive. But Lebanon's is the more romantic barrier to fail at; there's real dignity in butchering French, Arabic, and English in the same sentence and having someone laugh with you, not at you, before switching effortlessly to whichever one you're least destroying.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *HiNative — paraphrased: a respondent debunked the "everyone in Lebanon is fluently trilingual" myth, explaining that comfort levels vary enormously by generation and community, and assuming uniform fluency leads to awkward over-expectation from visitors.*

> *Expat.com Malaysia forum — paraphrased: a newcomer was advised that learning Bahasa Malaysia, even basics, opens far more doors than relying on English alone, since it signals respect across all ethnic communities rather than favouring one.*

> *Expat Forum For People Moving Overseas — paraphrased: a longtime resident noted that English "gets you by more than half the time" in Malaysia's cities, but warned newcomers not to assume that coverage extends evenly to older generations or rural areas.*

## Conclusion

Both countries will disabuse you, fast, of any notion that multilingualism is a niche skill reserved for European diplomats and overachieving toddlers on YouTube. In Lebanon and Malaysia, juggling three or four languages within a single day is simply Tuesday. The difference is texture: Lebanon blends its languages into one continuous, gorgeous mess you're invited to join badly; Malaysia keeps its languages distinct and expects you to notice which lane you're in. Either way, the lesson is the same one every over-confident monolingual tourist eventually learns the hard way — the barrier was never the local's problem to begin with. It was always yours, and pretending otherwise just delays the inevitable, slightly humiliating, thoroughly deserved reckoning with a phrasebook.
