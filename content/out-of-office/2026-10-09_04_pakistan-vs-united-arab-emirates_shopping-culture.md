# One Country Haggles for Sport. The Other Built Entire Cities Around Not Having To.

🇵🇰 Pakistan 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Fourteen moves and sixty countries in, I've developed a theory: you can learn more about a place's relationship with trust, class, and climate from its shopping culture than from almost anything else short of its traffic. Pakistan and the UAE sit an hour's flight apart and might as well be shopping on different planets.

Pakistan's retail life runs through bazaars where the first price quoted is a conversation starter, not an answer, and where your tailor knows your measurements better than your doctor does. The UAE's retail life runs through malls so vast they have indoor ski slopes and aquariums, built, quite deliberately, as a climate-controlled answer to a desert that makes outdoor commerce genuinely punishing for half the year. One rewards patience and social fluency. The other rewards a decent pair of walking shoes and a high tolerance for air conditioning.

## Do's & Don'ts: Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Haggle, confidently and without guilt — it's expected, and paying the first-quoted price marks you as an easy target | Shop alone for your first few bazaar trips; go with a local friend until you've learned fair local pricing |
| Build a relationship with one tailor rather than shopping around — loyalty gets you better fabric and faster turnaround | Assume malls have replaced bazaars; Dolmen and Packages exist, but daily life still runs through Anarkali and Empress Market |
| Visit markets early morning or after evening prayers when vendors are freshest and crowds thinner | Expect fixed, displayed prices to be the real price; the displayed number is a starting bid, not a rule |

## Do's & Don'ts: United Arab Emirates

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat the mall as a legitimate day out, not just a shopping trip — ski slopes, aquariums, and cinemas are part of the retail model here | Try haggling at a mall chain store; save it specifically for the Gold Souk and traditional markets, where it's still expected |
| Time big purchases around the Dubai Shopping Festival or major sale periods — discounts are real and substantial | Underestimate how much social life genuinely happens inside malls; they're the de facto public square in 40°C heat |
| Check the Gold Souk's live gold rate before buying jewellery — pricing is transparent and tied to the daily market rate | Assume every resident shops at luxury malls; plenty of UAE life, especially among South Asian communities, runs through Deira's bazaar-style streets |

## Pakistan: Commerce as Conversation

Shopping in Pakistan is not a transaction so much as a relationship conducted at speed. Walk into Lahore's Anarkali Bazaar or Karachi's Empress Market and the first price you hear is less a number than an opening move in a conversation both parties understand perfectly and will enjoy having. Haggling isn't rude here; refusing to haggle is the socially odd choice, the thing that marks you instantly as a tourist who doesn't know the rules of the room. Pakistan Bureau of Statistics estimates place a huge share of the country's retail economy in this informal, bazaar-based sector, and it's not hard to see why once you've spent an afternoon inside one: the fabric sellers, the spice stalls stacked in pyramids of colour, the tailors who will take your measurements, disappear for three days, and return with something that fits better than anything you've bought off a rack in your life.

That tailor relationship deserves special mention, because it's one of the genuinely underrated parts of Pakistani retail culture: bespoke clothing here isn't a luxury reserved for the wealthy, it's the default way most people dress, and a good tailor, once you've found one, becomes a semi-permanent fixture in your life the way a hairdresser might be elsewhere. Yes, Western-style malls exist now — Packages Mall, Dolmen Mall — and the middle class increasingly splits time between both worlds. But the bazaar remains the beating heart of daily commerce, and the social fluency it demands — knowing when to walk away, when a price is actually fair, when the vendor is testing you versus genuinely offering a deal — is a skill that takes months to build and, honestly, is one of the more satisfying things to get good at as an expat.

## United Arab Emirates: Commerce as Climate Control

The UAE took one look at a desert that regularly hits 45°C and decided the rational response was to build some of the largest indoor retail spaces on Earth — and then fill them with so much else that "going to the mall" stopped meaning "going shopping" and started meaning "going outside," in the loosest possible sense of the phrase. The Dubai Mall has an aquarium and an ice rink. Mall of the Emirates has an actual indoor ski slope. This isn't excess for its own sake, or not only that — it's a genuinely sensible civic response to a climate that makes outdoor public life a non-starter for roughly six months of the year, and the mall has become, as a direct result, the UAE's real public square: where teenagers hang out, where families spend entire Fridays, where first dates happen under artificial snow.

That said, the haggling culture hasn't vanished entirely — it's just been relocated and specialised. The Gold Souk in Deira still runs on negotiation, with jewellery priced against a transparent, publicly tracked daily gold rate that savvy buyers check before walking in. And the broader South Asian retail culture that built so much of Dubai's early economy is still visibly alive in Deira's bazaar-style streets, where Pakistani, Indian, and other South Asian shopkeepers have built retail enclaves — the Gulf News has documented entire neighbourhoods that function as a genuine slice of home for the UAE's enormous Pakistani diaspora. The Dubai Shopping Festival, meanwhile, turns the whole retail calendar into an event, with discounts and promotions substantial enough that locals genuinely time major purchases around it, the way other countries time purchases around tax season.

## The Verdict

I'm giving this one to Pakistan, and I'll die on this hill. The UAE's malls are objectively impressive — I will never stop being faintly delighted by an indoor ski slope — but impressive isn't the same as meaningful, and there's something a little hollow about a retail culture built entirely around climate avoidance rather than human connection. Pakistan's bazaars demand something of you: patience, social intelligence, a willingness to be wrong about a price and learn from it. That demand is exactly what makes the reward worth having — a tailor who knows you, a spice vendor who remembers your order, a haggling win you'll be mentioning at dinner parties for weeks. The UAE sells you convenience. Pakistan sells you a relationship. I know which one I'd rather be buying.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Pakistan — a newcomer confessed to paying triple the fair price on their first bazaar visit, and the top reply was simply: "everyone does this once, you'll learn, bring a local next time."*

> *Internations Dubai — a recent arrival described realising, three months in, that nearly their entire social calendar had quietly relocated inside air-conditioned malls, calling it "accidentally becoming a mall person."*

> *expat.com Lahore forum — a long-term resident recommended visiting the same tailor at least three times before judging the quality, since "the first order is them testing you as much as you're testing them."*

## Conclusion

Neither of these shopping cultures is wrong, exactly — they're both rational responses to real constraints, one social, one climatic. But only one of them will teach you how to read a stranger's face for the moment a price actually becomes fair, and only one of them will hand you a lift pass to an indoor ski slope on your lunch break. Choose based on what kind of story you want to be telling in ten years. I know which one makes a better dinner party anecdote, and it isn't the aquarium.
