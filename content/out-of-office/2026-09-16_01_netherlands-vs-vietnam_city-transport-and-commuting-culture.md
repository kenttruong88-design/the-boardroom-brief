# The Bicycle Is a Religion in One Country and a Near-Death Experience to Watch in the Other

🇳🇱 Netherlands vs Vietnam 🇻🇳
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have been flattened by a Dutch cyclist doing the school run one-handed, phone in the other hand, toddler on the back, groceries on the front, and not so much as a wobble. I have also stood on a street corner in Hanoi for eleven minutes, working up the nerve to cross, before a local eleven-year-old took pity on me and towed me across by the sleeve. Two countries, two entirely different relationships with the wheel, and both entirely convinced they've got it right.

The Netherlands treats the bicycle the way other nations treat the rule of law — sacred, non-negotiable, and enforced with quiet, terrifying confidence. Vietnam treats the motorbike as a state of being, an extension of the nervous system, and possibly a philosophical statement about the impossibility of ever fully stopping. Neither will apologise to you for the culture shock. You're the one who has to adapt.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇳🇱 Netherlands**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to signal turns with your arm like you mean it | Walk in the bike lane — it is not a wide pavement, it is a weapons range |
| Lock your bike with two separate locks, always | Buy an expensive bike as your first bike — it will be stolen within a month |
| Ring your bell early and often | Assume eye contact means the cyclist is slowing down. It does not |

**🇻🇳 Vietnam**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Cross the road at a slow, steady, predictable pace | Stop suddenly mid-crossing — you become an obstacle they'll simply flow around, until you move |
| Get a Grab (motorbike taxi) app the day you land | Try to "wait for a gap" in Hanoi traffic — there isn't one, ever |
| Wear a proper helmet, not the decorative plastic kind | Assume traffic lights are a binding legal contract |

## The Netherlands: Infrastructure as Ideology

The Dutch didn't stumble into cycling supremacy. They built it, deliberately, after a spate of 1970s child traffic deaths sparked a national movement literally called Stop de Kindermoord — Stop the Child Murder — that rewired Dutch urban planning for good. Now there are more bicycles than people, separated cycle paths wider than some countries' motorways, and a level of infrastructure confidence that borders on smugness. Amsterdam's bike parking garage under Centraal Station holds over eleven thousand bicycles. Eleven thousand. That's not a transport solution, that's an entire subculture with its own real estate.

What nobody tells the newly arrived expat is that Dutch cycling isn't recreational, it's structural — you will cycle in sleet, in gale-force headwinds that make you question your commitment to the bit, in the dark at four in the afternoon in December, because that is simply how one gets to work. There is no "fair weather cyclist" option. You buy the rain trousers or you buy a car and accept social exile. Bike theft is so endemic it has its own black humour — buy a bike so ugly and ancient looking that no one wants it, is the standing advice, delivered with total sincerity by people who've had four bikes stolen in three years.

The unspoken hierarchy of the cycle path is its own minor tyranny. Cargo bikes hauling three children and a week's shopping have absolute right of way, delivered with the moral authority of a mother who has not slept since 2019. Tourists wobbling on rental bikes are treated the way Londoners treat someone standing on the left of the escalator — with a specific, silent, generational contempt. You will be overtaken by an eighty-year-old woman with a cigarette in her mouth and a dog in her front basket, and you will simply have to live with that.

## Vietnam: Organised Chaos, Emphasis on Organised

Hanoi traffic looks, to the untrained eye, like entropy given an engine. It is not. It is one of the most sophisticated unwritten negotiation systems on earth, running on a shared assumption that everyone is watching everyone else and adjusting in real time, rather than obeying fixed rules. There are nearly six million motorbikes registered in Hanoi alone, a city that predates the automobile by roughly a thousand years and has no intention of retrofitting itself for one now.

The trick — and it took me three humiliating weeks to learn this — is that Vietnamese traffic rewards predictability, not caution. Hesitation is what gets you hit, because everyone else has already calculated your trajectory and built their own path around it; freeze mid-crossing and you've broken the algorithm. Walk at a steady pace, don't make sudden moves, and the traffic parts around you like a school of fish, mildly annoyed but entirely unsurprised.

Owning a motorbike here isn't a lifestyle choice, it's basic infrastructure — the transport your groceries, your furniture, your extended family, and on one memorable occasion I witnessed, a full-sized wardrobe come to you via someone's Honda Wave. Families of four ride on a single bike with the kind of casual confidence that would have a Dutch safety inspector fainting into their bicycle helmet. Public buses exist, technically, and are cheap, but they run on a schedule best described as "eventually," and most expats give up on them within a fortnight in favour of the far more reliable Grab app.

## The Verdict

If you want infrastructure that respects you, choose the Netherlands — it will make you a better, fitter, more punctual person, and it will steal your bike to teach you humility along the way. If you want infrastructure that reads you like a poem and reacts to your soul in real time, Vietnam wins, provided you can suppress every safety instinct you were raised with. The Dutch system is a machine you slot yourself into. The Vietnamese system is a conversation you have to learn to join.

Neither country will ever, under any circumstances, consider building a proper pavement for pedestrians. You are, in both places, an afterthought on wheels-adjacent infrastructure that was never designed with your comfort in mind. Pick your chaos.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/expats — a newcomer to Amsterdam describes cycling through their first proper Dutch storm and concluding that the phrase "there's no such thing as bad weather, only bad clothing" was invented specifically to torment foreigners into buying a €200 rain suit.*

> *Internations Hanoi — a longtime expat recalls the exact week they stopped flinching at near-misses and started, almost against their will, enjoying the traffic as a kind of city-wide dance they'd finally learned the steps to.*

> *expat.com Netherlands forum — a poster catalogues their fourth stolen bike in two years with the weary tone of someone reporting a recurring subscription charge they've simply accepted as a cost of living.*

## Conclusion

Both countries will convert you, eventually, whether you like it or not. The Dutch will turn you into someone who judges other cities for their inadequate cycling infrastructure at dinner parties, insufferably, for the rest of your life. Vietnam will turn you into someone who can cross an eight-lane roundabout with a coffee in hand and a baby on your hip without breaking stride, and who then can't understand why everyone back home makes such a fuss about a pedestrian crossing. You will not be able to go back to simply "getting the bus" ever again. Consider yourself warned, and pack accordingly — rain trousers for one, nerves of absolute steel for the other.
