# One Country Will Bankrupt You Slowly, The Other Will Do It In One Afternoon

🇲🇽 Mexico vs 🇺🇸 United States

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Let's deal with the premise everyone tiptoes around: Americans fly to Mexico for root canals and MRIs the way other people pop out for milk, and nobody in Mexico is flying to America for the reverse. That alone should tell you everything about which direction the medical tourism actually flows, and yet half of corporate relocation literature still writes about Mexican healthcare with the faint, patronising concern usually reserved for a country that has just discovered indoor plumbing. It has had indoor plumbing. It also has board-certified bilingual physicians who still make house calls, which is more than I can say for anywhere I've lived in the last decade.

The United States, meanwhile, runs the most technologically dazzling healthcare system on the planet, provided you can afford the cover charge. It is genuinely extraordinary medicine wrapped in a billing structure designed by someone who hated you personally. Mexico's system is humbler, patchier at the margins, and roughly a quarter of the price for comparable private care. One country will hand you a bill that requires a second mortgage. The other will hand you a bill you can pay in cash, on the spot, and still have money left for dinner. Guess which one expats keep choosing on purpose.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Mexico**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use private hospitals for anything beyond routine care — equipment and English-speaking staff are consistently rated higher than public facilities | Assume IMSS or Seguro Popular/IMSS-Bienestar covers every medication — specialty drugs often have to be bought privately |
| Budget for genuinely low out-of-pocket costs — most American expats report spending under $100 a month on healthcare including medications | Skip supplemental private insurance entirely — public coverage has real limits, especially for specialists and imaging |
| Ask about English-speaking doctors first — many private-practice physicians trained partly in the US and speak fluently | Expect US-style instant specialist access everywhere — rural and smaller-city facilities vary significantly in quality and wait times |
| Get a second opinion locally before assuming "cheap" means "lesser" — patient satisfaction ratings for Mexican physicians run very high among US expats | Pay for a routine procedure without asking the price first — unlike the US, prices are often negotiable and transparent if you ask |

**United States**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comprehensive insurance before you land — a single ER visit without coverage can run well into five figures | Walk into an ER for anything non-urgent — it's the most expensive way to be seen, and the article record on this is brutal |
| Review your employer's health plan line by line before accepting a job — it materially affects your finances | Assume your home country's insurance covers you — most plans need supplemental US-specific coverage |
| Ask for an itemised bill and negotiate — hospitals frequently reduce charges when pushed | Expect price transparency — the same MRI can cost wildly different amounts at two hospitals ten minutes apart, and nobody will tell you in advance |

## Mexico: Cheap Isn't The Right Word. Honest Is.

Here's a number that should embarrass several wealthier nations: medical care in Mexico runs roughly 75% less than in the United States for comparable private treatment, according to industry cost comparisons. A routine checkup costs $12–15 against $265 north of the border. An MRI runs $600–800 instead of $1,325. Dental work is half the price — a root canal at $250 versus $1,500, an implant at $750 versus $1,800 — and prescription drugs are 30–60% cheaper; a vial of insulin that costs $300–500 in the US runs $30–50 here. These aren't black-market workarounds. This is the standard private-sector rate, openly posted.

The system itself is a patchwork, and I won't pretend otherwise. IMSS (Instituto Mexicano del Seguro Social) is the backbone of formal-sector public healthcare, alongside IMSS-Bienestar, which replaced the old Seguro Popular for those outside formal employment. Coverage gaps are real — one expat forum poster on Expat.com, going by "gudgrief," noted that Seguro Popular lacked several medications he needed, forcing him to buy them privately, though he was blunt about the trade-off: a six-day private hospital stay for pneumonia in Xalapa cost him roughly $4,500, versus an estimated $10,000-plus back home, and "I am very satisfied with the level of care." Another poster on the same thread, Mel01, put it more pointedly: her doctor "doesn't rush you thru like cattle," in direct contrast to a US Medicare copay experience she found both pricier and colder.

The satisfaction numbers back up the anecdotes rather than contradicting them. Survey data cited by Expatsi.com found 84% of American expats rate Mexican healthcare very good or excellent — against 72% of Americans rating US care that highly in comparable self-reported surveys — and more than three-quarters rated their Mexican physicians very good or excellent, many of whom still make house calls, a phrase that will sound like science fiction to anyone who has tried to get a same-day US appointment. Nearly 40% of American expats in Mexico spend under $50 a month on healthcare including medications; almost two-thirds spend under $100. That's not "cheap because it's bad." That's cheap because the markup that defines American healthcare pricing simply doesn't exist here.

Private health insurance for expats, when people bother with it, is its own pleasant surprise: a comprehensive international plan through a Mexican insurer frequently runs a fraction of a comparable US premium, and many retirees simply self-insure, banking the difference and paying out of pocket when something comes up, confident the bill won't be the kind that ends a retirement. Border-town medical tourism has also professionalised itself well beyond the old stereotype of a dodgy dental clinic — cities like Tijuana and Los Algodones now run entire pedestrian districts built around US patients walking across for dental implants, prescription refills and elective surgery, then walking back the same afternoon, procedure done and wallet intact.

## United States: Extraordinary Medicine, Catastrophic Billing

I want to be fair to American healthcare, because the actual clinical medicine is often superb — world-class specialists, cutting-edge treatment, extraordinary surgical outcomes. The problem has never been the doctors. It's the invoice. A journalist cited in Expat.com's own reporting on the system was billed $11,000 for four hours in a hospital following a medical emergency — four hours, not four days. A general practitioner or paediatrician visit runs a baseline of $200 before anything is actually done. Maternity bills range from $15,000 to $50,000 depending on the state, which means giving birth in America can cost more than a car. A dermatologist consultation averages $400. A simple blood test can run anywhere from $100 to $3,000 for reasons no patient has ever successfully had explained to them.

The structural issue is that the US has no universal insurance system — coverage comes through an employer, purchased privately, or not at all — and per KFF's 2025 Employer Health Benefits Survey, the average annual family premium for employer-sponsored coverage has climbed to nearly $27,000, a 6% jump in a single year, with workers themselves covering a meaningful share of that before a single doctor's visit happens. Expats are explicitly warned to check whether their home-country coverage survives the move at all, because in most cases it doesn't, and "additional coverage is often highly recommended" is corporate-speak for "you will be financially exposed without it." Emergency rooms, meanwhile, get used for non-urgent issues simply because there's nowhere else fast enough to go, which only inflates the bills further for everyone behind them in the queue. Some free clinics exist — New York alone runs about ten, often staffed by medical students — but they're a patch on a wound, not a system.

Even insured Americans aren't fully protected from the arithmetic, since deductibles — the amount you pay out of pocket before coverage kicks in at all — routinely run into the thousands of dollars before a plan contributes a cent. Surprise out-of-network bills remain a live risk even at in-network hospitals, because an anaesthesiologist or radiologist on a given shift may simply not be covered by your plan, a fact nobody discloses to you while you're unconscious on the table. Medical debt is, by a wide margin, the single most common reason Americans file for personal bankruptcy — not overspending, not bad luck with the stock market, just the ordinary act of having gotten sick.

## The Verdict

Mexico wins, and it isn't close. American medicine is clinically excellent and financially predatory; Mexican medicine is clinically solid, increasingly specialised in the private sector, and priced like a country that still believes healthcare should be something a person can actually afford without selling a kidney to pay for the kidney. The 84% satisfaction rate among American expats who've tried both systems isn't nostalgia or exoticism — it's arithmetic. The US system punishes you for getting sick twice: once with the illness, once with the bill. Mexico just treats you and sends you home with money still in your pocket. That's not a lesser system. That's a system behaving the way a system is supposed to.

## Conclusion

If you want to see the two countries' values laid bare, skip the embassy tours and go straight to the hospital billing department. America will save your life and then invoice you like it's collecting a ransom. Mexico will treat you with the same competence, charge you what the procedure actually costs, and not make you feel like a financial liability for having a body. I've lived in both systems. I have exactly one scar from the experience, and it isn't medical — it's from reading a US emergency room bill while fully conscious.
