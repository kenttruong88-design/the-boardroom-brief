# France Will Charge You for the View and the Attitude. Vietnam Will Just Adopt You for the Length of Your Stay

🇫🇷 France 🇻🇳 Vietnam
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

French hospitality operates on a principle of earned formality: the grander the hotel, the more precisely calibrated the distance between staff and guest, an elegant choreography where warmth exists but must be unlocked through correct behaviour, correct French, and correct patience. Vietnamese hospitality, particularly at family-run hotels and homestays, skips the choreography entirely and goes straight for something closer to informal adoption, where by day two you're being fed extra portions at breakfast and asked, with real concern, whether you've been sleeping enough.

I've checked into a boutique hotel in Paris's 6th arrondissement and been made to feel, through nothing more than a slightly raised eyebrow at my slightly-too-casual greeting, that I had already committed a minor social infraction before reaching my room. I've also checked into a family-run guesthouse in Hoi An where the owner's mother had, by the second morning, decided I looked tired and prescribed a specific herbal tea, unprompted, delivered directly to my room. Only one of these experiences required me to have done anything to deserve it.

## Do's & Don'ts: France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet staff with "Bonjour" before anything else — always, without exception | Skip the greeting and launch straight into a request; it reads as rude |
| Expect precise, formal service that warms gradually over your stay | Expect casual chumminess from staff on day one, even at friendly gîtes |
| Book boutique and heritage properties for genuine character | Assume star ratings mean the same thing as in the US or UK — French ratings run stricter |

## Do's & Don'ts: Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the extra breakfast portions and unsolicited advice graciously | Be surprised by highly personal questions about your health, sleep, or love life |
| Negotiate long-stay rates directly with family-run hotel owners | Expect the same negotiation flexibility at large international chains |
| Say goodbye properly when checking out — it genuinely matters to the family | Treat the stay as purely transactional; relationship is part of the product |

## France: Formality That Warms on Its Own Schedule

French hospitality culture, particularly in the boutique and heritage hotel sector the country does so well, runs on a formality that many first-time visitors mistake for coldness when it's really closer to precision. The "Bonjour" rule isn't a quaint suggestion, it's a genuine social contract: greet staff properly before making any request, and doors open, both literally and socially, with a warmth that can be genuinely surprising given the initially reserved presentation. Skip it, launch straight into "where's the elevator," and you'll get correct, efficient, unmistakably chillier service in return.

The French star rating system, overseen with a rigour that makes some other countries' hotel classifications look almost decorative, means a French four-star genuinely delivers four-star service, amenities, and attention to detail, rather than the somewhat elastic standards found in destinations where ratings function more as marketing than measurement. This precision extends to the aesthetic experience too: French boutique hotels, particularly the countryside gîtes and heritage properties converted from old châteaux or farmhouses, treat design and atmosphere as inseparable from hospitality itself, curating everything down to the specific linen weight with an intentionality that borders on the artistic.

What surprises many guests is how genuinely warm French hospitality becomes once the initial formality has been correctly navigated. Staff who seemed reserved on check-in often become notably friendlier by the second or third day, recognising returning guests, remembering small preferences, offering local recommendations with real enthusiasm once the baseline of mutual respect has been established. The warmth was always there. It simply had to be earned through correct social sequencing rather than assumed from the outset.

Complaints, when necessary, also follow their own etiquette. French hotel staff generally respond far better to calm, precisely articulated concerns delivered in (even imperfect) French than to loud, immediate demands, and guests who understand this distinction tend to get resolutions delivered with genuine grace rather than the defensive friction that impatience tends to provoke.

## Vietnam: Hospitality as Genuine, Immediate Kinship

Vietnamese accommodation culture, especially the enormous and thriving sector of family-run hotels and homestays found from Hanoi's Old Quarter to Hoi An to the Mekong Delta, operates on an entirely different emotional model: warmth offered immediately and personally, often escalating into something that feels genuinely closer to being informally adopted for the duration of your stay. The distance between staff and guest that defines French formality simply doesn't exist in the same way here, particularly at smaller, family-operated properties where the owner's actual relatives are often working the front desk, the kitchen, and the housekeeping simultaneously.

This produces a hospitality style built on personal attentiveness rather than institutional polish. Extra food appears at breakfast, unprompted, if a host decides you look like you need it. Questions that would feel intrusively personal in a Parisian context, about your sleep, your health, your romantic situation, arrive within days, asked with genuine warmth rather than idle curiosity, and are generally best met with equally genuine, if briefer, honesty rather than polite deflection, which can read as oddly cold in return.

Negotiation forms a real and expected part of the relationship at these smaller properties, particularly for longer stays. Approaching an owner directly to discuss a weekly or monthly rate isn't considered aggressive or cheap, it's simply how business gets done, often resulting in genuinely better rates than anything available through a booking platform, alongside a relationship that continues to soften and deepen for the length of the stay. This dynamic doesn't extend to Vietnam's large international hotel chains, which operate with far more standardised, Western-influenced formality, but the family-run sector, which dominates the mid-range and budget accommodation market, remains gloriously personal.

What nobody quite prepares visitors for is how genuinely it stings to check out. Departures from a well-loved Vietnamese family homestay often involve real emotion, photos, exchanged contact details, promises to return, sometimes gifts pressed into your hands at the door, because for the family running it, you were never purely a transaction. You were, briefly, part of the household, and the goodbye reflects that.

## The Verdict

France wins on precision, on an aesthetic and service standard so exacting it becomes its own art form once you've learned to unlock it correctly. Vietnam wins on heart, on making a guest feel genuinely, immediately cared for without requiring any social password to access it.

I'd take a French boutique hotel for a honeymoon and a Vietnamese family homestay for anything that actually needed to feel like coming home. One will perfect your linen count. The other will worry about whether you've eaten enough, and honestly, by the end of most trips, that's the version of hospitality I actually needed.

## Conclusion

France treats hospitality as a precise, formal art that rewards those who learn its rules with genuine, gradually unlocked warmth. Vietnam treats it as an immediate act of kinship, extended before you've done anything to deserve it and deepened simply by staying long enough to be fed properly. Both leave a mark. Only one of them will make you cry at the front desk on your way out, and it probably won't be the one with the better thread count.
