# One Nation Grinds Because It Must, The Other Grinds Because The Mirror Is Watching

🇹🇿 Tanzania vs 🇦🇷 Argentina

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nobody in Dar es Salaam pays a monthly subscription to walk up a hill, mostly because everyone already walks up several before breakfast, carrying something heavy, for free. Fitness in Tanzania isn't a lifestyle choice bolted onto an otherwise sedentary existence — it's the byproduct of a life that refuses to sit still. Meanwhile, four thousand miles across the Atlantic, Argentina has turned the human body into an ongoing civic project, one that requires a gym membership, a trainer named Fede, a protein shaker the size of a fire extinguisher, and roughly four hours a week of Instagram discourse about it.

Both countries move. Only one of them has commodified the movement into an aesthetic. I'll let you guess which, though the mirror selfies in the leggings have probably given it away.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇹🇿 Tanzania**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a local football match on any patch of dirt after 5pm — everyone's welcome and nobody's keeping score of your form | Expect a 24-hour gym on every corner; opening hours are suggestions, not commitments |
| Try a sunrise run on the beach in Dar or a hike near Moshi — the scenery does the motivating for you | Assume "gym" means the same thing it does at home; many are one room, one fan, and a lot of character |
| Respect that manual labour counts as a full workout — porters on Kilimanjaro could deadlift you into next week | Wear skimpy gym kit outside an actual gym; modesty norms matter more than your moisture-wicking fabric |
| Ask locals about sukuma wiki and ugali as fuel — traditional diets do more heavy lifting than any supplement | Turn your nose up at a gym with no air conditioning; sweat is not optional here, it's the point |

**🇦🇷 Argentina**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a running club (there's one on every corner of Buenos Aires) if you want an actual social life | Skip leg day and then wear shorts to the gym — Argentines will notice, and they will judge |
| Try a CrossFit "box" or a football five-a-side — Argentina takes both with religious seriousness | Expect a quiet solo workout; gyms here are as social as a café, complete with unsolicited advice |
| Learn the rhythm: late-night training sessions are normal when dinner doesn't start until 10pm | Assume mate and empanadas cancel out; the gym bros know the maths and will remind you |
| Compliment someone's physique if it's clearly a point of pride — vanity isn't an insult here, it's a hobby | Be surprised by the price of a decent membership; inflation treats gym fees like everything else, brutally |

## Tanzania: Fitness as a Side Effect of Being Alive

Ask a Tanzanian about their "workout routine" and you'll get a confused look, because the question assumes exercise is scheduled rather than baked into the day. In Dar es Salaam, informal gyms — some no more than a shipping container with weights welded from scrap metal — do exist, and they're glorious in their unpretentiousness. No mirrors, no influencers filming between sets, just people doing brutal circuits because that's what's available and it works. The WHO has repeatedly flagged that structured "leisure-time" activity is lower across much of Sub-Saharan Africa than in wealthier regions, yet its own data on daily incidental activity — walking, farming, carrying, building — tells a different story. Tanzanians aren't under-exercised. They're under-counted by people who only recognise exercise when it happens in Lycra.

Dar's newer commercial gyms, aimed at NGO staff and the small expat contingent, are a different animal — glass-fronted, air-conditioned, suspiciously similar to something in Nairobi or Lisbon, and priced wildly out of step with local wages. Outside the capital, in Arusha or Moshi, fitness bends toward function: guides and porters who summit Kilimanjaro dozens of times a year possess cardiovascular capacity that would embarrass most marathon runners, achieved with zero interest in ever calling it "training." Football remains the great democratizer — pitches are flattened dirt, teams assemble themselves, and everyone from eight-year-olds to grandfathers plays with a seriousness that has nothing to do with looking good and everything to do with winning. It's fitness stripped of ego, which is more than can be said for most gyms anywhere else on earth.

## Argentina: The Gym as Confessional, Runway, and Support Group

Argentina has taken physical self-improvement and turned it into performance art with a monthly fee. Buenos Aires alone has a gym ecosystem so socially loaded that choosing one is closer to choosing a political party than a place to lift weights. IHRSA's global fitness data consistently ranks Argentina among Latin America's most gym-penetrated markets, and anyone who's spent a Tuesday evening in a Palermo CrossFit box can confirm it: the grunting is theatrical, the playlists are aggressive, and the post-workout selfie is practically contractual.

What makes Argentine gym culture distinct isn't the equipment — it's the sociability bolted onto it. Argentines don't go to disappear into headphones; they go to be seen, to gossip, to debate Boca versus River between sets, and to eat a medialuna on the way out without a flicker of irony. Running clubs have exploded across Buenos Aires, less because Argentines suddenly discovered cardio and more because a run followed by mate and conversation is a more Argentine way to socialize than sitting still. Euromonitor has tracked steady growth in the country's health-club sector even through years of brutal inflation — Argentines will absorb a currency crisis before they'll cancel a gym membership. Football remains the deeper current beneath it all: every neighborhood kid who ever dreamed of being Messi still shapes how the whole country thinks about bodies, discipline, and public judgment of both.

## The Verdict

If the metric is "who moves more," it's a tie, arguably leaning Tanzania, where daily life provides a full-body workout nobody needs to name. If the metric is "who built an entire subculture, economy, and personality trait around fitness," Argentina wins by a landslide it never stopped celebrating. Tanzania treats the body as a tool for getting through the day; Argentina treats it as a canvas for narrating the day to everyone else. Neither approach is wrong, but only one requires an Instagram account, a matching set, and a standing 7am appointment with a trainer who charges in a currency depreciating faster than your squat form. My money, sardonically and completely, is on Argentina — not because it's healthier, but because it turned exertion into theatre, and theatre is always more fun to watch, even from the sidelines, mate in hand, doing absolutely nothing.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/expats — paraphrased: warns that in Dar es Salaam, gyms marketed to foreigners cost more per month than many locals earn in weeks, and the "budget" alternative is a rusty rack in a tin shed with no AC and a judgmental goat outside.*

> *Internations Buenos Aires — paraphrased: notes gym membership prices in the city seem to reprice monthly thanks to inflation, and newcomers are advised to confirm the current rate in person rather than trust anything quoted online.*

> *expat.com Dar es Salaam — paraphrased: cautions that hotel gyms are often the only reliably equipped option for visitors, since local fitness clubs can have unpredictable hours and equipment that may or may not work on a given day.*

## Conclusion

Both countries have quietly weaponized their national character against the treadmill industrial complex, just in opposite directions. Tanzania refuses to separate fitness from life, making the concept of a "fitness culture" almost a category error — you don't need a gym when the day itself is the workout. Argentina, ever the drama enthusiast, built an entire emotional infrastructure around the gym: judgment, community, vanity, and mate, served in equal measure. Neither country needs your Peloton subscription or your opinion on intermittent fasting. They were doing this long before wellness became a marketing category, and they'll keep doing it long after the rest of us move on to whatever comes next. Pack accordingly, and leave the ego — yours, not theirs — at home.
