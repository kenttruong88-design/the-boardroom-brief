# One Country Celebrates Spring by Pinning Tiny Charms to Everyone's Coat; the Other Shuts Down Entirely Every August Out of Sheer Principle

🇷🇴 Romania 🇮🇹 Italy
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every March 1st, Romanians exchange *mărțișor* — tiny red-and-white string charms — with the sincerity of a nation that genuinely believes spring needs a formal welcoming committee. Every August, Italy essentially closes for business, in full, for *Ferragosto*, a national holiday so deeply embedded that entire cities empty out and newcomers arrive expecting a shop to be open and instead find a shuttered storefront and a handwritten sign suggesting, vaguely, a return sometime in September. I have been handed a mărțișor by a stranger on a Bucharest street who wanted nothing from me except to witness spring arriving. I have also tried, fruitlessly, to buy aspirin in a small Tuscan town on August 15th and been met with the kind of silence usually reserved for a funeral.

Both countries treat their festival calendars not as optional cultural flourishes but as actual civic infrastructure, deeply, non-negotiably observed. The difference is in scale, in rhythm, and in just how much either nation is willing to let the economy grind to a halt in service of tradition.

## Do's & Don'ts: Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept and wear a mărțișor if offered on March 1st — refusing is genuinely odd | Assume rural festival calendars match urban ones; village traditions run deeper and longer |
| Attend a regional folk festival (Sibiu, Maramureș) for genuinely unpolished tradition | Expect Bucharest's festival scene to represent the whole country's cultural rhythm |
| Try traditional foods tied to specific holidays — timing really matters to the dish | Schedule business meetings around major Orthodox Easter — it shifts and dominates the calendar |
| Learn the Orthodox calendar's major dates; they shape the year more than the Western one | Confuse Western and Orthodox Easter dates — they frequently fall weeks apart |

## Do's & Don'ts: Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan around Ferragosto (August 15th) — expect closures well before and after the date itself | Expect any non-tourist business open in smaller towns for most of August |
| Attend a local patron saint festa — every town has one, and locals take it personally | Underestimate how disruptive these local closures are to transport and services |
| Book accommodation and trains months ahead for major regional festivals | Assume "festival" means small — some draw crowds that overwhelm entire towns for days |
| Embrace the long, slow civic pride in these events — they are identity, not entertainment | Rush a festa's rhythm; the procession, the food, the fireworks each have their own unhurried pace |

## Romania: A Calendar Governed by the Orthodox Year

Romanian public life runs on a rhythm shaped heavily by the Orthodox Church calendar, which frequently diverges from the Western Gregorian holiday schedule in ways that regularly catch newcomers completely off guard. Orthodox Easter can land weeks after Western Easter, and when it does, it dominates the cultural and culinary calendar with a seriousness that makes Western Easter look like a minor seasonal footnote by comparison — fasting periods beforehand, elaborate feasting after, church services that run through the night with candlelit processions that feel genuinely ancient rather than performed for tourists.

Mărțișor, the March 1st spring-welcoming tradition, captures something distinctly Romanian: small, sincere, almost quietly anti-commercial. The red-and-white charms, exchanged between friends, family, and sometimes complete strangers, aren't sold as a product so much as passed along as a genuine good wish, and the custom has survived decades of political upheaval with its sincerity fully intact, which is more than can be said for most national traditions anywhere.

Beyond the capital, Romania's regional festival culture gets genuinely spectacular and remains refreshingly unpolished by international tourism standards. Maramureș's wooden church festivals, Sibiu's folk gatherings, and countless village-level harvest and saint-day celebrations operate on a scale and sincerity that Bucharest's more cosmopolitan calendar doesn't fully capture. These are not performances staged for visitors — they are communities continuing to do what they've always done, and a curious traveller who shows up respectfully will be welcomed, fed, and probably handed something homemade before the day is out.

## Italy: Shutting Down as an Act of National Will

Ferragosto is, structurally, the most Italian thing imaginable: a mid-August public holiday so comprehensively observed that it effectively shuts down huge swathes of the national economy for the better part of a month, not out of laziness but out of a deeply held cultural belief that rest, specifically communal, uninterrupted, family-centred rest, deserves the same civic protection as commerce does the rest of the year. Shops close. Restaurants close. Entire towns, particularly in central and southern Italy, essentially relocate to the coast, leaving behind a handwritten sign and a faint smell of espresso that nobody's around to make.

What makes this genuinely remarkable, rather than merely inconvenient, is how little anyone apologises for it. A newcomer expecting late-August service in rural Tuscany or Umbria will be met with the same polite bafflement a Roman might show someone asking why the Colosseum isn't open at 3am — the question itself reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of priorities, and Italians feel no particular need to accommodate that misunderstanding.

Beyond Ferragosto, Italy's *festa del patrono* tradition — every town, however small, celebrating its own patron saint with processions, fireworks, and food specific to that exact place — reveals something deeper about Italian identity: it is intensely, proudly local before it is national. A visitor who happens upon a small Umbrian town's saint day will find a festival with zero interest in international appeal, zero compromise for outsider convenience, and total, unselfconscious civic pride. This is not a show. This is simply what the town does every year, has done for centuries, and will keep doing long after you've gone home and forgotten the saint's actual name.

## The Verdict

Romania wins on sincerity and spiritual depth — its festival calendar feels genuinely lived rather than performed, rooted in a religious and folk tradition that survived considerable historical pressure intact. Italy wins on sheer, unapologetic scale — nowhere else shuts down this completely, this proudly, in service of the idea that rest and local identity matter more than commerce. If I want to witness a tradition that feels untouched by tourism, Romania's villages win easily. If I want to understand a nation's priorities by watching what it's willing to sacrifice for a holiday, Italy's empty August streets tell you everything.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Romania — paraphrased: a foreign resident asked why their Romanian coworkers kept giving out tiny string charms in early March and learned, somewhat sheepishly, it was considered mildly bad luck to refuse one.*

> *Reddit r/italy — paraphrased: a newcomer planning a mid-August move was told flatly by multiple locals to simply reschedule, since nothing — movers, utilities, bureaucracy — would function properly during Ferragosto week regardless of what was booked.*

> *expat.com Rome — paraphrased: a relocated professional described driving two hours to find an open pharmacy on August 15th, eventually realising the entire regional network had synchronised its closure almost to the hour.*

## Conclusion

Romania and Italy both treat their calendars as sacred, but the shape of that sacredness differs entirely: one is quiet, spiritual, and deeply personal; the other is loud, total, and unapologetically collective. Plan your travel, your paperwork, and your aspirin purchases accordingly. Romania will hand you a charm and wish you well. Italy will simply close the door, go to the beach, and expect you to understand why without further explanation.
