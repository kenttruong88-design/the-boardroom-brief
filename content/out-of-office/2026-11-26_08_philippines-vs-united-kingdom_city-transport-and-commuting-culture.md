# Manila Takes Three Hours and London Takes Your Dignity

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every capital believes it has the worst commute in the world. Most of them are wrong. Manila has a real claim to the title, and London has the nerve to compete on principle. One city sends millions of people to work on a patchwork of jeepneys, buses, tricycles and a train line with a personality disorder. The other sends millions of people to work on a Tube that is nominally efficient and emotionally devastating.

I have done both. In Manila I spent two and a half hours in a van with eleven strangers and one working fan. In London I spent forty minutes pressed into a carriage with nobody speaking, nobody looking up and everyone mentally composing a complaint to the mayor. Both were miserable. Only one had a story.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇭 Philippines ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Leave far earlier than you think you need to | Trust a journey time from a map app |
| Learn the jeepney and UV Express routes before you rely on them | Assume there is a printed timetable |
| Keep small change and a charged phone | Flash an expensive device in a crowd |
| Use Grab when you need certainty and can afford it | Expect it to be cheap at rush hour |

| 🇬🇧 United Kingdom ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stand on the right of the escalator | Block the left and wonder why you are being stared at |
| Let people off before you get on | Barge in the moment the doors open |
| Keep your headphones in and your eyes down | Strike up a conversation with a stranger |
| Check the service status before you leave | Assume the line you want is running |

## 🇵🇭 The Philippines

The Manila commute is an endurance sport. According to a 2019 Vice feature by Lia Savillo, the MRT recorded over 500 glitches in 2017, and close to 400,000 vehicles pass along EDSA daily, the great arterial road around which the city organises its misery. Commutes can take three hours. These figures are a few years old, but anyone who has lived there will tell you the shape of the problem has not changed.

The system is a mixture of rail, buses, jeepneys, vans and tricycles. Culture Trip lists the three rapid rail lines as LRT 1, LRT 2 and MRT 3, plus the PNR, and says UV Express vans carry ten or more passengers at fixed fares, while tricycles are barred from major roads. Grab is the only major ride-hailing app after Uber suspended operations, so it is the main option for anyone who wants to book a car. The flag-down taxi rate was 40 pesos at the time of the Culture Trip writing, which dates that line to a different economy.

What strikes outsiders is how much patience this takes, and how little complaining it produces. A Vice interviewee called Mia, an 18-year-old writer, says trains do not keep a strict schedule, and that a skip-train can leave you waiting. Another, Jaime, a 27-year-old architect, notes that commuters on the Ayala Avenue bus route rarely give way to women, pregnant people or people with disabilities. An Inquirer columnist in 2019 writes that the jeepney is part of daily life, but that the MRT and LRT still feel hard to use alone. The same column describes the morning when the MRT 3 stopped and 508 passengers walked along the tracks between Ayala and Magallanes.

That is the Manila commute: part transport, part survival, part community. You learn the route, the shortcuts and the people who ride with you every day. The jeepney, with its painted panels and shared coins, is the best public design in Asia and the least comfortable.

The government's own answer is the PUV modernisation programme, which the Inquirer's 2019 column says targeted June 2020 for replacing ageing jeepneys. Whatever its progress since, it tells you how seriously Manila takes the idea of reform, and how complicated reform is when the vehicle in question supports hundreds of thousands of livelihoods and is also a cultural icon. A jeepney is not just a bus. It is a small business, a family inheritance and a rolling piece of folk art, all at once.

## 🇬🇧 The United Kingdom

The British commute is a very different kind of suffering. There is a timetable. There is a map. There is even, occasionally, a train. What you do not have is space, silence or any sense that anyone is on your side. The Tube is the national theatre of avoidance: hundreds of people crammed into a metal tube, each pretending the other does not exist.

The etiquette is unwritten and merciless. You stand on the right of the escalator. You let people off before you get on. You do not talk to strangers, you do not make eye contact and you do not, under any circumstances, eat something that smells. Breaking these rules does not cause a scene. It causes a sigh, and in Britain a sigh is a serious sanction.

Delays are an institution. A signal failure at a station you have never heard of becomes a reason to cancel half a line, and the passengers' response is a polite, bitter silence. The weather adds to it. Leaves on the line are a national joke that is also a real operational hazard, and strikes arrive with the regularity of the seasons.

I have not verified current fares or timetable data for this piece, so check the operator's own site before you plan anything, but the cultural truth is settled. The British commuter does not expect it to be good. The British commuter expects it to be bad, and takes quiet pride in being able to endure it without making a fuss. It is the same endurance as Manila's, in a smaller, colder, more embarrassed form.

What both cities share is a population that has adapted. Manila commuters stagger their hours, learn the quiet routes and keep a mental map of where the road floods. London commuters learn which door of which carriage opens at the right exit and which station is quicker to walk between than to change at. Everyone in both places carries a body of local knowledge that no timetable contains, and a newcomer who wants to survive should find a colleague willing to share theirs.

## The Verdict

Manila is worse and London is more dispiriting, which is an odd thing to say. A Manila commute is a daily expedition, but you are surrounded by other people who are also on it, and there is a strange solidarity in that. A London commute is a daily rehearsal for isolation, and the isolation is the cruelty.

If I measure the two by the hours they cost, Manila loses badly. Three hours for a trip that should take one is a tax on human life, and the people who pay it deserve better infrastructure than an ageing rail line and a hopeful fleet of jeepneys. London's time cost is gentler, and its trains mostly run.

But I did not say which was more comfortable, I said which I preferred. I pick Manila, narrowly, because I would rather be stuck with people who are talking, laughing and sharing a fan than be carried efficiently to work by strangers who will not look at me. It is the wrong answer for my back and the right answer for my morale.

## Conclusion

A commute is a country's national mood in motion. Manila shows you a city that has too many people and too few options and still turns up on time. London shows you a city that has an enormous network and too few smiles and still turns up late.

Whichever you land in, carry a charged phone, small change, a book and a spare layer. Learn the unwritten rules before you break them. And accept, with whatever grace you can find, that the journey is part of the job and that everyone around you has made peace with it, which is more than you can say for the people who designed it.
