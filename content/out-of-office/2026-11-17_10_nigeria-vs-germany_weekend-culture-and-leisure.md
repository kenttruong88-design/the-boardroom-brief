# Nigeria Spends the Weekend Out Loud and Germany Spends It Under a Noise Regulation

🇳🇬 Nigeria vs 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A Lagos weekend does not begin on Saturday. It begins on Friday evening with a decision about which of three parties to attend, continues with a wedding that has been advertised in lavish colour for six months, passes through a church service of heroic length and ends with a family lunch that has no fixed finish line. Somewhere in the middle there is a nap. The nap is the only quiet moment of the weekend and it is defended like a border.

A German weekend begins at the supermarket on Saturday, where you buy everything you need for Sunday because on Sunday everything is shut. Then it goes quiet. The city exhales, the washing machine stops, the lawnmower is put away and a nation of orderly adults goes for a walk in the forest with the quiet intensity of a people who have been told that rest is mandatory.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Nigeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the invitation and arrive when the party actually starts | Turn up on the printed time and sit alone with the caterers |
| Dress up for weddings and celebrations | Wear a casual shirt to an owambe |
| Greet elders properly and stay to eat | Leave without saying goodbye to the host |
| Plan for traffic on every journey | Schedule two big events across the city on one afternoon |

**Germany**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Do your shopping before Sunday | Plan a Sunday grocery run and discover closed doors |
| Respect quiet hours in your building | Run a loud vacuum or drill on a Sunday afternoon |
| Walk, cycle or visit a park on Sundays | Treat the day as a work catch-up |
| Book restaurants and activities ahead | Turn up expecting a table on a popular weekend |

## Nigeria

Nigerians have a gift for weekends. The country treats Saturday as a social duty and Sunday as a spiritual and familial one, and the combination produces a rhythm that outsiders find thrilling and slightly exhausting. Zikoko, the Nigerian youth media brand, has published pieces on what makes a typical Nigerian weekend and on how to weekend properly in Lagos, and the common theme is that leisure is communal, loud and rarely planned in a Western sense.

The parties are the headline. Weddings, naming ceremonies, birthdays and funerals can all turn into grand social events, and the famous owambe, a lavish celebration where guests wear matching fabric and the music never drops below a conversational roar, is a national institution. Culture Custodian has written about how young Lagosians often prefer to party at night, which shapes the city's entertainment: lounges, clubs and events that come alive after dark and run until the small hours.

Church or mosque is the other major anchor. Sunday services can be long, lively and full of music, and for many families the service is followed by a meal and a visit. The Guardian Nigeria's article on what Nigerians do for fun shows how broad the list is: music, football, food, family gatherings, film, travel, and the endless art of talking. Nollywood films, Afrobeats and a thriving food scene give the city a sense of continuous entertainment.

The challenges are practical. Traffic can ruin a plan, power cuts can interrupt a gathering, and security considerations shape where and when you go out. Cost is another factor: a lavish event can strain a budget, and the pressure to attend and to contribute is real. But the pay-off is a social life of remarkable richness. You will never be bored, never be alone and never be allowed to leave a party without a plate of something wrapped for the road.

## Germany

German leisure is the opposite temperament, and it is no less serious. The Sunday rest tradition, known as Sonntagsruhe, shapes the whole country. Most shops are closed on Sundays, and many people treat the day as one for family, walks, a long lunch and perhaps a visit to a museum. The famous quiet hours, the Ruhezeit, mean you are expected to keep noise down in apartment buildings, and a neighbour who hears a drill on a Sunday will make their feelings known, politely and firmly.

The weekend is for activity, but organised activity. Germans hike, cycle, swim, join clubs and take part in a staggering variety of associations known as Vereine, from football and choirs to chess and allotment gardening. A Saturday morning might be a market, a Sunday afternoon a long walk and coffee-and-cake at a café, the Kaffee und Kuchen ritual that is observed with near-religious devotion. Evenings are for friends, a beer garden in the summer and the sort of quiet dinner where nobody looks at a phone.

The Goethe-Institut's series on food crossroads explores the encounter between cultures through cooking, which is relevant for any Nigerian arriving in Germany and wondering where to find a decent pot of jollof. Larger cities have African shops, restaurants and communities, and many Nigerians find that a weekend with friends from home fills the gap left by the closed shops and the quiet streets.

The adjustment is real. A Nigerian expat in Germany may find the weekend peaceful to the point of loneliness. Invitations are made well in advance, spontaneity is rare, and a surprise visit is a minor offence. The upside is the opposite of Lagos: space, calm, order and the knowledge that nobody will stage a drum solo outside your window on Sunday morning. For many, that is a relief, and for others, a loss.

I will say one more thing in defence of both. The weekend is where a culture reveals what it truly values when nobody is making it work. In Nigeria, that value is connection, and the weekend is a long, loud proof that people matter more than schedules. In Germany, that value is autonomy, and the weekend is a protected space where nobody is allowed to demand anything from you. Seen that way, the clash between the two is not between fun and boredom but between two sincere ideas about what a good life is for. The expat who understands that stops criticising and starts borrowing: a long, joyful lunch from Lagos, a silent, unhurried forest walk from Berlin, and the confidence to say no to a plan when you need the rest.

## The Verdict

Nigeria wins on joy, and Germany wins on rest. If the question is where a weekend feels most alive, I will take Lagos every time: the colour, the music, the food, the sheer human abundance. If the question is where a person can recover from a hard week, Germany has built a whole civilisation around the principle that you are allowed to do nothing.

The truth is that the two countries are answering different questions. Nigeria asks: how do we celebrate being together? Germany asks: how do we protect time from being consumed? Both are valid, and the best expat life takes something from each, with the party on Saturday and the quiet walk on Sunday.

My own vote is unsentimental. I would live in Germany and visit Lagos for the weekends. Rest is wonderful, but nobody ever told a good story about a Sunday in a quiet flat.

## Conclusion

A weekend is a country's real religion. Nigeria worships company, rhythm and the long party that runs into Sunday service. Germany worships rest, order and the sacred right to a silent afternoon. Both demand devotion and both reward it.

If you are moving from Nigeria to Germany, buy your groceries on Saturday, find your community and learn to enjoy the quiet. If you are going the other way, bring an appetite, a good outfit and a flexible sense of time. Either way, the weekend belongs to the people who show up.
