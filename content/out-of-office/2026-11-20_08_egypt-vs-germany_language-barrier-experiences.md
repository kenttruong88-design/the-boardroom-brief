# Egypt Will Happily Switch to English and Germany Will Politely Wait for You to Learn Dative

🇪🇬 Egypt vs 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Egypt is a country of linguistic generosity. Arabic is the mother tongue, a dialect of enormous charm and impossible speed, and the Cairo street will switch to English, French or a determined combination of gestures the moment it sees you struggling. Shopkeepers, taxi drivers and the man selling tea at the corner will meet you more than halfway. Nobody is embarrassed on your behalf, and nobody expects you to be fluent.

Germany is a country of linguistic principle. German is the language of the office, the form, the neighbour and the man at the bakery who wants to know whether you would like the bread sliced. English works in many workplaces and some cities, and it vanishes the moment you reach a counter that stamps things. For an Egyptian professional arriving with excellent English and a rich linguistic background, the discovery that your fluency is irrelevant at the Burgeramt comes as an education.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Egypt**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn greetings and a few phrases in Egyptian Arabic | Rely on a textbook dialect that nobody speaks on the street |
| Accept the invitation to practise with locals | Hide behind English every time |
| Be patient with the speed and the idioms | Assume someone is angry because they are loud |
| Use the polite phrases and honorifics | Skip the greetings and get straight to business |

**Germany**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Start a German course in your first month | Postpone it until you feel settled |
| Bring a German speaker or translator to official appointments | Assume the officer will speak English |
| Use the formal Sie until invited to switch | Use first names with a stranger |
| Read the letters you receive and respond in time | Ignore a document because it looks complicated |

## Egypt

Arabic in Egypt is not a single language but a family of registers. There is the formal written Arabic of newspapers and speeches, and there is the Egyptian dialect, which is the language of films, songs, jokes and everyday life, and which much of the Arabic-speaking world understands because of the country's cinematic reach. For a foreigner, the dialect is the one that matters, and it is the one that most textbooks neglect.

Egyptians are famously warm to learners. Mispronounce a word and you will be corrected cheerfully, applauded for the attempt and invited to tea. Business is conducted in a blend of Arabic and English, depending on the sector, and many professionals are comfortable switching between them mid-sentence. In tourist and corporate areas, English is widely understood. Away from them, patience, gestures and a smile do the heavy lifting.

The social lesson is that language is a relationship before it is a tool. Greetings are long, enquiries about family are expected and the ritual of saying hello matters more than the content. Rushing straight to the point is read as cold. A language barrier in Egypt is therefore softer than elsewhere, because the culture itself is built to bridge it.

The challenge arrives with writing and bureaucracy, where formal Arabic and official procedures are less forgiving. But even there, someone will usually take you by the elbow, explain the form and walk you to the right window. It is not efficient, but it is deeply human.

Digital tools have changed the game, but not erased it. Translation apps, language-learning platforms and online tutors let you practise before you arrive, and a Cairo-based learner can reach decent German before boarding a plane. The trick is to combine apps with human contact, because only a person can correct your accent and laugh at your mistakes in a way that sticks.

## Germany

German is demanding, and Germany does not apologise for it. The cases, the genders, the compound nouns that stretch across a page like a freight train all reward persistence and punish shortcuts. Expat forums on the importance of the local language return to the same blunt conclusion: you can survive in some international bubbles without it, but you cannot truly live in Germany without it.

Official Germany is the hardest test. Offices operate in German, forms are in German and letters arrive in the dense, formal prose that German authorities consider a courtesy. A guide for expats on navigating government offices advises preparation: bring documents, bring a translator if possible and learn the key vocabulary before you go. The Local, which covers the country for foreigners, has run pieces on how difficult it is to settle, and the language is frequently at the centre of the story.

Yet the situation is not hopeless. Cities with large international communities have English-friendly workplaces and services, and a growing number of employers operate in English. Language courses are plentiful, from intensive classes to evening groups and online platforms. An Arabic teacher who moved from Cairo to Berlin has described the experience of learning German as an adult, and it reads as a story of effort, humility and eventual reward.

German culture also rewards the attempt. Germans may be reserved, but they respect a person who tries, and a clumsy sentence in German is received with more warmth than a perfect one in English. It will not win you friends overnight, but it will earn you a grudging smile.

A final note on tone. German directness, combined with a language that does not decorate, can feel blunt to a speaker of a more ornamental tongue. It is rarely hostile. It is simply economical. Once you adjust, you may find the clarity restful, and you may find yourself saying exactly what you mean, which is a surprisingly good habit.

## The Verdict

Egypt wins for the newcomer who wants an easy life. The language barrier is real, but it is padded with generosity, and almost everyone is willing to help.

Germany wins for the newcomer who wants to be taken seriously. The barrier is higher and less forgiving, but once you cross it you are treated as a full member of society, not a guest.

I give the edge to Germany for results and to Egypt for atmosphere. The truth is that you will learn German because you have to, and you will love Egyptian Arabic because you want to. That distinction explains most of what you need to know about both places.

## Conclusion

A language is a doorway, and the two countries have hung very different doors. In Egypt, the door is open and someone is holding it. In Germany, it is closed and there is a polite sign explaining the procedure for opening it.

Learn German, learn it early and learn it badly rather than not at all. And if you are ever in Cairo and someone laughs at your Arabic, remember that they are laughing with you, in the only way the Egyptians know: loudly, affectionately and with a second glass of tea on the way.
