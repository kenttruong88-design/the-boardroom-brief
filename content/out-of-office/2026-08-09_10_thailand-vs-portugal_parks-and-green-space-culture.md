# Bangkok Turns Its Parks Into A 6 A.M. Communal Aerobics Class Before The Heat Becomes A Weapon. Lisbon Just Hands You A Free View Better Than Anything You'd Pay For, And Calls It A Garden.

🇹🇭 🇵🇹 *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Green space tells you what a city is fighting against. Bangkok's parks exist in a running battle with heat and density — vast, humid, tropical, and used most intensely in the narrow, precious windows of early morning and early evening when the temperature briefly becomes survivable. Lisbon's green spaces exist in a gentler negotiation with topography — a city of seven hills that turned its inconvenient slopes into miradouros, hillside gardens with sweeping river views, functioning as much as social lookout points as actual parks.

I've done stretches alongside sixty strangers in Bangkok's Lumpini Park at 6 a.m. to a blaring aerobics soundtrack, sweating before the sun was even properly up. I've also sat on a bench in Lisbon's Miradouro de Santa Catarina at golden hour with a two-euro bottle of wine, watching the Tagus River do absolutely nothing spectacular and somehow being completely content about it.

## Do's & Don'ts: Thailand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit major parks like Lumpini or Benjakitti before 8 a.m. or after 5 p.m. — midday heat and humidity make daytime use genuinely uncomfortable | Skip the free communal aerobics or tai chi sessions happening at dawn; joining, even awkwardly, is welcomed and genuinely fun |
| Bring water and expect real crowds at peak times — Bangkok's parks are intensely used given the city's limited green space per capita | Expect Western-style solitary "quiet park" culture; Thai park use is communal, social, and often quite loud with music and group activity |
| Respect monitor lizards if you spot them (Lumpini Park is famous for them) — keep distance, don't feed them | Assume parks are open 24 hours; most close gates at set evening times for safety and maintenance |

## Do's & Don'ts: Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Seek out miradouros (hillside viewpoints) rather than just formal parks — they're Lisbon's real green-space specialty and mostly free | Expect flat, easy park walking; Lisbon's hills mean genuine gradient is involved in reaching the best green spaces |
| Bring a picnic and something to drink for golden hour at a miradouro — it's a deeply normal, low-key local ritual, not a tourist-only activity | Rush through; Portuguese park and viewpoint culture rewards lingering slowly over an hour or two, not quick visits |
| Explore beyond the famous viewpoints — smaller neighbourhood gardens (jardins) offer the same charm with far fewer crowds | Assume all green spaces are formally landscaped; some of Lisbon's best spots are informal, semi-wild hillside patches locals have simply claimed |

## Thailand: Green Space As Communal Survival Ritual

Bangkok is, by land area, one of the more green-space-starved major cities in Asia, which makes the parks it does have — Lumpini, Benjakitti, Chatuchak — function with an intensity that outsiders don't initially expect. The real rhythm of use is dictated entirely by climate: a narrow dawn window before the heat and humidity turn any outdoor activity into an endurance event, then a similar window again in the early evening. Within those windows, the parks come alive with a specifically communal energy — free public aerobics classes with blaring music and enthusiastic instructors, groups practising tai chi in loose unison, joggers circling in genuine crowds rather than isolated solo laps.

This communal intensity is the defining difference from more individualistic park cultures elsewhere: Thai park use rarely reads as quiet, solitary retreat. It's social, loud, shared, often involving strangers falling into step together for a lap or two before peeling off. Lumpini Park's famous monitor lizards — genuinely enormous, genuinely numerous, sunning themselves along the lake edges — have become an oddly beloved local landmark rather than a source of alarm, treated with the same relaxed acceptance Thais extend to most unexpected disruptions.

The scarcity of green space citywide has also pushed real innovation: elevated parks built on old rail infrastructure, rooftop gardens on shopping centres, and pocket parks squeezed into leftover urban corners, all reflecting a city working hard to claw back breathing room within an intensely built, intensely hot environment.

## Portugal: Green Space As Framed View

Lisbon's relationship with green space is shaped less by scarcity than by geography — a city built across seven steep hills, where the most rewarded green spaces aren't necessarily the largest, but the ones positioned to catch a view. The miradouro tradition turns hillside gardens into genuine civic institutions: small, often modestly landscaped spaces whose real value is the sweeping vista over terracotta rooftops to the Tagus River, used daily by locals for everything from a solitary coffee to a full evening picnic with wine and a guitar.

What distinguishes Portuguese green-space culture is its unhurried, almost meditative pace. Where Bangkok's parks are used in intense, purposeful bursts around the punishing climate, Lisbon's miradouros and jardins invite lingering — an hour, two hours, watching the light change over the river as the city's famous golden-hour glow settles in, a ritual so normalised that locals plan entire evenings around simply "going to watch the sunset" from a specific favourite spot.

Beyond the famous viewpoints, Lisbon's neighbourhood jardins — smaller, quieter, often tucked behind unassuming gates — offer the same tranquil charm with a fraction of the crowds, rewarding those willing to wander off the well-worn tourist circuit. The city's relatively mild climate for much of the year also means green space use isn't confined to narrow daily windows the way Bangkok's is; a Lisbon park is usable, and used, across most of the day, most of the year.

## The Verdict

Bangkok's parks are earned through discipline — show up at dawn, join the communal sweat, and you'll be rewarded with genuine civic energy in a city that fights hard for every patch of green it has. Lisbon's miradouros are earned through patience — climb the hill, sit down, and let an unhurried evening happen around a view that costs nothing and somehow outperforms every paid rooftop bar in the city. If you want green space that pulses with shared, sweaty urgency, Bangkok wins. If you want green space that simply asks you to slow down and watch the light change, Lisbon wins, easily. I've sweated through Lumpini Park aerobics I never signed up for. I've also lost an entire evening to a Lisbon miradouro I only meant to visit for ten minutes. Only one of those regrets stayed with me, and it wasn't the wine.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Bangkok — a visitor described being unexpectedly pulled into a full aerobics routine at Lumpini Park by an enthusiastic regular, and admitted it became the best part of their entire trip.*

> *Reddit r/Lisboa — a local recommended skipping the most famous, crowded miradouro entirely in favour of a smaller neighbourhood one two streets over, insisting the view was nearly identical with a fraction of the tourists.*

> *expat.com Lisbon — a longtime resident noted that bringing your own wine and snacks to a miradouro at sunset was far more normal, and far cheaper, than any of the rooftop bars marketed to tourists nearby.*

## Conclusion

Thailand and Portugal have both built green-space cultures shaped entirely by what their geography demands of them — one an intense, communal negotiation with heat, the other an unhurried, view-chasing negotiation with hills. Neither city has much spare land to work with, and both have turned that constraint into something genuinely distinctive rather than apologising for it. Show up early and sweat in Bangkok. Show up late and linger in Lisbon. Both, in their own way, will teach you something about making do with less space and somehow getting more out of it.
