# Ghana and Britain Share a Language and Absolutely Nothing Else. Ask Anyone Who Has Tried to Order a Coffee in Glasgow

🇬🇭 Ghana vs 🇬🇧 UK

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

It is one of the great myths of migration that if you already speak English, the language barrier does not apply to you. Ghana and Britain share the language, the colonial paperwork and a surprisingly large number of Premier League opinions. They do not share an accent, a pace of speech, or a sense of what a perfectly reasonable sentence sounds like at eight in the morning.

I am British, and I will admit something I do not say enough: my countrymen are terrible at being understood. We mumble, we swallow vowels, we speak with a speed that would be rude if we noticed it. And then we wonder why a perfectly fluent newcomer looks at us as if we had begun speaking in code.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Ghana**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Speak English clearly, and mix in Twi or Fante among friends | Assume everyone abroad will follow Ghanaian English |
| Expect warm greetings and a lot of small talk | Skip the greeting and go straight to the request |
| Ask people to repeat themselves without embarrassment | Pretend you understood |
| Use local language to build trust with fellow Ghanaians abroad | Be shy about it |

**UK**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask people to slow down, politely and often | Nod along when you did not catch it |
| Learn that a British question is often a greeting | Treat 'you alright?' as a medical enquiry |
| Give yourself five or six months to adjust | Panic in the first fortnight |
| Expect regional accents to differ sharply | Assume one British accent covers the country |

## Ghana

Ghana is an English-speaking country in the official sense, and in the practical one. English is the language of government, education and business, and it sits alongside Twi, Fante, Ewe, Ga and a long list of others. A Ghanaian professional moving to Britain does not arrive with a language problem. They arrive with a listening problem, which is a different and more humiliating thing.

Ghanaian English has its own rhythm and its own vocabulary, and it is spoken with a politeness that Britons often fail to match. Greetings matter. The question about family, health and day is not filler, it is part of the transaction. Skip it and you are impolite. I have seen British colleagues blunder through this in Accra with the sensitivity of a shopping trolley.

What surprised me, reading the Ghanaian press coverage of this subject, is how many people are willing to say publicly that they cannot follow British speech. They are not embarrassed. They are baffled, and they are comfortable saying so. A woman who posted a video in 2024 said she struggled to hear her neighbours, friends and colleagues in her new country, and the response was a flood of reader comments from people who understood exactly what she meant.

One commenter named Akosua Boatemaa Asuming offered the most perfect anecdote of the lot: "One of my managers asked me a question, and I said very soon, not knowing he was asking 'how are you'?" That is the whole problem in a single sentence. The greeting arrived as noise, and the answer came out as nonsense. Nobody was rude, and nobody understood each other.

Another reader, who goes by EL Maestro, wrote in the mix of English and pidgin that Ghanaians use online: "My headache oooo interview paaaa den pardon pardon like 40 tyms and dey asked me am I having an ear problem." I cannot improve on that. Forty pardons in a job interview is a nightmare I would not wish on anyone, and it is exactly what happens when two kinds of English meet at full speed.

## UK

The British have a talent for making English hard. We use words in a way that depends on tone, class and region. A question like "you alright?" is a greeting, not an enquiry, and the only correct answer is "yeah, you?" You are not meant to discuss your health. If you do, you will be treated with great kindness and mild alarm.

Then there is the speed. A Ghanaian healthcare assistant named Abena, who said she came to the UK in 2023, told a news site that she could not follow the English spoken in Scotland. Her words, reported in November 2025, were: "I beg you, I am unable to hear English spoken by the Scots, so I have returned to England." She compared the Scots unfavourably, in terms of speed, with Asante Twi and Fante Twi, saying that "theirs is a bit faster." The article also said her own adjustment took five to six months.

I want to be careful here. That report is inconsistent about whether she left Scotland, and the comments mix British and American experiences, so I will not pretend it is a rigorous survey. But the pattern it describes is real and familiar to anyone who has watched an English person try to follow a Glaswegian. The British do not all speak the same language. We speak a family of accents that happen to share a dictionary.

The NHS is a particular pressure point, because that is where many Ghanaian professionals work. Phone consultations, shift handovers and quick conversations on a ward all demand rapid comprehension in noisy places. Training helps. Time helps more. After about half a year, as Abena's own timeline suggests, most people find their ear.

The kindest thing I can say about British English is that it is learnable. The unkindest is that nobody will help you, because they do not realise there is anything to learn. Ask them to slow down, and most will oblige, with an embarrassed laugh. That is the only real strategy: speak up, and ask again.

The practical lesson is to treat comprehension as a skill and not as a character flaw. People who are fluent, educated and professionally confident can still feel stupid in their first British month, and it is worth saying aloud that the problem is the signal, not the listener. Read the subtitles. Watch regional television. Ask a colleague to explain the slang you keep hearing in the corridor. Within a few months the rhythm begins to resolve into words, and the same people who frightened you on the telephone become entirely ordinary. The ear adapts, and it adapts faster than the pride.

## The Verdict

Ghana has the clearer language culture, and the UK has the harder listening test. I would give the point to Ghana, where greetings are generous, speech is courteous and nobody swallows half a word to save time. A British person arriving in Accra can learn to follow the pace in a month. A Ghanaian arriving in Glasgow can need half a year.

But the verdict is not about superiority. It is about who owes whom an effort. The speaker with the fast, regional, mumbled English has a duty to slow down, and the British are slow to accept it. Until we do, the burden falls on the newcomer, which is unfair and also entirely predictable.

If you are moving from Ghana to the UK, treat the first six months as a hearing course. Ask for repetition without apology. Record the phrases that baffle you, and ask a friend to explain them. It will get easier. It will not get less funny.

## Conclusion

A shared language is a loan, not a gift. You get the vocabulary, and you pay the interest in accent, speed and idiom. Ghanaians arriving in Britain discover this within the first week, and the British, rather charmingly, never discover it at all.

So say it again, slower. Ask them to repeat themselves. And if somebody asks how you are, the answer is not "very soon". It is "fine, thanks, you?" Memorise that, and the rest will follow.
