# One Country Spends Its Weekend in a Hot Pool. The Other Spends It Praying at 4,000 Metres.

🇧🇹 Bhutan vs 🇮🇸 Iceland — Weekend Culture and Leisure

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ask an Icelander what they're doing this weekend and the answer is, invariably, "the pool," said with the same casual tone an Italian might use for "lunch." Ask a Bhutanese person the same question and you might get "hiking to a monastery clinging to a cliff face" delivered with equal nonchalance. Both nations have quietly built entire identities around a specific weekend ritual, and both will look at you like you're the strange one for not immediately understanding why.

Neither country does "brunch and scroll on the sofa." Iceland treats leisure as a geothermal birthright. Bhutan treats it as a spiritual obligation dressed up as a hobby. I have never felt lazier in my life than I did standing next to a Bhutanese grandmother who'd already done a three-hour pilgrimage before I'd finished my coffee.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇧🇹 Bhutan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a weekend archery match if invited — it's the national sport and a genuine social event | Wander into a dzong or monastery in shorts or a tank top |
| Budget real time for weekend hikes to Tiger's Nest or local monasteries — locals treat the trek as leisure, not extreme sport | Rush the experience; Bhutanese weekend pace is deliberately unhurried |
| Bring a small offering if visiting a temple with local friends | Photograph inside temples without explicit permission |

### 🇮🇸 Iceland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shower thoroughly, naked, before entering any public pool — it's mandatory and strictly enforced | Wear your swimsuit into the pre-pool shower area |
| Treat the Saturday "pool date" as a genuine social institution, not just exercise | Be shocked when strangers chat with you in the hot tub — it's the norm, not an intrusion |
| Get outside regardless of weather — locals hike, swim, and picnic through wind that would cancel a British bank holiday | Assume "weekend plans" means indoors; Icelanders default to outdoors, always |

## Bhutan: Leisure With a Vow Attached

Bhutan is the only country I've been to where "what are you doing this weekend" can honestly be answered with "increasing my merit," and nobody blinks. Weekend leisure here is inseparable from spiritual life — families hike to monasteries not as tourists ticking a box but as a genuine, recurring social and religious activity, often multi-generational, often with a packed lunch and a level of physical stamina that will humble anyone who thinks their gym membership makes them fit.

Archery is the real national obsession, and a weekend match is less a sport and more a full community event — singing, dancing, mild psychological warfare aimed at distracting the opposing team's aim, and an afternoon that stretches on for hours because nobody in Bhutan seems to believe leisure should be rushed. You will be invited to join, badly miss every shot, and be applauded anyway, because the point was never really the arrow.

What outsiders get wrong about Bhutanese weekends is assuming the famous "Gross National Happiness" framework means everyone's just relaxing. It doesn't. It means leisure is taken seriously as a civic value — walking, prayer, community festivals, and archery are treated with the same institutional respect other countries give to GDP growth. The pace is slow by design, not by laziness, and rushing through a monastery visit to "see more sites" marks you instantly as someone who has profoundly missed the point.

Access matters too — much of the country's most striking weekend leisure, from festivals to remote hikes, still requires permits and a level of planning that Icelandic spontaneity would find baffling. Bhutan doesn't do impulsive weekend trips. It does considered ones.

## Iceland: The Pool Is Church

Icelanders have built an entire social contract around the local sundlaug — the public swimming pool — and treating it as merely "exercise" marks you as a foreigner within seconds. Every town, no matter how small, has one, usually geothermally heated, usually packed on a Saturday morning with people who are there to talk, soak, and be seen rather than swim laps. The hot tubs function as the nation's actual town square.

The pre-pool shower ritual is not a suggestion. You strip fully, you shower thoroughly, attendants genuinely check, and skipping it is treated as a minor social crime rather than a personal choice — a rule that catches every single tourist off guard exactly once. After that, though, Icelandic leisure culture is remarkably unfussy: strangers strike up conversation in the hot pot with a directness that would read as forward anywhere else, and by the time you leave you may know more about your neighbour's divorce than you do about their surname.

Weather does not factor into Icelandic weekend plans the way it would almost anywhere else. Horse riding, hiking, and open-air swimming continue through winds that would shut down a British train line, because Icelanders have simply decided that "bad weather" is a foreign concept invented by people who haven't bought the right coat. The famous blue-and-black skies of an Icelandic winter weekend are treated as scenery, not an obstacle.

What Iceland lacks, compared to Bhutan, is any real sense of leisure-as-ritual beyond the pool — weekends are casual, unstructured, and largely about being outside or in hot water with people you like. It's leisure stripped of ceremony. Which, depending on your mood, is either wonderfully relaxed or a little thin on meaning.

## The Verdict

Bhutan makes you work for your weekend and rewards you with something that feels like it actually mattered. Iceland makes your weekend effortless and rewards you with a very good soak and an unplanned conversation about a stranger's ex-husband. If you want leisure that feels sacred, Bhutan wins outright. If you want leisure that feels like a hot bath and a shrug, Iceland has you covered. I know which one left me more exhausted, and I know which one left me more changed. They are not the same country.

## Conclusion

Neither country understands the concept of a wasted weekend, which is either deeply admirable or faintly exhausting depending on how much you enjoy doing absolutely nothing. Bhutan will send you home spiritually rearranged. Iceland will send you home smelling faintly of sulphur and strangely willing to discuss your feelings with people you met forty minutes ago in a hot tub. Both are better use of forty-eight hours than whatever you were planning to do instead.
