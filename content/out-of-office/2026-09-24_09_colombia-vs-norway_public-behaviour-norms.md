# In Colombia, Personal Space Is a Suggestion. In Norway, It's Basically Sacred Ground.

🇨🇴 Colombia 🇳🇴 Norway
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular Norwegian bus stop phenomenon so well documented it's become a national meme: passengers standing at precise, evenly spaced intervals, sometimes several metres apart, in what looks less like a queue and more like a diagram from a social distancing manual designed a decade before anyone needed one. There is an equally well documented Colombian phenomenon in which a "queue" at a bank or bus stop dissolves the instant anyone shows the faintest hesitation, reforming as a dense, warm, slightly chaotic cluster in which physical space between strangers is treated as an eccentric foreign import nobody asked for.

I've stood in both formations — rigidly alone at a Bergen bus stop, wondering if I'd committed some invisible offence by existing near another human, and cheerfully crushed into a Medellín collectivo where a stranger's elbow was in my ribs and nobody, least of all me, particularly minded. Colombia treats closeness as warmth. Norway treats distance as respect. Both are entirely sincere expressions of care for the people around you — they've just arrived at opposite conclusions about what caring looks like in practice.

## Colombia: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept close physical proximity on public transport as entirely normal | Expect strict, orderly queuing everywhere — lines can be fluid, especially in informal settings |
| Greet with warmth — a handshake, cheek kiss, or hug depending on context | Take loud, animated conversation nearby as aggression; it's usually just enthusiasm |
| Embrace spontaneous small talk with strangers — genuinely welcomed | Assume silence in a shared space is the norm; comfortable noise is the default |

## Norway: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Leave a genuinely wide gap at bus stops and on public transport unless it's full | Sit directly next to a stranger on an empty bus or train — it will be noted, silently, forever |
| Queue precisely and patiently — order is respected without needing enforcement | Make loud small talk with strangers in shared quiet spaces like trains or waiting rooms |
| Value comfortable silence as a sign of respect, not awkwardness | Stand too close in a conversation — Norwegian personal space extends further than most of Europe |

## Colombia: Warmth Measured in Proximity

Colombian public life operates on the assumption that closeness — physical, verbal, social — is a form of respect rather than an intrusion, and this shows up everywhere from crowded TransMilenio buses in Bogotá to the dense, cheerful chaos of a Medellín market. Personal space, in the Northern European sense of a protected invisible bubble, simply isn't the operating default; conversations happen at close range, strangers lean in, physical touch during conversation — a hand on the arm, a shoulder pat — is common and not remotely loaded with the significance it might carry elsewhere. Queuing culture reflects this same fluidity: formal lines exist and are respected in more institutional settings like banks or government offices, but in informal contexts — a street food stall, a crowded bus stop — the "line" often becomes a loosely ordered cluster held together by mutual, unspoken trust that everyone basically knows their turn.

Noise operates on a similarly generous register. Colombian public spaces are rarely quiet by choice — music plays from shops, conversations carry, laughter is loud and frequent, and this isn't considered rude so much as evidence of a shared public life being actively, enthusiastically lived rather than merely tolerated. Visitors from quieter cultures sometimes misread this as chaotic or overwhelming, but Colombians themselves generally experience it as the opposite: a public sphere that feels alive, connected, and unafraid of taking up shared space together. The flip side, which some expats do note with genuine frustration, is that this same warmth can occasionally shade into a looser relationship with punctuality and structured order — the famous "Colombian time" phenomenon — where the same fluidity that makes public space feel warm can make scheduled processes feel maddeningly elastic.

## Norway: Silence and Space as a Love Language

Norway sits at the opposite extreme of nearly every metric above, and Norwegians are entirely aware of their own reputation — the bus-stop spacing phenomenon is such a well-worn national joke that Norwegians will bring it up themselves, unprompted, with a mixture of self-deprecation and quiet pride. Personal space in Norway extends considerably further than in most of Europe, let alone Latin America; standing or sitting close to a stranger without clear necessity reads as a genuine social violation, and the instinct to leave an empty seat between yourself and anyone you don't know is close to reflexive. This isn't coldness — Norwegians are, by most social trust surveys, among the most trusting and community-minded populations in the world — it's simply that trust and warmth are expressed through respecting someone's space and quiet rather than through closeness or conversation.

Queuing in Norway functions almost eerily smoothly precisely because it doesn't need active enforcement — the social contract is understood and near-universally honoured, whether it's a formal numbered ticket system in a post office or an informal line at a food truck. Silence, too, carries entirely different weight: a quiet train carriage isn't awkward, it's simply respectful, an unspoken agreement that shared public space is not an invitation for shared noise. Small talk with strangers is genuinely rare and can even make Norwegians visibly uncomfortable if initiated without clear social permission — a stark contrast to Colombia's default assumption that strangers are simply friends who haven't spoken yet. Both approaches, properly understood, are about consideration for others; they've just built entirely opposite architectures for expressing it.

## The Verdict

Colombia wins on sheer human warmth — there is something genuinely lovely about a public culture that assumes closeness rather than guards against it, and I've made more real connections with strangers in a single Bogotá bus ride than in a month of Norwegian commutes. But Norway wins on functional respect for individual comfort — nobody's personal boundaries are ever silently violated, nobody feels crowded or pressured into unwanted interaction, and there's a quiet dignity in a system that simply trusts everyone to know the unwritten rules without being told. I'd rather be crushed warmly into a Colombian collectivo than stand awkwardly alone at a Norwegian bus stop — but I'd rather queue in Oslo than anywhere else on Earth, no exceptions.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/Colombia — paraphrased: tried to enforce strict British-style queuing at a busy panadería out of habit and just ended up waiting twice as long as everyone who simply joined the friendly cluster around the counter.*

> *Quora — paraphrased: someone asked if Norwegians really avoid sitting next to strangers on an empty bus and the top answer was simply "yes, and if you do it anyway they will silently judge you and possibly move," delivered with total sincerity.*

> *Internations Oslo — paraphrased: made the mistake of striking up cheerful small talk with a stranger on a quiet train and watched the entire carriage's demeanor shift, learned to save the chat for actual social settings after that.*

## Conclusion

Neither culture is wrong about how to share public space with strangers — they've just built opposite answers to the same underlying question of what respect actually looks like. Colombia says respect is closeness, noise, and warmth freely given. Norway says respect is distance, quiet, and space freely protected. Bring your Colombian instincts to Oslo and you'll get some very confused looks. Bring your Norwegian instincts to Bogotá and you'll be gently, warmly, relentlessly pulled out of your bubble whether you like it or not.
