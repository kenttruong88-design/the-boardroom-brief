# China Pays With a Glance, Canada Wants to See Your Whole Life First

🇨🇳 China vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Banking is where a country shows you its character in the plainest possible way. In China, you hold up a phone and the money moves, so smoothly that cash feels like a quaint hobby. In Canada, you walk into a branch with a passport, a work permit and a mild sense of dread, and discover that proving you exist is a separate product with its own queue.

I have opened accounts on four continents and been refused on at least two, and I can tell you the refusals teach more than the approvals. Banking systems are not about money. They are about trust, and each country has a very different opinion of how much of it you deserve on day one.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇨🇳 China ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your passport and visa documents when you open an account | Expect a branch to open one for you on a tourist visa |
| Link your local bank card to a mobile wallet early | Rely on foreign cards alone for everyday spending |
| Carry a small amount of cash as a backup | Assume every vendor will accept a foreign card |
| Ask which services require a local phone number | Forget that many apps verify identity by SMS |

| 🇨🇦 Canada ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book an appointment at a branch with your documents | Show up without proof of address and expect a miracle |
| Ask about newcomer packages, which often waive fees | Accept the first account offer without comparing |
| Start building a credit history with a secured card | Assume your foreign credit record follows you |
| Keep every statement and letter for future applications | Throw away the bank letter that proves your address |

## 🇨🇳 China

The first thing a newcomer notices is that the system is two systems. There is the formal banking world of branches, passbooks and cards, which opens to anyone with the right documents, and there is the mobile payment world of wallets and QR codes, which now handles a vast share of everyday spending. Learning to live in both is the quiet skill of the expat in China.

Opening a bank account usually means presenting your passport and the relevant visa or residence documents, often with a local phone number. Branches are efficient when the paperwork is right, and the staff are used to foreigners. If the paperwork is not right, you will be sent home with a list, which is a form of kindness that feels like a defeat.

Once you are inside, convenience takes over. Bills, transfers, taxis, takeaway food and market stalls are all handled by tapping and scanning. The experience is fast and oddly pleasant, and it takes about a week for a visitor to start feeling that cards are a prehistoric technology. The catch is that foreign cards and wallets do not always plug into the same ecosystem, and for years visitors have run into shops where the only accepted method was a local app.

The bureaucratic side is more formal than the digital side suggests. Identity checks, transfer limits and rules on moving money in and out of the country are real, and they are enforced with a patience that foreigners tend to underestimate. A well prepared expat keeps documents tidy, learns what each limit means, and never tries to improvise around a rule.

My own lesson was simple: do the human part first. Get the account, link the wallet, then enjoy a world where a bowl of noodles costs a thumb gesture.

There is also the question of language and customer service. Major Chinese banks have English-speaking staff at the larger branches in big cities, but app interfaces and phone support are mostly in Chinese, so expats often rely on a colleague or a translation tool for the first few weeks. A little preparation, such as having your address written in Chinese characters, saves a surprising amount of effort.

## 🇨🇦 Canada

Canada's banking is polite, safe and very slow to trust a stranger. The country has a small number of large banks that dominate the market, which gives you stable service and not much choice. Each of them offers a newcomer package with reduced fees, a starter credit card and a friendly welcome, which is a very Canadian way of saying that they would like your business and your paperwork.

To open an account, you typically need government issued identification, proof of address and your immigration documents. Many banks let you start the process online or in a branch, and an appointment saves enormous time. The advice I give to every arrival is the same: book it before you land, and bring a folder.

The real obstacle is credit. Canada runs on a credit history, and a person who has just arrived has none, whatever they earned elsewhere. Without it, renting a flat, getting a phone plan or applying for a mortgage becomes a slow climb, and the newcomer packages exist mostly to help you start that climb. A secured card and a habit of paying it off in full are the first rungs.

Payments are almost entirely by tap, and e-transfers between friends are universal. It is convenient, but nobody pretends it is exciting. The surprises tend to be small: cheques are still a thing, fees on certain accounts are easy to miss, and a customer service call can last long enough to learn a language.

Canadians are proud of the stability of their banks, and they have reason to be. It is simply that stability and speed have never been introduced.

Canadian bureaucracy extends well beyond the bank. A social insurance number, a health card, a driver's licence and a lease all feed into one another, and each office wants to see the others' paperwork first. Newcomers describe a loop that eventually resolves itself, and the advice is to start the slowest item first and to keep every letter, because the next office will ask for it.

## The Verdict

China wins on convenience and Canada wins on predictability, and which one you prefer depends on how much you value your time versus your peace of mind. For daily life, China is a revelation. A phone, a wallet and a bank card cover everything from a market stall to a train ticket, and the friction of cash and cards has been removed from the street.

For long term security, Canada has the edge. The rules are clear, consumer protections are strong, and a newcomer who plays by them is rewarded with a credit history that opens doors for decades.

I would rather live in China and bank in Canada, which sounds like a joke until you realise that is exactly what many expats do. They keep a Canadian account for the serious things and a Chinese wallet for the nonsense of daily life. It is a rare case where owning two systems is not a failure of imagination.

The sting is that each country is quietly proud of its approach and quietly embarrassed by the other's strengths. China wonders why Canada still writes cheques. Canada wonders why China does not worry more about who is watching the transaction.

A good rule in both countries is to treat every official document as if you will need it again, because you will. The Chinese system tends to be fast and digital once you are in, and the Canadian one tends to be slow and paper-based until you are in. The skill that carries you through both is an orderly folder and a calm voice.

## Conclusion

Banking is the one area of expat life where a bad week can become a bad year. Get it right in China and you are living in the future. Get it right in Canada and you are building a future, slowly and with a lot of paperwork.

My parting advice is practical: set up a local account within your first fortnight, keep digital and paper copies of everything, and learn which of your documents each country treats as sacred. In China it is the phone number. In Canada it is the proof of address.

Whichever way you travel, bring a patient face. Both countries will eventually say yes, and both will make you earn the word.
