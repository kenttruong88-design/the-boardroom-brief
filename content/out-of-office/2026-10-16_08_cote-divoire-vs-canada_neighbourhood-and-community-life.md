# In Abidjan, Your Neighbours Know Your Business. In Canada, They Don't Even Know Your Name.

🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire 🇨🇦 Canada
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular kind of silence that greets you in a Canadian cul-de-sac at 6pm on a Tuesday — garage doors shut, blinds drawn, a single dog barking at nothing, everyone home and nobody visible. I have stood in that silence in Mississauga, in Burnaby, in a particularly smug part of Calgary, and thought: this is what happens when a civilisation optimises for property values over people. It is immaculate. It is safe. It is, if you are being honest with yourself at 2am, a little bit like living in a well-maintained mausoleum.

Fly fourteen hours south and the opposite problem announces itself before you've even unpacked. In Abidjan, your neighbour will ask after your mother's health, your job prospects, and your marital status within the first week, usually from across a shared wall, usually while someone's cousin is frying alloco four feet away. Community in Côte d'Ivoire is not optional, not scheduled, and not particularly interested in your boundaries. Community in Canada is a bylaw, a committee, and an email chain nobody replies to. Both are exhausting in their own ways. Only one of them will fine you $200 for not shovelling by 10am.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet every neighbour you pass, every time, properly — ask about family and health before anything else | Rush a greeting or skip straight to your request; it reads as hostile, not efficient |
| Expect to be asked for small loans, favours, or childcare help, and budget for reciprocating | Keep a mental ledger and expect it to balance within the month, or ever |
| Learn your block's maquis and show up regularly — it is the real community noticeboard | Assume privacy is the default; walls are thin and business travels fast |
| Contribute to collective events — funerals, weddings, naming ceremonies — even for people you barely know | Opt out of "forced solidarity" obligations without a very good, very public excuse |

### 🇨🇦 Canada
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shovel your sidewalk promptly — many cities fine you, and a cleared walk still signals good neighbouring | Assume someone else, or the city, will handle it; only a handful of cities actually clear sidewalks for you |
| Read the condo or HOA bylaws before you move in — they govern more of your life than you'd like | Renovate, decorate, or even get a pet before checking; boards have fined and sued over exactly this |
| Introduce yourself once, clearly, and accept that follow-up is entirely on you | Expect a knock on the door or a casserole; the onboarding ritual mostly disappeared decades ago |
| Join the one mailing list or Facebook group that actually organises the street party | Wait for organic community to simply happen; in most suburbs, it won't without a volunteer committee |

## Côte d'Ivoire

Let's be precise about what "neighbourhood" means in Abidjan, because it bears almost no resemblance to the sanitised Western version. In Cocody, the leafy, consular-heavy quarter where diplomats and well-off locals cluster, you'll get something closer to privacy — walls, gates, a watchman. Everywhere else — Treichville, Marcory, Yopougon, the dense, market-loud communes where most of the city actually lives — neighbourhood is a full-contact sport. You are known. Your comings and goings are known. Your financial situation is, alarmingly, also known, because someone's cousin works at the bank.

The engine of all this is the maquis — the open-air, plastic-chair, grilled-fish-and-beer institution that functions as living room, town hall, and World Cup viewing party rolled into one battered courtyard. It emerged in the mid-twentieth century and has since metastasised into a genuinely essential piece of civic infrastructure: you don't just eat at your local maquis, you are accounted for there. Miss a few nights and someone will ask why.

This density of obligation has an academic name — researchers studying West African kinship networks call it "forced solidarity," and it is not a compliment. You are expected to lend money to relatives and neighbours you may barely know, attend funerals for people three degrees removed from you, and contribute to naming ceremonies, weddings, and school fees as a matter of course, not generosity. Refuse, and you don't just lose face — you lose your safety net the next time you need one. Expats arriving in Abidjan consistently report the same disorientation on forums: the hardest part isn't the heat or the traffic, it's realising that nobody is coming to "check in" on you in the Western sense — you are simply, immediately, inside the machine, whether you consented or not.

The upside, infuriatingly, is that loneliness as Canadians understand it barely exists here. The downside is that your weekends, your salary, and occasionally your sanity belong to the collective now. Ivorian neighbourliness isn't a nicety. It's a tax, paid in attention.

## Canada

Canada has achieved something remarkable: a system of neighbourhood life so proceduralised that community itself requires an agenda item. Move into a condo in Toronto or Calgary and you will, within weeks, receive a bylaw document running anywhere from twenty to seventy pages, because that's what happens when volunteer boards get hold of multi-million-dollar budgets and nothing to do with their Tuesdays. One Toronto board was fined nearly $60,000 by a court for failing to enforce its own noise rules against a dance studio upstairs. A Montreal board spent $315,000 adjudicating a dispute over a five-year-old child running in a hallway. This is not community. This is litigation with a Christmas potluck attached.

Outside the condo, in the land of the detached house and the two-car garage, the defining neighbourhood ritual is not conversation — it's snow. Toronto gives you 24 hours to clear your sidewalk, 12 downtown, with fines plus surcharges for failure. Calgary, Edmonton, Regina, Vancouver all run similar regimes, with fines from $100 to $200, occasionally $2,000 for businesses. Only a handful of cities — Montreal, Ottawa, Winnipeg, Halifax — bother clearing it for you. Everywhere else, your relationship with your neighbour is measured in whether they shovelled your walk while you were in Florida, and whether you reciprocated. It is, perversely, the single most reliable act of community Canadian suburbs still perform — a wordless, mittened contract.

Beyond that, don't expect much. Over 10% of Canadians over 15 report feeling lonely often or always, rising past a third for seniors over 65, per Statistics Canada and a 2023 Women's College Hospital study — numbers serious enough that corporations now write loneliness-epidemic think-pieces. The potluck and the block party survive mainly as volunteer-organised events that require a Facebook group and somebody's relentless enthusiasm to exist at all. Nobody is coming to your door uninvited. Ever. You will have to build your village from scratch, with a sign-up sheet.

## The Verdict

If you want to disappear, Canada will let you — gloriously, completely, for years, until a bylaw officer finally notices your sidewalk. If you want to be held, Côte d'Ivoire will hold you so tightly you may need to renegotiate your own salary with your neighbours before Friday. Neither is "better," whatever the relocation brochures imply. Abidjan's version of neighbourliness is a safety net woven so densely it occasionally strangles; Canada's is a safety net so loose you sometimes can't find it in an actual emergency, because everyone assumed someone else would knock.

What's indisputable is which one scales with money. Canadian community is increasingly something you opt into — a committee, a group chat, a $40 potluck contribution — while Ivorian community is something you're born or dropped into, no opt-out clause available. My own fourteen relocations suggest the honest answer is to steal from both: take Abidjan's instinct to actually notice your neighbours exist, and Canada's instinct to put boundaries in writing before someone sues you over a trampoline. Pure versions of either system will eventually make you either bankrupt or invisible.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Côte d'Ivoire forum — paraphrased: newcomers repeatedly describe needing a local contact just to be "placed" properly in a neighbourhood — without one, you're an outsider indefinitely, no matter how long you stay.*

> *Canadian community/suburban-life forum threads (general theme) — paraphrased: people describe living next to the same family for years on a quiet street without ever learning their names, and note that any real connection only happened once someone personally organised a street event.*

> *Quora (paraphrased recurring question) — "How fast are you expected to greet and engage with neighbours in Abidjan?" — the consistent answer from those who've lived there: immediately, properly, every single time, or it will be noticed and remembered.*

## Conclusion

Neither country will hand you the version of community that's actually convenient. Côte d'Ivoire offers belonging as a condition of residence — rich, loud, occasionally bankrupting, never lonely. Canada offers independence as the default setting — clean, quiet, politely administered, and quietly starved of the thing it's supposedly built for. If you're relocating for "community," ask yourself honestly which kind of exhaustion you'd prefer: the kind where everyone knows your business, or the kind where you have to schedule a meeting to make a friend. I've done both. I now shovel my own walk and contribute to maquis tabs I didn't start, and I regret neither decision, though my accountant disagrees on both counts.
