# One Country Shops in a Mall Food Court. The Other Shops From a Plastic Stool on a Motorbike-Choked Street.

🇺🇾 Uruguay 🇻🇳 Vietnam
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Uruguay is the kind of country that looks at Latin American chaos, sighs, and builds a tidy, air-conditioned mall instead — Montevideo Shopping and Punta Carretas feel less like retail centres and more like the national id made architectural. Vietnam, meanwhile, has leapt from motorbike-and-market informality straight into hyper-digital Shopee and TikTok Shop commerce without ever bothering to pass through a mall-culture phase most of the developed world assumed was mandatory.

I have bought a kilo of tomatoes from a woman squatting on a kerb in Hanoi who weighed them on a scale that looked older than her grandmother, and I have bought the exact same tomatoes from a chilled, humming supermarket aisle in Montevideo where nobody made eye contact with me once. Both transactions were efficient. Only one of them felt like an actual human exchange, and it wasn't the one in the mall.

## Do's & Don'ts: Uruguay 🇺🇾

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit a feria (street market) at least once — Feria Tristán Narvaja on a Sunday is the real Montevideo, malls are the polite fiction | Expect feria prices or haggling culture inside a shopping centre — fixed price, full stop, every time |
| Carry cash for the feria and smaller shops — card acceptance thins out fast once you leave the mall ecosystem | Assume shops open on a tidy schedule — Sunday and Monday closures, long lunch pauses, are genuinely common outside malls |
| Use the mall food courts without embarrassment — Uruguayans treat them as legitimate social destinations, not tourist traps | Confuse "mall culture" with "cheap" — imported goods in Uruguayan malls carry serious markups thanks to steep import duties |

## Do's & Don'ts: Vietnam 🇻🇳

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Haggle at wet markets and street stalls — it's expected, good-natured, and refusing to engage just confuses the vendor | Haggle inside a modern supermarket like Co.opmart or VinMart — fixed prices there, same as anywhere else |
| Get comfortable on Shopee, Lazada or TikTok Shop — livestream selling is now a dominant retail channel, not a gimmick | Assume online means slow — same-day and next-day delivery in major cities is often faster than finding parking at a mall |
| Shop markets early morning for the best produce and the real local rhythm, before the midday heat sets in | Bring a giant shopping list mentality to a wet market — it rewards buying what looks good that day, not planning two weeks out |

## Uruguay: The Mall as National Comfort Blanket

Uruguay's retail landscape is a study in a small, stable country quietly deciding it would rather be boring and functional than vibrant and chaotic, and mostly succeeding. Montevideo Shopping, Punta Carretas, Nuevocentro, Portones — these aren't just shopping centres, they're genuine civic gathering points where families spend entire Sunday afternoons, teenagers conduct their entire social lives, and the food court functions as a de facto town square. This is not a country embarrassed by mall culture the way some larger Latin American capitals occasionally are; Uruguayans have made peace with it as simply how modern life here is organised.

And yet the feria tradition refuses to die, and thank God for that. Feria Tristán Narvaja, sprawling through central Montevideo every Sunday morning, is an entirely different register of commerce — books, antiques, actual farm produce, secondhand everything, vendors who've worked the same stretch of pavement for decades, and a social density that no air-conditioned atrium could replicate. The two systems coexist without much tension: Uruguayans shop the mall for the predictable and the feria for the soul of the thing, and nobody considers this contradictory.

What trips up foreigners is the price structure. Thanks to Uruguay's genuinely steep import tariffs, anything imported inside a mall — electronics, branded clothing, a decent espresso machine — carries a markup that will make a visiting European or American audibly gasp in the aisle. Locals have adapted by either buying secondhand, crossing into Argentina or Brazil for big purchases when the exchange rate favours it, or simply accepting that consumer goods cost what they cost here and building a life around it. It's a oddly dignified kind of acceptance, the national shrug applied to retail economics.

## Vietnam: Skipping the Mall Entirely

Vietnam did something genuinely unusual in global retail development: it never fully committed to the Western mall phase before vaulting straight into mobile-first e-commerce. There are malls — Vincom Center and Takashimaya in Ho Chi Minh City are glossy enough to satisfy any brand-conscious shopper — but they occupy a narrower cultural slice than their equivalents almost anywhere else, mostly serving a growing but still minority urban middle and upper class rather than functioning as the default shopping mode.

The actual engine of Vietnamese retail is the wet market and the street stall, still handling the overwhelming majority of fresh food and much of daily household commerce, run by vendors who know their regular customers' names, preferences and running tabs. The sensory assault of a market like Ben Thanh or Hanoi's Dong Xuan — the smell of fish sauce and durian colliding, the motorbikes idling two feet from the produce, the haggling conducted at a volume that sounds aggressive but is actually just Tuesday — is not a tourist performance, it's genuinely how most households still provision themselves.

Where Vietnam has leapt ahead of almost everyone is digital commerce. Shopee dominates, Lazada remains strong, and TikTok Shop has exploded into a retail channel so significant that livestream sellers — ordinary people, not celebrities — move serious volume of clothing, cosmetics and electronics in real time, narrating product demos to thousands of concurrent viewers who buy on impulse mid-stream. Same-day delivery via a fleet of motorbike couriers is routine even in secondary cities, a logistics feat that plenty of wealthier nations with actual mall infrastructure still haven't matched. The result is a bizarre, functional duality: grandmothers haggling over morning glory vegetables at a wet market stall, while their grandchildren buy sneakers from a TikTok livestream on the ride home, and nobody finds this remotely strange.

## The Verdict

Uruguay wins on comfort and predictability — you know exactly what you'll pay, exactly how clean the floor will be, exactly how the Sunday will unfold. Vietnam wins, decisively, on sheer retail vitality: a market culture that's still alive and economically central, layered with a digital commerce scene genuinely ahead of much of the developed world. If "shopping" should feel like a transaction, book the Uruguayan mall. If it should feel like a living, slightly chaotic organism you briefly get to participate in, Vietnam wins every single time, and it's not especially close.

## Conclusion

Both countries have made peace with their contradictions in their own way — Uruguay by letting the mall and the feria coexist without comment, Vietnam by letting a centuries-old market tradition run in parallel with some of the most aggressive e-commerce growth on the planet. Neither place is confused about what it's doing. The rest of us, wandering between a food court and a fish stall within the same week, are the ones who need a moment to catch up.
