# Poland Renovated the Building. Czechia Never Renovated the Attitude.

🇵🇱 Poland vs 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Let's get something straight before anyone reaches for the word "exotic": these are two Central European countries with excellent trains, serious beer, and hotel sectors recovering nicely from several decades of state ownership and several years of nobody travelling anywhere. That's it. That's the premise. Now, the accommodation.

Poland has spent the last twenty years treating its hospitality sector like a renovation project with a point to prove — gleaming towers in Warsaw, boutique conversions in Kraków, a tourist count up 11.6% year-on-year in the first half of 2025 according to the Statistical Office in Rzeszów. Czechia, meanwhile, has the better-looking postcard and the worse-looking poker face: gorgeous Habsburg stock, a five-star sector that's currently outgrowing every other category according to the national statistical office, and reception staff who will check you in with the warmth of a tax audit. Both countries will happily take your money. Only one of them will pretend to enjoy it.

## ✅❌ Do's & Don'ts

**🇵🇱 Poland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Move fast on a listing — decent flats in Warsaw and Kraków get snapped up within a week | Expect air conditioning as standard; most mid-range stock still treats it as a luxury extra |
| Budget for a deposit of one to three months, sometimes more if you're a foreigner with no local payslip | Assume a furnished rental is the default — unfurnished is still the norm outside short-let platforms |
| Learn the recycling colour codes before your first bin day; fines exist and get issued | Expect the agent's fee to come out of the landlord's pocket — it's almost always yours |
| Bring a Polish-speaking friend, or at least Google Translate, to Otodom listings | Be shocked by a "Soviet-era block from the outside, immaculate renovation inside" situation — it's practically the national housing genre |

**🇨🇿 Czechia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register your address ("hlášení pobytu") promptly if you're a non-EU resident — it's a legal requirement, not a suggestion | Expect a landlord to hold a flat for you longer than about a month without a signed contract |
| Treat a Czech-speaking agent as a serious asset; the best listings barely make it to English-language sites | Mistake curt service for hostility — it's closer to indifference, and it is not personal |
| Clarify exactly what "poplatky" (service charges) covers before you sign anything | Expect your breakfast order to be adjusted without warning, or a substitution flagged — it occasionally just happens |
| Enquire about a rental no more than two months ahead — any earlier and nobody will take you seriously | Assume five-star polish in the capital means five-star warmth; the staff-to-smile ratio is its own separate metric |

## 🇵🇱 Poland: Built It Shiny, Still Figuring Out the Service Bit

Poland's accommodation story is fundamentally one of infrastructure outpacing attitude, which is a very Polish sentence to write and also just true. The country logged roughly 18.9 million tourists and 44.9 million overnight stays in the first half of 2025 alone — up double digits on the year before — and the hotel stock has scrambled, genuinely admirably, to keep up. Warsaw's skyline now has the kind of glass-and-steel hotel towers that used to be strictly a Frankfurt flex. Kraków has quietly become one of the better boutique-hotel cities in the region, repurposing townhouses with the sort of restraint that suggests someone finally hired a decent architect instead of a cousin.

The long-stay side is where the real character shows up. Expat Arrivals is blunt about it: demand outpaces supply in every city that matters, unfurnished is still the default, and agents charge at least a month's rent — paid by you, obviously, because why would a system built this fast also be built fair. On the expat.com forums, the complaints have a rhythm to them: one Warsaw-bound poster found the whole thing "so expensive" relative to the local economy, another noted that anything half-decent in the centre "is gone in the matter of a week," and a third — correctly — pointed out that a huge share of the available stock sits inside what they called "post-communist mega apartment complexes," which is either a housing crisis or a design movement depending on your mood.

Here's the thing nobody tells you: those blocks are frequently magnificent inside. Grey Soviet concrete shell, immaculately renovated three-bedroom within. It's the national trick — invest in substance, let the facade do whatever it wants. Deposits run one to three months, occasionally six if you're foreign and unproven, winters apparently kill off any pest problem you might have had (a diplomatic spouse's report on Warsaw life mentions this with real affection), and heating costs in January will remind you that this is still a country with actual winters, not a Instagram caption. Poland's hospitality sector has done the hard part — building the thing. The soft-skills rollout is still in progress.

## 🇨🇿 Czechia: Gorgeous Buildings, a Service Culture With Opinions

Czechia's hotels look like money because the buildings were already expensive before hospitality got involved — you're mostly renovating Habsburg-era bones, not inventing a skyline from scratch. The national statistics office's Q4 2025 release shows nearly 5.2 million guest arrivals in the quarter, hotels up 3.6%, and five-star properties posting the single largest gain of any category. Translation: the top of the Czech hotel market is doing very nicely, thank you, largely on the strength of buildings nobody had to build.

What nobody mentions in the brochure is the staff. Radio Prague International ran a first-person account from an American expat that reads like a field report from the front lines of Central European hospitality: a waiter visibly annoyed by a request to skip the pork, another who swapped out a lamb order for pork with zero warning, and a house style built entirely around "it is not possible." The author, fairly, traces this back to the state-owned, zero-competition service model of the communist era — nobody was fighting for your custom, so nobody developed the instinct to pretend they wanted it. That DNA hasn't fully left the building. It shows up at breakfast buffets, at reception desks, and occasionally in restaurant kitchens that have decided, unilaterally, what you're eating tonight.

The rental side runs on similarly brisk, no-nonsense logic. Prague rents sit close to Western European levels while local salaries do not, and expats.cz's own partner advice column is explicit that you shouldn't even enquire about a flat more than two months out, because landlords simply won't wait for you. Non-EU arrivals must register their address within days. Czech-speaking agents are less a convenience than a survival tool, because the good listings rarely make it to the English-language sites at all. It's a transactional culture, efficiently so — just don't expect anyone to apologise for the efficiency.

## The Verdict

If you want your hotel experience to feel aspirational — gleaming lobby, staff auditioning for warmth, a sense that the building itself is trying — book Poland. If you want your hotel experience to feel honest — gorgeous architecture, zero performance, a waiter who will tell you "no" and mean it — book Czechia. Neither is wrong. One is customer service as theatre, the other is customer service as a concept the staff have heard of but not personally endorsed.

For long-term accommodation, though, Poland wins on one unglamorous metric: speed of improvement. The flats behind those grim facades are getting better faster than Czech reception desks are getting friendlier. Czechia has the prettier buildings and the pricier five-star sector, no argument. But a beautiful building staffed by someone radiating quiet contempt is still, at the end of the day, a beautiful building with a mood problem. Poland's mood is improving. Czechia's is a personality trait at this point, and personality traits are notoriously resistant to renovation.

Winner: Poland, by a nose, purely on trajectory. Ask again in five years and Czechia might still be saying it's not possible.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com forum, Poland rental board — paraphrased: a user who'd been looking at flats in Kraków and Warsaw described decent central listings vanishing within a week of being posted, and said much of the available long-term stock sits in what they called "post-communist mega apartment complexes."*

> *Radio Prague International, first-person account — an American expat recounts a waiter growing visibly annoyed at a simple request to modify an order, and another swapping a lamb dish for pork without telling the table, tracing the "it is not possible" house style back to the no-competition service culture of the communist era.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Warsaw — a diplomatic spouse described their Warsaw apartment as "roomy with decent storage" when they arrived, only for it to feel "very cramped" by the time they left two years and two children later — and noted, with something like gratitude, that the brutal Polish winter at least kept the pest population at zero.*

## Conclusion

Here's the uncomfortable truth both countries would rather you didn't notice: the building was never the problem. Poland worked out the building and is still working out the people. Czechia worked out neither the people nor, in fairness, much reason to bother — the architecture does the selling for them, and it's sold for decades without help. If you're relocating for work, pick Poland and bring patience for the paperwork. If you're relocating for the view, pick Czechia and bring low expectations for the small talk. Either way, book fast, read the deposit clause twice, and stop calling any of this "exotic." It's bureaucracy with better architecture. I've seen sixty countries do worse with far less charm.
