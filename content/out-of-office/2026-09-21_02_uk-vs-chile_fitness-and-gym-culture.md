# One Nation Apologises Before It Lifts. The Other Doesn't Know What an Apology Is.

🇬🇧🇨🇱 By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office

The British gym is a masterclass in collective avoidance. Forty strangers, all sweating within four feet of one another, all performing an elaborate pantomime of not making eye contact, apologising to the squat rack as though it might file a complaint. The Chilean gym, by contrast, is loud, unbothered, and occasionally features someone's uncle doing bicep curls in jeans. I have sweated through both. Only one made me feel like I was intruding on a private, silent grief.

Britain treats exercise as a moral obligation performed with maximum embarrassment. Chile treats it as a social event that occasionally involves weights. Neither is wrong, exactly. But only one of them will actually talk to you.

## Do's & Don'ts: UK

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wipe down every machine, unprompted, like your life depends on it | Make small talk mid-set — it will be received as an act of hostility |
| Book classes online days in advance; spots vanish fast | Show up to a popular class five minutes late expecting entry |
| Bring your own towel, always, even to a "towel provided" gym | Assume the sauna will be warm; British saunas run suspiciously cool |
| Read the cancellation clause twice before signing a contract | Expect anyone to explain the cancellation clause to your face |

## Do's & Don'ts: Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say hello to the room; it's expected, not optional | Wear headphones so loud you miss someone greeting you |
| Negotiate the membership price — it's rarely fixed | Assume the quoted price is the price everyone else is paying |
| Use outdoor gym equipment in parks; it's genuinely popular | Expect air conditioning in most mid-range gyms |
| Bring exact change or a card for informal add-on classes | Rely on a gym's official hours; actual hours run looser |

## The UK: A Nation That Trains in Silence and Shame

British gym culture is built on a foundation of profound social anxiety, dressed up as politeness. You arrive, you make eye contact with precisely no one, you complete your circuit with the grim determination of someone serving a sentence, and you leave having said fewer than six words, most of them to the receptionist. This isn't coldness, exactly — it's a national commitment to not being a bother, extended to its most absurd possible conclusion: better to wait eleven minutes for a bench than ask the person using it how many sets they have left.

The upside, and there is one, is competence. British gyms run on rules everyone quietly agrees to follow: wipe the machine, don't hog the squat rack, don't film yourself for content in a space full of strangers who did not consent to being background extras in your fitness journey. The class booking systems are efficient to the point of ruthlessness — miss the 6am release window for a popular spin class and you're waiting a week, no exceptions, no pleading with the front desk, because the front desk has heard your plea a hundred times already this month and remains unmoved.

Where it curdles is in the sheer emotional labour of it all. The British gym-goer apologises for existing in physical space — sorry, just reaching past you, sorry, is this machine free, sorry, sorry — until the entire session becomes an extended exercise in conflict avoidance with dumbbells attached. You will get an excellent workout. You will not make a single friend. You may, after six months, be on nodding terms with the man who always uses the rowing machine at 7am, and this will feel, genuinely, like a significant relationship milestone.

## Chile: The Gym as Extended Family Gathering

Walk into a Santiago gym and the first thing that happens is someone says hola — the trainer, the guy racking weights, occasionally a stranger who has decided, unilaterally, that you look like you need encouragement. Chilean gym culture doesn't pretend fitness is a solitary, silent pursuit. It's social infrastructure. People chat between sets, offer unsolicited form corrections, and treat the whole enterprise with a warmth that would give a Londoner a small stroke.

This extends to the business side in ways that can genuinely trip up the unprepared foreigner. Membership pricing in Chile is often a starting position rather than a fixed number — ask, and you may well get a better rate, particularly if you're paying for several months upfront or joining with a friend. Hours posted on the door are, generously, a suggestion; the gym that "closes at 10pm" may quietly wind down at 9:40 if it's a slow Tuesday, and nobody will apologise for this the way a British establishment would over-apologise for a two-minute delay.

The outdoor culture deserves its own mention. Chile takes genuine, sustained advantage of its geography — public parks across Santiago and beyond are dotted with free outdoor gym equipment, used constantly, by everyone from teenagers to grandmothers doing tricep dips at dusk. It's unpretentious in a way British fitness culture, for all its efficient class booking systems, rarely manages. The trade-off is that "systems" in the British sense barely exist. Air conditioning is inconsistent, class schedules shift without notice, and the whole experience runs on a looser, warmer, more improvisational logic that will delight some expats and quietly drive the more type-A among us up the wall.

## The Verdict

If I want a workout that respects my time and my personal space, Britain wins, grudgingly. If I want to actually enjoy the forty-five minutes I spend there, Chile wins by a landslide. British gym culture optimises for efficiency and produces isolation as a side effect nobody asked for. Chilean gym culture optimises for warmth and produces the occasional scheduling chaos as the price of admission. I'd rather wait ten extra minutes for the treadmill while chatting to a stranger about the weather than get my workout done in eleven silent, hyper-efficient minutes and leave feeling like I've just completed a hostage negotiation. Sorry, Britain. Actually — no. I'm not sorry. That's the whole point.

## Conclusion

Fitness culture, it turns out, is just culture wearing gym shorts. Britain's rigid politeness and Chile's loud warmth aren't really about exercise at all — they're the same social operating systems that govern the pub and the asado, just relocated to a room with mirrors and mats. Go to Britain if you want your workout to run like clockwork and don't mind doing it entirely alone. Go to Chile if you want to be greeted, occasionally overcharged, and possibly adopted by the regulars before your third visit. Either way, bring your own towel. Some things, mercifully, translate everywhere.
