# Canada's Parks Might Have a Bear in Them. Korea's Parks Definitely Have a Chicken Delivery.

🇨🇦 Canada vs 🇰🇷 South Korea — Parks and Green Space Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Canada's relationship with green space is almost unnervingly casual given how much of it could, at any moment, contain something that wants to eat you — ravines and urban parks back directly onto genuine wilderness, coyote and bear warnings sit calmly beside the swings, and Canadians stroll through it all with the relaxed confidence of people who've simply decided the risk is part of the charm. South Korea's parks, particularly Seoul's Hangang riverside network, are a different species of green space entirely — meticulously landscaped, densely programmed, and functioning less like nature and more like the country's real living room, complete with delivery chicken arriving by scooter to your exact picnic mat coordinates.

I've been quietly warned about coyotes on a Toronto ravine trail and I've had fried chicken delivered to my precise spot on the Han River within twenty minutes of ordering. Both are, genuinely, park experiences. Only one required me to know my GPS coordinates.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the wildlife warning signs at trailheads — bear and coyote encounters are real, not decorative | Leave food unattended at a picnic site, even in a city park; wildlife here is genuinely opportunistic |
| Carry bear bells or make noise on more remote ravine trails, especially at dawn or dusk | Assume every green space is "tame" just because it's inside city limits — many Canadian urban parks border real wilderness corridors |
| Bring layers regardless of season; park weather shifts fast, especially near water | Approach any wildlife for a photo; the "respectful distance" rule exists because people keep ignoring it |

### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring or rent a picnic mat for the Han River parks; sitting directly on the grass isn't the norm | Forget to check the river park's designated zones — some areas ban alcohol or open flame grilling entirely |
| Order delivery directly to your riverside spot using the park's numbered zone markers | Assume all Seoul parks are as manicured as Hangang; smaller neighbourhood parks vary widely in upkeep |
| Join a group exercise class if you see one starting — many are informally open to anyone nearby | Play music loudly without headphones; shared quiet enjoyment is still the norm outside designated party zones |

## Canada: Wilderness Politely Interrupted by a City

Canadian parks operate on a premise that takes visitors from more manicured countries genuine time to internalise: the wilderness was here first, the city grew up around it, and the boundary between "park" and "actual forest with actual predators in it" is often more theoretical than physical. Toronto's ravine system, one of the largest urban forest networks on the continent, threads directly through residential neighbourhoods, and it's entirely normal to walk fifteen minutes from a coffee shop into forest dense enough to lose your phone signal and encounter a coyote warning sign that is not, in any way, decorative.

This proximity to real wilderness shapes an entire etiquette most Canadians absorb without ever being formally taught it: food gets packed away properly, dogs stay leashed on certain trail sections, and wildlife sightings get reported to municipal wildlife lines rather than approached for a better photo, because Canadians have collectively internalised that a coyote or black bear encounter is a "give it space and back away calmly" situation, not an Instagram opportunity. Newcomers who treat the warnings as tourist-board theatre tend to have exactly one incident before adjusting their behaviour permanently.

What nobody warns you about is how quiet these parks can be. Unlike the dense communal programming found elsewhere, a Canadian ravine trail on a weekday afternoon can feel almost eerily empty — genuine solitude, minutes from downtown, which is either the most restorative thing you'll experience abroad or a little unsettling if you're used to green space doubling as social space. Canada's parks are for retreating into. They are not, generally, for performing a picnic at.

## South Korea: The Park as Communal Living Room

Seoul's Hangang River parks represent the opposite philosophy entirely: green space here is dense civic infrastructure, engineered and programmed to be used constantly, communally, and with maximum convenience. The riverside parks stretching along both banks of the Han River are numbered into zones specifically so that food delivery — famously, actual fried chicken and beer delivered by scooter directly to your picnic spot — can find you precisely, a level of infrastructure-meets-leisure integration that no Canadian ravine trail would ever attempt or need.

Weekend afternoons transform these parks into a genuine cross-section of the city's social life: groups doing coordinated exercise routines, couples renting tandem bikes, families spread across picnic mats they specifically brought or rented because sitting directly on Korean park grass isn't really the cultural default, and street food vendors doing steady business alongside the delivery apps. This isn't wilderness adjacent to a city; it's the city's outdoor room, as intentionally designed and socially active as any indoor public space.

What nobody prepares you for is the sheer density of amenity. Public restrooms, exercise equipment, designated zones for different activities, even zones with specific rules about alcohol and open flame — Korean urban parks are managed with a level of granular planning that turns "going to the park" into something closer to visiting a well-run public facility than escaping into nature. It's a fundamentally different promise: not solitude, but a shared, well-organised good time, chicken included.

## The Verdict

Canada wins on genuine wildness — nowhere else gives you actual, unsimulated proximity to nature this close to a major city, coyotes and all. South Korea wins on communal design — nowhere else turns green space into such a seamlessly serviced social hub, complete with delivery infrastructure most countries haven't dreamed of. If you want a park that reminds you nature doesn't care about municipal boundaries, choose Canada. If you want a park that will bring you chicken exactly where you're sitting, choose South Korea. Just don't expect a coyote warning sign in Seoul, or a chicken delivery service in a Toronto ravine — you'll be disappointed on both counts, for opposite reasons.

## Conclusion

Canada's parks ask you to respect a wilderness that was never fully tamed, just politely fenced. Korea's parks ask you to enjoy a public space engineered down to the delivery zone for maximum communal comfort. I've backed slowly away from a coyote warning in one and ordered chimaek to a numbered riverside spot in the other, and both, somehow, count as a good afternoon outdoors. Just know which country you're in before you either approach the wildlife or forget to bring a picnic mat. Both mistakes are memorable. Neither is recommended.
