# Fiji Taxes You for Imported Cereal. Canada Just Wants You to Buy It in Bulk From Costco.

🇫🇯 Fiji 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Fiji will happily sell you a punnet of papaya for pocket change at the municipal market and then charge you the equivalent of a small ransom for a box of imported breakfast cereal twenty metres away. Canada has no such moral tension — it simply wants you to buy forty breakfast cereals at once from Costco, at a price that only makes sense if you have a basement freezer and a family of nine.

I've grocery-shopped on a tiny Pacific archipelago and across a country so large its grocery prices change meaningfully by province, and both experiences taught me the same unglamorous lesson: nowhere on earth is grocery shopping actually about food. It's about geography, shipping routes, and which specific inconvenience a country has decided to make your problem.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇫🇯 Fiji**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shop the municipal market for produce — fresher, cheaper, and the genuine social hub of grocery life | Assume supermarket prices reflect "island cheap" — imported packaged goods often cost more than back home |
| Budget seriously for imported cereal, cheese, and snacks — the import markup on these is real and persistent | Skip the market for convenience alone — you'll pay a noticeable premium for that shortcut every single time |
| Favour local staples — cassava, taro, and fresh local fish — over imported equivalents whenever possible | Expect deep, Western-style brand variety — supply chains mean specific products vanish for weeks at a stretch |

**🇨🇦 Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a Costco membership if you're staying more than a few months — it is a genuine national institution, not a niche habit | Assume rural stores stock what urban ones do — distances between full grocery stores can be significant outside cities |
| Stock up seriously before winter sets in — a "quick grocery run" means something different at minus twenty-five | Ignore how much grocery prices vary by province — there is no single, nationally uniform price for anything |
| Sign up for a loyalty program (PC Optimum, Scene+, and similar) — the points genuinely add up meaningfully over a year | Forget your own bags — plastic bag bans or fees are now the standard across most provinces, not the exception |

## Fiji: Beautiful Produce, Punishing Imports

The municipal market is where Fiji's grocery culture actually lives, and it is a genuinely wonderful place to shop: stacked with cassava, taro, breadfruit, and fish pulled from the water that morning, sold by vendors who will negotiate with you cheerfully and expect you to negotiate back. For anything grown or caught locally, Fiji is one of the most affordable places I've shopped, full stop. The betrayal arrives the moment you walk into an actual supermarket chasing something familiar — a specific brand of cereal, decent cheese, or packaged snacks — and discover that shipping an entire archipelago's packaged-goods supply across the Pacific is not a cheap undertaking, and that cost lands squarely on the shopper.

Fiji's own Consumer Council has documented exactly this pattern in recent reporting, with everyday grocery costs surging well into double digits on imported staples, a squeeze that hits hardest exactly where newcomers feel it first: the packaged, Western-adjacent comfort items they assumed would be a minor expense. Add in the very real disruption that follows any cyclone season — shipping delays that empty shelves of specific goods for weeks — and you get a grocery culture split cleanly in two: gorgeously cheap and fresh for anything local, startlingly expensive and unreliable for anything that had to arrive by container ship. The expats who adapt fastest are the ones who stop trying to recreate their home pantry and start cooking around what the market actually has that week.

## Canada: Bulk or Bust

Canada's grocery culture, by contrast, is built entirely around scale, distance, and the brutal practicality of a winter that makes small, frequent shopping trips genuinely unpleasant for months at a stretch. Costco isn't just popular here — industry surveys consistently rank it as the single most-shopped grocery destination in the country, a position earned by a national habit of buying in bulk that long predates any cost-of-living squeeze. A "normal" Canadian pantry stock-up can mean a flat of yogurt, a kilogram bag of frozen berries, and enough paper towels to survive a minor apocalypse, purchased in one trip specifically so the next trip can wait until the weather improves.

What surprises newcomers most is how unevenly priced groceries are across a country that feels, from the outside, like a single unified market. A litre of milk, a dozen eggs, a bag of the same-brand chips can cost meaningfully different amounts in Alberta versus Newfoundland versus the territories, shaped by transport distance, provincial taxation quirks, and how remote the community actually is — genuinely remote northern communities face food costs that would make a Fijian import tariff look modest by comparison. Add in province-by-province plastic bag bans, deposit-return bottle systems that differ by region, and a loyalty-points ecosystem (PC Optimum, Scene+, and a handful of bank-card tie-ins) that rewards patient, planned shopping over spontaneous trips, and you get a grocery culture that rewards exactly the kind of advance logistics Canadians apply to everything else about surviving their own winters.

## The Verdict

Fiji wins on pure quality-of-life-per-dollar for anything local — there is nothing in Canada's grocery experience that touches the joy of a Suva market morning, fresh fish in hand, vendor banter included free of charge. But Fiji's import-cost squeeze is a genuine, structural hardship, not a quirky inconvenience, and it falls hardest on residents who can't simply shrug and eat more cassava.

Canada wins on resilience and predictability — bulk-buying culture exists for sound practical reasons, and a Costco membership genuinely does pay for itself within a season for anyone settling in longer-term. But the provincial price variance and the sheer distances involved mean "going to the grocery store" carries a logistics tax Fiji's compact geography simply doesn't impose. Different scarcities, same underlying lesson: check what the land and the shipping routes have actually decided to make expensive before you build your weekly budget.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Suva forum — a new arrival described their first supermarket bill as "a genuine shock" after assuming island living would mean cheap groceries across the board, only to realise the surprise was entirely confined to the imported aisle.*

> *Internations Fiji — a longtime member recommended building an entire weekly menu around whatever the market has that morning rather than shopping with a fixed list, calling it "the single biggest adjustment, and eventually the best habit I picked up."*

> *Reddit r/PersonalFinanceCanada — a thread on Costco membership value broke down exactly how many trips it takes to recoup the annual fee, with most commenters agreeing the real savings only show up once you commit to genuinely bulk-sized planning rather than treating it like a regular supermarket.*

## Conclusion

Grocery shopping, it turns out, is one of the most honest windows into how a place actually works — not its postcard image, its real supply chains, its real distances, its real relationship with the rest of the world. Fiji will teach you to love what grows ten minutes from your kitchen and resent anything that had to cross an ocean to reach you. Canada will teach you to think in flats, cases, and seasons rather than meals. Pack a sense of humour about both, and in Canada's case, pack a second freezer.
