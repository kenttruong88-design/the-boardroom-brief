# Niamey Will Sell You a Supermarket Tomato at European Prices. Accra Will Just Triple the Price Because You're Foreign.

🇳🇪 Niger vs 🇬🇭 Ghana
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Niger is one of the poorest countries on earth by GDP per capita, and its supermarket prices do not appear to have gotten the memo — a monthly grocery budget runs around $90 against a one-bedroom rent of roughly $140, with imported goods priced in line with European supermarkets because the country has almost no food-processing industry of its own. Ghana, considerably wealthier and considerably better stocked, runs on an entirely different mechanism: haggling theater, where the first price quoted to a foreigner is assumed, by multiple independent accounts, to be roughly triple the real one.

One country prices you honestly and expensively. The other prices you dishonestly and leaves the real number for you to negotiate down yourself. I've shopped in sixty countries. These two broke my brain in genuinely new ways.

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇳🇪 Niger

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the traditional markets — Grand Marché, Petit Marché, Katako — for fresh produce at genuinely competitive prices | Expect mail-order relief — there's no reliable postal system, so packaged goods from home arrive on a diplomatic-style weekly shipment at best |
| Rinse all fresh fruit in a bleach solution before eating — this is routine embassy-level advice, not paranoia | Expect cards to work outside the largest supermarkets — this is overwhelmingly a cash economy |
| Budget for import-driven prices on anything "Western" — Score, Casino and Marina Market run close to European price levels | Assume poverty means cheap groceries across the board — the 2022 price shock saw a single clove of garlic rise from 100 to 250 CFA francs |

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Open negotiations at half the first quoted price and ask for the "last price," especially at the Arts Centre market | Accept the first price at face value — it's routinely marked up roughly triple for foreigners |
| Bring a Ga- or Twi-speaking local if you want the best outcome — one expat writer hit her target price about 90% of the time with a local negotiating | Expect every vendor to be hands-off — goods can get physically pressed into your hands, and photographing people without asking can provoke real anger |
| Use supermarkets (Shoprite, Palace, Melcom) for imported and packaged goods, and markets for fresh local produce | Carry only cards — many markets and even some sit-down restaurants don't take them, or the machine conveniently "isn't working" |

## Niger: Honest Prices, Dishonestly High

Niamey's grocery scene is defined less by trickery than by sheer import dependence. The country has virtually no domestic food-processing industry, so anything resembling the goods a relocating professional expects — recognizable brands, packaged staples, the comforts of home — has to be shipped in, and the price tag reflects every mile of that journey. One expat.com poster puts it plainly: prices run roughly in line with European supermarket levels, a genuinely strange outcome in one of the world's poorest countries by GDP per capita. A monthly grocery budget for an expat is estimated around $90, which sounds modest until you set it against a one-bedroom rent of roughly $140 — groceries aren't a minor line item here, they're a major proportional expense, closer to half your housing cost than the rounding error it is in most places.

The mechanics of actually shopping reinforce the sense that you're operating in a cash-first, trust-based system rather than a modern retail one. Supermarkets like Score, Casino, Marina Market and Minimarket Azar stock imported goods at prices that sting, but only the largest of them take credit cards at all — even some "farmers market" stalls take plastic before ordinary grocery shops do, a genuinely backwards hierarchy of payment modernity. There's no reliable postal system for mail-order relief either; expats lean on a weekly diplomatic-pouch-style DPO shipment and pack American staples — dish soap, mayonnaise, salad dressing — because local equivalents are either pricier or simply absent. And once you've secured your groceries, the advice from embassy-linked residents is uniform and specific: rinse all fresh fruit in a bleach solution before eating it, a routine precaution against stomach illness that nobody treats as optional.

Underneath the import economy sits the real, functioning dual-track system: traditional markets — the Grand Marché, Petit Marché, Marché Albarka, Katako — for fabric, spices, incense and fresh produce at genuinely competitive local prices, running in parallel to the supermarkets rather than competing with them. A 2022 inflation shock made the fragility of that local-price stability visible in real time: a single clove of garlic rose from 100 to 250 CFA francs, and a market shopper named Hadjia Hamsatou told a news outlet her usual 50,000 CFA budget "no longer buys the same products," with vendors blaming poor rains, the Sahel drought, COVID aftershocks and regional insecurity all at once. The lesson for a newcomer is that Niamey's prices aren't just high — they're volatile in a way that genuinely tracks regional instability, not just global supply chains.

## Ghana: A Theatrical, Triple-Priced Negotiation

Accra, by contrast, isn't short on supply — it has "plenty" of supermarkets stocking American, European and Middle Eastern products, per a 2025 diplomatic post-report, with recognizable names like Shoprite, Melcom, Palace and the newer Lulu Hypermarket all in reasonable reach. The complaint isn't availability; it's that prices run "a little at the high end," confirmed by actual Numbeo data showing a gallon of milk at $10.22 and a dozen eggs at $2.41 — genuinely steep numbers by regional standards, even as Ghana's overall food inflation has collapsed dramatically, falling to 4.9% in December 2025 from 27.8% a year earlier, a swing of nearly 23 percentage points in a single year.

Where Ghana really distinguishes itself is at the market stall, where shopping becomes an active, often theatrical negotiation rather than a passive price-accepting transaction. The opening price quoted to a foreigner is routinely assumed to be triple the real one, and the accepted countermove, per a market-survival blogger, is to open at half the first quoted figure and explicitly ask for the "last price" — a genuine verbal ritual with its own vocabulary. The outcome measurably depends on who's doing the negotiating: one expat writer reports that bringing along a Ga- or Twi-speaking local companion got her close to her target price roughly 90% of the time, a staggering difference that has nothing to do with the goods themselves and everything to do with who's standing next to you. The experience at the stalls can get physically immersive in a way that catches newcomers off guard — vendors pushing items directly into your hands, draping fabric over your shoulders mid-pitch — and photographing people without asking first can provoke real anger or a swatted-away camera, a boundary worth respecting before you reach for your phone.

The card-versus-cash divide adds its own quiet friction: large supermarkets accept cards readily, but markets and even some sit-down restaurants either don't take them or have card machines that conveniently fail at the worst moment, meaning cash remains non-negotiable even for the most "modern" shopping trip. Food still makes up roughly 43% of the average Ghanaian household budget, which puts the triple-price haggling tax in real context — this isn't a quirky cultural game, it's a meaningful cost that locals themselves are actively managing every single week.

## The Verdict

Niger's prices are high but at least they're the real number, stable in their dishonesty if nothing else. Ghana's prices are a starting bid in a negotiation you're expected to actually fight, and the fight itself is winnable if you show up with the right local ally. I'll take Accra's theater over Niamey's quiet, imported sticker shock — at least in Ghana, the price is a conversation, not a wall.

## Conclusion

Both countries will cost you more than you expected at the till, just through completely different mechanisms. Niger's grocery bill is honest and brutal, inflated by an import dependence no amount of charm will negotiate away. Ghana's grocery bill is a starting offer, inflated specifically because you're a foreigner, and entirely negotiable if you're willing to haggle and smart enough to bring backup. Pack bleach for Niamey's produce. Pack patience, and ideally a Twi-speaking friend, for Accra's markets.
