# One Country Treats Parks Like Church, The Other Treats Them Like a Waiting List

🇻🇳 Vietnam vs 🇩🇰 Denmark — Parks and Green Space Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Show me how a country uses its public parks and I'll tell you everything about its relationship with time, noise, and personal space, none of which it will admit to out loud. Vietnam treats green space as a communal stage that opens at dawn and closes the second the heat becomes unbearable. Denmark treats it as a scarce, quietly coveted resource you queue for, sometimes literally, sometimes for a decade.

I have done tai chi badly alongside seventy strangers in a Hanoi park at 5:45am, sweating through a t-shirt before the sun had fully committed to rising, and I have sat in a Copenhagen park so serene it felt like trespassing on someone's private thought. Both experiences taught me that "going outside" means wildly different things depending on which hemisphere's rulebook you're operating under.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Vietnam**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get to the lake or park by 6am if you want to see it at its best — and busiest | Expect solitude; Vietnamese parks are communal by design, not contemplative retreats |
| Join in — badminton, aerobics classes, ballroom dancing circles all welcome curious foreigners | Bring a picnic and expect a quiet patch of grass to yourself on a weekend evening |
| Bring cash for the inevitable street vendor selling coconut water or grilled corn at the gate | Wear headphones and miss the loudspeaker aerobics music — it's part of the experience, like it or not |

**Denmark**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for a kolonihave (allotment garden) early if you actually want one within your lifetime | Play loud music, shout, or otherwise disrupt the general hush that Danish parks operate under |
| Bring your own everything — blanket, food, drink — parks are BYO, not vendor territory | Assume a "public" park bench near a private garden colony is fair game; some feel proprietary |
| Cycle to the park; it's faster, expected, and how most Danes actually get there | Expect anyone to strike up conversation — Danish park culture is companionable silence, not chat |

## Vietnam: The Park As Town Square, Gym, and Social Contract

Hanoi's parks and lakeside promenades operate on a schedule so precise you could set a watch by it, if watches were still a thing anyone under sixty wore unironically. By 5:30am, Hoan Kiem Lake is ringed with people doing synchronized aerobics to music blasting from a portable speaker with the subtlety of a foghorn, elderly men playing badminton with a ferocity that suggests old scores being settled, and groups of women in matching outfits practicing fan dancing routines with the seriousness of Olympic athletes. By 9am, the heat has usually chased everyone indoors or under shade, and the park empties as decisively as it filled.

This isn't incidental — Vietnamese cities are dense, apartments are small, and the park is where the actual living happens: exercise, socializing, courtship, gossip, the works. Nobody goes to a Hanoi park for quiet contemplation, because quiet contemplation is not on offer and asking for it marks you as someone who has fundamentally misunderstood the assignment. Foreigners who show up expecting a leisurely stroll are usually swept, gently but firmly, into a stranger's aerobics routine within about four minutes, which is either the most charming thing that will happen to you all week or a minor personal crisis depending on your tolerance for spontaneous group activity before caffeine.

Green space itself is a genuine scarcity in Vietnam's booming cities — Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City have expanded so fast that parks are treasured, overcrowded, and fiercely used rather than admired from a distance. There's no Danish-style hush here, no unspoken rule about volume. The park is loud because life is loud, and the two are not considered separable. What it lacks in serenity it makes up for in the sheer, unpretentious joy of watching an entire neighborhood exercise, flirt, and argue about badminton scores in the same fifty square meters before the sun gets serious.

## Denmark: The Park As Sacred, Rationed Silence

Denmark, by staggering contrast, treats green space the way it treats most things: with quiet reverence, careful planning, and an underlying assumption that everyone else is also trying to enjoy this peacefully so please, for the love of Hygge, keep it down. Copenhagen is genuinely one of the greenest capitals in Europe, threaded with parks, lakes, and cycling paths that make car ownership look almost eccentric. But access to the good stuff — a proper allotment garden, a coveted kolonihave with your own little cottage and vegetable patch — often involves a waiting list measured in years, sometimes over a decade, inherited almost like property, occasionally passed down within families with the same gravity as an actual house.

The etiquette in a Danish park is its own quiet minefield for the uninitiated. Loud conversation, music without headphones, or anything resembling the exuberant chaos of a Hanoi lakeside will earn you looks that Danes have perfected into an art form — disapproval so understated it takes a full ten seconds to register as disapproval at all. Picnics are common, sociable in a contained way, and almost always self-sufficient: you bring your own blanket, your own wine, your own everything, because the idea of a vendor wandering through selling snacks would strike most Danes as a faint imposition on the collective calm.

What Denmark does exceptionally well is design: bike paths that actually connect to parks, lakes clean enough to swim in within the city center, green space woven into urban planning rather than squeezed in as an afterthought. What it asks of you in return is patience, restraint, and an instinctive understanding that shared space means shared silence, not shared noise — the opposite lesson Hanoi teaches, learned the hard way if you get it backwards.

## The Verdict

If you measure a park by how much life it contains per square meter, Vietnam wins in a landslide — nowhere else will you find quite so much unfiltered humanity crammed into a lakeside path before breakfast. If you measure a park by tranquility, design, and the sheer civic miracle of a clean urban lake, Denmark wins comfortably, assuming you can tolerate a silence so complete it occasionally feels judgmental.

The real difference is philosophical: Vietnam's parks are for doing things together, loudly, in public, without apology. Denmark's parks are for doing very little, quietly, in parallel, with excellent bike access. Pick whichever suits your personality, but don't try to bring Hanoi's speaker system to Copenhagen. You will be reported, politely, to absolutely no one, and simply frozen out socially for the rest of your natural life.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Vietnam forum — a new arrival described being pulled into a group aerobics session by a stranger at 6am before they'd even had coffee, and said it became the highlight of their week within a month.*

> *Internations Copenhagen — a member noted it took nearly nine years on a waiting list to get a kolonihave allotment, and that they were advised to add their child's name to a separate list the day they were born.*

> *Quora — someone asked why Hanoi felt "asleep" by mid-morning despite being loud at dawn, and the top answer simply said: "the heat wins, always, and everyone already knows it."*

## Conclusion

Vietnam and Denmark have both figured out, in opposite directions, that public space is a mirror for public values — one reflects a culture that lives loudly and communally because privacy is scarce, the other reflects a culture that guards quiet and order because it can afford to. Neither is wrong. But if you show up to a Copenhagen park with a boombox, or to a Hanoi lakeside expecting silence, you will learn the local customs the fastest and most humiliating way possible: by being the only person there confused about what everyone else already knew.
