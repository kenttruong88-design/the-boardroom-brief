# In Portugal the Supermarket Has a Loyalty Card. In Peru, the Market Has Your Life Story.

🇵🇹 Portugal vs 🇵🇪 Peru — Supermarket and Grocery Shopping Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Portugal will hand you a loyalty card within your first shop and expect lifelong fidelity in return, like a mildly possessive partner who tracks your every purchase of bacalhau. Peru will hand you nothing except unsolicited advice from a stranger about which potato you're clearly doing wrong, because there are apparently four thousand varieties and you've picked the worst one.

Both countries take food shopping seriously, which is refreshing after years of watching other nations treat it as a chore to be minimised. But where Portugal has industrialised the ritual into a tidy, card-scanning, self-checkout affair, Peru has kept it delightfully, chaotically human — a market where the vendor remembers your name before you've told them, and definitely remembers if you tried to lowball her on quinoa.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇵🇹 Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Sign up for the Continente or Pingo Doce loyalty card immediately — the discounts are real and substantial | Forget to weigh your own fruit and veg before checkout — unlabelled produce holds the queue hostage |
| Bring your own bags; plastic bags are taxed and mildly judged | Block the aisle chatting; Portuguese shoppers move with quiet efficiency |
| Shop small mercearias for bread and cheese even if you're doing the big shop elsewhere | Assume Sunday hours match weekday ones — many stores close early or entirely |

### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit the mercado for produce, meat, and fish rather than defaulting to supermarkets | Grab produce yourself without asking — vendors select and weigh for you, it's part of the service |
| Bring small bills and coins — mercado vendors rarely have change for large notes | Haggle aggressively over a few soles; gentle negotiation is fine, hard bargaining reads as rude |
| Greet the vendor before diving into business — a quick "buenos días" changes the whole interaction | Expect fixed opening hours everywhere; many stalls run on their own informal schedule |

## Portugal: Efficiency With a Loyalty Card Attached

Portuguese supermarket culture runs on two dominant chains — Continente and Pingo Doce — and choosing sides is treated with the mild tribal seriousness usually reserved for football clubs. Locals will happily tell you which one has better wine deals, which one's own-brand olive oil is worth the money, and which one to avoid on a Friday evening when it turns into a genuine contact sport.

The system itself is efficient to the point of coldness. You weigh your own fruit and vegetables at a self-service scale before reaching the till, and forgetting this single step will get you sent back by a cashier with a look of pure, weary disappointment — she has seen this mistake ten thousand times and will see it ten thousand more. Bagging is on you, plastic bags cost money and carry a faint whiff of environmental shame, and the whole experience moves at a brisk, no-nonsense clip that rewards people who know what they're doing and mildly punishes people who don't.

What redeems Portuguese grocery shopping from pure supermarket sterility is the mercearia — the small neighbourhood shop that refuses to die despite decades of chain-store pressure. Here, buying bread or cheese becomes a genuine daily social ritual, the shopkeeper knows your order before you say it, and there's a warmth entirely absent from the fluorescent efficiency of Continente's self-checkout lanes. Most Portuguese households run a hybrid system: big weekly shop at the supermarket, daily bread and pastries from the local padaria, because apparently even the most streamlined society in Western Europe can't quite let go of fresh bread bought in person.

Sunday and holiday hours remain stubbornly, charmingly Catholic — plenty of stores close early or entirely, a fact that catches out every newly arrived expat exactly once, usually on a Sunday evening with an empty fridge and a rising sense of panic.

## Peru: The Market Knows Your Name

If Portugal has streamlined grocery shopping into an efficient transaction, Peru has kept it as what it actually is: a relationship. The mercado — and Lima's are legendary, Surquillo chief among them — is where real grocery shopping happens, and supermarkets like Wong or Plaza Vea are treated as a backup for packaged goods rather than a genuine source of fresh produce.

The etiquette here rewards patience and a bit of social nerve. You don't grab your own fruit — you tell the vendor what you want, she selects it, weighs it, and often throws in a little extra "de yapa," a small gift on top of your purchase that exists purely as a gesture of goodwill. Skip the greeting and dive straight into business, and you'll get served, technically, but without the warmth that makes the whole experience worthwhile. Say "buenos días" first, ask how she's doing, and suddenly you're getting the good avocados, the ones not on display.

Bargaining exists but operates on a completely different register than the aggressive haggling tourists expect from South American markets. A little gentle negotiation on a large purchase is normal; hard bargaining over small change reads as insulting, especially once the vendor has decided she likes you, at which point continuing to argue over a sol or two becomes its own small social failure.

The sheer variety on offer humbles anyone who thought they understood potatoes — Peru grows several thousand native varieties, and a good mercado vendor will steer you toward exactly the right one for your dish with a confidence that borders on personal offence if you try to overrule her. Peruvian grocery shopping isn't a chore. It's an ongoing, mildly theatrical negotiation with people who take food more seriously than most countries take their constitutions.

## The Verdict

Portugal gives you a clean, efficient, loyalty-card-optimised system that respects your time and mildly judges your bagging technique. Peru gives you a market vendor who will remember your name, upgrade your avocados, and throw in a free lime because she likes you. If you want groceries handled like a transaction, Portugal delivers flawlessly. If you want groceries handled like a relationship, Peru wins by a landslide. I've never once been given a free lime at Continente. That tells you everything.

## Conclusion

Both countries will feed you well, but only one of them will make you feel like a regular at a place that knows your name within a fortnight. Portugal's system is faster, tidier, and entirely indifferent to you as a person. Peru's is slower, occasionally chaotic, and treats every transaction as the start of a small friendship. Choose your grocery run based on whether you want efficiency or an actual relationship with the woman who sells your onions. I know which one made me a better cook.
