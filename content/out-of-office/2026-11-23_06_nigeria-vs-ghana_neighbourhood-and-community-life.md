# Lagos Builds a Wall; Accra Builds a Fence Short Enough to Gossip Over

🇳🇬 Nigeria vs 🇬🇭 Ghana

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a standing joke between Nigerians and Ghanaians that the two countries are brothers who have never quite stopped arguing about the rice. Jollof is the casus belli, naturally, but the real disagreement is about how to live next to other people. One side has a historic and wholly rational belief that a good neighbourhood begins with a high wall. The other thinks a good neighbourhood begins with a short fence and an open door, and that anyone who needs more than that is up to something.

I have lived beside both and I can report that each approach has the same underlying logic: you arrange your surroundings to match what you expect from the people around them. Lagos expects the worst and hopes for the best. Accra expects the best and tends to get it. It is not that simple, and the differences are partly the product of money, history and the particular pressures of a megacity of twenty million people. But the fences tell you most of what you need to know.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇳🇬 Nigeria ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Choose a gated estate or a secure compound | Assume a quiet street is a safe street |
| Greet neighbours properly and build the relationship over time | Expect strangers to drop by unannounced |
| Keep a generator and a plan for power cuts | Rely on the grid alone |
| Attend the weddings and the big family occasions | Skip an invitation because it will run late |

| 🇬🇭 Ghana ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet people on the street and take your time over it | Rush a conversation to get to the point |
| Learn that many streets in Accra have no names and ask for landmarks | Expect a postcode-and-street address to work |
| Go to the funeral if you are invited, because it is the big occasion | Treat a funeral as a private family affair |
| Keep your boundary lines low and your manners high | Build a wall that says you do not trust the street |

## 🇳🇬 Nigeria

Nigerian neighbourhood life is, to put it kindly, an exercise in managed intensity. The country is large, loud and entrepreneurial, and its cities are built on the premise that you will have to look after yourself. In Lagos, that tends to mean high walls, gates, a security guard and a generator for when the grid inevitably goes dark. Deborah Dzifa Makafui, a Ghanaian who grew up in Lagos and wrote about the contrast for Face2Face Africa, says it plainly: Ghana's boundary fences are short, where Lagos fences are tall for security, and a short fence in Lagos would be read as an invitation to trouble. She also notes that Nigerian bank entrances are protected by entry systems after armed robberies, where Ghanaian ones are open.

That is the unromantic reason for the walls, and I do not think it is paranoia. It is a response to a system in which the state cannot always be relied on to provide light, water and safety, and so the household provides them. The social consequence is a certain withdrawal from the street. Neighbours may know each other well by reputation and not at all by sight. Community life is thick, but it lives behind the gate: in the estate association, the family compound, the church, the mosque and the weddings. Makafui mentions that in Nigeria weddings are the big event, with the aso ebi uniforms that tie a party together, where in Ghana funerals tend to be.

There is real warmth in this world. A Nigerian neighbour will arrive with food, a favour and an opinion within a week of your moving in. The compound is a village in miniature, and the estate chairman is a political figure of some consequence. It is simply that the intimacy is curated: you are invited in, and you do not wander in. For an expat, that means the first month can feel isolating, and the second month can feel suffocating, because everybody suddenly knows what you eat.

The pace is the other thing. Lagos moves fast and loudly, and the neighbourhood reflects it. You will be woken by generators and street sellers and the extraordinary acoustic confidence of a bus conductor. It is exhausting, and it is alive, and if you grew up in a quieter place you will take a season to adjust.

## 🇬🇭 Ghana

Ghana is calmer on the surface and, in my experience, no less social underneath. Makafui describes the Ghanaian preference for shorter fences, and the writer Kola Muhammed, in a Pulse Nigeria piece about two weeks in Accra, notes a steadier power supply, with just two outages in his stay, and a less hurried air. He also mentions that Ghanaians stretch words for emphasis, that bus conductors are called mates and that commercial buses are modest eighteen-seaters. Many Accra streets, Makafui adds, lack names altogether, which is a practical difficulty for visitors and a reminder that local knowledge is the real postal system.

The community life, in that setting, is more public. You greet people. You greet them properly, and slowly, and ask after the family. The funeral is the great social institution: Makafui says funerals are the big events in Ghana, in the way weddings are in Nigeria. They draw whole neighbourhoods, and the social obligation to attend is serious. If you are invited, go. It is one of the clearest windows into how a Ghanaian community actually works.

A Nigerian writer, Humphrey Onyeukwu, describes Ghanaians in a 2020 piece for Heirs Holdings as "noisy neighbours" in football and friendly in person, praising the street-level optimism about the country. His prices are dated, so I will not quote them, but the mood he describes, of a place where people seem pleased with their own prospects, matches what I have seen. There is a quiet national confidence in Accra that takes some of the aggression out of the street.

The caveat, which I think is fair, is that these are impressions from visitors and writers, not survey data. Accra is a city of millions with its own tensions and inequalities, and a short fence is no guarantee of safety. But the cultural default, of a welcome that is spoken aloud and a street that is meant to be walked on, is real. As an expat you will find people want to chat, and that the speed of the conversation is a feature, not a delay.

## The Verdict

Ghana wins for the neighbourhood and Nigeria wins for the party, and I would live in Accra and holiday in Lagos. A short fence is a pleasure, and the willingness to leave your gate open, or at least not armed, is worth more than a good postal code. Ghana's slower pace and its easy, public sociability make settling in simpler than anywhere I have lived in West Africa.

Nigeria asks more and gives more in return, especially if you are the sort of person who likes a crowd, a wedding with two thousand guests and a neighbour who will fix your generator at midnight. But the walls are real, and the price of the energy is paid daily. If your priority is a quiet, easy first year, take Ghana. If your priority is a story, take Nigeria and bring a very good gate.

## Conclusion

Lagos will put a wall between you and the street and then invite you to the party. Accra will leave the fence low and ask why you are standing outside. Both are communities, and both are intensely social. They simply keep the front door in different places. Learn the greetings, go to the funeral or the wedding as the local custom demands, and understand that the height of the fence is a clue to the history, not an insult to you.
