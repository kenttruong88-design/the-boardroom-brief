# Green Space Is a Luxury Good in One of These Countries, and a Birthright in the Other

🇪🇬 Egypt vs 🇮🇳 India — Parks and Green Space Culture
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Let's dispense with the postcard fiction immediately: neither Cairo nor Delhi is a garden city, and anyone who tells you otherwise is trying to sell you a flat. What both cities share is twenty-million-plus populations stacked on top of infrastructure built for a fraction of that number. What they do not share is how each one has responded to the resulting scarcity of anything green, and that difference tells you more about each country's relationship with public life than any museum ever will.

I have lived in both. I have jogged past armed guards outside a "public" garden in Cairo that required a ticket, a bag search, and a small existential crisis to enter. I have also sat in a Delhi park at 6 a.m. surrounded by four hundred strangers doing laughter yoga with the sincerity of a religious revival. These are not comparable experiences, and I refuse to pretend they are for the sake of a tidy thesis.

## Do's & Don'ts: Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat Al-Azhar Park as a genuine destination worth the entry fee | Expect your neighbourhood to have anything resembling it |
| Go to the Nile Corniche in the evening, when the city exhales | Assume "park" on a map means grass, shade, or benches |
| Bring your own water and shade — infrastructure will not provide either | Picnic on a traffic median and call it "green space," even if locals do |

## Do's & Don'ts: India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to a colonial-era garden (Lodhi, Cubbon, Victoria Memorial grounds) before 8 a.m. | Expect the same calm by 10 a.m. — it becomes a cricket pitch, a wedding photoshoot, and a protest simultaneously |
| Respect the unofficial "sections" — walkers, yoga groups, courting teenagers, elderly card players — each has staked a claim | Jog through the middle of a group meditation session; you will be glared at in at least three languages |
| Appreciate that entry is usually free or near-free | Assume free means unmaintained — many municipal gardens are lovingly, obsessively tended by retirees who've appointed themselves groundskeepers |

## Egypt: Green as an Amenity, Not a Right

Cairo has, depending on which report you trust and how generously it counts a decorative traffic roundabout, somewhere around half a square metre of green space per resident. The World Health Organization suggests nine. This is not a rounding error; it is a civilisational choice, made under decades of population pressure, desert geography, and urban planning that treated parks as an afterthought behind housing, roads, and whatever military-adjacent development happened to be fashionable that decade.

What green space exists tends to be gated, ticketed, or attached to something else — a club, a compound, a hotel. Al-Azhar Park, built on a former rubbish dump with Aga Khan money, is spectacular precisely because it is such an outlier: manicured, shaded, genuinely beautiful, and priced (modestly, but deliberately) to keep it that way. It is less a public park than a very good argument for what Cairo could be if anyone had the land and the will. Everywhere else, "green" means whatever survives on a highway median or in the walled garden of a compound you need a resident card to enter.

Expats adjust by either paying for access — gym memberships with rooftop space, compounds with private gardens, the Gezira Club if you can wrangle membership — or by giving up on the concept entirely and doing their unwinding indoors, which is its own quiet tragedy. The Nile Corniche is the great equaliser: free, breezy, chaotic, and the closest Cairo gets to democratic green-adjacent space, even though it's mostly concrete. Egyptians treat it the way other cities treat parks, because it's the best they've got, and I have enormous respect for a population that has made peace with that and still shows up every evening to enjoy what little air movement the Nile provides.

## India: Green as Contested, Beloved, Chaotic Commons

India's relationship with public parks is the opposite kind of intense. Where Cairo's green spaces are scarce and gatekept, India's are scarce and fought over — by joggers, cricket teams, wedding parties, political rallies, stray dogs, cows (yes, still, in places), and the aforementioned laughter yoga contingent, all sharing the same patch of grass with a determination that borders on the spiritual.

Delhi's Lodhi Garden or Bangalore's Cubbon Park function as genuine social infrastructure — free, walkable, used by every income bracket simultaneously, which is more than can be said for most public amenities in a country this stratified. The retired uncles who arrive at dawn to power-walk in tracksuits older than some nations have unofficially appointed themselves the parks' moral custodians, and heaven help you if you let your dog off the lead near their walking loop.

The catch is maintenance, which ranges from "lovingly obsessive" (see: those same uncles, who will personally scold litterers) to "essentially none," depending entirely on which municipality you've landed in and how recently an election happened. Expats new to India are often startled by how full parks are — genuinely full, at nearly every hour that isn't monsoon season or 45-degree noon — because unlike Cairo, there is no cultural expectation that green space is a private amenity. It's a commons, defended fiercely and used relentlessly, which makes it wonderful and occasionally means you cannot find a bench.

## The Verdict

If you want green space that looks nice in photographs and requires a ticket to enjoy, Egypt has quietly perfected the boutique park as luxury good — Al-Azhar is genuinely lovely, and genuinely an anomaly. If you want green space that is loud, crowded, free, occasionally cow-adjacent, and used with a fervour that makes European park etiquette look positively catatonic, India wins by a landslide. My honest allegiance is with India here, not because the parks are objectively better maintained — they often aren't — but because a park that's fought over by four generations at 6 a.m. is doing its actual job. A park you need a bag search to enter is doing something else entirely, and it isn't democracy.

## Conclusion

Neither country solved the problem of not having enough land for the people who live on it. Egypt responded by privatising the good bits and calling it aspiration. India responded by cramming everyone into the same shrinking commons and calling it community. Both are coping mechanisms dressed up as culture, and both, infuriatingly, kind of work. If you're relocating and green space matters to your sanity, budget for a club membership in Cairo and a pair of noise-cancelling headphones in Delhi — you'll need one or the other, possibly both, and no amount of Instagram scrolling through either country's tourism board will prepare you for which.
