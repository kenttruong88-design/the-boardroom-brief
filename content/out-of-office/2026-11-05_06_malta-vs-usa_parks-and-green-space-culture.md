# One Country's "Park" Is the Size of Your Living Room. The Other's Problem Is That You're Not Allowed to Enjoy Yours.

🇲🇹🇺🇸 Malta and the USA, reviewed by a woman who has measured her satisfaction with a country by how far she has to walk to sit on grass

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Malta has, officially, on its own government's books, over two hundred "parks." Eighty percent of them are smaller than a five-a-side football pitch, and a handful are, I am not exaggerating because the report itself isn't, roughly the size of a three-bedroom apartment. Meanwhile America has more park acreage than practically anywhere on Earth, a record-breaking $12.2 billion spent on park systems in a single year, and a Homeowners Association three streets over that will fine you for owning a gas grill. One nation doesn't have enough green space to argue about. The other has plenty, and has turned the argument into a hobby.

## Do's & Don'ts: Malta

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Recalibrate your definition of "park" immediately — most of the 200+ on the government's own list are glorified playgrounds, not green expanses | Expect a five-minute walk to greenery — only 22% of Maltese get that, versus half of Europeans overall |
| Lean into festa season instead — late May through September, the street itself becomes the shared public space, fireworks included | Assume Malta's lack of parkland means a lack of community outdoor life — it just moved the ritual to the piazza |
| Head to Chadwick Lakes or Buskett if you want genuine acreage — they're the rare exceptions, not the rule | Compare Malta unfavourably to larger EU neighbours without the context — it ranks dead last in the EU on easy green-space access, by a wide margin |

## Do's & Don'ts: USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |

## Malta: The Street Is the Park, Because There Isn't One

Let's start with the number that should embarrass a country roughly the size of a mid-sized US county: just 51% of Maltese say access to green space is easy, the single lowest figure anywhere in the EU, where the broader average sits above 75% and Denmark hits 100%. Twenty-one percent of Maltese walk more than thirty minutes to reach any green space at all — the highest share in Europe, against an EU average of just 3%. Only 22% live within a five-minute walk of green space, compared with half of Europeans generally; only Romania, at 16%, does worse. For a hot, dry island, even Cyprus manages 42%.

And it gets more specific, and funnier, the closer you look. An analysis of the Maltese environment ministry's own parks database found that of 217 sites analysed, 80% are smaller than a standard football pitch, nearly a third are roughly five-a-side-pitch sized, and a handful are, per the report's own blunt conclusion, about the size of a three-bedroom apartment. Collectively, all of Malta's officially designated parks cover just 13.9% of the islands' land area. Most are run hyper-locally by individual town councils — of which there are a great many, packed onto a genuinely tiny landmass — while the handful of larger sites, Chadwick Lakes, Buskett, Xrobb l-Għaġin, fall under national bodies like Project Green and Ambjent Malta. The honest editorial conclusion of the Maltese press itself: most things on the government's own "park" list are really small playgrounds with amenities, not parks in any conventional sense.

What Malta does instead, brilliantly, is reroute the entire human need for shared outdoor community life into something that doesn't require acreage at all: the village festa. Running roughly late May through September, with nearly every weekend claimed by some town's patron-saint celebration somewhere on the islands, residents repaint and redecorate their homes in advance in their parish's specific festoon colours — a visible, genuinely competitive display of local allegiance. Main streets fill with banners, papier-mâché statues mounted on columns, lit festoons strung across balconies and rooftops, flags on both public and private buildings. A typical festa runs three-plus days and closes most nights with fireworks — both ground and aerial — plus firecrackers, alongside stalls selling Maltese nougat and sweets. This is, functionally, Malta's substitute park experience: when you don't have the grass, you make the street itself the commons, decorate it accordingly, and set it on fire at the end as a celebration rather than a protest. I find this an entirely reasonable trade.

## The USA: Abundant Green Space, Heavily Regulated Enjoyment

America's park numbers are, by comparison, almost obscene in their generosity. Seventy-six percent of Americans now live within a ten-minute walk of a park, the highest share since tracking began in 2012, underwritten by a record $12.2 billion spent on park and recreation systems in 2024 alone — the Trust for Public Land's CEO flags, soberly, that much of that spending leans on pandemic-era federal infrastructure money that may not be replaced, which is the kind of caveat that should worry anyone enjoying the current abundance. Washington, D.C. topped the 2025 ParkScore rankings; Irvine, California jumped to second, credited to its sprawling "Great Park" megaproject; Minneapolis, St. Paul, Denver and Cincinnati rounded out a competitive top ten. National parks alone pulled 323 million recreation visits in 2025, a slight dip from 2024's record year but still enough to set 26 individual site records, led by the Blue Ridge Parkway at 16.5 million visits and Golden Gate's National Recreation Area at 15.7 million.

The dog park has, delightfully, become its own tracked municipal subcategory, with real per-capita leaderboards: Boise, Idaho leads the country at 7.6 dog parks per 100,000 residents, followed by Portland and Henderson, Nevada — a concept with, as far as I can tell, no Maltese equivalent whatsoever, because Malta is still working out where to put a bench.

And then there's the catch, and it's a genuinely good one: having abundant personal green space in America does not mean you're free to use it as you like. Real, multi-year Homeowners Association forum discussions document an astonishing range of restrictions — one community originally permitted flying only the American flag, until a state law forced a change after roughly four years of internal lobbying. Across HOA communities broadly: no parking on the lawn, no car washing or oil changes in the driveway, no gas or charcoal grills, no yard sales, balcony furniture restricted strictly to "chairs and tables," trash bins allowed out only on pickup morning, clotheslines banned outright, children's toys banned from visible decks. Tree removal requires sign-off. This is the real, under-discussed punchline of American green-space culture: the problem was never supply. The problem is a committee of your neighbours deciding, by vote, what you're allowed to do with the supply you already have.

## The Verdict

Malta loses on raw acreage so comprehensively that comparison feels almost unfair, but wins, decisively, on honesty about what it's doing with the alternative: it knows it doesn't have parks, so it built an entire multi-month festival calendar instead of pretending a playground counts as green space. America has the grass, the acreage, the record-breaking budgets and the dog parks — and then hands the whole thing over to a volunteer board that will fine you for a clothesline. If I'm choosing which failure mode to live inside, I'll take Malta's honest scarcity over America's abundance-with-an-asterisk every time. At least nobody in Valletta is going to cite me for leaving a kayak visible on my own balcony.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *BritishExpats.com, USA HOA thread — paraphrased: a poster describes a homeowners association that, for years, permitted flying only the American flag, until a state law forced a change — a process that took roughly four years to resolve.*

> *BritishExpats.com, USA HOA thread, continued — paraphrased: multiple independent posters confirm the same pattern of bans — no gas or charcoal grills, no yard sales, clotheslines forbidden outright, trash bins only out on collection morning — describing it as simply how suburban life there works, not an exception.*

> *MaltaToday, reporting EU survey data (used here in place of an unreachable forum thread, as disclosed above) — the striking real sentiment is structural, not anecdotal: roughly half of Maltese respondents say access to green space is difficult, the worst self-reported figure anywhere in the EU.*

## Conclusion

Go to Malta for the festa, not the foliage — you'll get more genuine community out of a decorated street corner there than you will out of most American parks, which, however green, are frequently empty because everyone's inside arguing with their HOA about a grill. Go to America for the acreage, but read the covenant before you unpack. Both countries will teach you that "green space" was never really about the grass. It's about who gets to decide what happens on it, and neither answer is as straightforward as the real estate listing implied.
