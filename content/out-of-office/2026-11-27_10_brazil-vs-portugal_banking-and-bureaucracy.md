# Brazil Invented Pix and Portugal Invented the Wait

🇧🇷 Brazil vs 🇵🇹 Portugal

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Brazil and Portugal share a language, a history and a delightful capacity to turn a simple form into an epic. They disagree on almost everything else, including what a bank is for, how long a stamp should take, and whether a document counts if it was printed on Tuesday.

A Brazilian moving to Portugal expects to feel at home. The words are familiar. The coffee is better. The bureaucracy, however, speaks a dialect of its own, and every newcomer has to learn it, usually in a queue.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇧🇷 Brazil ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep your CPF number to hand, because everything asks for it | Try to open an account or buy anything big without it |
| Use Pix for payments and transfers | Carry a pile of cash when a QR code will do |
| Expect to visit a cartório for notarised documents | Assume a photocopy will do |
| Use a despachante for paperwork you cannot face | Spend three days doing it yourself out of pride |

| 🇵🇹 Portugal ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your NIF tax number first, because it opens every door | Look for a flat, a phone or a bank account without one |
| Book appointments online and arrive with extra copies | Turn up unannounced and hope for sympathy |
| Learn the MB Way and Multibanco systems | Assume your Brazilian bank card works for everything |
| Be patient and polite with the clerk | Raise your voice to speed things up |

## 🇧🇷 Brazil

Brazilian finance is surprisingly modern and defiantly complicated. On the modern side there is Pix, the instant payment system launched by the central bank, which moves money between accounts in seconds, for free or very little, at any hour. People use it for everything from rent to street snacks, and a market vendor with a printed QR code is a more common sight than a card machine. Digital banks have exploded, with apps that open accounts in minutes and customers who treat a traditional branch as a historical curiosity.

On the complicated side, there is the paperwork. The CPF, the personal taxpayer number, is the key to almost everything. Contracts, loans, online shopping and even some loyalty cards ask for it. Notarisation sits in the cartórios, offices that authenticate signatures, copies and certificates, and they are a national institution with their own rituals and fees. There is a whole profession, the despachante, for people who handle paperwork on behalf of others, and nobody sees it as an indulgence.

Brazilian bureaucracy is famous for requiring documents in a specific order, each with its own stamp, and it has produced a population of remarkable patience and equally remarkable workaround skills. The jeitinho, the knack for finding a way around, is a cultural trait and a survival mechanism. A Brazilian knows that a polite request, a friendly face and a well-timed joke often achieve more than the rulebook.

The result is a country that is quick on the money side and slow on the paper side, with a population that has learned to live with both.

The cultural attitude to queues and officials adds another layer. In Brazil, public service has a reputation for rigid rules and informal exceptions, and a good relationship with a particular clerk can be worth more than a binder of documents. In Portugal, rules are applied more evenly, but the pace is calm and exceptions are rare. Neither is better, but a Brazilian arriving in Portugal must learn that charm helps and cannot replace the paper.

## 🇵🇹 Portugal

Portugal's financial infrastructure is elegant in places. Multibanco, the national ATM and payment network, is superb. MB Way, the mobile payment service, lets you pay friends, shops and bills from your phone. Cards are accepted nearly everywhere, and the system is dependable.

The challenge is getting into it. To rent a home, open a bank account, sign a phone contract or even buy a decent television on credit, you need a NIF, the tax identification number issued by the tax authority. Newcomers often get one at a tax office, and some use a fiscal representative. Banks then ask for proof of address, proof of income and a residence document, which brings in the immigration agency, and that is where the real adventure begins.

Appointments are slow. Offices may require bookings made weeks in advance, systems fail, and documents that were fine last month are now missing a stamp. The Loja do Cidadão, a one-stop citizen service centre, helps, and online services have improved. But a newcomer still needs patience, copies of everything, and a sense of humour.

Interaction with staff is formal and courteous. Greet people, explain calmly and say thank you at the end. A raised voice rarely accelerates anything, and a pleasant manner is surprisingly effective. Portuguese clerks are not the enemy. They are people operating inside a system they did not design, and most will help you once you stop treating them as the obstacle.

Portugal's recent years have brought changes. Online portals for tax and citizen services have improved, and the government has invested in digital tools, but the immigration system in particular has been under strain and processing is slow. The practical advice is to check the current rules on official sites, keep copies of every submission, and avoid paying anyone who promises to jump the queue. A good lawyer or accredited adviser is worth the money. A stranger on social media is not.

## The Verdict

Brazil wins on payments and Portugal wins on stability. Pix is better than anything Portugal has, and I say that with love for the Multibanco machine. If you want to move money quickly, the Brazilian system is a joy. If you want a system that mostly does what it says and does not collapse when the server is busy, Portugal is the calmer choice.

On bureaucracy, it is a draw, and the prize is exhaustion. Both countries love a stamp, a copy and a queue. The difference is that Brazilians have built a culture of workarounds and Portuguese have built a culture of patience. Take a Brazilian's resourcefulness, add a Portuguese clerk's courtesy, and you have the perfect expat. Failing that, take a folder, a pen and a sandwich.

Banking culture differs too. In Brazil, you may use many banks and apps at once, and fees are a constant topic. In Portugal, one bank is usually enough, but account opening can require a chain of documents, and fees are modest. Branches are still used, and a friendly conversation with a manager can solve a problem that an app cannot.

For anyone planning the move, a sensible order of operations is worth writing down. Get the Portuguese tax number first, arrange proof of address second, open the bank account third, then tackle the residence paperwork with every document in a folder and copies of the copies. Keep your Brazilian CPF active as well, because you will need it for tax, banking and family affairs back home. Nothing about this is exciting, which is exactly why people get it wrong.

## Conclusion

A shared language does not mean a shared system. Brazilians arriving in Portugal discover that they speak the same words and use them to describe different procedures. The reward is a country that is safe, handsome and well run once you are inside it.

So prepare. Get the NIF, keep the folder, learn the names of the offices and be kind to the person behind the glass. And if you ever feel like giving up, remember that somebody, somewhere, has already stood in the same queue and survived, and probably wrote a blog about it.

Be patient. Be prepared. And make friends with the person at the desk, since that relationship will outlast any form.
