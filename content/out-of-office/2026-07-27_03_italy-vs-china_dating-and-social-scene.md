# One Country Dates Through Its Mother. The Other Dates Through a Spreadsheet.

🇮🇹 Italy vs 🇨🇳 China — Dating and Social Scene

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Italy, dating someone means, eventually and inevitably, dating their entire family — the nonna who studies you like a suspicious cut of meat, the uncles with opinions, the childhood friends who all get a vote. In China, dating someone in a major city increasingly means being quietly cross-referenced against a checklist of salary, property ownership, hukou registration, and height, sometimes literally on a laminated profile pinned to a folding umbrella in a public park by a parent who has taken the matter entirely into their own hands.

Both approaches treat romance as something too important to leave purely to the two people involved, which will horrify anyone raised on the Anglophone fantasy of dating as a private, individual pursuit. I've watched an Italian grandmother interrogate a date over four courses without once breaking eye contact, and I've watched Shanghai parents haggle over their adult children's marriage prospects in People's Park with the calm efficiency of antiques dealers. Neither culture is going to apologise for it.

## Do's & Don'ts: Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to meet the family relatively early and take it seriously | Rush a relationship — courtship is meant to be lingered over |
| Dress properly for a date, even a casual one — effort is expected | Show up underdressed to dinner; it reads as disinterest |
| Let meals run long; dating happens over hours, not quick coffees | Split the bill on a first date without at least offering to cover it |
| Be prepared for loud, opinionated involvement from friends and family | Assume privacy — everyone will know about your relationship quickly |

## Do's & Don'ts: China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Be upfront about intentions — ambiguity is not appreciated | Joke about marriage or long-term plans lightly; it's taken seriously |
| Expect questions about salary, housing, and family background early | Be surprised if parents are directly involved in vetting a partner |
| Use established apps like Tantan or WeChat groups to meet people | Assume Western dating app norms (casual swiping) translate directly |
| Take gift-giving and thoughtful gestures seriously | Show up empty-handed to a first meeting with a partner's family |

## Italy: Romance as Theatre, Family as Chorus

Italian dating is, structurally, a performance with an audience, and the audience has opinions it will absolutely share. Courtship still carries real ceremony — the drawn-out dinners, the deliberate dressing up even for a casual aperitivo, the sense that a date is an event rather than a transaction to be optimised. Italians will happily spend three hours over a meal getting to know someone, which either sounds romantic or sounds like a hostage situation depending on your tolerance for eye contact.

The family involvement is not incidental — it's central. An Italian partner isn't just dating you; they're auditioning you for a permanent role in an extended, opinionated ensemble cast that includes parents, siblings, cousins, and at least one elderly relative who will ask pointed questions about your intentions before the pasta course has even arrived. This isn't interference in the Anglo sense of overbearing parents — it's a cultural assumption that romantic partnerships are family business, and family gets a say. Resent it and you'll struggle. Lean into it and you'll find a warmth that dating-app culture elsewhere has quietly engineered out of existence.

Where it gets complicated is the gap between the romantic theatre and the demographic reality. Italy has one of the lowest marriage rates and highest average ages at first marriage in Europe, largely thanks to economic precarity that keeps young Italians living with parents well into their thirties — which, delightfully, means the family involvement in your dating life isn't just emotional, it's often literally happening under the same roof.

## China: Love as a Logistics Problem

Chinese urban dating, particularly in cities like Shanghai and Beijing, has been reshaped by decades of intense social and economic pressure into something closer to matchmaking with spreadsheets. The infamous "marriage markets" — informal gatherings in public parks where parents post their adult children's vital statistics (age, salary, apartment ownership, height) on paper or umbrellas, hoping to arrange an introduction — sound like satire until you've actually walked through one on a Sunday morning and watched grandparents negotiate their children's romantic futures with genuine commercial seriousness.

This isn't purely traditional stubbornness; it's a rational response to intense social pressure. Property ownership is often treated as a near-prerequisite for marriage, particularly for men, given deeply entrenched expectations about providing a home before a wedding is even considered viable. Combine that with a shrinking pool of eligible partners after decades of the one-child policy, and dating stops being purely a matter of chemistry and becomes something closer to asset matching, uncomfortable as that framing is.

That said, younger urban Chinese daters — particularly in Tier 1 cities — are pushing back hard against the transactional framing, using apps like Tantan and dating-focused WeChat groups to date on their own terms, away from parental oversight. There's a genuine generational tension playing out: parents who see marriage as a logistics problem to be solved efficiently, and children who increasingly want what looks, from the outside, a lot more like the Western model of dating for connection first and practicalities later.

## The Verdict

Italy wins on romance as an experience — nobody does the long, theatrical, family-soaked courtship better, and there's something genuinely moving about a culture that still treats a first date as worth three hours and four courses. China wins on brutal honesty about what dating actually involves once you strip away the fantasy — money, property, and family expectations are on the table immediately, rather than being quietly discovered eighteen months in. If you want romance with a chorus of opinionated relatives, go Italian. If you want a culture that will tell you exactly what's expected before you've even ordered a coffee, China's marriage-market parents have already done the spreadsheet for you.

## Conclusion

Italy and China have arrived, from wildly different histories, at the same underlying conclusion: dating is too consequential to be left entirely to the two people falling in love. Italy wraps that conclusion in candlelight, long dinners, and a grandmother's steely gaze. China wraps it in spreadsheets, property deeds, and a parent's laminated umbrella pitch in a public park. Both will feel intrusive to anyone raised on the myth of dating as a purely private affair. Both, in their own maddening way, are onto something — because a relationship that survives an Italian family dinner or a Chinese marriage-market negotiation has already survived more scrutiny than most Western couples face in a decade.
