# 🇿🇲 Zambia vs 🇦🇺 Australia: One Knows Everyone on the Street, the Other Knows Everyone's Car

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Lusaka, your neighbours will know your business before you've finished unpacking — not out of nosiness exactly, more out of a deep cultural conviction that community is a mutual obligation, not an optional extra. In an Australian suburb, you can live three doors down from someone for a decade, wave at their car twice a week, and never learn their surname, their job, or the fact that they moved out eight months ago, because nobody mentioned it and, more importantly, nobody asked.

I have been fed uninvited in one country and ignored, politely and consistently, in the other. Let's discuss which one actually constitutes community.

## Do's & Don'ts: Zambia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet every neighbour you pass, properly, with time for a real exchange — rushing a greeting reads as genuinely rude | Decline communal obligations like compound clean-up days or contribution requests for a neighbour's event without a good reason |
| Expect to be fed, checked in on, and gently interrogated about your life — this is affection, not surveillance | Keep your gate permanently shut and your blinds permanently drawn; it will be noticed, and it will be discussed |
| Learn the local community leadership structure (ward chairs, compound committees) — it genuinely mediates disputes and local matters | Assume privacy works the way it does at home; your business is, functionally, shared property here |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wave. Always wave. It is the entire socially mandated interaction and it is sufficient | Expect the wave to escalate into conversation uninvited — reciprocated silence afterward is normal, not cold |
| Attend a street barbecue or block party if one's organised — this is where actual bonding, rare as it is, happens | Knock on a neighbour's door without real reason; it reads as alarming, not neighbourly |
| Respect fence lines and boundary etiquette scrupulously — overhanging branches and loud renovations are the closest thing to real neighbourhood drama here | Assume quiet means unfriendly; it usually just means Australian, and that's a complete explanation |

### Zambia: neighbours as an extended, obligatory family

Community life in a Lusaka residential compound operates on principles that would strike most Western newcomers as almost aggressively intimate, until you've lived inside them for a few months and realise how much genuine security they provide. Greeting rituals aren't brief — they're proper exchanges, with time taken, health and family inquired after specifically, and skipping them (even when you're in a hurry, even when you're a stressed corporate transferee late for a call) is read as a real social failure, not an understandable modern inconvenience. Compound committees and ward-level community structures handle disputes, organise shared responsibilities, and function as a genuine layer of local governance that most Western suburbs simply don't have an equivalent for, because most Western suburbs decided somewhere along the way that neighbours managing each other's business was a bad idea.

What surprises transferees most isn't the warmth — most people expect warmth from descriptions of African community life, often in ways that flatten real cultural complexity into something sentimental, and I'd ask you to resist that. It's the obligation underneath the warmth: you are expected to participate, contribute, show up for compound clean-ups, attend a neighbour's child's naming ceremony, and generally understand that living somewhere means being accountable to the people around you, not merely co-existing beside them. This isn't quaint. It's a functioning social contract that does real work — reducing crime through informal surveillance, providing childcare and eldercare through informal mutual aid, and catching problems (illness, financial trouble, a kid skipping school) long before any formal system would notice. It also means your business genuinely isn't private, and accepting that trade-off is the actual adjustment, not the greetings.

### Australia: the wave as a complete social contract

Australian suburban neighbourliness runs on a principle I've come to think of as "friendly non-involvement," and once you understand it, the apparent coldness resolves into something closer to a mutually agreed, extremely polite boundary. The wave — from the car, over the fence, across the road during the bin run — is not a brush-off, it's the actual, sufficient unit of neighbourly interaction, acknowledging the other person's existence and goodwill without demanding anything further from either party. It took me an embarrassingly long time, moving from cultures with stronger community obligation norms, to understand that the wave isn't a failed attempt at friendship. It's the finished product.

This isn't universal — community does exist, often organised around specific triggers: a street barbecue, a kids' sports team, a shared concern about a development application, a bushfire evacuation that suddenly makes everyone on the street intensely, briefly, genuinely connected. But the baseline, day-to-day texture of Australian suburban life is privacy-first, and the social expectation runs opposite to Zambia's: knocking on a neighbour's door without a specific, legible reason is treated as mildly alarming, not warmly spontaneous. Corporate transferees from more communal cultures often read this as loneliness or social failure on Australia's part; longtime Australian residents will tell you, with total sincerity, that they find it restful, and that the fence is doing exactly the job they want it to do.

### What's actually guarding the house tells its own story

Look at how each culture physically secures a home and the underlying community logic becomes literal. In Lusaka, compound walls, gates, and security guards are common even in relatively modest residential areas, which looks, on first glance, like it contradicts everything I've just said about communal openness — until you realise that the wall keeps out strangers specifically so the known, trusted community inside and around it can move freely between each other's homes without a second thought. The security apparatus and the social intimacy aren't opposites, they're a package deal: a hard boundary against the unknown enables genuine softness within it. Australian suburbs run almost the opposite logic — low or no fences at the front, open sightlines, minimal visible security for most middle-class streets — precisely because the social contract doesn't actually require trusted intimacy with the people on the other side of that boundary, just reasonable mutual non-interference. Neither approach is about fear exactly. Both are simply building the exact amount of boundary their actual social expectations require, no more, no less.

## The Verdict

Zambia wins on substance — a community that genuinely shows up for you, materially and socially, in moments that actually matter, built through obligations that feel intrusive right up until the week you need them. Australia wins on sustainability of boundaries — not every culture needs, or wants, its neighbours fully involved in its business, and there's something to be said for a social contract that asks almost nothing of you and delivers exactly that. I know which one I'd want during an actual crisis. I also know which one lets me eat dinner without three separate check-ins about why I'm eating alone.

## Conclusion

Neighbourhood life, more than almost any other category in this column, exposes what a culture actually believes community is for — mutual obligation and shared visibility in Zambia, or respected privacy and selective, occasion-triggered connection in Australia. Neither is wrong. Both will, in their own completely different ways, eventually make you feel at home — one by refusing to let you be alone, the other by refusing to make you explain yourself. Pick accordingly, and for heaven's sake, learn to wave properly before you move to the suburbs.
