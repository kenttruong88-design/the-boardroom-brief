# One Country Rewrote Its Entire Calendar for Leisure. The Other Never Needed Permission to Enjoy Itself.

🇸🇦 Saudi Arabia 🇻🇪 Venezuela
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Weekend culture is where a country's real priorities surface, stripped of the performative seriousness of the working week. Saudi Arabia has spent the last several years deliberately, officially rewriting what leisure is even allowed to look like — switching its weekend to Friday-Saturday, opening cinemas, concerts, and entertainment venues that didn't exist in any public form a decade ago. Venezuela has never needed that kind of top-down permission; its leisure culture runs on beaches, baseball, and an improvisational joy that persists, stubbornly, despite an economic crisis that would have flattened the recreational life of almost anywhere else.

I've spent a Friday in Riyadh watching a cinema fill up for a film in a country where that sentence would have been unthinkable not so long ago, and I've spent a Sunday on a Venezuelan beach watching a family produce an entire multi-course lunch out of a cooler the size of a briefcase, music going, dominoes out, utterly undeterred by circumstances that would make most people stay home. Both countries taught me that leisure culture isn't really about resources. It's about what a society decides it refuses to give up.

## Saudi Arabia: Leisure as a National Project

Saudi Arabia's weekend culture is undergoing one of the most rapid, deliberate transformations of any country's leisure landscape in recent memory. The move to a Friday-Saturday weekend, aligning more closely with global business schedules while preserving Friday's religious significance, was itself a significant structural shift. But the more visible change has been the sheer expansion of what there is to actually do: cinemas operating widely for the first time in the modern era, concerts and festivals drawing serious international acts, family entertainment complexes, and a tourism sector being built from a near standing start as a matter of explicit national strategy.

What makes this fascinating to watch as an outsider is the pace. Entertainment options that didn't exist in any public form a decade ago are now genuinely competitive weekend draws, and the social norms around them are still visibly settling — what's considered a normal Friday night out in Riyadh today would have been unrecognizable to residents of the same city fifteen years ago, and the culture is still working out its own comfort level with the change, venue by venue, city by city. Jeddah, with its more historically cosmopolitan Red Sea trading-port character, tends to feel a step ahead of Riyadh in its ease with the new leisure landscape, though both are moving in the same direction.

Family remains the gravitational centre even amid all this new entertainment infrastructure. Weekend leisure in Saudi Arabia is still overwhelmingly a family affair first — multi-generational gatherings, desert camping trips, extended lunches — with the new public entertainment options layered on top as additional choices rather than replacements for the core social unit doing the leisure together. Newcomers who assume the new cinemas and concerts have displaced the family-centred weekend are missing that the expansion has been additive, not substitutive.

## Venezuela: Leisure as an Act of Quiet Defiance

Venezuela's weekend culture operates under circumstances that would reasonably be expected to have extinguished it — a sustained, severe economic crisis, hyperinflation that reshaped daily life, significant emigration that's hollowed out entire communities. And yet the core leisure institutions have proven remarkably durable, adapting in scale and spending but not disappearing in spirit. The beach remains the beach. The baseball remains fiercely, nationally beloved — Venezuela's relationship with the sport runs deep enough that it's produced a steady stream of Major League talent, and watching a weekend game, professional or informal, remains one of the most reliable windows into the country's actual emotional temperature.

Domino games deserve specific mention as a genuine social institution rather than a mere pastime — the clack of tiles on a table, often outdoors, often accompanied by rum that's become harder to afford and is savoured more deliberately as a result, is as much a weekend fixture as any beach trip. The games run long, the trash talk runs longer, and being invited to join one is a small but real marker of social acceptance.

What's most striking to an outsider is the resourcefulness woven into the leisure culture by necessity. A beach day assembled from whatever a family can collectively afford, a multi-course lunch built from a single shared cooler, music from a single phone speaker passed around — none of it reads as diminished. It reads, instead, as leisure stripped down to the actual point of the exercise: being together, deliberately, without the crisis being invited to the table. Politics, pointedly, often isn't discussed at these gatherings; the weekend is protected, consciously, as a space the crisis doesn't get to colonize alongside everything else it's touched.

## The Verdict

Saudi Arabia wins if you measure leisure culture by sheer trajectory and new infrastructure — nowhere else is building an entertainment sector from scratch this fast, this deliberately. Venezuela wins if you measure it by resilience — a leisure culture that has refused to collapse under pressure that would have flattened almost anywhere else's recreational life is doing something genuinely remarkable, even if nobody's writing glossy tourism campaigns about it. One country is building its weekend culture up. The other has spent years refusing to let its weekend culture be taken away. Both are forms of the same underlying stubbornness.

## Conclusion

Both countries refuse the easy assumption that leisure is a luxury that disappears under pressure or only arrives with permission. Saudi Arabia is proving that an entire entertainment culture can be built deliberately, at speed, without losing its family-first centre of gravity. Venezuela is proving something harder to engineer and more quietly impressive: that joy, properly practiced, is remarkably difficult to actually take away from people who've decided to keep it. Visit either on a weekend and you'll see a country insisting, in its own register, that leisure isn't optional. It never was.
