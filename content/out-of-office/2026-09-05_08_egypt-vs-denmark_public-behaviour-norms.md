# One Country Negotiates Everything Out Loud, the Other Negotiates Nothing, Ever, With Anyone

🇪🇬 Egypt × 🇩🇰 Denmark
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have haggled over a taxi fare in Cairo with a level of theatrical outrage on both sides that, to an outsider, would look like the beginning of a blood feud and was, in fact, just Tuesday. I have also stood next to a Dane on a Copenhagen train for forty-five minutes in a silence so complete and so mutually respected that speaking would have felt like a small act of vandalism.

Public behaviour tells you what a culture considers a stranger's business and what it considers strictly none of your concern. In Egypt, nearly everything in public is negotiable, loud, and communal — prices, opinions, personal space. In Denmark, nearly everything in public is fixed, quiet, and deeply private, right down to the unspoken agreement that eye contact with a stranger is something to be avoided at almost any cost.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇪🇬 Egypt**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Haggle enthusiastically — it's expected and even enjoyed | Accept the first price at a market stall; it insults the ritual |
| Engage warmly with strangers who strike up conversation | Assume a loud disagreement means real hostility — it's often just talk |
| Accept tea or coffee if offered before a transaction | Rush a negotiation; patience is part of the game |
| Expect close physical proximity in queues and crowds | Expect Western-style personal space bubbles in public |

**🇩🇰 Denmark**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Queue precisely and wait your exact turn | Jump a queue, even by accident — it will be noticed |
| Respect silence on public transport as the default | Strike up conversation with a stranger uninvited |
| Give people generous personal space at all times | Stand too close in a line or on a platform |
| Accept a fixed price without expecting negotiation | Try to haggle in a shop; it will just confuse people |

## Egypt: Everything Is a Conversation, Including the Price

Public life in Egypt runs on volume, warmth, and negotiation as a genuine social ritual rather than a purely transactional necessity. Buying anything in a market — a scarf, a spice mix, a taxi ride — isn't a simple exchange of currency for goods, it's a performance with expected beats: an opening price that both parties understand is theatrical, a counter-offer delivered with mock offence, tea offered mid-negotiation as a gesture of goodwill, and an eventual price landing somewhere both sides can live with, often accompanied by genuine warmth rather than lingering resentment. Skip the haggling and pay the first number, and you haven't been polite — you've been strange, and possibly a little insulting to the whole exchange.

This same public expressiveness extends well beyond commerce. Disagreements happen loudly, in the street, with gestures and raised voices that would read as the prelude to a fistfight almost anywhere in Northern Europe, and then dissolve just as quickly, often ending in laughter or a shared cigarette. Strangers insert themselves into other people's business with a casualness that Western visitors initially find intrusive — a taxi driver offering unsolicited relationship advice, a shopkeeper commenting frankly on your appearance or your choices — but which locals experience as basic human connection rather than overreach.

Personal space, similarly, operates on a much tighter radius than many visitors expect. Queues can compress into something closer to a scrum, physical proximity on public transport is simply normal rather than an imposition, and touch — a hand on the arm during conversation, a warm embrace between men who are simply friends — is unremarkable. None of this is chaos exactly; it's a different, more porous boundary between individual and communal space, one that assumes connection over isolation as the default setting for public life.

## Denmark: Silence Is Respect, Space Is Sacred

Danish public behaviour operates on almost the exact inverse logic: minimal interaction with strangers isn't coldness, it's understood as a form of consideration. Speaking to someone unprompted on a train, holding eye contact a beat too long, or standing slightly too close in a queue are all small violations of an unspoken but very real social contract that says: your space is yours, and I will not intrude on it uninvited. This produces public spaces that can feel eerily quiet to outsiders — buses and trains where forty people sit in total silence, staring at phones or out windows, nobody remotely bothered by the lack of interaction because nobody expected interaction in the first place.

Queueing is treated with near-religious precision. There is an order, everyone knows it, everyone follows it, and any deviation — even an accidental one — will be noted, if not necessarily commented on aloud. Prices, similarly, are fixed and non-negotiable; attempting to haggle in a Danish shop will not be met with the theatrical enjoyment of an Egyptian market exchange, but with mild, genuine confusion, because the entire concept assumes a negotiation neither party actually wants to have.

What redeems this reserve, and it does redeem it, is that Danish directness applies once a relationship is actually established. Danes aren't avoiding connection out of unfriendliness; they're protecting a fairly small circle of genuine relationships with real intensity, and once you're inside that circle — invited to a home, included in a hygge evening — the warmth is completely real, just calibrated to arrive later and more deliberately than in cultures where warmth is the public default. The silence on the train isn't rejection. It's simply not yet an invitation.

## The Verdict

Egypt treats public space as shared, negotiable, and loud — a place where strangers become part of your day whether you planned for it or not. Denmark treats public space as private, fixed, and quiet — a place where strangers remain strangers, respectfully, until circumstances say otherwise. Neither approach is ruder than the other; they're just built on opposite assumptions about what strangers owe each other in passing.

I'll confess Egypt suits my temperament better — I'd rather haggle badly and laugh about it than sit in respectful, flawless silence for an hour-long train ride. But there's an honesty to Danish reserve too: nobody there is performing friendliness they don't mean, and when the warmth finally does arrive, you know it's real.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/Egypt — paraphrased: a visitor described accepting the first asking price at a market and being gently scolded by the vendor himself for not negotiating, as if they'd skipped a step out of politeness that ruined the fun for everyone.*

> *Internations Copenhagen — paraphrased: an expat said it took nearly a year of living in Denmark before a neighbour spoke to them unprompted, and when it finally happened, the warmth of the actual conversation caught them completely off guard.*

> *Quora, "Why do Danes avoid small talk with strangers?" — paraphrased: a respondent explained that small talk is seen as somewhat insincere in Danish culture, and silence is preferred over conversation that doesn't mean anything.*

## Conclusion

Egypt and Denmark have built two of the most opposite public cultures you'll find anywhere, and both work precisely because everyone within them has agreed to the same unwritten rules. Egypt says strangers are simply people you haven't talked to yet. Denmark says strangers are people whose space you're obligated to protect until invited otherwise. Travel between the two quickly enough and you'll whiplash from haggling over a scarf to standing in devout silence on a train — and somehow, both will feel completely correct once you're standing in them.
