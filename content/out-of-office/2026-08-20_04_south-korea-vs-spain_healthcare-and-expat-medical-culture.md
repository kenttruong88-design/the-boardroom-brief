# South Korea Will See You in Twenty Minutes. Spain Will See You Eventually, and Honestly, You'll Probably Live

🇰🇷 South Korea 🇪🇸 Spain
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

South Korea has built a healthcare system so fast and so cheap that visiting a dermatologist feels less like a medical event and more like popping into a very efficient convenience store. Spain has built a healthcare system so fundamentally decent, so quietly excellent at outcomes, that nobody particularly minds waiting three weeks for a non-urgent specialist appointment, because when you finally get seen, the actual care is superb.

I've walked into a Seoul clinic with a mild ear infection and walked out forty minutes later, diagnosed, medicated, and roughly twelve dollars poorer. I've also waited six weeks for a routine dermatology appointment in Spain through the public system, and the sheer unbothered calm of everyone around me in that waiting room told me everything about how Spaniards have made peace with a trade-off most other nationalities would riot over.

## Do's & Don'ts: South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for National Health Insurance as soon as you qualify | Assume walk-in clinics need appointments — most don't |
| Use Naver Map to find English-speaking doctors near you | Expect long GP relationships — specialists are the default entry point |
| Take advantage of same-day imaging and blood work | Be surprised by minimal small talk — visits are brisk and clinical |

## Do's & Don'ts: Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with your local centro de salud immediately | Expect private-speed appointments through the public sanidad system |
| Carry a European Health Insurance Card if eligible | Assume pharmacies can't help — farmacéuticos handle a lot without a doctor |
| Consider private insurance for faster specialist access | Panic about wait times for non-urgent care — outcomes remain strong |

## South Korea: Speed as a Feature, Not a Luxury

South Korean healthcare runs on a premise that would strike most Western systems as almost reckless in its efficiency: you should not have to wait. Walk-in clinics are the default entry point for nearly everything, and the concept of booking a GP appointment weeks in advance for a minor concern simply doesn't map onto how the system operates. Feel unwell, walk into a clinic, be seen within the hour, often within twenty minutes if it's a quiet morning. This isn't private-tier luxury care reserved for the wealthy, it's the baseline experience for anyone enrolled in National Health Insurance, which covers citizens and long-term foreign residents alike at genuinely modest cost.

The specialist-access model compounds the speed. Korea doesn't really do the tiered gatekeeping structure where a GP must refer you before you can see a dermatologist, an ENT, or an orthopaedist. You simply go directly to the specialist that matches your symptom, book (or walk in) same-day in most cases, and get seen. Blood work, imaging, and diagnostics happen with a rapidity that makes European and American systems look positively glacial by comparison — same-day MRI referrals aren't unusual for anything remotely concerning.

What this efficiency trades away is warmth. Korean medical visits are brisk, clinical, and largely free of the bedside chat Westerners often expect. Doctors move quickly because the system is built for volume, and a visit that feels curt isn't a sign of poor care, it's simply the cultural default. Expats used to a GP who asks about their holiday plans before checking their blood pressure often mistake this efficiency for coldness, when it's really just throughput, optimised.

The other quiet marvel is cost. Even without insurance, out-of-pocket costs for most procedures remain shockingly low by international standards, which is precisely why medical tourism to Korea, particularly for dermatology and cosmetic procedures, has become its own significant industry. Koreans themselves treat a dermatologist visit the way other nationalities treat a haircut: routine, affordable, unremarkable.

## Spain: Slow, Public, and Quietly Excellent

Spain's public healthcare system, the sanidad pública, operates on entirely different logic: universality and outcome quality matter more than speed, and Spaniards have collectively decided this trade-off is not just acceptable but obviously correct. Non-urgent specialist appointments can take weeks, sometimes over a month for less pressing concerns, and nobody treats this as scandalous, because the system's actual clinical outcomes consistently rank among the best in Europe, urgent and emergency care remains genuinely fast, and the whole thing is funded through taxation rather than the anxiety-inducing insurance gymnastics of, say, the American system.

The centro de salud, the local public health centre, is the entry point for almost everything, and registering with one as a resident is a straightforward, low-friction process that gives you access to a GP who becomes your ongoing point of contact, referring onward to specialists as genuinely needed. This gatekeeping does slow things down for non-urgent cases, but it also means the system isn't clogged with worried-well visits for things a pharmacist could resolve in five minutes.

And Spanish pharmacists deserve their own paragraph, because they function as an entire informal tier of primary care that most visitors don't expect. Farmacéuticos are highly trained, empowered to advise on and dispense treatment for a wide range of common ailments without a doctor's involvement, and Spaniards use them constantly as the first stop for anything minor, which meaningfully reduces pressure on the GP system and gets people faster relief for everyday complaints.

For anyone finding the public wait times genuinely untenable, private insurance in Spain is remarkably affordable by international comparison, often a fraction of equivalent US premiums, and buys near-immediate specialist access. Many long-term expats and even many Spaniards run a hybrid model: public system for anything serious or ongoing, private top-up for speed on the smaller stuff. It's a pragmatic compromise that keeps the overall system sustainable while giving anxious patients an escape valve.

## The Verdict

South Korea wins, decisively, on speed and cost for immediate care. Spain wins on the quieter, harder-to-market metric that actually matters most: outcomes, universality, and a system that doesn't buckle under its own volume. If you want to be seen today, Seoul delivers. If you want a system that will still be standing, functioning, and treating you well in twenty years regardless of your income, Spain's patience pays off.

I'd take Korea for anything urgent and Spain for anything requiring an actual doctor who remembers my name. Ask me again after my next inexplicable rash, and the answer may well change, along with my flight booking.

## Conclusion

South Korea has optimised healthcare for velocity, treating speed itself as a form of patient care, while Spain has optimised for equity and quiet clinical excellence, treating patience as a reasonable price for universal, high-quality treatment. Neither approach is objectively correct, but both make the same point from opposite directions: a health system says more about a country's values than almost anything else it builds. Korea says convenience matters. Spain says everyone matters equally, even if that means you wait. Choose your emergency accordingly, and pack paracetamol either way.
