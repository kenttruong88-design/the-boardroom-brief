# Forty-Eight Hours, Two Nations, Zero Apologies

🇲🇩 Moldova 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nobody asks what Moldovans do on a Saturday because nobody asks much about Moldova at all, which is precisely the kind of lazy oversight I've built a career correcting. Meanwhile everyone has an opinion about the Canadian weekend, usually involving a cottage, a lake, and a smugness so well-insulated it could survive a polar vortex. Both reputations are, naturally, half true and entirely too tidy.

I have spent weekends in both. One country treats the day off as a sacred negotiation between family, food, and a bottle of something fermented in a cave. The other treats it as a logistics exercise requiring a truck, a dock, and roughly four hundred dollars of gear before anyone's had a swim. Both nations will insist, with total sincerity, that their version is relaxing. Only one of them is lying, and I intend to find out which before this column ends.

Let's get into it, because "weekend culture" is where you learn what a country actually values — not what its tourism board says it values, and definitely not what its expats post before they've lived through a full calendar year of it.

## Do's & Don'ts: Moldova

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept every invitation to someone's grandmother's village house — this is where the real weekend happens | Expect anything to be open or quiet on a Sunday morning; church comes first, brunch does not exist |
| Book a winery visit at Cricova or Milestii Mici well ahead — the underground cellars run tight schedules | Show up to a wine tasting planning to "just have one glass" — that is not a concept here |
| Learn to say no gracefully to a third helping of sarmale, or just give up and eat it | Assume Chisinau's nightlife compares to a capital with triple the budget — it has exactly what it needs, not more |
| Treat the central market as a Saturday ritual, not a chore | Wear gym kit to the park and expect to blend in — the outdoor workout bars are a local fixture, not a fashion statement |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy proper winter gear before your first October — boots, a real coat, the works | Try to "power through" a Canadian winter weekend in a fashionable but useless jacket |
| Learn enough hockey to nod convincingly at a rink-side conversation | Book anything requiring a car on a long-weekend Friday without checking the highway reports first |
| Say yes to a cottage invitation even if you don't fully understand what a "cottage" entails | Assume a quiet weekend at home makes you antisocial — 43% of Canadians prefer exactly that |
| Budget separately for "leisure," because gyms, kids' sports, and rinks all cost more than you think | Expect Sunday retail hours or spontaneous nightlife in most mid-sized cities — plan ahead or stay home |

## Moldova: The Weekend as Inheritance

Moldova doesn't manufacture weekend culture, it inherited one, and that's the whole difference. According to the UNDP's own analytical work using the National Bureau of Statistics' Time Use Survey, leisure and entertainment in Moldova aren't a scheduled category bolted onto a workweek — they're treated as a direct readout of quality of life itself, varying by town versus village, income, and gender in ways the survey takes seriously enough to measure nationally. That's not marketing copy. That's a government deciding your Saturday matters enough to count.

What that produces on the ground, per actual expat accounts rather than brochures, is a weekend built around proximity and produce. Families in Chisinau head to Orhei Vechi, Soroca, or across the border into Romania for day trips; vegetable gardens are not a hobby but a household system; the central outdoor market functions as the weekly social and provisioning event rolled into one. And then there's the wine. Moldova's tourism board isn't shy about the scale here — six wineries with on-site accommodation, forty offering tastings and tours, entire Council of Europe-recognized routes through the Codru and Ștefan Vodă regions, cellars at Cricova and Milestii Mici that function as underground cities. This is a country where "popping out for the weekend" can mean descending into a limestone cave to drink something made from a Fetească Neagră grape nobody outside the region has heard of, and that is simply Tuesday-tier normal here.

What Moldova doesn't have is noise. No sprawling nightlife economy, no influencer-ready skyline of rooftop bars. One expat report flatly noted the social scene leans on "some bars and a trivia night," full stop — and found that more than sufficient, because the real social currency is the evening walk where you run into six people you know. It's intimate to the point of claustrophobia for some, revelatory for others. I know which camp I'm in.

There's also a quietly stubborn resistance to turning leisure into commerce. Spa days at the Woloshin banya, cooking classes, craft fairs, water slides at Vara Vara, hiking at Pe Toloaca — these exist, they're genuinely good, and none of them have been dressed up into a lifestyle brand. A gym membership runs anywhere from roughly $40 to over $100 a month, which tells you plenty about which leisure economy is monetized and which one simply happens in a park, for free, because the outdoor workout bars are municipal furniture, not a wellness trend somebody's trying to sell you.

## Canada: The Weekend as Infrastructure Project

Canada, by brutal contrast, has turned the weekend into something closer to civil engineering. Statistics Canada now runs an entire interactive visualization tool off its 2022 Time Use Survey specifically to let citizens compare how they spend a 24-hour day against gender, age, and — tellingly — weekday versus weekend, because the distinction is apparently significant enough to merit its own government dataset. Where Moldova treats leisure as cultural inheritance, Canada treats it as something to be measured, optimized, and occasionally legislated around a long weekend.

And the Canadians have opinions about that long weekend, opinions Ipsos has been polling for years. The headline split: 43% want a quiet weekend at home, 36% want the cottage or the countryside, and only 11% fancy a restaurant or bar patio — a nation that, when finally given two consecutive days off, overwhelmingly chooses to go somewhere even quieter than its own apartment. The cottage, incidentally, is not a metaphor; cabin ownership is practically a provincial sport, and missing that memo as a newcomer means missing the entire point of July.

Winter complicates all of this spectacularly. Expat reports from Ottawa describe a city where "summer is amazing but winter is long and dreary," where learning to tolerate — eventually enjoy — tobogganing, skating, and snow removal costs (one poster happily reported under $200 for a season of mechanized plowing, down from years of shoveling by hand) is simply the price of admission. Leisure here isn't free-flowing; it's seasonal, gear-dependent, and faintly competitive, right down to whether you've learned enough hockey to survive a rink-side conversation without embarrassing yourself.

Even the geography gets regimented. One forum poster in the Okanagan Valley described summer weekends built entirely around "wineries, fruit orchards, lakes, air conditioning, swimming pools," which, credit where due, sounds almost Moldovan in its simplicity — until you remember the Okanagan is the exception, not the rule, and most of the country is negotiating a much colder bargain with its calendar. Multiply that across provinces with wildly different weekend defaults — Ontario fireworks-watchers, Alberta's extra-day-off enthusiasts, Quebec's near-total indifference to both — and you get a country whose "weekend culture" is really several regional weekend cultures wearing one passport.

## The Verdict

If you want a weekend that feels like a warm, slightly overfed family reunion, Moldova wins outright. If you want a weekend that feels like a logistics briefing with excellent production values, Canada wins. Neither is wrong; they're simply answering different questions about what a day off is for.

But I'll pick a side, because that's the job. Moldova's weekend culture is more honest. It isn't curated for export, isn't bracketed into "activities" with price tags, and doesn't require $200 of specialist outerwear just to leave the house in February. It simply exists, has existed for generations, and will keep existing whether or not anyone writes about it. Canada's weekend, for all its cottages and civic hockey literacy, has a faint whiff of performance to it — a nation proving, survey after survey, how well it rests.

Moldova doesn't need to prove anything. It's already at the winery.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *TalesMag, Chisinau expat report — paraphrased: the social scene for newcomers is thin by design, often amounting to a handful of bars and a weekly trivia night, and anyone expecting a bustling expat social circuit will be recalibrating fast.*

> *expat.com, "Everyday life in Canada" thread — paraphrased: several posters converge on the same warning — budget separately and seriously for winter gear and leisure costs, because what looks like an affordable country on paper gets expensive fast once skating rinks, kids' sports, and proper boots enter the picture.*

> *TalesMag, Ottawa expat report — paraphrased: newcomers are repeatedly told the social and leisure calendar effectively splits in two, with an "amazing" summer offset by a winter described candidly as long and dreary, and surviving it well is treated as a rite of passage rather than optional.*

## Conclusion

Here is the uncomfortable truth both countries would rather I didn't print: weekend culture isn't about the activities, it's about what a society has decided is worth protecting from the workweek. Moldova protects the table, the garden, the cellar, the walk where everyone knows your name. Canada protects the cottage, the rink, the carefully insured leisure hour. Both are legitimate inheritances. Neither is "exotic," and if you've made it this far into an expat-culture column and still use that word, we need to have a separate, much sterner conversation.

Pick your weekend accordingly. Just don't pretend either one is simple, and don't you dare call either one quaint.
