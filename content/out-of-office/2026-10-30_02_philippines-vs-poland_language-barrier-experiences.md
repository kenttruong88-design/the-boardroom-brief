# Everyone In Manila Will Charm You In English And Nobody In Warsaw Will Admit They Can

🇵🇭 Philippines 🇵🇱 Poland
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular kind of smugness that settles over a first-time visitor to Manila when they realise, somewhere around their second jeepney ride, that they haven't needed a phrase book once. The call centre agent, the barista, the security guard at your office lobby — all of them slide between English and Tagalog with the ease of a radio changing stations, often mid-sentence, occasionally mid-word. Fly four thousand miles northwest to Warsaw and the exact opposite confidence trick awaits: everyone can, in fact, speak passable English. They simply do not see why they should have to, and will make that philosophical position clear through sustained, dignified silence.

Having worked in both, I can report that the "language barrier" conversation is really a conversation about different things entirely — colonial history and call-centre economics in one country, pride and post-communist self-reliance in the other — and knowing which one you're dealing with will save you a great deal of unnecessary apologising.

## Do's & Don'ts: Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect fluent, idiomatic English almost everywhere in business settings | Mistake English fluency for cultural fluency — the rules underneath are very different |
| Learn a handful of Tagalog phrases anyway; it delights people disproportionately | Assume every region speaks Tagalog as a first language — there are over a hundred languages here |
| Enjoy "Taglish" code-switching; it's not laziness, it's a genuine dialect | Correct someone's grammar mid-meeting — it reads as needlessly hostile |

## Do's & Don'ts: Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Polish greetings before you arrive — effort is noticed and rewarded | Assume fluent English in a shop or office means warmth; it often just means transaction |
| Expect younger professionals in Warsaw and Kraków to be excellent in English | Expect the same from an older relative at a family gathering, or a rural town hall clerk |
| Use formal titles (Pan/Pani) until explicitly told otherwise | Launch into breezy first-name English small talk; it can read as presumptuous |

## The Philippines: A Nation Fluent By Design

English in the Philippines isn't a skill people acquired, it's infrastructure, baked in from decades of American colonial administration and an education system that still runs substantial instruction in English from primary school onward. The result is a country where you can land a $30-an-hour outsourced customer service job specifically because your English carries no detectable accent to an American ear, and where entire industries — business process outsourcing alone employs over a million people — exist because of this linguistic inheritance.

What makes it genuinely dizzying for a newcomer isn't the English itself, it's the code-switching. Conversations slide between English and Tagalog, sometimes within the same sentence, a phenomenon locals call Taglish and treat as entirely unremarkable. You'll hear someone say something like "grabe, I was so late kasi traffic talaga," and if you don't yet know that "grabe" means something like "intense" and "kasi" means "because," you'll nod along, pretending comprehension, the international expat's most practiced skill.

The deeper trap is assuming linguistic fluency equals cultural transparency. It very much does not. Filipino communication runs on indirectness, face-saving, and a concept called "pakikisama" — group harmony — that means a colleague fluently telling you "yes, that should be fine" in perfect English might actually mean something closer to "absolutely not, but I would rather die than tell you that to your face." You will learn this the hard way, usually via a missed deadline that nobody flagged because flagging it felt confrontational. And the linguistic map itself is far from uniform — step outside Manila and Cebuano, Ilocano, Hiligaynon and dozens of other languages dominate daily life, with Tagalog and English functioning as the shared professional layer rather than the whole picture.

## Poland: Fluent, Polite, and Not Here For Your Convenience

Poland presents the inverse puzzle entirely. English proficiency here, especially among anyone under 40 working in Warsaw, Kraków, or Wrocław's booming tech and finance sectors, is genuinely excellent — the EF English Proficiency Index consistently ranks Poland among Europe's stronger non-native performers. And yet the lived experience, especially outside the big corporate towers, can feel like negotiating with a country that has collectively decided English is a tool to be deployed, not a courtesy to be extended freely.

Part of this is pragmatic: Poland spent decades under Soviet influence where Russian, not English, was the mandatory second language, and the pivot to English fluency is a genuinely recent, hard-won achievement that Poles are proud of rather than casual about. Part of it is cultural self-reliance — a strong sense that this is Poland, the national language is Polish, and the onus to adapt sits with the newcomer, not the host. You will not be met with the instinctive linguistic hospitality of Manila. You will be met with competent, slightly guarded English, deployed exactly as far as the transaction requires and not one syllable further.

Formality compounds this. Address a Polish colleague with breezy first-name informality before you've earned it, and you will register, however unfairly, as a presumptuous foreigner who hasn't done their homework. Learn even basic Polish — "dzień dobry," "proszę," "przepraszam" — and the entire tone of an interaction shifts, often dramatically, from guarded transaction to genuine warmth. The barrier here isn't comprehension. It's an unspoken contract about effort, and Poland will quietly judge you for not signing it.

## The Verdict

The Philippines will seduce you into thinking you've mastered the place because you can order lunch and understand the office memo without effort — a dangerous illusion, since the real cultural fluency sits several layers beneath the English you're hearing. Poland will do the opposite: convince you, through politeness and competence, that nobody understands you, when in fact they understand perfectly and are simply deciding, moment by moment, whether you've earned the warmer register. If I had to choose which barrier is more honest, it's Poland's — at least you know exactly where you stand, which is "outside, knocking, politely." The Philippines will let you in immediately and then spend the next two years quietly revealing how little you actually grasped.

## Conclusion

Language barriers aren't always about language, and nowhere is that clearer than in these two countries. The Philippines hands you fluency and asks you to earn the cultural subtext for years afterward. Poland withholds easy warmth and asks you to earn the right to it with a few dozen memorised words and some patience. Both are forms of the same test, dressed up in opposite costumes — one generous on the surface and demanding underneath, the other guarded on the surface and generous underneath, once you've proven you're worth the trouble. Show up assuming English fluency means the whole job is done, in either country, and you will be gently, politely, devastatingly wrong.
