# Chișinău Commutes in a Minibus Held Together by Optimism and Dublin Commutes in a Queue Held Together by Apology

🇲🇩 Moldova vs 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Chișinău the commute is a contact sport with a very low entry fee. You flag down a marshrutka, a minibus that follows a numbered route with the confidence of a man who has never once read a timetable, you pass your coins forward through four strangers, and you stand with your face in someone's armpit while a driver conducts a private argument with the road. It is cheap, it is fast when it wants to be, and it has the moral structure of a flock of starlings.

In Dublin the commute is a queue with weather. You tap a Leap card, you wait for a bus that is, by the sacred law of Irish public transport, either three at once or none at all, and you apologise to the driver for existing. Both cities will get you to work eventually. One does it by improvisation and the other by managed disappointment, and I have never been able to decide which is more insulting to the intelligence.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Moldova**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry small coins and pass the fare forward | Wave a large note at the driver and expect change |
| Learn the route numbers of your own corridor | Assume a stop is marked on anything you can read |
| Stand firm and hold on, because the driver will not wait | Treat a marshrutka like a taxi with manners |
| Ask the passenger beside you where to get off | Rely on a map app to know which minibus is real |

**Ireland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a Leap card before your first week is over | Pay cash every time and wonder why it costs more |
| Leave earlier than the timetable suggests | Treat the printed time as a promise |
| Thank the bus driver as you step off | Ignore the driver like a Parisian |
| Carry a raincoat, not an umbrella | Bring an umbrella into a Dublin crosswind |

## Moldova

Chișinău is a city built for a Soviet planner's idea of the working class and then abandoned to its own devices, and the transport shows both halves of that history. There is no metro. What there is, according to expat guides such as the ExpatExchange page on getting around the capital, is a trolleybus network, ordinary buses, and the marshrutka minibuses that do most of the actual work. The trolleybuses are the grandparents of the system: slow, wire-bound, and strangely dignified. The marshrutkas are the teenagers: fast, loud and not entirely insured.

The first thing a newcomer learns is that the minibus is a social institution. You board, you hand money forward, the change comes back along the chain of strangers with an honesty that would embarrass a bank, and nobody speaks except to say something practical. It is a system that depends on a degree of casual cooperation that would collapse in a more anxious country. Hand a Londoner a marshrutka and he would file a complaint. Hand it to a Chișinău grandmother and she will organise the entire vehicle by the second stop.

The weaknesses are not subtle. Routes change, numbers get reused, and there is no single clean map for a foreigner who does not read Romanian or Russian well. Comfort is a rumour. In winter the windows fog, in August the cabin turns into a pressure cooker, and at rush hour you will learn exactly how many human beings fit in a vehicle designed for fourteen. Safety is a matter of faith. A great many people who can afford it simply take taxis or use ride-hailing apps, and expats mostly follow them within a month.

What nobody tells you is how cheap and how reachable everything is. A marshrutka will take you almost anywhere in the city for the price of a coffee-shop napkin, and you never wait long because there is always another one coming. The commute is exhausting, but it is also oddly freeing: no card to top up, no app to download, no ticket inspector, just a coin and a decision. For a few weeks you will hate it. Then you will come back from a trip and miss it.

## Ireland

Ireland has the opposite problem. It has systems, and they are all slightly broken in an unmistakably Irish way. A guide such as the Expat Arrivals page on Dublin lists the pieces: Dublin Bus, the Luas tram with its two lines, the DART along the coast, commuter rail out to the suburbs and beyond. The Leap card, which Transport for Ireland promotes as the way to pay, is the key that opens most of it at a discount. In theory you can get nearly anywhere in the city without a car. In practice you can get nearly anywhere in the city provided the bus is not stuck behind a delivery van on a street that was laid out before the invention of the wheel.

The Irish commute is a lesson in restraint. Queues form with a politeness that borders on the liturgical. People say thank you to the driver as they get off, a custom that astonishes anyone from a country where drivers are invisible. The Luas arrives with an almost Swiss cleanliness and then stops for ten minutes because of something happening at a junction nobody can see. The DART is genuinely lovely, hugging the sea on a good morning, and genuinely cancelled on a bad one.

Weather does the rest. Wind, rain, and the particular grey light that makes a bus shelter feel like a punishment all combine into the real Dublin commute, and no timetable ever accounts for it. Housing is the deeper problem: the rents push workers further out each year, so the commute gets longer even as the transport improves, and the people who can least afford it spend the most time in transit. The expat who lands in a pleasant city-centre flat and cycles to work is the exception, not the rule.

And yet the system works enough that you build a life around it. The buses are frequent on the main corridors, the real-time arrival boards are mostly honest, and a Leap card in your pocket feels like a small victory over bureaucracy. You complain about it constantly, which is the national sport, and quietly you rely on it, which is the national character.

## The Verdict

Ireland wins on paper, and I say that with the weary resentment of someone who has stood in a Dublin shelter at seven in the morning. It has timetables, real-time boards, integrated payment, and a network that reaches the suburbs, the coast and the airport. If you have a job and a pass, you can live a respectable life without owning a car, and that is an achievement.

Moldova wins on spirit and on price. The marshrutka is cheaper, quicker to flag, and more entertaining, and it forces a kind of daily cooperation that Dublin's polite silence has no equivalent for. It also loses on every metric a risk-averse employer cares about: comfort, safety, predictability, accessibility, and the sheer dignity of a seat.

If I am being asked who gets the crown, I will say Dublin and then hum Chișinău under my breath. A commute should get you to work. A great commute should also give you something to tell people afterwards, and Moldova delivers that in the first five minutes. Ireland delivers it only when the bus drivers go on strike.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *ExpatExchange, Chisinau community answers — newcomers are told to learn the marshrutka routes of their own corridor early, because the minibuses are how most residents actually move around and the signage rarely helps a foreigner.*

> *National College of Ireland support hub — students are pointed to the Leap card as the standard way to pay, a reminder that in Ireland the card is the entry ticket to cheaper fares, not an optional extra.*

> *Dwellworks, Dublin guide — the advice for relocating workers is to test the route to the office before the first real day, because the walk to the stop and the wait in the rain are part of the commute.*

## Conclusion

A commute is a small country you visit twice a day. Chișinău teaches you improvisation, shared coins and the gentle art of not caring where your elbow goes. Dublin teaches you patience, queues and the strangely moving habit of thanking a man for driving a bus.

If you are moving from Moldova to Ireland, buy a Leap card, learn to say thank you to the driver, and accept that nobody will pass your change forward. If you are going the other way, bring small coins, a sense of humour and a very flexible idea of personal space. In both places, the journey is the same: you will arrive late, a little damp, and strangely fond of the whole absurd machine.
