# One Country Banned the Plastic Bag So Thoroughly You'll Get Searched for One at the Airport. The Other Built a Market So Pretty It's Basically a Tourist Attraction.

🇷🇼 Rwanda 🇱🇺 Slovenia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Rwanda enacted one of the world's strictest and most successfully enforced plastic bag bans back in 2008, a policy so thoroughly embedded in national life that customs officials will genuinely search incoming luggage for contraband plastic bags, and Kigali's supermarkets and markets alike operate on a cloth-and-paper economy most countries only pay lip service to. Slovenia, meanwhile, has turned grocery shopping into something approaching civic theatre: the Ljubljana Central Market, designed in part by the legendary architect Jože Plečnik, is as much an architectural landmark as a functioning produce market, drawing tourists who came for the building and stayed for the fresh trout.

I have been genuinely asked to open my suitcase at Kigali airport specifically to check for plastic bags, and complied, mildly impressed by the national commitment. I have also bought cheese at a Ljubljana market stall beneath a colonnade so architecturally serious I half expected to need a ticket. Both countries take grocery shopping more seriously than most. Only one of them has made it enforceable by customs law.

## Do's & Don'ts: Rwanda 🇷🇼

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your own reusable bags everywhere — cloth, woven, or paper, genuinely any non-plastic option, before you even reach the till | Pack or bring plastic bags into the country at all — they're confiscated at the border, and this is enforced, not symbolic |
| Shop Kigali's local markets (Kimironko is the big one) for the best produce prices and selection, alongside the growing supermarket chains | Assume Kigali's supermarket prices mirror the rest of East Africa — imported goods in particular carry a real premium here |
| Join or at least respect Umuganda, the last-Saturday-of-the-month national community work morning — many shops close until it concludes | Expect extensive shopping hours on Umuganda Saturday mornings — most retail genuinely pauses nationwide until the community work session ends |

## Do's & Don'ts: Slovenia 🇸🇮

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit Ljubljana's Central Market (tržnica) on a Saturday morning — it's the fullest, liveliest version of the experience, Plečnik architecture and all | Expect Sunday opening hours from most supermarkets or the central market — Slovenia maintains genuinely limited Sunday retail by law |
| Buy direct from the riverside fish and produce stalls for the freshest options — much of it is sourced from nearby Slovenian farms and the Adriatic coast | Assume all of Slovenia shops like Ljubljana — rural and smaller-town grocery culture leans far more toward standard supermarket chains |
| Explore Mercator and Spar for everyday shopping — Slovenia's supermarket chains are modern, well-stocked and genuinely competitively priced regionally | Haggle at the central market the way you might at a market elsewhere in the world — prices here are generally fixed, polite negotiation isn't the local custom |

## Rwanda: A Plastic Ban That Actually Worked

Rwanda's 2008 plastic bag ban is one of the genuine, underappreciated policy success stories of modern African governance, and the gap between "enacted a plastic ban" and "actually enforced a plastic ban for over fifteen years" is the entire story here. Unlike dozens of countries that have announced similar bans with little follow-through, Rwanda built real enforcement teeth into the policy: customs checks at the airport and land borders specifically target plastic bags, fines exist for violations, and the cultural norm has shifted so completely that Rwandans themselves now view carrying a plastic bag as faintly embarrassing rather than merely illegal.

The practical effect on grocery shopping is visible everywhere. Kigali's supermarkets — Simba, Kigali Heights' retail offerings, the growing presence of regional chains — package everything in paper, cloth, or simply hand you your items loose, trusting you've brought your own bag as everyone genuinely does by this point. Local markets like Kimironko, Kigali's largest and most vibrant, operate on the same principle, with vendors and shoppers alike carrying woven baskets and cloth sacks as unremarkable daily equipment rather than an eco-conscious statement. The city, partly as a result, is visibly, genuinely cleaner than most regional comparators, a fact Rwandans take real pride in and foreign visitors notice almost immediately upon arrival.

Grocery culture here also intersects meaningfully with Umuganda, the mandatory last-Saturday-of-every-month national community service morning, during which most commercial activity, supermarkets included, pauses until the community work session concludes around midday. This isn't a minor scheduling quirk; it's a genuine, nationally observed civic institution that shapes the entire rhythm of a Rwandan Saturday, and newcomers who show up to a shuttered supermarket at 9am on the last Saturday of the month, confused, quickly learn to simply plan their shopping around it rather than against it.

## Slovenia: Grocery Shopping as Architectural Pilgrimage

Slovenia's grocery culture operates at a genuinely different register of ambition, centred on Ljubljana's Central Market, a riverside complex designed substantially by Jože Plečnik, one of the twentieth century's most significant Central European architects, whose colonnade and market hall turned what could have been purely functional infrastructure into a genuine architectural landmark that draws visitors regardless of whether they need groceries at all. Saturday mornings here are the full experience: farmers from the surrounding countryside, fishmongers with Adriatic catch, flower sellers, and a social density that makes grocery shopping feel like a civic ritual rather than an errand.

Beyond the market's architectural fame, Slovenia's everyday supermarket infrastructure is genuinely well-developed and competitively priced by regional standards — Mercator, the dominant domestic chain, alongside Spar and other international entrants, provides modern, reliably stocked shopping comparable to most Western European countries, a reflection of Slovenia's relatively smooth post-independence economic development compared to some of its Balkan neighbours. The retail landscape rewards a kind of dual approach that many Slovenians practise without much comment: market produce for the fresh, local, seasonal essentials, supermarket chains for the packaged and everyday staples, each serving a distinct and well-understood role in the weekly shop.

What catches foreign visitors off guard is Slovenia's genuinely restrictive Sunday trading law, which keeps most supermarkets closed and significantly limits the central market's own Sunday operations, a policy rooted in labour-rights tradition and cultural weekend protection that several other European countries have abandoned under commercial pressure but Slovenia has largely held onto. The result is a grocery culture that rewards planning your week around a Saturday peak and a Sunday pause, a rhythm that feels almost quaintly old-world in an era when most of the developed world has normalised round-the-clock retail availability.

## The Verdict

Rwanda wins on sheer policy execution — a plastic ban that's actually, verifiably stuck for over fifteen years is a harder achievement than it sounds, and the visible cleanliness payoff is undeniable. Slovenia wins on pure shopping experience — few countries have turned a produce market into an architectural landmark worth visiting independent of your grocery list. If the metric is "government policy that actually changed daily behaviour for the better," Rwanda takes it decisively. If the metric is "most beautiful place to buy a tomato," Slovenia isn't just winning, it's barely being challenged.

## Conclusion

Rwanda proves that a genuinely strict environmental policy can stick if a government actually enforces it rather than merely announcing it, and the result is a grocery culture built entirely around cloth bags and civic pride. Slovenia proves that grocery shopping, done with enough architectural ambition and a stubborn Sunday-closing law, can become something closer to a weekly pilgrimage than a chore. Bring your own bag to one. Bring your appetite and a free afternoon to the other.
