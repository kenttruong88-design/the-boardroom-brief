# A Braai Will Fix Everything, Apparently, Except the Portuguese Won't Sit Still Long Enough to Eat One

🇿🇦 South Africa 🤝 🇵🇹 Portugal
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

South Africa treats a public holiday as an invitation to set something on fire in the backyard and call it nation-building. Portugal treats a public holiday as a reason the entire municipality should be in the street until 4am, singing, eating grilled sardines off newspaper, and jumping over small bonfires for reasons nobody under sixty can fully explain. Both countries are, in their own ways, dead serious about having a good time on command. Neither will apologise for it.

What I find genuinely instructive, moving between the two, is how differently each country uses its calendar as a tool. South Africa's holidays are mostly solemn-turned-social — Heritage Day, Freedom Day, Human Rights Day — occasions born from a very specific, very recent history, that the public has quietly rerouted into braai smoke and beer. Portugal's holidays are older than anyone's grievances, saint-shaped, parish-specific, and treated with the kind of casual, structural seriousness that only a country with four hundred years of practice at throwing a street party can manage. One nation turned trauma into tailgating. The other turned religion into a logistics exercise nobody questions. Pack accordingly.

## South Africa: Do's & Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring meat, tongs, and an opinion about fire-lighting method to any braai you're invited to — showing up empty-handed is a minor scandal | Call it a "barbecue." It is a braai. Using the wrong word in the wrong company earns a look that lasts the rest of the afternoon |
| Treat 24 September (Heritage Day, unofficially "National Braai Day") as the biggest unofficial unifying ritual on the calendar — it was deliberately rebranded in 2005 to give a fraught date a shared, cross-racial activity | Assume every public holiday is a solemn affair. Several are explicitly historical and political — Freedom Day, Human Rights Day — and get discussed, not just observed |
| Expect holidays that land on a Sunday to roll over to the Monday — it's standard practice and nobody treats it as a bonus | Plan anything requiring government offices, banks, or a quick administrative favour on or around a long weekend. Everything simply stops |
| Ask what a holiday actually commemorates if you don't know — South Africans generally want you to understand the history, not just enjoy the day off | Treat Heritage Day as merely "the braai one." It carries real cultural-diversity weight for a lot of people, even if the smoke gets all the attention |

## Portugal: Do's & Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the difference between a national holiday and a municipal one — Lisbon's Santo António (12-13 June) is a city-specific holiday, not observed nationwide, and this trips up newcomers constantly | Assume one region's festa calendar applies elsewhere. Porto celebrates São João, Lisbon celebrates Santo António, and mixing them up marks you instantly as new |
| Eat the grilled sardines (sardinhas assadas) off a slice of bread in the street during the santos populares — it is the entire point, not a tourist add-on | Expect anything resembling peace and quiet in June if you live near a festa route — the manjericos (basil pots), paper decorations, and street parties run late and loud |
| Jump the small ritual bonfires on São João night in Porto if invited, and hit a stranger lightly on the head with a soft plastic hammer or leek — yes, that's real, yes, everyone does it | Confuse the religious origin (saints' feast days) with a purely secular street party — locals hold both frames simultaneously and get mildly irritated if you flatten it to "just a festival" |
| Check the municipal holiday calendar for your specific city, not just the national one — several towns add their own patron-saint day as a local public holiday | Book travel assuming shops will be open. Small-town Portugal takes its saint's day as seriously as Christmas, commercially speaking — which is to say, completely closed |

## South Africa, Deep Dive

South Africa's public-holiday calendar reads, on paper, like a syllabus for a difficult history course: Human Rights Day, Freedom Day, Youth Day, National Women's Day, Day of Reconciliation. Every one of them points directly at apartheid, its end, or the work still happening in its aftermath. That's not incidental — these dates were chosen deliberately, post-1994, as a way of writing the new constitutional order into the rhythm of ordinary life. What's genuinely interesting is what the public actually *does* with that seriousness. It largely socialises it.

Heritage Day is the clearest case. Officially about recognising the country's cultural diversity, it was reframed in 2005 by a beef industry-adjacent campaign — "Braai4Heritage" — into what is now functionally National Braai Day, and the rebrand stuck completely. Archbishop Desmond Tutu himself lent his name to it as patron, on the logic that a shared plate of meat over an open fire is one of the only rituals genuinely common to every South African community regardless of background, language, or history. Whether you find that a little too neat or genuinely moving probably depends on your mood, but it worked: 24 September is now braai smoke from the Cape Flats to the Jozi suburbs, and the politics sit underneath, present but unforced.

Practically, as an expat: the braai invitation is a real social institution, not a cliché you can skip. You bring something — meat, drinks, a side — and you have a strong opinion about wood versus charcoal whether you've earned one or not. The deeper etiquette is less about the fire and more about who's at the table; South African social life after apartheid is still actively, visibly working out its own integration, and a braai is one of the places that happens casually, over tongs. Expect long, unstructured afternoons. Expect the holiday itself to come up in conversation, seriously, before the meat does. And expect, if a public holiday falls on a Sunday, for everyone to simply assume the Monday is also off — it's automatic, not generous. Human Rights Day and Freedom Day, in particular, tend to generate genuine public discussion in the days around them — radio call-ins, newspaper op-eds, dinner-table debate about how far the country still has to go — which is a very different texture of "holiday mood" from a purely celebratory long weekend, and worth being prepared for if your only prior experience of a public holiday was a quiet day off work.

## Portugal, Deep Dive

Portugal's holiday calendar runs on an entirely different operating system: Catholic liturgical time, layered with municipal patron-saint days that predate the modern state by centuries. The headline event for most of the country, especially in June, is the santos populares — the "popular saints," Santo António, São João, and São Pedro — a run of summer feast days that functions as the country's real, unofficial party season, far louder in practice than any national holiday on the books.

The catch, and the thing that catches out almost every newcomer, is that these are not evenly observed. Santo António (13 June) is Lisbon's holiday — a municipal public holiday *only* in the capital, where it absorbs the whole city into grilled-sardine smoke, paper streamers, and all-night street dancing through neighbourhoods like Alfama. Porto, meanwhile, holds its own, arguably louder, São João on 23-24 June, complete with the genuinely strange and completely sincere tradition of bopping strangers on the head with a soft plastic hammer (traditionally a leek, if you're being authentic about it) as a greeting. Neither city particularly observes the other's night. If you've just moved from Lisbon to Porto expecting the same calendar, you will be wrong, loudly, in public.

What makes Portuguese holiday culture distinct from South Africa's is the texture of the seriousness. Nobody's debating the political meaning of São João at the dinner table — the santos populares are treated as straightforwardly joyful, communal, slightly pagan-adjacent fun layered onto a saint's name, and the religious origin and the street-party present coexist without much friction. The friction, instead, is logistical: smaller towns genuinely shut for their local patron saint's day, commerce included, and an expat assuming Portugal operates on a single national holiday calendar will be caught out by a closed pharmacy in a town they've lived in for years. The lesson, as ever, is hyper-local: check your parish, not just your country.

## The Verdict

Here's the real tension: South Africa makes you reckon with history before it lets you enjoy yourself — the braai is a release valve for weight the calendar is still carrying. Portugal just hands you a sardine and a reason to stay up until the small hours, no homework required, the saints having quietly absorbed whatever politics there ever was centuries ago. If you want a holiday culture that makes you *think*, pick South Africa. If you want one that makes you *dance in the street until your feet hurt while an elderly man hits you with a plastic hammer*, Portugal wins, outright, no contest. I know which one I'd rather be hungover from.

## Conclusion

Neither country is doing this halfway. South Africa turned its most painful anniversaries into the country's most reliable excuse for a shared plate of meat, and somehow didn't cheapen either the history or the meat in the process. Portugal took four centuries of saints' days and never once felt the need to secularise them, mostly because nobody there sees the contradiction you're looking for. What they share is a flat refusal to let a public holiday be merely administrative — it has to mean something, or at minimum, it has to be loud. You will not get a quiet long weekend in either place, and if that's what you were hoping for, I have genuinely terrible news about the rest of your relocation.
