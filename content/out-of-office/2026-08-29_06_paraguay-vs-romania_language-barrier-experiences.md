# One Country Will Let You Coast in English. The Other Speaks a Second Language You Didn't Know Existed

🇵🇾 Paraguay vs 🇷🇴 Romania — Language Barrier Experiences

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There's a very specific kind of humility that arrives the moment you realise the conversation happening two feet away from you has quietly switched into a language you don't speak, and everyone involved seems to consider this completely unremarkable. Romania did this to me in French, briefly, at a dinner party, as a kind of parlour trick. Paraguay did it to me constantly, unglamorously, in Guaraní, as simply how people talk to each other when they're not performing politeness for a foreigner.

Romania's language situation is, structurally, generous to outsiders: young, urban Romanians are often startlingly multilingual, fluent enough in English that entire relationships with the country can be conducted without ever properly learning Romanian, which is either a gift or a trap depending on how seriously you take integration. Paraguay offers no such shortcut, because underneath the Spanish that gets you through banks and hospitals sits Guaraní, an indigenous language so thoroughly woven into daily life that you can speak fluent Spanish for years and still feel, at certain dinner tables, like the last person let in on the joke.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇵🇾 Paraguay**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn functional Spanish within your first year — banking and healthcare demand it | Assume English will carry you past the airport |
| Pick up a few Guaraní greetings — it signals you're staying, not passing through | Expect to master Guaraní; almost no foreigner does, and that's accepted |
| Expect conversations to code-switch mid-sentence between Spanish and Guaraní | Take it personally when a room shifts into Guaraní around you |
| Embrace the bilingual culture as part of the experience, not an obstacle to it | Rely on formal institutions to operate in anything but Spanish |

**🇷🇴 Romania**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take advantage of widespread English among younger, urban Romanians | Assume the same fluency exists in rural areas or with older generations |
| Learn basic Romanian anyway — it changes how warmly you're received | Rely on English alone for bureaucracy, healthcare, or smaller towns |
| Try an immersion course or homestay if you're serious about integrating | Expect everyone to appreciate you never bothering to learn the local language |
| Use your multilingual Romanian friends' skills as a bridge, not a permanent crutch | Confuse "getting by" in English with actually understanding the culture |

## Paraguay: Spanish Gets You In the Door, Guaraní Owns the House

Paraguay's official bilingualism sounds, on paper, like a tidy arrangement: Spanish for formal life, Guaraní as a charming cultural accent. The reality, once you've lived there longer than a holiday, is that Guaraní isn't a garnish, it's load-bearing. Expats manage perfectly well running errands, opening bank accounts, and navigating healthcare in Spanish alone — nobody's pretending you need Guaraní for institutional survival — but the emotional register of the country runs through the indigenous language, and conversations shift into it constantly, unpredictably, and without any obligation to translate for you.

What nobody adequately prepares you for is the specific loneliness of standing in a kitchen or a work meeting while the room fluidly moves between two languages, understanding one perfectly and catching nothing of the other, and slowly realising that this isn't rudeness, it's simply how Paraguayans talk among themselves. Almost no foreigner achieves real fluency in Guaraní, and that's an accepted, unembarrassing fact of expat life here — but the ones who bother to learn a greeting, a thank-you, a single well-placed phrase, get rewarded disproportionately, because it signals something Spanish alone never quite can: that you're not just passing through, you're actually trying to belong. I picked up exactly four Guaraní phrases in eighteen months and I'm still coasting on the goodwill they bought me.

## Romania: The Trap of Being Too Easily Understood

Romania's language situation flatters you into complacency, and I say this as someone who fell for it entirely. English proficiency among younger, urban, professionally connected Romanians is genuinely excellent, and many are fluent in several additional languages besides — French, German, Spanish rotate through casually depending on who you're talking to — which means an expat can build an entire social and professional life in Bucharest or Cluj without ever seriously tackling Romanian, and nobody will particularly stop you.

The catch, obviously, is that this isn't the whole country, and it isn't even the whole point. Step outside the major cities, deal with an older generation, or try to navigate bureaucracy without a Romanian-speaking friend on standby, and the safety net vanishes fast. More importantly, treating Romanian as optional — because you technically can — quietly forecloses the parts of the culture that only open up in the local language: the customs, the specific warmth Romanians extend to foreigners who make a genuine effort, the jokes that don't survive translation. Language schools and immersion programs have expanded specifically to meet growing expat demand, and the ones who take them seriously consistently describe learning Romanian as the actual key that unlocked the country, not a box-ticking exercise they endured for visa paperwork. The generous English proficiency is a gift. Treating it as a permanent substitute for Romanian is the trap.

## The Verdict

Paraguay is honest about its difficulty: you will never fully arrive linguistically, and the country doesn't pretend otherwise, so the goalposts stay realistic. Romania is dishonest in a much gentler, more seductive way — it lets you believe you've arrived long before you actually have, because the English is good enough to fool you into thinking integration is optional. I'll take Paraguay's upfront difficulty over Romania's comfortable trap any day; at least nobody in Asunción lets you believe you're fluent in the culture just because you can order dinner.

## Conclusion

Both countries will let you function without their local language, technically. Only one of them will let you get away with it long-term. Paraguay's Guaraní ceiling keeps everyone, foreigner or not, permanently a little bit outside the room, which is oddly equalising. Romania's English fluency is generous enough to let you fool yourself entirely, right up until the day you realise fifteen years in and you still can't read your own lease. Learn the local language in both. In one, it's survival. In the other, it's the only thing standing between you and a very expensive, very long holiday you mistook for a life.
