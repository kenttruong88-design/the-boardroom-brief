# Prague Moves a Million People a Day on Trust and Fines. Australia Moves Them Into Cars and Calls It Freedom

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Prague commuting is a gentle form of civic faith. Trams arrive when the board says they will, the metro hums beneath a city that does not want to be driven through, and the barrier that should stop you boarding without a ticket is simply not there. You are trusted to pay, and then, with a slight smile, inspected.

Australia's commuting culture is built on distance. The cities sprawl, the suburbs are wide, and the national instinct is to solve a gap on the map with a car and a long road. Public transport exists, in some cases quite impressively, but it competes with a culture that treats driving as a birthright. One country made a transit network its pride. The other made a motorway its personality.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Czechia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a ticket or an annual pass before you board | Assume no gate means no inspector |
| Stand on the right of the escalator and walk on the left | Block the left side with your suitcase |
| Give up your seat to the elderly, pregnant and very young | Pretend to be asleep |
| Use the PID app for tickets and timetables | Rely on memory for connections |

**Australia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Top up your transport card before you travel | Turn up expecting to pay cash on board |
| Plan around timetables, especially off-peak and weekends | Assume a train every few minutes outside the big hubs |
| Allow for long commutes and bring something to listen to | Underestimate distances on the map |
| Learn when a car is worth the cost | Assume you can live without one everywhere |

## Czechia

Prague's transit network is a rare example of a city getting something right and not making a fuss about it. Expats.cz reports that a TimeOut survey of over fifty cities put Prague's public transport second in the world, with 96 per cent of Prague respondents saying it is easy to get around. The metro carries about a million riders a day on roughly 65 kilometres of track, trams run over 800 vehicles and about 800,000 daily riders, and buses carried 418 million passengers in 2019. About a third of residents use public transport every weekday.

The system is organised around the PID integrated network, with time-based tickets, not distance-based ones. According to the 2026 Expat.com guide, 30 minutes costs about 30 CZK, 90 minutes about 40 CZK, a day ticket about 120 CZK and 72 hours about 330 CZK, and an annual pass about 3,650 CZK. That annual pass is the common advice from locals and expats alike: Jaroslav, quoted in an Expats.cz piece of advice for newcomers, tells people to buy one.

The system is not a utopia. Most stations have no gates, but inspections are frequent, and fines start at about 1,000 CZK. Marcelina, a Polish resident quoted in the same article, warns that ticket inspectors are everywhere, and Julia, also Polish, recommends the PID Lítačka app so you do not end up explaining yourself on a platform. Prague has no direct rail link to the airport, which a Czech journalist reportedly called practically incompatible with world-class transit, and a 2017 Politico index ranked the city only twelfth for commuters overall, noting that just one per cent of Praguers cycle to work.

But the central point stands. A Praguer can live without a car, and many do, with an ease that most large cities have long forgotten. The network is cheap, frequent and dense enough to forgive the odd delay, and the commute itself, a tram along the river, is part of the pleasure of living there.

Prague's own statistics are worth a closer look, because they show a city that is good without being perfect. A 2017 Politico index said Prague had the third cheapest single ticket among twenty European cities, behind Budapest and Warsaw, and the third highest share of mass-transit use at 43.3 per cent. The city planned about thirty new tram lines, and the metro D line from náměstí Míru to Písnice is described in 2023 reporting as planned, although the 2026 guide lists only three lines. Ride-hailing through Uber, Bolt and Liftago costs about 150 to 300 CZK a ride, and the pink Rekola shared bikes fill the gaps. A resident with a pass and a good pair of shoes can reach nearly anywhere without any of it.

## Australia

The Australian half of this piece is general background, not drawn from a fetched source, so I will be modest about numbers. Australian cities are built on a different logic. Sydney, Melbourne and Brisbane each have rail networks, buses and in Melbourne's case a famous tram system, and in each of them there is a card to tap on and off. The systems work, often well, but they serve cities that grew outward rather than upward, with suburbs where the nearest station can be a bus ride or a car trip away.

The result is a commuting culture shaped by distance and weather. A long drive is normal, a train ride of an hour or more is not unusual, and the weekend timetable can feel like a polite suggestion. The car is not a luxury but a default, because the geography assumes it. A new arrival from Prague will quickly notice how much of life is organised around parking, petrol and the ability to get from A to B without asking anyone's permission.

There is a freedom in that. You can go where you like, when you like, and nobody will inspect you on the way. But there is also a cost: time stuck in traffic, money spent on a vehicle and a city that is harder to enjoy without one. The tap-on, tap-off card is efficient. The commute is the price.

I do not want to be unfair. Australian transit is improving and it is far better in the inner suburbs than in the outer ones. But as a culture of getting around, it is organised around the individual vehicle, and a place built for cars will always treat the pedestrian as a visitor.

A quick note on tone. When people from Australia move to Prague, the first surprise is the absence of a gate, and the second is the absence of an apology if you are caught without a ticket. In my experience of cities with honour systems, there is always a moment when a tourist is fined for ignorance, and the Czech version is not rude, just exact. When Czechs move to Australia the surprise runs the other way: the card must be topped up, the stop may be a long walk and the next service may be some time off. Neither is a catastrophe. Both are a reminder that a city's transport is the first thing a newcomer learns about its character.

## The Verdict

Prague wins, and the margin is wide. A commute that is cheap, frequent and mostly pleasant is a quiet kind of luxury, and the Czech capital delivers it every day without fuss. You can put a child on a tram, read a book on the metro and arrive without a parking bill.

Australia deserves credit for space, for good weather and for the ease of owning a car once you have bought one. If you love driving and hate crowds, the open road is a fine thing. But the price of that freedom is a day lost to distance.

If I had to commute for twenty years, I would choose the tram. If I had to cross a continent, I would choose the car. The sensible answer is to live somewhere you can do the first.

## Conclusion

A city's transport system is its social contract in motion. Prague's says: pay your fare, stand on the right and we will get you there. Australia's says: here is the road, good luck.

Whichever you choose, learn the rules, buy the pass and keep your ticket where you can find it.

And if a stranger in a Prague tram asks for your ticket, do not argue. You will not win, and he has probably done this before.
