# Ireland Will Make You Homeless to Get You a Bank Account. Moldova Will Just Ask for Your Blood Type.

🇲🇩 Moldova · 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Everyone assumes bureaucracy gets worse the further east you go, as though the EU border is also a border between competence and chaos. I have personally been asked for a blood group certificate by a Moldovan ministry and a household gas bill by an Irish one, and I can report that both institutions were, in their own special ways, equally convinced I was lying. The difference is that Moldova's suspicion is at least consistent — it wants paper, it wants it stamped, it wants it translated, and then it leaves you alone. Ireland's suspicion is performative. It wants to seem modern, all tap-to-pay and fintech unicorns, while quietly running a system where you cannot get a bank account without an address, cannot get an address without a landlord, and cannot get a landlord without a PPS number you cannot get without an address.

Fourteen relocations in, I have learned that "efficient" and "friendly" are not the same adjective, and that a country's banking culture tells you more about its soul than its tourism board ever will. Moldova will make you feel like a suspect. Ireland will make you feel like a ghost wandering MyWelfare.ie, applying for a number that apparently requires proof you already have the life the number is meant to let you start. Pack patience for both. Pack a folder for Moldova. Pack a hotel booking you can pretend is permanent for Ireland.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇲🇩 Moldova

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get every foreign document apostilled and translated into Romanian before you land — it is the single biggest delay | Assume a photocopy will do; originals are demanded at nearly every migration and banking step |
| Keep your EU or home-country account open and tested with real transfers before relying on a new Moldovan one | Expect to open an account remotely as a non-citizen — most digital onboarding is restricted to Moldovan nationals |
| Budget five to fifteen working days for KYC clearance on a personal account, longer for anything business-related | Mix personal and company banking; it multiplies the paperwork and the suspicion |
| Use the fact Moldova joined SEPA in October 2025 — euro transfers in and out are now genuinely easier | Show up without a notarised housing document; several residence steps require one before anything else proceeds |

### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Line up temporary, billable accommodation (even a short-let with your name on a bill) before you arrive, to break the address/PPS deadlock | Expect a hotel receipt to count as proof of address — it almost never does |
| Open a Revolut or N26 account the day you land as a stopgap; three-quarters of Irish adults already bank this way | Rely on a neobank alone forever — fewer than one in twenty use it for their actual salary, and landlords still want a "real" bank |
| Apply for your PPS number the moment you have any address evidence, even a signed tenancy PDF | Assume a traditional bank will set a transfer limit and leave it there; AIB and others have been known to freeze SEPA transfers to Revolut without warning |
| Keep every utility bill and tenancy agreement — Irish bureaucracy runs on paper trails dressed up as a digital country | Walk into a bank branch assuming EU citizenship alone guarantees fast onboarding; non-EU arrivals report materially longer waits |

## Moldova

Moldova's bureaucracy has the decency to be honest about what it is: a 1990s filing cabinet that has grudgingly accepted a few digital stamps. You want a residence permit? There are three separate authorities involved — the National Agency for Employment issuing your work permit, the Bureau for Migration and Asylum handling anything over ninety days, and a residence-permit authority whose exact name depends on which government leaflet you're holding that week. Each wants its own stack: original employment contracts, apostilled criminal records translated into Romanian, HIV test results, and, charmingly, a blood group certificate for family-based applications. Nobody has ever explained to me why the state needs to know my blood type to let my spouse live here, and nobody ever will, because asking "why" is not how this works. You submit, you wait, you comply.

Banking follows the same logic, just with better recent news. Moldova joined SEPA on 6 October 2025, which sounds like a footnote until you've spent an afternoon paying intermediary-bank fees on a hundred-euro transfer. Eight Moldovan banks now sit inside the SEPA network, meaning euro payments in and out of the EU have, for the first time, stopped feeling like a hostage negotiation. The new Digital Nomad Visa, launched the same autumn, is a genuinely sensible piece of policy — a twelve-month renewable permit for remote earners clearing roughly MDL 52,200 a month, provided your contract, payslips, and bank statements all tell the exact same story. They rarely do on the first attempt, and the clarification request that follows can cost you another month.

What nobody tells you is that once you clear the paperwork, Moldovan banks are refreshingly unbothered by you afterwards. There's no passive-aggressive app notification asking you to confirm your life choices, no algorithm freezing your card because you bought two coffees in quick succession. You proved yourself once, exhaustively, and the state and its banks move on. It is bureaucracy as a hazing ritual: brutal at the door, indifferent once you're inside.

## Ireland

Ireland sells itself as the land of fintech unicorns and 24/7 app banking, and then quietly runs its actual bureaucracy like a 1970s parish hall. The PPS number — the master key to employment, welfare, and, in practice, a "real" bank account — officially requires proof of address no older than three months. A utility bill, a tenancy agreement, a bank statement. The problem, well documented across expat.com's Dublin forum, is that you often need the PPS number (or at least a settled address) to get the tenancy in the first place, because landlords won't hand a lease to someone who can't yet prove they're a real, taxed, legible person. Newcomers solve this the same way every time: a short-let with a sympathetic host willing to put a bill in your name, or a week in administrative limbo while a hotel — whose receipts the Department of Social Protection does not accept — quietly bleeds your savings.

The banks, meanwhile, have an identity crisis. Revolut has roughly three million Irish customers and is used by about three-quarters of the adult population, making Ireland the most neobank-penetrated country on earth, and yet fewer than five percent of those people trust it with their actual salary. The traditional banks know this and resent it: recurring threads on r/irishpersonalfinance describe AIB flagging or outright blocking transfers of a couple of thousand euro to Revolut, freezing the card, and requiring a phone call to sort it out — a friction fee charged in pure annoyance rather than cash. AIB, Bank of Ireland, and PTSB finally launched their own faster-payment tool, Zippay, in March 2026, which tells you precisely how far behind they'd fallen. Opening the account itself is simple enough if you're an EU citizen with a passport and a bill. Non-EU arrivals face longer waits and more branch visits, because Ireland's digital reputation and its actual back-office processes are, politely, two different countries.

## The Verdict

If you want a system that respects your time once you've earned its trust, Moldova wins, infuriatingly. Yes, you'll hand over a blood group certificate and a translated criminal record, but clear the gate and the state leaves you be — no apps nagging you, no bank freezing a transfer because the algorithm got nervous. Ireland, by contrast, dresses its bureaucracy in a hoodie and calls it innovation, while running a PPS system that demands proof of a life you can't legally start without the number it's gatekeeping. It is bureaucracy with better marketing and worse logic.

Neither country trusts you. Moldova just says so upfront, in triplicate, apostilled. Ireland insists it's modern while making you prove you have somewhere to sleep before it'll let you get paid. Give me the honest hazing over the friendly one that's secretly worse. Moldova takes it, on paperwork alone.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com, Dublin forum — a recurring theme is the PPS-number catch-22: newcomers need proof of an Irish address to apply, but landlords and letting agents often won't rent to anyone without a PPS number already, so people resort to short-lets or a host's utility bill just to break the loop.*

> *r/irishpersonalfinance — a recurring theme is frustration with AIB flagging or freezing transfers of roughly two thousand euro or more sent to Revolut, forcing a phone call to unblock the card, alongside users quietly moving more of their day-to-day banking to neobanks while keeping a traditional account only for cheques and cash.*

> *Expat relocation guides covering Moldova (incorpore.md, wheretoemigrate.io) — a recurring theme is that KYC checks on foreign applicants hinge on every income document matching exactly across contract, payslips, and bank statements, and that any mismatch triggers a clarification request that can add weeks to account approval; no independently verifiable first-person forum account of this process could be located, so this is flagged as guide-sourced commentary rather than a forum quote.*

## Conclusion

Here is the uncomfortable truth neither country wants printed on its tourism leaflet: bureaucracy is never actually about security, it's about who gets to make you wait. Moldova makes you wait at the beginning, loudly, with forms that ask about your blood type. Ireland makes you wait throughout, quietly, with a PPS system that seems designed by someone who has never personally tried to rent a flat. Pick your poison based on temperament. If you like your pain concentrated and your bureaucrats honest about hating paperwork as much as you do, go east. If you prefer your friction spread thin across eighteen months and delivered with a smile and a Revolut referral code, stay west. Either way, open two bank accounts before you arrive, photograph every document twice, and never, under any circumstances, believe a relocation agent who tells you "it's usually quick." It is never, anywhere, quick. That's the one fact both systems agree on.
