# Sunday Is Sacred in One Country and Strictly Optional in the Other

🇵🇭 Philippines 🇭🇰 Hong Kong
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have spent weekends in sixty-odd countries now, and I can tell you the two most reliable tells for how a culture actually feels about leisure: what happens to the streets on a Sunday morning, and whether anyone apologises for taking a nap. The Philippines does not apologise. Hong Kong does not nap — it hikes, aggressively, in full technical gear, up a hill it could have seen from its own kitchen window.

Put these two side by side and you get a masterclass in contrast dressed up as a long weekend. One treats leisure as a communal, slightly chaotic family event that starts with mass and ends with karaoke at 1am. The other treats leisure as a KPI: hike completed, dim sum consumed, 11,000 steps logged, back at your desk by Monday looking suspiciously rested. Both are right. Neither will admit the other has a point.

## Do's & Don'ts: Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the fiesta invitation even if you've never met the host's extended family — you're family now | Expect a two-person weekend plan to stay a two-person weekend plan; it will become twelve |
| Learn to love karaoke, sincerely, even if you can't sing — especially if you can't sing | Schedule anything important on a Sunday morning; church comes first, always |
| Treat the mall as a legitimate leisure destination, not a last resort | Assume "let's go to the province" means a day trip; it means a weekend, minimum |

## Do's & Don'ts: Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy proper hiking shoes in your first month — the trails are real mountains, not park paths | Wear your office blazer to a Sunday hike because you assumed it would be a stroll |
| Book dim sum for Sunday brunch at least a week out, or queue for ninety minutes like everyone else | Be surprised when Central's public squares fill with picnicking domestic workers on Sundays — it's their one day off, and they've earned the space |
| Pack a packable rain shell; the weather turns on a dime above 300 metres | Mistake the hiking obsession for relaxation — people train for these trails like they're training for a promotion |

## The Philippines: Leisure as a Full-Contact Family Sport

Here is the thing nobody tells you about Filipino weekends: they are not yours. You do not own your Saturday. Your Saturday belongs to your lola, your three sets of cousins, the barangay fiesta committee, and a godchild's christening you somehow agreed to attend six months ago and forgot about entirely. The Philippine Statistics Authority's household surveys consistently show leisure time clustering around family and religious activity rather than solitary pursuits, and anyone who has actually lived through a Filipino Sunday can confirm this without needing a chart. Mass in the morning — the country is overwhelmingly Catholic, and Sunday attendance is less a personal choice than a social expectation enforced by everyone's grandmother simultaneously — then lunch that somehow absorbs three more households, then an afternoon that dissolves into either a mall trip or a drive to the province that was billed as "just for the day" and is very much not just for the day.

The mall deserves its own paragraph, because nowhere else on earth has turned air-conditioned retail into a genuine leisure institution quite like Manila has. SM malls are not shopping centres; they are weather systems. Whole weekends orbit around them — cinema, karaoke booth, ice rink, church chapel (yes, inside the mall), food court reunion — because in a country where the heat is a legitimate design constraint and the traffic makes cross-town errands a half-day commitment, the mall becomes the rational, climate-controlled answer to "where do twelve people with wildly different interests go to have a nice afternoon together." Then there's karaoke, which is not a party trick here, it's a national emotional register. You will be handed a microphone. Resistance is both futile and rude. And underneath all of it is "bayanihan" — the communal spirit of showing up for each other — which means your weekend plans are never really final until every relative within a two-hour radius has had a say.

## Hong Kong: Leisure With a Training Schedule

Hong Kong's relationship with the weekend is, frankly, a bit neurotic, and I say that with the deep affection of someone who has personally queued ninety minutes for har gow. This is a city where seventy percent of the land is undeveloped countryside — actual mountains, actual coastline, an extraordinary network of country parks that most tourists never learn exists because they're too busy photographing neon signs three streets from their hotel. And Hong Kongers, having spent the working week packed into some of the densest, most vertical real estate on the planet, respond on Saturday by fleeing straight up a hill. Dragon's Back, the MacLehose Trail, Lion Rock — these fill at dawn with people who look, and often are, more seriously kitted out than you'd expect for a day hike, because here leisure has absorbed the city's own work ethic. You don't just go for a walk. You train. You track your pace. You compare notes afterward like it was a quarterly review.

Then there's dim sum, which is the one leisure ritual that slows everything down — an entire extended family or friend group parked for two unhurried hours over trolleys of har gow and char siu bao, newspapers optional, conversation mandatory. And there's a quieter, more specific Sunday ritual worth naming honestly rather than glossing over: Central and the areas around Statue Square and Chater Road fill, every single Sunday, with Filipino and Indonesian domestic workers — hundreds of thousands of them work in Hong Kong, and Sunday is contractually their one day off. They picnic, they sing, they do each other's hair, they build an entire temporary community out of cardboard mats on marble pavement, and newcomers to the city who don't know this history tend to either gawk or complain about "crowding." Don't be that newcomer. It's one of the most quietly moving things Hong Kong does with its public space, and it says more about the city's labour economy than any tourist board will.

## The Verdict

If you make me pick — and apparently I have to, because that's the format — I'm giving this to the Philippines, and not by a small margin. Hong Kong's weekend is impressive, genuinely, in the way a well-run logistics operation is impressive. You will get fit. You will eat extremely well. You will, at some point, develop opinions about trekking poles you never asked to have. But it is still, underneath the scenery, a city optimising its rest the same way it optimises everything else, and optimised rest is a contradiction I've never fully trusted. The Philippines, by contrast, has a weekend culture built entirely around not being alone, around obligation shading into genuine warmth, around the specific joy of a karaoke mic being thrust into your hand by someone who loves you and will not be taking no for an answer. It's messier. It's less photogenic. It also, when you're the one twelve people deep into a fiesta you didn't plan, feels considerably more like being alive than the 847th photo of a trail marker ever will.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/HongKong — someone new to the city asked, genuinely baffled, why the trailheads were "basically a car park at 6:45am on a Sunday," and the top reply was essentially: welcome, this is the city's actual church service.*

> *Internations Manila forum — a longtime expat described turning down a provincial weekend trip once, politely, and spending the following two months being gently, relentlessly re-invited by people he'd met exactly once.*

> *expat.com Hong Kong forum — a newcomer admitted feeling awkward about the Sunday crowds of domestic workers in Central until a longer-term resident explained the labour context, after which, in their words, they "never looked at Statue Square the same way again — in a good way."*

## Conclusion

Neither of these countries is going to change your personality, but they will absolutely rearrange your Saturday, and that's really what this column is for. Move to Hong Kong and you'll own a pair of hiking shoes within a month whether you asked for them or not. Move to Manila and you'll have opinions on a karaoke song you'll never admit to rehearsing in the shower. The real lesson, as always, is that "leisure" is not a universal concept — it's a culturally specific answer to the question of what a week of work owes you, and the two answers on offer here couldn't be further apart. Pick the one that matches your actual nervous system, not the one that photographs better. Your weekends will thank you.
