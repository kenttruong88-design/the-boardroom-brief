# One City Is Measuring Its Parks In Square Metres Per Person, The Other Is Measuring Them In Millions Of Trees

🇪🇬 Egypt vs 🇦🇪 UAE

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Cairo has 0.74 square metres of green space per resident. The World Health Organization's bare minimum recommendation is 9. Dubai, a city built on sand that had to import the concept of "lawn" the way other places import wine, is currently promising its residents 11 square metres per person by 2040 and has already added 1.7 million square metres of new green space in a single reporting period. Sit with that for a second: the desert city is out-parking the city on the Nile. This is the sort of detail that should feature on a government's annual report under the heading "things we did not expect to have to say out loud."

Egypt didn't lose its green space by accident — it lost it to roads, bridges, and the slow, deliberate math of treating parks as the most negotiable item on any city planner's list. The UAE, by contrast, has treated artificial abundance as a point of national pride, the same way it treats indoor skiing: an expensive flex that happens to also be genuinely pleasant to use. Neither approach is "natural." One is managed decline. The other is managed excess. Both will leave you standing in a park at golden hour wondering how much the view actually cost someone, and in Dubai's case, you can usually find the exact figure in a press release.

## Egypt: A Capital That Has Been Quietly Losing Its Lungs

The headline figure is almost unbelievable until you've actually tried to find a park in Cairo: 0.74 square metres of green space per resident, against a World Health Organization minimum recommendation of 9 square metres and an ideal target closer to 50. Since 2006, the city has lost more than 2.1 million square metres of green space, and a 2022 Al-Azhar University study found that 40 of 57 examined parks had become physically harder to reach, not because they'd vanished, but because road expansions and overpasses had lengthened the walking routes around them into irrelevance. Al-Azbakeya Park, once a genuine civic landmark, has lost 60 to 80% of its original footprint since the mid-20th century. In Heliopolis, roughly 2,500 mature trees were removed in 2019–2020 alone to accommodate road and bridge projects linking the city to the New Administrative Capital — a decision that reads, charitably, as a city choosing connectivity over shade, and uncharitably as a city that has simply stopped pricing green space as an asset at all.

Al-Azhar Park is the honourable exception, and it's a genuinely remarkable one: 74 acres built on a former landfill, opened in 2005, and treated by almost every green-space article written about Cairo since as Exhibit A for what the city could be doing more of. It just isn't doing more of it. Diplomatic household reports from Cairo describe the daily reality in blunter terms — one 2010 respondent noted flatly that there is "just one park with green spaces in the whole city," another called it "not very green, and very very dusty — everything is dusty," and a third observed that "very few people have gardens." One report advised bringing indoor activities for children specifically because they "will not just be able to run outside and play after school" — a sentence that, in most expat-relocation contexts, signals a city has quietly deprioritised something most families assume is a given. The workaround most long-term residents land on is small-scale: balcony planters, rooftop gardens, or simply booking a flight to somewhere greener when the dust gets to be too much. Cyprus, incidentally, is about an hour away by air, and more than one Cairo household report treats that fact as a genuine coping strategy rather than a punchline.

The irony is that Cairo wasn't always short on green ambition. Garden City, the leafy diplomatic quarter laid out in the early 20th century, was explicitly designed around curving, tree-lined streets and private gardens, and the Nile Corniche itself was conceived as a public promenade people would actually want to walk. Both still exist, technically, but decades of informal construction, traffic infrastructure and sheer population pressure have hemmed them in until the original intent is barely legible. It's less that Cairo never valued green space, and more that it kept quietly trading it away for something else, one overpass at a time, until the trade became the default.

## UAE: Green Space As A Government KPI

Where Cairo lost its parks to infrastructure, the UAE built its parks as infrastructure — deliberately, expensively, and with the kind of specificity usually reserved for corporate quarterly targets. Dubai Municipality's Director General, Marwan bin Ghalita, announced a 2040 plan to plant approximately 3.5 million trees, expand green space to 187 square kilometres, hit 11 square metres of green space per capita, and draw 95 million annual visits to parks and recreational facilities across the emirate. This is not vague civic optimism; it's a dashboard. Dubai has already reported adding 1.7 million square metres of new green space and planting 170,000 trees in a single year. None of this is "natural" in any meaningful sense — it's irrigated, engineered, and maintained at real environmental and financial cost in a desert climate — but it is unambiguously there, and residents use it hard.

The actual culture of use is seasonal and specific. Winter is when the parks genuinely come alive: Sharjah National Park becomes what one reporting piece called "a perennial hangout for families come winter," full of picnics and barbecues, while Abu Dhabi runs at least 28 public parks with dedicated BBQ spots and keeps its Corniche Park open 24 hours, free of charge. An expat forum thread on Dubai's barbecue culture is almost comically specific about the hierarchy of preferred locations — one poster ranked Mushriff Park as "very good and peaceful" while dismissing Mamzar Beach Park as "not our type," only for another commenter to immediately counter that Mamzar is, in their words, "the best place for bbq," which tells you everything about how seriously this subject is taken. Zabeel Park hosts impromptu cricket matches and seasonal artisan markets; Ras Al Khaimah's Pawsome Park has become a magnet for dog owners from across neighbouring emirates. Summer, by contrast, empties these spaces almost entirely — the heat simply wins, and diplomatic household reports from Abu Dhabi note families often leave the country altogether for months because "there is little for them to do in the heat." It's abundance with an asterisk: extraordinary when the climate allows it, irrelevant when it doesn't.

## The Verdict

The UAE wins on raw access, and it isn't close — you cannot out-argue 1.7 million square metres of new green space with sentiment about authenticity. But Egypt's 0.74 square metres per person is a genuine, quantifiable loss, not a cultural quirk, and no amount of UAE irrigation budget changes the fact that Cairo is actively worse off than it was twenty years ago, while Dubai is actively, aggressively better. I'll say the unfashionable thing: manufactured abundance, built with intent and maintained with municipal discipline, beats organic scarcity every time. Nobody gets nostalgia points for a park that used to exist.

## Conclusion

Egypt will give you history, light, and the Nile at dusk, and absolutely nowhere comfortable to sit and enjoy any of it unless you've already found the one park that counts. The UAE will give you a government-engineered oasis with a press release attached, usable for roughly six months of the year and genuinely lovely for all of them. Pick your trade-off: authenticity with no shade, or artifice with excellent shade. I know which one I'm bringing a picnic blanket to.
