# One Country Sweats Through It, The Other Simply Refuses To Stay Indoors

🇯🇵 Japan 🇳🇴 Norway

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Weather doesn't just happen to a country; a country decides, culturally, what to do about it, and Japan and Norway have landed on two of the most committed, opposite responses I've encountered in fourteen relocations. Japan's summers are an genuine endurance event — punishing humidity, heat that clings rather than simply arrives, a typhoon season that reshapes travel plans with real regularity — and the coping culture that's built up around it is intricate, product-driven, and taken completely seriously. Norway's answer to its own extreme — long, dark, cold winters, some regions losing the sun entirely for weeks — isn't indoor hibernation, which is what half the world assumes, but friluftsliv: a deeply held, almost philosophical commitment to going outside anyway, dressed properly, treating the cold as scenery rather than an obstacle.

Japan's summer heat is not abstract discomfort; locals and longtime residents alike describe specific, learned survival tactics, because the combination of high humidity and rising temperatures makes Japanese summers feel considerably worse than the thermometer alone would suggest, a fact repeatedly covered by both local meteorological reporting and the cottage industry of guides written specifically to help newcomers survive their first one. Layered onto the heat is typhoon season, which brings its own rhythm of tracked storm paths, train suspensions, and convenience-store runs for supplies that long-term residents treat as a genuinely normal seasonal chore, not a crisis each time. Norway's darkness, meanwhile, isn't something locals white-knuckle through waiting for spring; friluftsliv — loosely, "free-air life" — functions as something closer to cultural doctrine, the deeply held belief that time outdoors, regardless of temperature or daylight, is essential to wellbeing, a concept that's been increasingly discussed internationally as a genuine strategy for surviving long, dark winters with your mental health intact.

## Do's & Don'ts: Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in a proper hand fan, cooling towel, or portable neck fan — these aren't novelty items, they're genuinely standard summer kit | Underestimate humidity's effect on how heat actually feels — a "manageable" temperature on paper can feel brutal in practice |
| Track typhoon paths during the season and build flexibility into travel plans — train suspensions are common and locals plan around them, not against them | Skip hydration and salt intake during peak summer — heatstroke is a genuine, commonly discussed seasonal risk, not an exaggerated warning |
| Use convenience stores' seasonal cooling products (cooling sheets, drinks, sprays) — they exist because the demand is real and constant | Assume air conditioning is universal or always run aggressively — many spaces, including some homes, use it more sparingly than visitors expect |

## Do's & Don'ts: Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in genuinely good winter layers — the Norwegian saying "there's no bad weather, only bad clothing" is stated as literal fact, not proverb | Treat the dark months as a reason to stay inside — locals actively schedule outdoor time as a non-negotiable part of the week, even in polar night |
| Try a cabin (hytte) weekend if invited — it's a core cultural institution, not just a tourist activity | Assume everyone experiences the same darkness — daylight hours vary significantly by region, and far northern areas see genuine weeks-long polar night |
| Embrace outdoor exercise or walks specifically during the darkest weeks — it's treated as protective for mood, not optional extra effort | Underestimate how much locals plan around light — headlamps, reflective gear, and timing outdoor activity around whatever daylight exists are all taken seriously |

## Japan: Heat As A Managed, Seasonal Ordeal

Japanese summer coping culture has the texture of a well-rehearsed annual ritual, because it is one — humidity levels that make even moderate temperatures feel oppressive have produced an entire seasonal economy of cooling products, from handheld and neck-worn fans sold everywhere from convenience stores to train station kiosks, to cooling towels, sprays, and drinks engineered specifically for the season. Long-term residents describe the learning curve bluntly: your first Japanese summer teaches you, often the hard way, that "hot" in a humid climate behaves completely differently from dry heat, and that hydration and salt intake aren't optional caution but a real, actively discussed precaution against heatstroke, a risk taken seriously enough to feature in regular public health messaging. Layered on top of the heat itself is typhoon season, which brings a different, more logistical kind of coping: tracked storm paths, train operators pre-announcing suspensions well before a storm actually arrives, and a general, practised calm among residents who've learned to treat "the trains might stop tomorrow" as a normal planning input rather than a crisis. None of this reads as complaint, exactly — it reads as a culture that has simply, thoroughly optimised its response to an annual ordeal it can't change, armed with better gear and better information each year.

## Norway: Darkness As Scenery, Not Obstacle

Norway's weather culture inverts almost every assumption a newcomer arrives with. The instinct, coming from a culture that treats cold and dark as reasons to stay indoors, is to hibernate through the Norwegian winter — and that instinct is, by every local account, exactly wrong. Friluftsliv treats time outdoors as essential regardless of season, a belief embedded deeply enough in national identity that international media has repeatedly returned to it as a genuine model for winter mental health, particularly during periods when indoor isolation became a global concern. The oft-quoted local saying — that there's no such thing as bad weather, only bad clothing — isn't recited ironically; it's treated as straightforwardly, literally true, and Norwegians invest accordingly in genuinely excellent outdoor gear from childhood, layering systems that make sub-zero walks, ski outings, or simply standing outside at a cabin genuinely comfortable rather than an endurance test. The hytte — the Norwegian cabin, often remote, often without full modern amenities by choice — functions as a core cultural institution precisely because it forces outdoor engagement: fetching wood, walking, skiing, simply being outside in weather that would send many cultures straight back indoors. In the far north, where polar night strips weeks of direct sunlight entirely, the commitment to outdoor time doesn't relax; if anything, locals describe it becoming more deliberate, scheduled activity replacing what daylight alone might otherwise have prompted spontaneously.

## The Verdict

Japan has built the more sophisticated *endurance* culture — gear, information, and seasonal rhythm all optimised to help people survive an genuinely brutal, humid heat with good humour and functioning convenience stores. Norway has built something closer to a philosophy — not surviving the dark and cold but actively, doctrinally refusing to let it diminish quality of life, treating the outdoors as non-negotiable regardless of conditions. If you want a culture that's engineered clever, practical solutions to a genuinely oppressive annual climate event, Japan's summer playbook is hard to beat. If you want a culture that's decided, collectively, that weather simply isn't a good enough excuse to stay inside, Norway will hand you a headlamp and expect you outside by 4pm, dark or not.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/japanlife — paraphrased: a longtime resident said their first Tokyo summer taught them the hard way that "hot" needed an entirely new personal definition, and that a neck fan went from novelty purchase to daily essential within about a week.*

> *Substack "Tani" newsletter — paraphrased: a writer described tracking typhoon paths as routinely as checking a regular weather app, noting that locals discuss potential train suspensions with the same calm as discussing whether it might drizzle.*

> *Expat.com Oslo forum — paraphrased: a newcomer admitted dreading their first Norwegian winter, only to find colleagues genuinely confused by the idea of skipping an outdoor walk just because it was dark at 3pm, calling the adjustment "less about surviving winter and more about unlearning the urge to hide from it."*

## Conclusion

Japan's summer will humble you with humidity you didn't know was possible, and hand you a full toolkit — fans, cooling sheets, typhoon trackers — to get through it with dignity intact. Norway's winter will strip away your excuses entirely, insisting, gently but firmly, that the right jacket solves more problems than staying indoors ever will. Pack a cooling towel for Tokyo. Pack real wool layers for Oslo. Both countries have made peace with weather most people would simply avoid, and both, in their own climate-specific way, refuse to let it win.
