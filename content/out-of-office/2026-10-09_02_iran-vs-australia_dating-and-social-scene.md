# One Country Dates Through Its Mother. The Other Dates Through an App at the Pub.

🇮🇷 Iran 🇦🇺 Australia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Dating, properly understood, is just a culture's values wearing a slightly nicer shirt. Show me how two strangers are meant to fall for each other in a given country and I'll show you exactly what that country actually believes about family, risk, and who's allowed to be in charge of their own evening. Iran and Australia, as it happens, sit at opposite ends of this particular spectrum, and both would be baffled — genuinely baffled — by how the other one does it.

In Iran, the family is in the room, sometimes literally, often rhetorically, always eventually. In Australia, the family finds out roughly three weeks after you've already introduced the person as your "partner" at a barbecue. One culture formalises romance into something closer to a negotiation; the other treats it as a slightly chaotic group activity best conducted over beer. I have done both. Only one of them ended with someone's auntie assessing my employment history within the first ten minutes.

## Do's & Don'ts: Iran

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand that "khastegari" — a formal family visit to discuss marriage — is still a live tradition, not a historical curiosity | Expect public affection to be read as casual; it carries real social and legal weight here |
| Use VPN-accessible apps the way locals quietly do, and treat them as genuinely discreet, not a loophole | Assume every young Iranian follows the traditional script; attitudes in Tehran diverge sharply from smaller cities |
| Let family involvement signal seriousness of intent, not a lack of romantic agency | Mistake formality for coldness — the matchmaking process is often warm, funny, and deeply personal once you're inside it |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the "shout" — buying a round for the group — it's a genuine social contract, not just a drink order | Expect a first date to be a quiet, formal dinner; it's more likely a pub, a beach, or a group hangout that may or may not be a date |
| Be direct about where you stand; Australians generally prefer blunt clarity over romantic ambiguity | Panic if the relationship stays undefined for months — "seeing each other" is a legitimate, unhurried category here |
| Swipe confidently — dating apps are the dominant first-contact method, not a backup plan | Assume meeting the parents means anything is official; in Australia it can happen embarrassingly early and mean very little |

## Iran: Romance as a Family Project

The single biggest adjustment for anyone dating in Iran is accepting that the family isn't a hurdle to clear before the relationship starts — it's a participant in the relationship itself, from very early on. The khastegari tradition, where a man's family formally visits a woman's family to discuss a potential marriage, is alive and well, particularly outside the most cosmopolitan pockets of Tehran, and it reframes the entire emotional sequence Westerners are used to. You don't date your way into a decision; the decision — or at least the serious possibility of one — tends to come first, and the getting-to-know-you happens inside that frame, chaperoned, earnest, occasionally very funny once you stop being terrified of it.

That said, treating Iran as a monolith here would be dishonest, and Suki doesn't do dishonest. Tehran's younger, more secular crowd has built an entire parallel social life — underground parties, mixed-gender gatherings behind closed doors, dating apps accessed through VPNs with the kind of practised casualness that would make a Silicon Valley privacy engineer weep. Social media and messaging apps have become genuine courtship infrastructure, precisely because public space carries legal restrictions on mixed-gender interaction and public displays of affection that simply don't exist in most of the world. The result is a dating culture operating on two tracks simultaneously: a visible, family-sanctioned formal path, and a much more private, self-directed one running quietly underneath it. Neither track is "the real Iran." Both are.

## Australia: Romance as a Group Sport Played at the Pub

Australia, by contrast, treats dating with the kind of relaxed, low-stakes informality that can genuinely disorient anyone arriving from a more structured courtship culture. The pub is not incidental to Australian dating — it is the actual venue, the neutral ground where "are we dating or are we just mates who drink together" gets decided, slowly, over an indeterminate number of rounds. The "shout," where everyone takes a turn buying a round for the group, isn't just a drinking custom; it's a small test of reciprocity and social ease that Australians read instinctively and newcomers routinely fumble by either refusing to participate (rude) or trying to shout every single round (suspicious).

Dating apps have thoroughly colonised the first-contact phase here — Hinge and Bumble dominate the capital cities' social scenes the way khastegari dominates Iranian family life, just with vastly lower formal stakes and a much higher tolerance for ambiguity. Australians will happily exist in an undefined "seeing each other" state for months, resist the DTR (define-the-relationship) conversation with real commitment, and still somehow introduce you to their mates as a near-permanent fixture long before anything is official. It's a culture that prizes directness in speech — ask an Australian what they think and you will be told, bluntly, immediately — paired strangely with real avoidance around formalising a relationship's actual status. The beach, the barbecue, and the pub do the emotional work that a formal sit-down conversation would do elsewhere. Just don't expect anyone to name it.

## The Verdict

I'm not going to pretend these two are playing the same game, so picking a "winner" feels almost beside the point — but you've read this far, so here it is. Australia wins on sheer livability: nobody's auntie is assessing your salary over tea, and the stakes of a bad first date are approximately zero, which is its own kind of freedom. But Iran wins, and wins decisively, on depth of intention. When a khastegari visit happens, everyone in that room knows exactly why they're there and what's actually being discussed, which is more honesty than you'll get out of six months of "just seeing where things go" in Sydney. Australia optimises for low-pressure fun. Iran optimises for high-stakes clarity, wrapped in formality that looks rigid from outside and often isn't, once you're actually inside it. If I had to live inside one of these systems permanently, I'd pick Australia for my sanity and Iran for my wedding.

## Conclusion

What strikes me most, having now dated — badly, in both cases — across cultures this different, is that neither country would recognise the other's approach as "dating" at all. Iran would see Australia's version as aimless. Australia would see Iran's version as alarmingly serious for a Tuesday. But scratch beneath the surface and both are solving the same problem — how do two people figure out if they want a life together — with the tools their culture actually trusts: family wisdom in one case, personal instinct lubricated by beer in the other. Neither is backward. Neither is enlightened. They're just two very different bets on who should be allowed to make the call.
