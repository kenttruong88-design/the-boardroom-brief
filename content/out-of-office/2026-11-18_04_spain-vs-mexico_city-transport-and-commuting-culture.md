# Madrid Runs Its Metro Like a Swiss Watch and Mexico City Runs Its Like a Mosh Pit With a Subscription

🇪🇸 Spain vs 🇲🇽 Mexico

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two kinds of commuter in the Spanish-speaking world. One checks the bus-arrival screen, taps a card, and sits on a seat in a carriage cooled to a reasonable temperature. The other negotiates a platform, enters a train that appears to contain a second train, and arrives at work having practised an entire martial art. Madrid and Mexico City share a language and a taste for late dinners. They do not share a commute.

The Spanish capital has the quiet satisfaction of a city that has spent decades tidying itself up. Mexico City has the exhausted brilliance of a metropolis of twenty-odd million that has made public transport into a local sport. Neither is a fraud. But only one of them will let you read a book.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Spain**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a reusable Tarjeta Multi and top it up | Pay for single tickets every day |
| Check the bus arrival screens for GPS times | Run for a bus that is nowhere near |
| Mind your pockets on Cercanías at peak hours | Hang your phone out of a back pocket |
| Register for the eco-sticker if you drive | Enter the low-emission zones without one |

**Mexico**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the women-only carriages if relevant | Squeeze into the mixed carriage at rush hour on principle |
| Book rides through Uber or Didi | Hail a random taxi off the street |
| Learn the Metrobus lines and expect a crush | Expect a seat at peak |
| Carry small change and a charged phone | Count on elevators or announcements |

## Spain

Madrid is a transport system that works, which is the nicest and most boring thing one can say about any city. The Expat.com guide describes a metro with twelve lines, three light-rail lines and more than three hundred stations, running from about six in the morning to two at night. EMT buses number over two thousand vehicles across 227 lines, including 30 night routes, the *búhos* that run from midnight to six. Metro and bus are integrated on a reusable Tarjeta Multi, and regular commuters are told to buy a monthly pass. A single bus ticket, according to the guides, cost around €1.50 and a zone-A metro ticket between €1.50 and €2.00.

Beyond the centre are the Cercanías commuter trains, which the guides price from €1.70 to €5.50 by zone and which run from about five in the morning to midnight. Taxis start at €2.40 with a per-kilometre rate of a little over a euro. Drivers need an eco-sticker for the low-emission zones, and the parking zones are colour-coded in a way that rewards the patient reader.

The people on the ground are happy. A French expat on the Expat.com forum, used to the Paris Metro, says Madrid's is cleaner, calmer and less crowded, with working escalators, although it opens around six, which is awkward for early flights. A long-time resident says the buses and metro are efficient and among Europe's cheapest, adds that buses show GPS arrival times and warns that the metro has an extraordinary number of stairs. Another forum member insists it is safe late at night. A fourth corrects the opening hours: the metro closes around half past one and reopens about half past five.

The deeper point is that Madrid's system is designed for a person with a schedule. Integrated fares mean the cost of changing from bus to metro is invisible, and the night buses mean a late dinner does not end in a taxi negotiation. It is not glamorous, but it is the quiet foundation of the famous Spanish evening: you can stay out until the small hours precisely because the city has quietly arranged for you to get home. Nothing says European civilisation like a *búho* at two in the morning.

That final detail is the real Madrid. Everyone has an opinion on the timetable, nobody agrees, and the trains run anyway. The one thing the system lacks is drama, which suits me fine, as I have a book I never finish.

## Mexico

Mexico City is a different species. ExpatExchange's guide counts twelve metro lines, 195 stations and a flat fare of five pesos, about 25 US cents. The Metrobús bus-rapid-transit system has seven lines, with fares of six to thirty pesos depending on distance, and buses with cameras and GPS. There are fifteen trolleybus routes at four pesos and a single light-rail line from Tasqueña to Xochimilco at three pesos. Uber and Didi are widely used, with surge pricing at peak, and the guide advises using only stand-based or app-booked taxis. It describes the metro as generally safe, even at night, but very crowded at peak, with women-only carriages. The author concludes that living without a car is feasible.

The lived version is louder. A visitor's thread on Reddit's r/TTC found the Metrobús frequent and easy to board and felt reassured by officers at the gates, but criticised unannounced stops, stairs, elevators that are hard to find, the chaos of boarding minibuses, fares charged again at each transfer and aggressive driving. Commenters said the metro is underfunded and poorly maintained, the stations are very crowded, and the Metrobús lines are crushed. That poster is a visitor, not an expat, and I want to be precise about that. I have not found a real long-term Mexico City commuter in my research, which is a gap, not a verdict.

Mexico City's other transport reality is the informal layer, the minibuses and shared vehicles that fill the gaps between formal lines. The visitor's thread called boarding chaotic, and I will not pretend otherwise. But chaos is also an adaptive system, and it is how a city this large moves people to places the rails never reached. A newcomer should treat it as advanced coursework: begin with the metro and the Metrobús, graduate to ride-hailing for night trips and attempt the minibus only with a local who can tell you where to get off.

But the economics are staggering. A five-peso metro ride is a policy decision as much as a price, and it means millions of people can cross a gigantic city on a coin. There are trade-offs: crowding, delays and the occasional need to be a particular kind of assertive. The system is cheap, enormous and under strain, and it belongs to the people who use it in a way Madrid's tidy cards do not.

## The Verdict

There is a cultural difference in how the two cities talk about their transport, too. Madrileños complain about small things: a late bus, a lift that is out, an unusually long stair. Mexico City complains about big things, such as crowding, funding and safety, and then carries on with a patience that borders on the heroic. One city has a service to criticise; the other has a survival skill to teach.

Madrid wins the commute, and it is not close. It has the frequency, the information, the integration and the predictability. You can plan a life around it. If you work in an office with a start time and a manager who minds, this is your city.

Mexico City wins the argument. A five-peso fare that moves a metropolis is a civic achievement, and the system's sheer scale is humbling. It also teaches you things, such as how to hold a rail in a moving crowd, how to read a map without a phone and how to make a seat appear by looking tired.

If I had to commute every day, I would take Madrid and tip my hat to Mexico City from an Uber. If I wanted to understand a country, I would ride the metro in Mexico City once a week, with my bag in front of me.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com forum, French expat in Madrid — compared with the Paris Metro, Madrid's is cleaner, calmer and less crowded with working escalators, though it opens around six, which is awkward for early flights.*

> *Expat.com forum, another Madrid poster — the metro closes around half past one and reopens about half past five, so check before you rely on it after a late dinner.*

> *Reddit r/TTC, a visitor to Mexico City (not an expat) — the Metrobús was frequent and easy to board, but stops were unannounced, elevators hard to find and fares charged again at every transfer.*

## Conclusion

A commute is a daily referendum on how a city treats you. Madrid says: here is a card, here is a timetable, enjoy your seat. Mexico City says: here is a coin, here is a crowd, make yourself useful.

Move to Madrid and you will gain forty minutes of your life each week. Move to Mexico City and you will gain a stronger core, a better sense of humour and a very fine new appreciation for personal space. I would take the first on a Monday and the second on a Saturday, and I would keep my wallet in the front pocket in both.
