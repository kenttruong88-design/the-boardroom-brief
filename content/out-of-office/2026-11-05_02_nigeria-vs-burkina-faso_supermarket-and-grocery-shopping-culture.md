# One of These Countries Lost Its Entire Supermarket Industry. The Other Never Really Had One to Lose.

🇳🇬🇧🇫 Nigeria and Burkina Faso, reviewed by a woman who has bargained for butter on three continents and lost every time

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular kind of grief reserved for watching a supermarket die, and Nigeria has just finished grieving one. Shoprite — the South African chain that spent nearly twenty years convincing Lagosians that a trolley and fluorescent lighting constituted modern life — shut its final outlet this year, citing forex shortages, import tariffs and the kind of logistics costs that make a tin of chickpeas a geopolitical event. Burkina Faso, meanwhile, never had the luxury of that particular heartbreak, because its entire formal supermarket sector rests on one chain that started in 1989 as somebody's family business and is now propped up by actual World Bank money. Both countries will sell you groceries. Neither will let you forget, for even one shopping trip, exactly how precarious that sentence is.

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|

## Nigeria: The Supermarket Experiment Has Concluded, Thank You for Your Participation

Let's start with the body. Shoprite arrived in Nigeria in 2005, eventually operated roughly 25 stores across 13 states, sold its stake to a local consortium in 2021, and by early this year had closed its last surviving outlet — a near-twenty-year run ended by forex shortages, import tariffs, punishing logistics costs, and inflation, with empty shelves visibly foreshadowing the collapse as early as 2024. The sector-wide hit is estimated at ₦2.5 trillion. I want to be clear about what that means in practice: the entire concept of "popping out for a Western-style supermarket run" has, over two decades, been tried, scaled, and formally retired in Africa's largest economy. That is not a small thing to watch happen in real time.

What's replaced it, functionally, is a two-tier survival system. There's still food inflation — 21.87% as of August 2025, having mercifully eased from 37.52% the year before — but Nigeria's statistics agency feels obliged to clarify, with the weary patience of someone who has explained this before, that a falling inflation *rate* does not mean falling prices. It means prices are rising more slowly. That's not a technicality; it's a worldview. And the regional spread is genuinely startling: food inflation hit 36.67% in Borno and just 3.3% in Zamfara in the same month, in the same country, which means "grocery shopping in Nigeria" isn't one experience, it's several dozen, and anyone who tells you otherwise hasn't left Lagos.

On the ground, expats describe a workaround economy. Imported goods, when they're available, frequently arrive "melted, expired, or crushed" and still cost more than they would at home — one couple reported spending $150 a week buying only cheap staples. A kilo of apples in Abuja runs about $9; a tub of Philadelphia cream cheese, $8. The documented fix for the inevitable "expat markup" is to send your driver or housekeeper to shop instead, because vendors price differently for a Nigerian employee than for the foreigner who employs them. Bargaining isn't a skill here, it's an obligation — "wheeling and dealing is a big thing," as one forum poster puts it — and cash, not card, remains the operating currency, owing to a persistent local fraud problem that's made "cash rules" into something closer to a survival instinct than a preference. The one unambiguous bright spot, repeated by multiple sources independently: the farmer's markets genuinely beat the supermarkets, on produce quality and on price, no asterisk required.

## Burkina Faso: One Chain Is Doing All the Work

If Nigeria's story is a slow-motion collapse, Burkina Faso's is a story about scarcity that never had room to collapse in the first place. Marina Market — founded in 1989 as a small family operation and now running six supermarkets and shopping centres across Burkina Faso, Ghana and Niger — is close to a monopoly on what a "modern retail" grocery experience even looks like here, to the point that the International Finance Corporation, the World Bank's private-sector investment arm, put more than €800,000 into building it out further, explicitly framed as infrastructure for a market segment "still limited." When a chain family-owned since the Reagan administration needs World Bank money to expand, you're not looking at a competitive retail sector. You're looking at the retail sector.

That hasn't stopped it from opening with genuine fanfare — a new "Super U ZAD" centre in Ouagadougou launched this March stocking more than 30,000 items, deliberately blending "Made in Burkina Faso" branded goods with imports, creating 600 direct jobs and 3,000 indirect ones, with the military-junta government publicly leaning on the opening as a confidence signal for private investment. But the lived reality, reported with refreshing specificity by Burkina Faso's own state newspaper, Sidwaya, is a lot less triumphant: a tomato tin rising from 550 to 600 CFA at a Larlé grocery, Blue Band butter jumping from 750 to 900 CFA, and a supermarket manager — Majd Hachem, quoted by name — publicly begging customers to come back and calling for prices to drop so he can "recuperate" his clientele. That is an unusually candid thing for a retail manager to say on the record, and I respect it enormously.

Expats posted to Ouagadougou are told, bluntly, that "you can probably find everything you need, but not necessarily everything you want" — so the operative strategy is to buy a staple the instant you spot it, because restocking is not a promise anyone is making. Groceries run expensive enough that government postings come with an actual cost-of-living allowance to cover it, and one long-term diplomatic resident delivers the verdict that matters most for travellers hoping for a souvenir-shopping consolation prize: Ouagadougou is simply "not a shopping post," lacking the handicraft and antiques culture that livens up several of its West African neighbours. It's a city that will feed you adequately and disappoint you decoratively, in roughly equal measure.

## The Verdict

Nigeria had the supermarket and lost it; Burkina Faso never fully had one and built a single chain sturdy enough to need international development financing just to keep the lights on. If I'm grading on ambition, Nigeria wins — nowhere else have I watched a national grocery sector rise, scale to 25 outlets, and collapse within a window I was personally alive and shopping for groceries in. But if I'm grading on honesty, Burkina Faso takes it: a manager who publicly says "please come back, I'll drop my prices" has more integrity than an entire multinational retreat dressed up as "strategic realignment." Shop in both. Trust neither shelf fully stocked. Bring your own vanilla extract to West Africa generally — this is not me being precious, this is three separate forum posts telling you the same thing independently.

## Conclusion

Both countries will teach you, fast, that "grocery shopping" is not a neutral errand but a referendum on currency policy, import tariffs, and whoever controls the one supermarket chain within a hundred miles. Nigeria will make you nostalgic for a supermarket you never personally mourned until it was gone. Burkina Faso will make you grateful for whatever's actually on the shelf today, because tomorrow is not contractually obligated to have it too. Pack patience, pack cash, and under absolutely no circumstances describe either country's markets as "quaint" — Majd Hachem is still waiting for his customers to come back, and he does not have time for your travel-blog adjectives.
