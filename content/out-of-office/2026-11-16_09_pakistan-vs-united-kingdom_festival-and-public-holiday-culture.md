# Pakistan Closes the Country for Eid and Britain Asks You to Please Use Your Annual Leave

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Eid in Pakistan is not a holiday. It is a national event, a pause button pressed on an entire country. The streets empty, the markets overflow, and every family is a moving feast of new clothes, sweets, visits and the quiet pressure of cooking for everyone you know. Even people who were not particularly religious find themselves caught up, because it is hard to stand outside a celebration that the whole nation has agreed to.

The United Kingdom has Eid too, at least in the sense that millions of Muslim Britons celebrate it. What it does not have is a day off for it. Britain's public holidays follow a Christian and secular calendar, and everyone else negotiates. A Pakistani moving from one system to the other does not lose a festival. They lose the infrastructure around it, and that turns out to be most of the point.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Pakistan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan travel and shopping well before Eid | Leave Eid shopping to the last two days |
| Visit relatives and accept sweets and meals | Refuse hospitality casually |
| Respect fasting hours in public during Ramadan | Eat openly in front of people who are fasting |
| Be flexible about the exact date, which depends on moon sighting | Book fixed plans without a backup |

**United Kingdom**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Request leave for Eid early, ideally months in advance | Assume your employer knows when it falls |
| Explain Ramadan and Eid to colleagues in plain terms | Assume everyone understands |
| Ask whether your employer allows swapping holidays | Take unauthorised days off |
| Find a local community prayer and event | Celebrate alone by default |

## Pakistan

Pakistan's religious calendar is the rhythm of the year. Ramadan reshapes working hours, meal times and the mood of entire cities. Evenings turn into long, glorious feasts, with iftar tables, street food, late-night shopping and a general sense that the day has been converted into night. Then Eid arrives, and the country pauses. Offices close, families gather, children collect envelopes of money, and the whole nation changes into its best clothes.

The details are unforgettable. There is the anxiety of moon sighting, the late-night announcement that tomorrow is the festival, the scramble of last-minute preparations. Markets stay open into the small hours. Tailors are overbooked, bangle stalls are crowded, and mehndi artists work all night. Eid al-Adha brings the sacrifice, the sharing of meat with neighbours and the poor, and an intensity of family obligation that few Western holidays can match.

Add to this the other festivals and national days, the cultural rhythms of weddings, the regional celebrations, and the sense that the calendar is a shared emotional property. Public holidays in Pakistan are not just rest. They are collective experiences, built on the assumption that nearly everyone you meet is celebrating the same thing.

That assumption is what you lose when you leave. The sensation is not just homesickness for a family. It is homesickness for a country's synchronised mood, in which the pause is shared and the joy does not need explaining. A Pakistani abroad often feels this most keenly in the quiet of an ordinary Tuesday morning that is, to everyone else, nothing special.

## The United Kingdom

Britain has a calendar designed around Christianity, shaped by secular tradition. Christmas, Easter and bank holidays are the fixed points, and everything else is a personal arrangement. A HuffPost UK explainer on why Eid is not a public holiday makes the practical point plainly: because the date depends on moon sighting, employees must take annual leave or work, and some companies have introduced flexible systems. One digital bank, the piece says, lets staff swap regular public holidays for Eid or Diwali. That is a genuinely sensible idea and a sign of how far a good workplace can go.

A column in Pakistan's Daily Times, written by a man who moved to the UK and went from majority to minority status, offers the other side of the story. His office social committee marked Ramadan and Eid. He wrote an explainer that was emailed to more than a hundred staff. Non-Muslim colleagues attended a halal, alcohol-free Eid party, and two of them fasted in solidarity. It is a small, warm story, and it is a reminder that a workplace can do much of the cultural work a public holiday would otherwise do.

This is the British strength: not the calendar but the courtesy. Most colleagues are curious, willing to learn and happy to be educated. You will probably be asked what Ramadan is, what you eat, whether you are allowed water. The questions can be repetitive, but they are usually kind.

The challenge is the logistics. You will need to book leave months in advance, and you may not know the exact date until a few days before. Employers vary enormously, and some will be flexible while others will be rigid. Community life, mosques, cultural associations and family networks fill the gap. In cities with large Pakistani communities, you can find an Eid that feels almost like home, with the same sweets and the same noise. In smaller towns, you will have to build it.

## The Verdict

Pakistan wins on the festival itself, and it is not even close. A country that stops for a celebration and a country that asks you for a leave form are not in the same category. The depth of shared experience, the food, the family, the sense of national pause: this is something no calendar adjustment can reproduce.

Britain wins on one underrated quality, which is the freedom to be different without being punished. A Pakistani in the UK can celebrate Eid as loudly or quietly as they choose, and there are employers, like the ones in those stories, who go out of their way to help. The absence of a public holiday is a real loss, but the presence of tolerance is a real gain.

So the answer is Pakistan for the festival and Britain for the flexibility. If I had to pick one for Eid morning, I know where I would be. If I had to pick one for the other three hundred and sixty-four days, I would take a workplace that lets me swap a bank holiday and invites everyone to the party.

## Conclusion

Eid is about belonging, and belonging is easier to feel in a country that stops for it. Pakistan stops. Britain, on the whole, does not. But the second can still be generous, and the best British workplaces and neighbours prove that a festival can survive without a public holiday if the people around you decide it should.

If you are a Pakistani moving to the UK, ask for the leave early, explain the moon once, and invite your colleagues. Make the sweets and put them on the table. Most people will say yes to a plate. And if you are a Briton moving to Pakistan, bring a good appetite and an open diary. Eid will come to you whether you are ready or not, and it is going to be wonderful.
