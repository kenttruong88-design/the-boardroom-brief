# Manila Will Sell You A Membership In An Air-Conditioned Mall; Sydney Will Just Point You At A Pull-Up Bar On The Beach

🇵🇭 🇦🇺 *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Fitness, like everything else, is a mirror held up to a country's relationship with its own weather. The Philippines does fitness indoors, mirrored, air-conditioned, and increasingly franchised, because the alternative is doing burpees in 34-degree heat with humidity thick enough to swim through. Australia does fitness outdoors, free, at dawn, on sand, because the alternative is admitting you paid for a treadmill when there's a perfectly good ocean twenty metres away doing the same job for nothing.

I have done a spin class in a Makati mall basement so cold I nearly asked for a jacket. I have also done sprints on Bondi Beach at 6am surrounded by forty strangers in activewear who all seemed to know each other, which is somehow more intimidating than the cold.

## 🇵🇭 Do's and Don'ts: Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a gym inside a mall — Fitness First, Anytime Fitness and UFC Gym have all expanded aggressively here specifically because air conditioning is the actual product | Attempt an outdoor run at midday unless you enjoy the specific sensation of your lungs filling with warm soup |
| Book a class, not just a membership — group fitness culture here (Zumba, dance cardio, boxing) is social first, exercise second, and far more fun for it | Assume gym culture is purely vanity-driven; a lot of it, per recent reporting, is Filipino communities abroad using fitness as a genuine wellbeing and belonging anchor |
| Try a UFC Gym or CrossFit box if you want serious training — the scene has matured fast and the coaching quality now rivals anywhere in Asia | Skip the humidity factor when planning your week; locals schedule serious training for 5-6am or after 7pm for a reason |
| Respect the mall-gym as a legitimate third space — people socialise there as much as they lift, and it's a genuinely good way to make local friends | Expect a park-based "outdoor gym" culture like Australia's — public green space for fitness is far rarer here, so the gym really is the default |

## 🇦🇺 Do's and Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to a free outdoor "beach gym" at Bondi, Bronte or basically any coastal suburb — council-installed equipment, zero membership fee, maximum smugness | Assume the gym is the main event; for huge numbers of Australians, fitness means ocean swims, surf, and beach runs first, indoor training second |
| Get comfortable being watched; Bondi's outdoor gyms are famously, deliberately public, practically a spectator sport | Skip sun protection because "it's just a quick run" — Australian UV will burn you faster than you think, even in a cardigan-weather month |
| Join a run club or ocean swim group — the social-fitness scene here is enormous, and it's often the fastest way to build a genuine friend group as a newcomer | Expect gyms themselves to be especially cheap; YouGov's own research shows gym membership here is treated as a serious household expense, not a throwaway subscription |
| Try early morning training — 5:30-6:30am sessions are a genuine cultural institution, not just an efficiency hack | Underestimate how competitive, even performative, the outdoor fitness scene can get; that "casual" beach workout crowd is often alarmingly fit |

## The Philippines: The Mall Is The Gym, And That's Not An Insult

Philippine fitness culture has quietly become one of Southeast Asia's most interesting growth stories, and it is almost entirely a story about air conditioning. UFC Gym alone has pursued a 30-location expansion plan across the country, and homegrown and international chains alike have realised that the mall — already the Philippines' default social venue — is also its most natural gym location. You don't drive to a standalone fitness centre on the edge of town; you take the elevator up from the food court, train for an hour, and go back down for merienda. It's efficient, it's social, and it completely sidesteps the climate problem that makes outdoor training a genuine ordeal for roughly ten months of the year.

What's quietly fascinating, and under-reported outside the Philippines, is how fitness culture functions for the diaspora specifically — reporting from Filipino-Australian community media has highlighted fitness as something considerably bigger than weight loss for many Filipino migrants: a structure, a belonging mechanism, a replacement for the extended-family social fabric left behind. The same instinct that makes a Zumba class in Quezon City feel like a block party rather than a workout follows Filipino communities wherever they resettle.

The honest limitation is infrastructure: outdoor, free, public fitness space of the Australian variety simply doesn't exist at scale here. Parks are smaller, hotter, and less equipped. So the gym isn't a lifestyle choice stacked on top of an already-active outdoor culture — for most urban Filipinos, it's the only realistic option, which is exactly why the mall-gym model has exploded so completely.

Group class culture deserves special mention, because it's where the social engineering really shows. Zumba in particular has become something close to a national pastime, with free community sessions in barangay halls and basketball courts drawing crowds that have nothing to do with gym membership at all — local government units across the country routinely sponsor these sessions as public health initiatives, turning fitness into civic programming rather than a private purchase. It's a reminder that "gym culture" here is really two parallel systems: a commercial, mall-based, increasingly international one for those who can afford it, and a free, community-anchored one running underneath it that most foreign coverage never even mentions.

## Australia: Fitness Is The National Religion, And The Beach Is The Cathedral

Australian fitness culture starts outside and stays there. Bondi's open-air, council-funded gyms — rings, bars, benches, bolted to the sand — have become genuinely iconic, less a fitness amenity than a tourist attraction in their own right, because the entire set-up telegraphs something true about the national psyche: exercise here is public, social, and resolutely free. YouGov's own recent profiling of Australian gym-goers found a population that treats paid membership seriously, as a real financial commitment, which tells you something about how much the outdoor, free alternative already absorbs the casual end of the market.

The flip side is a culture that can feel, to a newcomer, quietly performative. That 6am beach session isn't three friends mucking about — it's often a genuinely accomplished local run club, moving at a pace that will humble you within four hundred metres. Fitness in Australia carries a faint undertone of competition even at its most casual, baked into a culture that treats an active outdoor lifestyle as close to a civic obligation.

What nobody warns you about is the sun. The same open-air culture that makes Australian fitness so appealing also makes it genuinely hazardous if you're used to training indoors — the UV here does not negotiate, and "just a quick run" at 10am can undo a week of careful skincare in twenty minutes.

Indoor gym membership, where Australians do pay for it, is treated with a seriousness that reflects YouGov's own profiling of the segment: it's disproportionately skewed toward people layering structured strength training on top of their free outdoor cardio, rather than using the gym as a substitute for it. This two-tier system — free, social, outdoor as the default; paid, serious, indoor as the deliberate add-on — is nearly the mirror image of the Philippines' mall-gym model, where the paid indoor option isn't an add-on at all, it's the whole available menu.

## The Verdict

The Philippines wins if you want fitness as community glue in a hostile climate — the mall-gym, Zumba-class model is warm, social, and smartly adapted to its environment. Australia wins if you want fitness as identity — free, public, slightly terrifying in its casual excellence, and genuinely available to anyone willing to show up at an unreasonable hour. Honestly, the Philippines has the better air conditioning and Australia has the better view; pick your discomfort.

Consider, too, what each culture is quietly optimising against. The Philippines is building fitness infrastructure around a climate that actively punishes outdoor exertion for most of the year, and it shows in every mall-adjacent gym franchise and every air-conditioned Zumba hall. Australia is building fitness infrastructure around a climate that actively rewards it, and around a national mythology that treats physical capability as close to a personality trait. Neither approach is transferable wholesale — a Bondi-style free outdoor circuit would simply melt in Manila's humidity, and a Makati mall-gym model would feel faintly absurd stuffed indoors a hundred metres from a usable beach. Context, as ever, decided the architecture before taste ever got a vote.

## The Parting Shot

One country sells you a membership to escape the heat; the other hands you a free pull-up bar and dares you to use it in front of strangers. Both will make you fitter. Only one will make you sunburnt by 7am, and it will not be the Philippines. Pack sunscreen for Sydney and patience for the Manila humidity, and accept that whichever one you end up in, someone twice your age will outperform you within the first week, loudly, and without the faintest trace of apology.
