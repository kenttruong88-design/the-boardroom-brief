# One Country Digitised Its Bureaucracy Into Oblivion, the Other Still Wants That Document Notarised in Triplicate

🇦🇲 Armenia 🇧🇷 Brazil
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Brazil has a reputation, well earned over decades, for bureaucratic labyrinths so elaborate they've spawned their own vocabulary: the "jeitinho," the informal workaround culture that exists specifically because the formal process was never designed to actually work smoothly. And yet, improbably, Brazil also built Pix, an instant payment system so fast and frictionless that Brazilians now transfer money between accounts faster than most Europeans can unlock their banking app.

Armenia, meanwhile, has quietly become one of the more startup-friendly, digitally modernised banking environments in its region, a small country that decided, apparently as a matter of national strategy, to make opening an account and registering a business dramatically easier than its post-Soviet neighbours. Both countries contain genuine contradictions. Neither will behave quite the way you expect.

## Do's & Don'ts: Armenia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your passport and proof of address; account opening is genuinely fast for non-residents | Assume all banks offer English-language service; confirm before committing to a branch |
| Use mobile banking apps; Armenia's digital banking infrastructure is notably modern | Expect notarised document processes to be quick; official paperwork still moves slowly |
| Take advantage of Armenia's growing fintech and remote-work banking friendliness | Assume cash is obsolete; many everyday transactions, especially outside Yerevan, still favour it |
| Keep both a local and foreign-currency account if working internationally; it's common and easy | Forget to ask about currency conversion fees; they vary significantly between banks |

## Do's & Don'ts: Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a CPF (tax ID number) immediately; nothing administrative works without one | Expect any government process to move quickly without it — the CPF is the master key |
| Set up Pix as soon as you have a Brazilian bank account; it's the default payment method | Rely on international cards for everyday purchases; Pix has replaced cash and cards almost entirely |
| Accept that some processes require a "despachante" (bureaucracy fixer) — it's normal, not shady | Try to fully DIY complex bureaucracy alone; even Brazilians hire help for serious paperwork |
| Bring physical copies of every document, always, even for things that seem digital | Assume one office visit will resolve anything; return trips are the norm, not the exception |

## Armenia: Small Country, Surprisingly Modern Machinery

Armenia has spent the past decade actively courting remote workers, entrepreneurs, and digital nomads, and its banking system reflects that deliberate strategic choice. Opening a bank account as a foreigner in Yerevan is, by regional standards, refreshingly straightforward: a passport and proof of address will typically get a non-resident account opened within a day or two at most major banks, a stark contrast to the weeks-long processes common elsewhere in the post-Soviet space.

Mobile and digital banking infrastructure has developed quickly and well. Armenian banking apps tend to be genuinely functional, supporting transfers, bill payments, and multi-currency accounts with an ease that surprises visitors expecting Soviet-era bureaucratic holdovers. This digital-forward push is very much intentional: Armenia has positioned itself as a low-friction base for remote workers and small business owners, and the banking sector has largely kept pace with that ambition.

Where the old bureaucratic instincts still surface is in notarised documentation and official government processes outside banking specifically: apostilles, residency paperwork, and business registration documents can still move at a pace that feels decidedly analogue, requiring physical presence, specific stamps, and the kind of patience Armenia's banks have otherwise made unnecessary. The contrast is genuinely jarring: you can open a multi-currency account on your phone in twenty minutes, then spend three days getting a single document notarised for an entirely unrelated process.

Cash still matters more than the modern banking apps might suggest, particularly outside Yerevan, where smaller vendors and rural transactions lean more heavily on physical currency than the country's fintech-forward image implies. The lesson, broadly: Armenia's formal financial infrastructure has modernised fast and well, but the surrounding bureaucratic culture hasn't fully caught up everywhere, and knowing which system you're dealing with, banking or government, changes your expectations considerably.

## Brazil: Beautiful Digital Payments, Baroque Everything Else

Brazil's Pix system deserves genuine, unqualified praise: launched by the Central Bank in 2020, it allows instant, free, 24/7 transfers between any Brazilian bank accounts using just a phone number, email, or ID as the identifier. It has been adopted so completely, so fast, that cash and even credit cards have become secondary in daily life for enormous swaths of the population; street vendors, taxi drivers, and small shops all expect and prefer it. In pure payment-technology terms, Brazil is arguably years ahead of most wealthy nations.

And yet this same country maintains a broader bureaucratic culture that has become genuinely legendary for its complexity. The CPF, an individual taxpayer registration number, functions as the master key to nearly everything: opening a bank account, signing a lease, buying a SIM card, receiving a delivery. Without it, functionally nothing official happens, and obtaining one as a foreigner, while not impossible, involves navigating a federal system that rewards patience and, often, professional help.

This is where the "despachante" enters the picture, a semi-formal bureaucracy fixer whose entire profession exists because Brazilian administrative processes are complicated enough that even Brazilians routinely pay someone else to navigate them rather than attempt it solo. Far from being a shady workaround, hiring a despachante is completely normal, openly discussed, and often the most efficient path through processes that would otherwise consume weeks of an ordinary person's time in queues and repeat office visits.

The broader "jeitinho brasileiro," the cultural instinct for finding a creative, informal workaround around a rigid formal obstacle, exists precisely because the formal system so often fails to work as designed. It's simultaneously a charming national trait and a fairly damning commentary on the bureaucracy that made it necessary in the first place, a contradiction most Brazilians will acknowledge, with a shrug, themselves.

## The Verdict

Armenia wins on sheer banking accessibility: fast account opening, genuinely modern digital tools, and a system clearly built with foreigners and remote workers in mind. Brazil wins on payment technology specifically, Pix is a genuine marvel, but loses badly, and knowingly, on almost everything bureaucratic surrounding it. If you want banking that just works without drama, Armenia edges ahead. If you want to experience the strange spectacle of world-class fintech duct-taped onto Kafkaesque paperwork, welcome to Brazil, and good luck with your CPF appointment.

## Conclusion

Armenia and Brazil both prove that a country's financial infrastructure and its bureaucratic culture don't have to move at the same speed, and often spectacularly don't. Armenia built fast, foreigner-friendly banking on top of a still-sluggish official paperwork culture. Brazil built a genuinely world-leading instant payment system inside one of the world's most notoriously tangled bureaucracies. Arrive in either expecting perfect consistency and you'll be disappointed. Arrive expecting contradiction, and both countries will, in their own strange way, make a great deal more sense.
