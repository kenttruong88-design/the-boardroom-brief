# Both Countries Will Shut Down For Days, Only One Will Warn You First

🇮🇳 India vs 🇳🇬 Nigeria — Festival and Public Holiday Culture
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nothing exposes a newcomer's planning failures quite like discovering, mid-relocation, that the entire country they've just moved to has quietly stopped functioning for a religious or cultural festival nobody put on the onboarding calendar. India and Nigeria both treat festival season as a genuine seismic event — banks close, offices empty, flights triple in price, and entire cities reorganise themselves around celebration rather than commerce. Both will humble you if you show up expecting business as usual.

Where they diverge is texture: India's festival calendar is dense, cyclical, and deeply regional, while Nigeria's runs on a mix of religious observance and one increasingly famous secular month-long party that has, somewhat improbably, become a genuine global tourism draw. Confuse the two rhythms and you'll either miss the point entirely or show up in entirely the wrong outfit.

## Do's & Don'ts: India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the regional festival calendar, not just the national one | Assume Diwali dates or customs are identical across every state |
| Accept sweets when offered during festival visits — refusing is genuinely rude | Show up empty-handed to a Diwali or Holi invitation |
| Book travel and hotels weeks ahead of major festivals | Wait until the week before Diwali to book domestic flights |
| Wear old clothes you don't mind ruining to a Holi celebration | Wear anything you like to Holi — the colour does not wash out easily |

## Do's & Don'ts: Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book Lagos accommodation for December months in advance | Assume you can find a hotel room in Lagos during Detty December |
| Dress to impress — festival season is genuinely a fashion event | Underdress; Nigerian festival style is loud, bold, and deliberate |
| Budget generously for "owambe" party culture if invited | Assume one invitation means one event — it rarely stops at one |
| Respect the mix of Christian, Muslim, and traditional holidays | Assume the whole country marks the same holidays the same way |

## India: A Calendar That Never Really Stops

India doesn't really have a festival season so much as a festival year, with major celebrations distributed across virtually every month and heavily inflected by region, religion, and local tradition, to the point where a national festival calendar functions more as a loose guideline than a reliable schedule. Diwali, the festival of lights, is the closest thing to a genuinely unifying national event — five days of celebration, home decoration, fireworks, and family gathering that effectively pauses much of the country's commercial activity — but even Diwali's exact customs and timing shift meaningfully between states, and assuming Delhi's version maps onto Kerala's is a fast way to look uninformed.

What nobody warns arriving expats about is the sheer logistical impact. Flights and trains during Diwali or Holi routinely triple in price and sell out weeks in advance, banks and government offices close for days at a stretch, and business plans that don't account for this run straight into a wall of closed shutters. I learned this trying to schedule a work trip during Diwali week in my first year in India and ended up stranded in a mostly shuttered business district wondering why nobody had mentioned any of this in the onboarding email.

The social expectations run deep too. Refusing offered festival sweets is a genuine faux pas, treated as a rejection of hospitality rather than a dietary preference. Holi, the festival of colour typically in March, comes with its own crucial warning that nobody delivers clearly enough: the coloured powder and dyed water thrown during celebrations do not come out of good clothing, ever, and showing up in anything you value is a decision you will regret by early afternoon. Gift-giving, home visits, and an expectation of generous hospitality run through nearly every major festival, and newcomers who treat these as optional social theatre rather than genuine cultural obligation tend to find their social circle quietly narrowing.

## Nigeria: Religious Devotion Meets a World-Famous Party

Nigeria's festival calendar runs on a genuinely pluralistic foundation — a roughly even split between Christian and Muslim populations means both Christmas and Eid al-Fitr, alongside Eid al-Adha, are treated as major national events, observed with real intensity depending on region and community, alongside traditional festivals like Lagos's colourful Eyo Festival, a masquerade tradition centuries old that still shuts down parts of Lagos Island when it runs.

But the phenomenon that's genuinely reshaped how outsiders think about Nigerian festival culture is "Detty December" — the informal but now internationally recognised name for Lagos's month-long convergence of concerts, weddings, homecoming diaspora visitors, and a party circuit dense enough that hotel prices in Lagos during December routinely rival, and sometimes exceed, peak-season European capitals. What started as a diaspora homecoming tradition has become a legitimate global tourism draw, with international visitors now specifically timing trips to Lagos around it, something almost nobody would have predicted a decade ago.

What catches newcomers off guard is the sheer density and expectation of the "owambe" party circuit — elaborate, lavish celebration parties for weddings, birthdays, and other milestones, marked by coordinated outfits called "aso ebi," live music, and an assumption of genuine, sustained generosity from guests. One invitation rarely stays one invitation during festival season; Nigerians socially embedded in a community can easily attend several owambe events in a single weekend, each requiring its own outfit consideration and gift budget. Underestimating the financial and sartorial commitment of Nigerian festival season is a classic expat miscalculation, one that usually gets corrected loudly, warmly, and in spectacular fashion by the second invitation.

## The Verdict

India's festival culture rewards advance planning and cultural literacy — know the regional calendar, respect the hospitality rituals, wear clothes you're willing to sacrifice to Holi. Nigeria's rewards financial and social stamina — budget generously, dress boldly, and accept that one party invitation is rarely the whole story. Both will absolutely derail your commercial plans for days at a time and both consider that a feature, not a bug. If pushed to choose, I'd hand this to Nigeria, purely for spectacle — Detty December has genuinely earned its international reputation, and there's something thrilling about a festival season so good it's rewritten the entire country's December tourism economy from the ground up.

## Conclusion

Both countries treat festival season not as a break from real life but as the realest, most concentrated version of it, and expats who show up trying to keep business-as-usual running through it are fighting a battle they will not win. Learn the calendar, budget for the hospitality, dress for the occasion — literally, in India's case, in clothes you're prepared to lose to a bucket of pink dye — and let the season actually happen around you instead of against you. It's genuinely more fun that way, and considerably less expensive than trying to book a last-minute flight against a national holiday you didn't know was coming.
