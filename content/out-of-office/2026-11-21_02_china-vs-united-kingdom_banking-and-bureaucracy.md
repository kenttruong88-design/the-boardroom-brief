# China Wants Your Face at the Counter; Britain Wants a Utility Bill You Do Not Have Yet

🇨🇳 China vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country's banking system tells you what it fears. China fears you being anonymous, so it needs your passport, your visa, your phone number, your face and ideally your landlord's grandmother's blessing, and it wants all of it in person, at a branch, on a Tuesday. Britain fears you being a stranger, so it asks for proof of address, and then, with a perfectly straight face, will not give you a proof of address until you have a bank account.

I have opened accounts in more countries than I have owned decent coats, and I can tell you that the paperwork is never the hard part. The hard part is the moment you realise the system was built for people who were already inside it. China built its financial life around a smartphone and a national ID. Britain built its around a postcode and a vague, deniable sense of respectability. Neither was designed with the arriving foreigner in mind, and you can feel it in the queue.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇨🇳 China ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go to the branch in person with your passport and residence documents | Expect to open an account online or remotely |
| Bring a Chinese-speaking colleague for anything beyond the basics | Assume the teller will switch to English because you are polite |
| Set up a local mobile wallet as soon as the bank account is live | Plan to live on cash and foreign cards |
| Ask the exact monthly cap on international transfers before you commit | Leave a large pile of renminbi without a plan to get it out |

| 🇬🇧 United Kingdom ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a tenancy agreement or employer letter as proof of address | Turn up with only a passport and a hopeful face |
| Try the digital banks first, they ask fewer awkward questions | Spend a month in a high-street queue before checking the apps |
| Keep every payslip and bill, the system runs on paper trails | Shred the letter that proves where you live |
| Check whether savings are protected under the FSCS | Assume an overseas branch carries the same cover |

## 🇨🇳 China

Opening a Chinese bank account is, at its best, a pleasant two-hour ritual with a form, a stamp and a teller who is secretly impressed that you arrived with the right documents. The Wise guide to the process is blunt: you usually have to visit the bank in person, and at most banks you will not be able to do it online or remotely. The initial deposit is small, in the region of ten to twenty yuan, which is almost an insult after the paperwork. What follows is the more important part: the account is the key to a phone wallet, and the wallet is the key to the entire country.

The trouble starts when you want to leave. One expat on Expat.com, newly employed and paid in renminbi, wanted to move wages home to the UK and found that a premium account at one big international bank required a balance of 100,000 yuan, which he could not reach quickly. Replies in the thread describe transfers out of China as a hassle, taking up to an hour each time, and being charged at both ends. Another poster noted that some digital transfer routes may need a Chinese citizen. This is the central bargain of Chinese banking: getting money in is a ceremony, and getting money out is an act of faith.

It is also, in everyday life, astonishingly efficient. Once you have the app, you can pay a street vendor, split a bill, pay rent and send a red envelope to a friend in thirty seconds. Foreign cards, by contrast, will sulk and fail in places that matter. An expat who has done the paperwork will live a smoother day-to-day life than one in London, right up until the moment they want to take their savings with them.

There is a human cost to the Chinese version of the system, which is that so much of it depends on having a local fixer. Foreigners who arrive through a large employer get a human guide to the counter, an HR colleague who has done this two hundred times and who knows which window to approach, which form to bring and when to smile. Those who arrive alone are in for an education. I have watched a grown executive from a large consultancy reduced to charades over a transfer limit, and I confess I enjoyed it more than I should. Bureaucracy is a leveller, and China levels everyone with rigour.

## 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

Britain's banking system is a gentleman's club that has been forced to accept paying members. For someone arriving from abroad the first obstacle is the circular logic of proof of address. Banks want a bill, landlords want a bank statement, and employers want both. The returning expat has it worse in a subtler way. Ikky in China, writing for people leaving China, warns that returning Britons often struggle to open UK accounts because most banks require proof of a UK address, and then there are the currency swings and the pension transfers to wrestle with.

It gets stranger the minute you try to save. A contributor on the MoneySavingExpert forum, three years into life in China and sitting on cash earning nothing in a Chinese bank, discovered that most UK fixed-term savings products require UK residency. Replies suggested that National Savings and Investments sometimes accepts expats who keep a UK current account, and that offshore branches in places like Gibraltar or the Isle of Man will take non-residents, though without the protection of the Financial Services Compensation Scheme and at poor rates. It is a perfect little parable: Britain will happily take your money offshore, but will not be seen with it at home.

And yet, once you are in, the system is gloriously boring. Your bank will not ask for your face at a branch in order to send ten pounds. Direct debits work, transfers arrive in seconds, and a surprisingly civilised ombudsman exists for when things go wrong. The challenge is entirely at the door. Get past it, and Britain will leave you in peace, which is, I will remind you, the highest compliment the British have ever paid anyone.

What the British system needs, above all, is a bit of honesty about who it is for. It is lovely for someone who grew up with a postcode, a mother with a joint account and a credit history stretching back to a student overdraft. For everyone else it is a series of polite rejections delivered by an algorithm that has never been told you exist. The digital banks are the exception, and a number of expats have told me that the newer apps were the first financial institution in Britain to treat them like a human being. That is a sentence that should embarrass the high street, and, I suspect, rarely does.

## The Verdict

Britain wins on getting your money in and out, and China wins on what you can do with it on a Tuesday. If you plan to stay a short time, the Chinese account is the better experience: tedious to open, magical to use, a little painful to close. If you plan to come and go between the two, I strongly advise you to treat your finances like a long-distance relationship and keep a copy of everything.

The honest conclusion is that neither country has solved the foreigner problem. China has simply declined to pretend that it has. It asks for your documents and tells you the rules in a flat, administrative voice. Britain buries its rules in friendliness and then punishes you for not having intuited them. I know which one I prefer to deal with at nine in the morning, and it is the one that is upfront about being difficult.

## Conclusion

Banking abroad is a test of whether you can hold two completely different versions of bureaucracy in your head at once. China will ask for everything at the start, and then be effortless. Britain will ask for nothing at the start, then slam the door on a technicality. Either way, bring your passport, three recent bills, a calm face and a plan to take your money home. And if a teller tells you something cannot be done, ask who can. In both countries, that person always exists. In neither country will they volunteer.
