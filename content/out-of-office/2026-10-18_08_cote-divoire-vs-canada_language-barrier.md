# Everyone Involved Insists They Speak French. Nobody Involved Agrees On What That Means.

🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is a sentence that will get you laughed out of two different rooms on two different continents: "I speak French, so I'll be fine." In Abidjan, that confidence survives approximately four minutes, until someone greets you in Nouchi and your textbook Parisian French sits there like a flat soufflé. In Montreal, it survives slightly longer, right up until a landlord switches to unaccented Québécois at native speed and you realize DELF certification did not prepare you for "chu plus capable."

Both Côte d'Ivoire and Canada will tell you, with a straight face, that French is one of their official languages. Both are technically correct. Neither mentions that "official" is doing an enormous amount of quiet, exhausted lifting. I have relocated fourteen times and eaten my way through sixty countries, and I can tell you the single most dangerous phrase in relocation is "we share a language." You don't. You share a word for the language.

## Do's & Don'ts: 🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn ten words of Dioula or Baoulé before you learn the metro map | Assume Parisian French and Ivorian French are the same product with different packaging |
| Greet people slowly and properly — greetings are a social contract, not small talk | Rush the greeting to get to your actual request; it will not land |
| Expect French to open doors for admin, work, and school | Expect French alone to get you invited anywhere real |
| Smile when you don't understand Nouchi slang — it's a badge of belonging, not an insult | Pretend you understood when you didn't; Ivorians will spot it in a heartbeat |

## Do's & Don'ts: 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check which province you're actually moving to before announcing "I speak the language" | Assume bilingual Canada means bilingual *you* will be fine everywhere |
| Learn that Quebec's workplace French is a legal requirement, not a vibe | Walk into a Montreal job interview assuming English will carry you, per the law |
| Appreciate the effort-based culture — attempt French, badly, and watch doors open | Expect warmth as a reward for *not* trying; some hostility is earned honestly |
| Research whether your target city is Toronto-English or Montreal-French before you sign a lease | Treat "Canada" as one coherent language policy; it is several countries wearing a trench coat |

## 🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire

French is Côte d'Ivoire's official language, the language of government, school, and the documents you'll be queuing to get stamped. That much is true and useful and exactly as far as it gets you. Underneath the official layer sits a country with more than sixty local languages — Dioula, Baoulé, Bété, Senufo, and dozens more — and sitting on top of both is Nouchi, the Abidjan-born street vernacular that functions as the city's actual shared tongue. Nouchi draws roughly a third of its DNA from French grammar and vocabulary and fills the rest in with local languages, which means your perfectly serviceable French gets you maybe sixty percent comprehension of a conversation happening three feet away from you, in public, about you.

The expat blogosphere is refreshingly honest about this. One well-circulated personal account of expat life in Abidjan notes plainly that not knowing French *or* a local language "can make communication and integration harder" — which is a diplomatic way of saying you will spend your first six months smiling at jokes you don't understand. The same writer reports that learning to say hello in a neighborhood's local language "opened many doors," which tracks with everything I've seen in sixty countries: fluency impresses nobody, effort impresses everybody.

What nobody tells you before you land is that French here is a formal costume, not a home language. It's what you use with officials, bosses, and your landlord's paperwork. The real social currency — the jokes, the market haggling, the taxi banter — runs through Nouchi and whichever regional language your neighborhood favors. Francophone newcomers arrive smug and leave humbled, usually within a month, usually at a market. The ones who do well treat their French as a starting credential, not a finish line, and start collecting Dioula phrases like other people collect fridge magnets.

## 🇨🇦 Canada

Canada's bilingualism is the most expensively maintained myth in the G7. The 2021 Census puts national English-French bilingualism at 18.0 percent — a record high, and also basically unchanged from 17.7 percent two decades earlier, because the national number is a magic trick performed by averaging two completely different countries. In Quebec, bilingualism has climbed steadily to 46.4 percent — nearly one in two people. Outside Quebec, it has actually *fallen*, down to 9.5 percent. Translation: "bilingual Canada" means "bilingual Quebec, and a rounding error everywhere else," and the brochure does not say this.

This produces the single funniest trap in expat relocation: assuming "Canada speaks English and French" means you, personally, will be fine in either. You will not. Move to Toronto expecting to practice your French and you'll find a monolingual anglophone metropolis that treats French as a grade-school memory. Move to Montreal expecting English to carry a banking job and you'll meet Quebec's language law, which requires French as the language of the workplace, full stop. A long-running expat.com thread on exactly this scenario is a small masterpiece of mixed messaging: one poster insists newcomers need to be "bilingual especially in [the] banking area," complete with a warning to "be ready to encounter hostile and unfriendly French-Canadians," while another cheerfully counters that locals will be "happy to work with you because you're trying," and a third simply advises giving up and moving to Toronto instead. Three answers, same thread, same city, zero consensus — which is Canada's entire language situation in miniature.

The sting: Canada's bilingualism isn't a feature you get to use. It's a postal code lottery you need to research before you pack.

## The Verdict

Côte d'Ivoire wins, and it's not close. Not because its language situation is simpler — it demonstrably isn't, with sixty-plus local languages stacked under a French veneer and a slang economy running underneath that — but because it's *honest* about the difficulty from the first handshake. Nobody in Abidjan will tell you French will carry you everywhere; the culture itself signals, immediately and warmly, that effort in a local phrase buys you more goodwill than fluency in the official one. Canada, by contrast, sells itself as a solved problem — "we have two official languages!" — and then quietly declines to mention that the two languages live in different postal codes with different laws and, per that expat.com thread, occasionally different tempers. Côte d'Ivoire's challenge is real but advertised. Canada's challenge is real and concealed behind a flag that comes in two languages on the same passport. Suki Nakamura does not reward false advertising.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com forum, Montreal non-French-speaker thread — a poster warns newcomers to "be ready to encounter hostile and unfriendly French-Canadians" if they show up to banking jobs without workplace-level French.*

> *ExpatExchange, Abidjan expat forum — an anonymous member names the language barrier itself as one of the single hardest parts of adjusting to local culture, ahead of most other complaints on the thread.*

> *expat.com forum, Montreal thread — a second poster disagrees entirely, insisting locals will be "happy to work with you because you're trying," proof that the exact same city produces wildly contradictory survival advice depending on who you ask.*

## Conclusion

The lesson, as always, is that "official language" is marketing copy, not a promise. Côte d'Ivoire tells you upfront that French is the formal shell around a much livelier, much harder, much more rewarding set of real languages, and lets you decide how deep to go. Canada tells you it's bilingual and lets you discover, usually at a job interview or a lease signing, that it meant something entirely different by that than you did. I've relocated fourteen times. I have never once been fooled by a country that undersold its difficulty. I have been fooled, repeatedly, by one that oversold its ease. Learn the local slang before you learn the brochure. The brochure was never written for you.
