# Jacobabad Will Test If Your Body Can Survive. Vienna Will Just Hand You a Ball Gown.

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇦🇹 Austria

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Most "how locals cope with the weather" articles are about jumpers and umbrellas. This one is not, because Pakistan contains a city that has officially exceeded the temperature-and-humidity threshold beyond which the human body cannot physiologically survive, and Austria has responded to a genuinely mild winter by inventing four hundred and fifty formal balls just to have something to do about it. These are not comparable problems. I'm comparing them anyway.

I have sweated through a Karachi power cut with no working fan and I have also sat, overdressed and slightly drunk on mulled wine, through an entire Viennese ball season lecture on correct waltz etiquette. Both experiences were, in their own way, about survival.

## Do's and Don'ts: Pakistan 🇵🇰

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Drink thaadal, lassi, or free roadside Rooh Afza syrup during heatwave season; the folk remedies work | Assume air conditioning solves anything; load-shedding power cuts of 6-16 hours a day make it moot regularly |
| Restructure your day around the worst midday heat, the way farmworkers and labourers already do | Underestimate the risk; Jacobabad is one of only two places on Earth confirmed to exceed the survivable wet-bulb limit |
| Budget for a borehole or filtered water source if you're somewhere with unreliable infrastructure | Forget the human cost; roughly 70% of the workforce is informal-sector with zero heat-related labour protection |

## Do's and Don'ts: Austria 🇦🇹

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a ball-season ticket at least once; 450+ formal balls run across Vienna in January and February alone | Overpack for brutal Alpine winters in the city itself; Vienna averages only about one snow day a month in peak season |
| Use the Kaffeehaus properly — sit for hours over one coffee; it's sanctioned, UNESCO-recognised loitering | Expect the cold to come up in casual conversation; it's so normalised locally that it barely registers as a complaint |
| Try the public sauna culture; mixed and ladies-only sessions are a genuine institutional answer to winter gloom | Wear swimwear in a traditional sauna; most venues explicitly forbid it, which catches visitors off guard every year |

Pakistan's heat is not a seasonal inconvenience; in parts of the country, it is a measurable survival event. Turbat in Balochistan recorded 53.7 to 53.8°C in May 2017, among the highest reliably measured temperatures anywhere on Earth, and Mohenjo-Daro and Jacobabad have each posted readings above 52°C in separate years. Jacobabad specifically carries a darker distinction: Loughborough University research, reported by The Telegraph and covered locally by Dawn, confirmed it as one of only two places on the planet — alongside Ras al Khaimah in the UAE — to have officially exceeded the wet-bulb temperature threshold beyond which the human body loses its ability to cool itself at all. The city hit 50°C in May 2021 and 51°C in both 2019 and 2020. Coping mechanisms here are a genuine mix of folk remedy and structural failure: thaadal (an almond-based sherbet), lassi, sugarcane juice, and free roadside Rooh Afza syrup handed out as a community gesture during the worst stretches; wealthier families temporarily relocate to Karachi, Quetta, or Jamshoro; farmworkers restructure entire shifts around midday; and households without reliable municipal water resort to private boreholes costing 20,000 to 30,000 rupees apiece, delivered by donkey cart when filtration plants fail during power cuts. Those power cuts are the real multiplier on the suffering: load-shedding runs six to sixteen hours a day during peak summer, which makes air conditioning largely theoretical for most of the population regardless of whether anyone can afford a unit. The Boston Globe's Karachi reporting put a face on the statistics: Shahbaz Ali, a 32-year-old motorcycle ride-hailing driver earning roughly eight dollars a day, described sleeping poorly through repeated power cuts and losing income entirely when heat illness stopped him working — "We are enduring, not living anymore." With roughly 70% of Pakistan's workforce in the informal sector, that sentence applies to a genuinely enormous share of the population, with essentially no heat-related labour protections attached to any of it.

Austria's weather "problem," by contrast, is almost comically mild once you check the actual data, and the coping mechanisms reflect a culture solving a social and psychological challenge rather than a physical one. Vienna's cold season — defined as average daily highs below 45°F — runs roughly three and a half months, from mid-November to early March, with a snowy period of about four months but genuinely modest accumulation in the city itself: around 2.4 inches in January and just over one snow day a month at the peak, a far cry from the Alpine-postcard stereotype most newcomers arrive expecting. What Austria has built instead is an entire social infrastructure around getting through the dark months with maximum ceremony: ball season alone runs over 450 formal balls across January and February — the Hunters' Ball, the Doctors' Ball, each with its own codified dress requirements and debutante traditions. Public sauna culture operates as a near-institutional answer to winter gloom, with swimwear explicitly forbidden at most traditional venues (a rule that catches unprepared visitors off guard with some regularity) and dedicated mixed and ladies-only sessions. Kaffeehaus culture, UNESCO-recognised as intangible cultural heritage, sanctions sitting for hours over a single coffee as a legitimate use of a winter afternoon, and the city even stages its own seasonal ritual response to "Blue Monday" — the third Monday in January, culturally marked as the calendar's psychological low point — complete with an outdoor ice rink, the Wiener Eistraum, set up directly in front of City Hall. Tellingly, an ExpatExchange thread on Vienna's toughest adjustments for newcomers didn't mention the cold at all; the challenges cited were formal etiquette and Austrians' private, slow-to-open-up social style — the weather itself is so thoroughly normalised through ritual that it barely registers as something worth complaining about.

Pakistan's government has occasionally responded to the heat with policy moves that read as both practical and faintly surreal: periodic bans on air conditioner use in official buildings during the worst grid-load periods, a measure meant to protect the broader power supply but one that leaves even state employees sweating through the same crisis as everyone else. Austria's response to its far milder winter has its own quietly specific rituals worth naming: the traditional cheese-fondue dinner, where whoever drops their bread in the pot owes the table a round of drinks or, depending on the house rule, a kiss — a tradition that exists purely because a three-and-a-half-month cold season needed something to do with its evenings, which is a luxury problem if ever there was one.

Set side by side, the contrast stops being about temperature at all and becomes about what a society is actually coping with. Pakistan is managing a genuine, escalating, life-threatening physical event with folk remedies and failing infrastructure standing in for the climate adaptation the state hasn't funded. Austria is managing a mild, entirely survivable seasonal mood dip with four hundred and fifty ballgowns and a UNESCO-recognised coffee habit. Only one of these is actually about weather.

## The Verdict

If "coping with the weather" means genuinely keeping people alive against measurable physiological limits, Pakistan's folk ingenuity — the free syrup, the restructured workday, the borehole dug by hand — deserves real respect precisely because the state has left so much of the burden to individuals and neighbours. If "coping with the weather" means turning a mild winter into the best-dressed season of the year, Austria wins by a mile, and I will defend the ball season to my grave. I'm giving the actual verdict to Pakistan, though, because resilience under an existential threshold beats resilience against slightly fewer sunlight hours, however good the waltzing is.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Boston Globe, Karachi, Shahbaz Ali, paraphrased: "It feels like living in a furnace" — sleeping badly through repeated power cuts, drinking free roadside Rooh Afza syrup, and losing income entirely when heat illness made it impossible to keep working.*

> *ExpatExchange, Vienna, paraphrased: the toughest adjustment for most newcomers isn't the winter at all — it's the strong emphasis on formal etiquette and how long it takes Austrians to genuinely open up socially.*

> *The Local Austria, paraphrased: ball season alone runs more than 450 formal events across January and February, and the city's unofficial cheese-fondue house rule says whoever drops their bread in the pot buys the next round, or owes a kiss.*

## Conclusion

Pakistan's heat will teach you, bluntly, what infrastructure failure actually costs a human body, and it deserves to be taken that seriously rather than filed under "exotic hardship" by people who've never lost a day's wage to it. Austria's winter will teach you that a mild, well-managed cold season is mostly an excuse for extremely good tailoring. Pack accordingly: a borehole fund for one, a ballgown for the other, and the humility to know which problem is actually serious.
