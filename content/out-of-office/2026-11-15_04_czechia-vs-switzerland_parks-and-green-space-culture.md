# Czechia Lets You Drink Under the Trees. Switzerland Laminated the Trees and Posted the Rules

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A Czech park is a place where people go to do things the flat will not allow: sit on a hill with a bottle of something, watch a concert, walk a dog the size of a sofa and argue about politics with the confidence of a nation that has seen several regimes come and go. It is not manicured. It is lived in, and it keeps a pub at the top of the hill in case things get serious.

A Swiss park, by contrast, is an exercise in municipal perfection. The grass is cut, the benches are level, the picnic area has a grill and a sign explaining the grill. Nature here is not an escape. It is a well-run department. Both countries are very green and neither would dream of being mistaken for the other.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Czechia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take a bottle and a blanket to the hill and stay for the sunset | Expect silence on a warm evening |
| Walk through the big royal parks rather than only the famous squares | Confine yourself to the tourist centre |
| Dress for a long ramble, not a short stroll | Turn up in city shoes for a forest |
| Greet people politely if you pass them on a path | Shout across a park |

**Switzerland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the equipped picnic areas and clean up completely | Leave a trace, including ash |
| Read the posted rules at the entrance | Improvise a barbecue |
| Take the train to the trailhead | Drive to a car park and assume it is free |
| Respect quiet hours and Sunday calm | Play music in a public space |

## Czechia

Prague is, on paper, absurdly green. The Expats.cz report on TravelBird's 2018 Green Cities Index says the city ranked sixth for green space per resident at 220.54 square metres, behind Reykjavik, Auckland and Bratislava, and first for the share of its area covered in parks and woodland, at nearly 57 per cent. The index comes from a Dutch holiday agency, so treat it as a marketing document with maps. But the story is believable the moment you walk anywhere.

VisitCzechia says Prague has about 200 parks, laid out gradually since the Middle Ages. Stromovka, in Bubeneč, covers 95 hectares and was founded in the thirteenth century as a royal game reserve. It has lakes, a summer palace and children's play areas, and on a good Sunday it behaves like the city's living room. Letná hosts rock concerts and summer festivals, and the tourism page notes that Pope John Paul II celebrated Mass there. Vojanovy sady is known for its magnolias and its peacocks, which is the sort of detail no planner would dare invent, and Grébovka in Vinohrady has grown vines since the days of Charles IV.

What distinguishes the Czech approach is that these are not museum pieces. A hill in Prague is an invitation, not a monument. You bring a bottle, a blanket and a friend, and nobody calls a warden. The royal gardens work the way public space was supposed to: nobody owns them and everybody does.

The weakness is unevenness. Some parks are beautifully kept and some are tired, and the city's relationship with litter is relaxed. But the culture of using green space, loudly and late and in all generations, is something the Swiss can only admire from a laminated distance.

There is more to the Czech picture than the famous names. VisitCzechia also lists Obora Hvězda, a game reserve with a summer palace shaped like a six-pointed star, which is popular for kite flying, and Průhonice, with its chateau and arboretum, described as one of the largest parks in Europe. Put those next to Stromovka and Letná and you get a city that treats its royal hunting grounds as a public commons. Nobody wrote a manifesto about it. The kings simply left, and the locals moved in with their sandwiches.

## Switzerland

Switzerland treats green space as national infrastructure. The Expat.com leisure guide lists the Swiss National Park, the Beverin, Chasseral, Ela, Thal, Doubs and Val Müstair nature parks and the Entlebuch biosphere, a UNESCO site. It notes the Kurpark in Bad Zurzach, with its extensive green spaces, and describes the Lauterbrunnen Valley with its 72 waterfalls and the Rhine Falls near Schaffhausen. The general expat guide calls the Alps and lakes the national playground and hiking the national pastime.

That is true, and, if anything, understated. The Swiss do not just have mountains. They have mountains with a timetable. The path is signposted, the train reaches the trailhead, the hut will be open and the toilets will be clean. You can take a child, a grandparent and a picnic into the wilderness without feeling that you have done anything brave, which is both the triumph and the limitation of the arrangement.

Green space in towns follows the same logic. The guide notes that many parks provide specially equipped picnic areas, which is the state furnishing nature rather than leaving it alone. The upside is cleanliness, safety and a mild sense that society is working. The downside is that spontaneity is administered. The grill is there, so is the sign about when to use it, and a Swiss neighbour will be happy to explain both.

I am teasing. I am also impressed. A country that lets you swim in a lake in the city centre and walk up a mountain by lunchtime has solved something most places have not. But it has solved it by regulating the fun, and some people will always prefer a messy hill to a perfect lawn.

Switzerland's ambition goes beyond the wilderness, and I should give it credit. The leisure guide describes Swissminiatur in Melide on Lake Lugano, with over a hundred replicas of castles and monuments, over fifteen thousand types of flowers and fifteen hundred tree varieties, which is a theme park built by people who want you to appreciate gardening. It mentions the Swiss section of the Camino de Santiago, a six-hundred-year-old route, and describes Lake Geneva as one of Europe's largest lakes and the Aletsch Glacier as the largest in the Alps. If a country can make a pilgrim path, a glacier and a miniature of itself all feel like part of the same national park, it has earned some bragging rights. It has also, I suspect, drawn a map for each of them.

## The Verdict

Switzerland has the better nature. Czechia has the better parks. They are not the same thing, and anyone who tells you otherwise has not tried to have an unplanned afternoon in Zurich.

If I want to climb something dramatic, I choose Switzerland without hesitation. If I want to spend a Sunday in the open air with friends, a bottle and no schedule, I choose Prague, and I do not choose it narrowly. The Swiss produce wonders. The Czechs produce afternoons.

The best expat life is one with a Czech weekend and a Swiss holiday. The worst is the reverse, in which you pay Swiss prices for a Czech hangover.

## Conclusion

Parks reveal what a society trusts. Czechia trusts people to behave like adults, even when the evidence is mixed. Switzerland trusts rules to do it for them, and the evidence is excellent.

Take what you need from each. Do your serious walking in the Alps, where the path is perfect and the hut is open. Do your idle afternoons on a Prague hill, where nobody will check your schedule or your bottle.

And wherever you end up, learn the local rule about the grill. It is always more important than it looks.
