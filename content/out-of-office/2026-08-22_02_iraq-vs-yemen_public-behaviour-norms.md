# Silence Isn't Respect Here, It's Suspicious

🇮🇶 Iraq and 🇾🇪 Yemen
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two countries the international press only ever photographs in ruins, which is its own kind of vandalism — because what actually happens on the street in Baghdad or Sana'a, on a completely ordinary Tuesday, is loud, warm, formal, and governed by a set of unwritten rules so precise you could set a watch by them, if anyone bothered wearing one instead of checking the position of the sun and the volume of the tea.

I will not pretend either country is a walk in a manicured European park. But the fixation Western coverage has on danger has crowded out the far more useful conversation: how do people actually behave in public here, and what happens to the foreigner who gets it wrong. Quite a lot, as it turns out, and mostly it's about hospitality rules you didn't know existed until you'd already broken three of them.

## Do's & Don'ts: Iraq

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept tea the first, second, and third time it's offered, in that order | Decline hospitality outright — a firm no on the first offer is heard as rudeness, not preference |
| Greet older men in a room individually, right hand over heart if you're not shaking hands | Address a group with a single blanket "hello" and consider your greeting duties discharged |
| Lower your voice and your eyes slightly when passing religious processions | Photograph mourners or religious gatherings without asking, ever |
| Let the eldest person at a gathering speak first, always | Interrupt an elder mid-sentence, even to agree enthusiastically |

## Do's & Don'ts: Yemen

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept qat-chewing gatherings as serious social and business infrastructure, not a curiosity to gawk at | Treat a qat session as a photo opportunity rather than the actual meeting it is |
| Dress conservatively in public regardless of your own background | Assume Western dress norms travel here unchanged; they don't, for any gender |
| Greet with the right hand and expect a longer, more formal exchange of pleasantries than you're used to | Rush a greeting — a hurried "salam" without the follow-up questions reads as cold |
| Let men initiate handshakes with men and follow local cues on cross-gender greetings | Extend a hand to greet across genders without reading the room first |

## Iraq: Loud Warmth, Precise Rules

The thing about Iraqi public life that never makes it into the coverage is how genuinely social it is, at a volume that would alarm a Londoner and delight anyone who grew up somewhere the extended family still runs the show. Streets in Baghdad, Erbil, or Basra are loud with commerce, argument, and greeting — and the greeting part is not casual. Men embrace, kiss cheeks up to three times depending on region and closeness, and ask after each other's families in a sequence so ritualised that skipping a step is noticed immediately, even by people who'd never admit to noticing.

Hospitality is the load-bearing wall of Iraqi public behaviour. Tea — sweet, strong, served in small glasses — arrives whether or not you asked, and the polite move is to accept it, not because you're thirsty but because refusing implies the host has failed you somehow. The dance of "no, I couldn't" followed by acceptance on the second or third offer is not indecision; it's the entire choreography, and skipping straight to "yes" on the first offer can actually read as presumptuous.

Public modesty norms are more conservative than most Western visitors expect, for men and women both, and this isn't a hangover from conflict — it long predates it. Loud arguments in the street are common and not automatically alarming; Iraqis argue about politics, football, and prices with a theatrical intensity that looks like it's about to become a fight and almost never does. What genuinely causes offence is disrespect toward elders, religious processions (particularly around Ashura, when entire cities transform and outsiders should watch, not intrude), and any hint that you find local customs primitive rather than simply different. Iraqis have absorbed decades of being spoken about as a cautionary tale; they have very little patience left for tourists who arrive already condescending.

## Yemen: Formality, Qat, and a Slower Clock

Yemen runs on a public rhythm almost nobody outside the region understands, and the qat gathering — the "mafraj" session where men chew the mildly stimulant leaf together in the afternoon — sits at the absolute centre of it. This is not a recreational vice to be photographed with amused detachment; it is where business is discussed, disputes are mediated, and social status is quietly negotiated over several unhurried hours. Foreigners who treat it as an anthropological curiosity rather than the genuine social infrastructure it is will find doors closing rather than opening.

Public dress codes are conservative by most global standards, for locals and visitors alike, and this extends well beyond religious buildings into everyday street life — a norm that has nothing to do with Yemen being "backward," a word I'd like permanently retired from travel writing, and everything to do with a coherent cultural logic around modesty that predates any Western opinion on the matter by well over a millennium.

Greetings in Yemen are longer and more formal than most Western travellers are prepared for. The exchange of "how are you, how is your health, how is your family" can run several rounds before anyone gets to the actual point of the conversation, and cutting it short to save time is read as brusque at best, disrespectful at worst. Gender norms around public interaction are more conservative here than in Iraq, and cross-gender greetings should be led by cues from Yemeni counterparts rather than assumptions imported from anywhere else. What foreigners consistently underestimate is how much genuine warmth sits behind the formality — once the ritual greeting is honoured properly, Yemenis are exactly as likely to insist you stay for a meal you didn't plan on eating as anyone else in the region.

## The Verdict

Iraq wins on sheer expressive volume — the arguments, the embraces, the tea offered like a small ceremony of insistence. It's a country that performs its warmth loudly and doesn't much care if you find it overwhelming; that's rather the point. Yemen wins on depth of formality — the qat session as genuine social infrastructure, the greeting ritual as a small act of mutual respect neither party is allowed to shortcut.

Neither country rewards the traveller who mistakes quiet Western reserve for good manners. Both reward patience, humility, and a willingness to sit through a greeting or a tea offer that takes four times longer than you think it should. My money's on Yemen for the deeper lesson in patience, Iraq for the better story to tell afterwards.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a former Baghdad-based consultant paraphrased: you will be offered tea by someone you've just met for the first time, and declining it flatly on the first pass will be read as an insult you didn't mean to deliver.*

> *expat.com forum — a Sana'a-based aid worker noted that skipping the full greeting sequence to "save time" in a meeting is the single fastest way to be quietly written off as untrustworthy.*

> *Internations Middle East forum — a longtime regional expat paraphrased: the qat session isn't a sideshow to the meeting, in Yemen it frequently is the meeting, and showing up without understanding that wastes everyone's afternoon.*

## Conclusion

Both Iraq and Yemen ask something Western public life has mostly forgotten how to do: slow down, greet properly, accept the tea. The countries get reduced in the press to a single grim narrative, and that narrative has nothing to say about the actual texture of a Tuesday afternoon greeting ritual or a four-hour qat session where the real business gets done. Show up humble, greet properly, and let the ritual take as long as it needs. Nobody here is impressed by how efficient your hello was.
