# Manila Worships the Mall Like a Cathedral. Dubai Built One the Size of a Small Country and Called It a Day Out

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇦🇪 UAE

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here are two countries that agree on one thing: a shopping centre is not a place to buy a shirt. It is a climate-controlled public square, a restaurant district, a cinema, a prayer room and a babysitter. The Philippines built its civic life around malls out of necessity. The UAE built its tourism around malls out of ambition. The result is a pair of countries where nobody has ever said, "I'm just popping to the shops."

And between them runs one of the great labour migration routes of the modern world. Filipinos in Dubai have made a small piece of the Gulf their own, and so I can compare the two retail cultures through the eyes of people who live in both. It is a better lens than any guidebook.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Philippines**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat the mall as a social outing, not an errand | Rush through in twenty minutes |
| Shop the sales at SM, Robinsons and Ayala | Pay full price on the first visit |
| Bring a jacket for the arctic air conditioning | Dress for the street temperature |
| Try the local brands | Assume imported means better |

**UAE**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Follow the dress guidelines posted at malls | Walk in wearing beachwear |
| Hunt for bargains in older districts like Karama | Do all your shopping at the flagship mega-mall |
| Use delivery for groceries | Carry heavy bags in the heat |
| Keep an eye on your spending | Treat credit like income |

## Philippines

The Philippine mall is a national institution. One American retiree who calls himself an SM mall rat names Robinsons, SM and Ayala as the big three and says SM is his favourite. He finds the supermarket does not price-gouge foreigners, mentions polo shirts from a local brand at around 480 to 520 pesos, and enthuses about a large cinema in the building. That is perfectly sensible. For a corporate expat in Metro Manila, the mall is where you find groceries, a pharmacy, a clinic, a bank and somewhere to take visitors.

The deeper truth is that the Philippines uses the mall because it has little else. Public space is limited, the heat is relentless and the traffic makes casual wandering a gamble. The mall solves all three. Families spend whole afternoons there, eat lunch there, meet there. I once watched a grandmother complete a full funeral-planning errand and a haircut in a single visit.

This also shapes the retail itself. Prices reflect local incomes, bargains arrive in waves of sales events, and the shops sell everything from branded basics to second-hand clothes in the markets nearby. Markets and street stalls are the counterpoint: cheaper, louder and more personal. A Filipino shopper switches between mall and market with the ease of a person changing channels.

The cultural cost is that the mall culture can feel like a monoculture. Every big mall looks alike, plays the same music and sells the same brands. But it is hard to complain when the whole thing works, the toilets are clean and the food hall is better than most restaurants.

The practical implications for a visiting executive are small but telling. A Philippine mall will usually have a pharmacy, a bank branch, a supermarket and a food court on one site, so the whole errand list can be completed without moving the car. Sales events arrive often and are taken seriously, which means that patience is a financial strategy as much as a virtue. The brand loyalty is real, too: the retiree blogger describes particular local brands with the affection other people reserve for football teams. If you want to understand Filipino consumer life, spend a Saturday in a mall and watch the whole family cheerfully refuse to hurry.

## UAE

The UAE runs a more theatrical version. Malls are an attraction, an economic engine and a status symbol. But the shopping lives in layers. Guidance posted in malls includes dress-code reminders, and delivery culture is so advanced that a Filipino writer described how a corner grocery brings her order within minutes of a WhatsApp message. Another friend, she said, once had a supermarket deliver a single onion on request. Convenience is the currency.

Yet the thing I find most telling is where Filipinos actually shop. Al Attar Shopping Centre in Karama, a five-storey building with a trade licence dating to 1984, hosts more than a hundred shops, nearly thirty kiosks and over two dozen beauty salons, along with six Filipino restaurants. It is nicknamed the Divisoria of Dubai, after Manila's famous bargain district. Shoppers told Gulf News they travel across the city because prices are low and Filipino products are on the shelves. One receptionist visits about five times a month for uniforms and Filipino goods. A salon worker said bundled hair treatments cost far less than elsewhere, and a haircut at one shop cost just 20 dirhams.

Bayut's guide adds that Filipino supermarkets in Deira and Al Muraqqabat are open round the clock. The mega-malls, in other words, are for tourists, and the real Dubai shopping belongs to the communities that built the city.

The risk is spending. A Filipina writer in Dubai described overspending on luxury goods in her early days, a confession that will sound familiar to anyone who has had a salary increase and a mall within walking distance. The UAE's retail is designed to make you feel rich. Budget accordingly.

The community dimension in the UAE deserves more credit than it gets. Filipino supermarkets that stay open around the clock, a mall with six Filipino restaurants under one roof and a market that sells brooms, taho and second-hand clothes are not retail novelties. They are infrastructure for people who left home for work and want a piece of it back. The big shopping destinations may draw the headlines, but the quieter districts are where the migrant economy actually lives, bargains, gossip and all. It is the part of Dubai the postcards leave out, and the part I find most human.

## The Verdict

The Philippines wins on warmth and the UAE wins on choice. If you want a shopping trip to feel like a family reunion, the Philippines is hard to beat. The malls are crowded, social and calibrated to the local wallet. If you want global brands, immaculate service and delivery within minutes, the UAE runs a shop that never closes.

But I think the honest winner is the district, not the country. Karama, with its Filipino perfume sellers, its ukay-ukay and its karaoke sets, tells a more truthful story about how migrants shop than any flagship mall. It is Manila by transplant, adapted to the Gulf and priced for people who send money home.

My vote goes to the Philippines for retail atmosphere, because it has never pretended that shopping was anything but social. The UAE gets the prize for infrastructure, and a stern warning for the way it makes spending feel so effortless. In Manila you leave tired and full. In Dubai you leave tired and slightly poorer.

## Conclusion

Shopping tells you who a society thinks it is. The Philippines thinks it is a family, so its malls are living rooms. The UAE thinks it is a destination, so its malls are monuments.

Take the best of each. Shop at the big mall for the air conditioning and the cinema, then go to Karama or the neighbourhood market for the prices and the stories. Keep your receipts, ignore the sales pressure and carry your own jacket.

And if somebody offers to deliver a single onion to your door, say yes. It is the most honest service on earth.
