# One Country Has Made Cash Basically Extinct, the Other Still Trusts a Handshake at the Market Stall

🇨🇳 China × 🇧🇼 Botswana
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I once tried to pay cash for a bottle of water in a mid-sized Chinese city and was looked at with the specific mixture of confusion and pity usually reserved for someone trying to pay in seashells. In Gaborone, meanwhile, I've watched a market vendor and a regular customer settle a price entirely through familiarity and eye contact, no app, no scanner, just years of buying vegetables from the same stall building the kind of trust no QR code could replicate.

China has leapt so far past cash that entire retail ecosystems now assume you're paying through a phone before you've said a word. Botswana, particularly outside its malls, still runs substantially on relationship-based commerce, where knowing your vendor matters more than any payment technology could. Both systems work extremely well. Neither one will make any sense to someone arriving from the other.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇨🇳 China**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Set up WeChat Pay or Alipay before you need to buy anything | Assume cash will be readily accepted at small vendors |
| Use mini-programs inside super-apps for almost every errand | Expect a separate app for every single retail interaction |
| Get comfortable with QR-code-first ordering, even in restaurants | Assume a foreign card will work smoothly everywhere |
| Embrace livestream shopping; it's a genuine mainstream retail channel | Dismiss livestream commerce as a niche or gimmicky trend |

**🇧🇼 Botswana**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a relationship with regular market vendors over time | Expect fixed, non-negotiable pricing at open-air markets |
| Carry cash for informal market and roadside stall purchases | Assume card payment is universal outside malls and supermarkets |
| Learn basic price negotiation norms; it's expected, not rude | Haggle aggressively without any warmth; it damages the relationship |
| Support local trading posts alongside mall-based retail | Treat malls as the only legitimate shopping option |

## China: A Retail World Built Entirely Around the Phone

China's shopping culture has moved so completely past cash that the country now represents one of the most cashless major economies on earth, and it happened astonishingly fast. WeChat Pay and Alipay aren't just payment options sitting alongside cash and card, they've functionally become the default and, in huge swaths of daily commerce, the only practical option — small vendors, street food stalls, and taxi drivers alike expect a QR code scan, not a wallet.

What makes this even more distinct is how deeply integrated shopping has become with social and entertainment platforms. Livestream commerce, where hosts sell products directly to viewers in real time with instant one-tap purchasing, isn't a fringe trend, it's a mainstream, enormous retail channel that has genuinely reshaped how millions of people discover and buy products, from cosmetics to fresh produce. The entire experience collapses browsing, socialising, and purchasing into a single continuous phone-based activity in a way few other retail cultures have replicated at this scale.

Visitors who arrive expecting a more traditional retail experience — separate payment terminals, cash as a reliable backup, distinct standalone stores — often find themselves quietly out of step within days. Foreign cards and cash still technically function in many places, but the friction of using them, compared to the frictionless one-tap norm locals expect, becomes obvious fast. China's shopping culture, at its core, has been rebuilt from the ground up around mobile-first convenience, to the point where the phone isn't a shopping tool anymore, it's effectively the store itself.

## Botswana: Commerce Built on Familiarity

Botswana's shopping culture, particularly outside Gaborone's shopping malls and formal retail chains, still runs substantially on something China's system has largely engineered away: personal relationships between buyer and seller. At open-air markets and informal trading posts, a regular customer's standing with a specific vendor genuinely affects price, quality of produce offered, and even flexibility on payment — this isn't nostalgia, it's an active, functioning commercial system built on repeated interaction rather than app-mediated anonymity.

Price negotiation at these markets isn't an awkward haggle to be endured, it's an expected, almost social part of the transaction, one that rewards warmth and familiarity over aggressive bargaining. Vendors remember regular customers, extend informal credit to trusted buyers, and adjust based on relationship in ways no fixed-price retail system would allow. This creates a shopping rhythm that's slower and more personal than the formal mall-based alternative sitting just a few kilometres away, and many Batswana move fluidly between both, choosing the market for produce and personal goods, the mall for anything requiring fixed pricing or card payment.

Cash remains genuinely essential in this informal economy in a way it simply isn't in urban China anymore — card and mobile payment infrastructure exists in Botswana's formal retail sector, but the open-air markets and roadside stalls that make up so much of everyday shopping still expect physical currency, and showing up without it at a market you actually want to buy from is a real practical problem, not a minor inconvenience. Botswana's shopping culture, in short, hasn't been replaced by technology so much as it continues to run alongside it, each serving a different kind of transaction.

## The Verdict

China has built a shopping culture so thoroughly mediated by the phone that cash has become the exception rather than the rule, collapsing browsing, paying, and even socialising into one continuous mobile experience. Botswana still runs meaningful commerce on personal familiarity and cash-based trust, particularly at the markets where the real texture of daily shopping lives. Neither is more advanced than the other — they're solving for different things, one for frictionless scale, the other for durable relationship.

If I'm honest, I find something genuinely warmer about a Gaborone vendor who remembers exactly how I like my tomatoes sorted than any one-tap livestream purchase in Shanghai — but I will also confess to a small thrill every time my phone buys me lunch without me touching a single note.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora, "Is it true you can't function in Chinese cities without WeChat Pay?" — paraphrased: a respondent said within days of arriving they realised cash had become more of a liability than a backup, since some smaller vendors genuinely didn't have change or the patience to make it.*

> *expat.com Shanghai — paraphrased: a new arrival described the specific disorientation of ordering food entirely through a table QR code without speaking to a single staff member, and how quickly that started to feel completely normal.*

> *Internations Gaborone — paraphrased: an expat said it took months of buying from the same three market vendors before prices started quietly improving, and realised that was the entire point of showing up regularly.*

## Conclusion

China and Botswana have arrived at two shopping cultures built on almost opposite foundations — one engineered around frictionless, app-mediated speed, the other sustained by slow, personal, cash-based trust. Bring China's phone-first instincts to a Botswana market stall and you'll find yourself oddly disconnected from a system that runs on faces, not screens. Bring Botswana's relationship-based patience to a Chinese retail app and you'll simply be standing still while everyone else has already checked out. Figure out which currency actually matters where you are — money, or familiarity — before you shop.
