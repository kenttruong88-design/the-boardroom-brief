# Poland Runs Its Trams Like Clockwork. Colombia Runs Its Buses Like a Group Chat.

🇵🇱 Poland · 🇨🇴 Colombia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Poland's public transport operates on the terrifying premise that a tram scheduled for 8:14 will arrive at 8:14, and if it's 8:16, someone at ZTM has personally failed you and will hear about it. Colombia's public transport operates on the premise that time is a suggestion, the bus will get there when it gets there, and if you're not psychologically prepared to be pressed chest-to-chest against six strangers on the TransMilenio, that's a you problem.

I have stood in Warsaw in January, watching my breath freeze, comforted only by the tram app's unnerving accuracy. I have also stood on a Bogotá platform being physically absorbed into a red bus like cargo, no personal space, no apology, no eye contact, just forward momentum and faith. Both systems get you where you're going. Only one of them makes you question your relationship with your own ribcage.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇵🇱 Poland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Validate your ticket the second you board — inspectors are relentless | Assume a driver will wait if you're sprinting for the tram; they will not |
| Download the Jakdojade app before you land | Eat on the metro — it's mildly frowned upon, unlike most of Europe |
| Give up your seat for the elderly instantly, without being asked | Expect small talk with strangers — commuting is a silent, serious business |

**🇨🇴 Colombia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a TransMilenio card and load it with more credit than you think you need | Ride alone late at night without checking which routes are safe with locals first |
| Use ride-hailing apps rather than hailing taxis on the street | Expect a fixed personal-space bubble — physical proximity is just the deal |
| Ask a local which entrance to use — some stations are notoriously safer than others | Get on without a plan for exiting — the crush at popular stops is not for the faint-hearted |

## Poland: A Country That Weaponised Punctuality

Warsaw's trams are, unfashionably, one of the most reliable things I have encountered anywhere in Europe, which is either a triumph of post-communist infrastructure planning or a national coping mechanism for decades of things being decidedly unreliable. Statistics Poland's transport data shows trams and buses carrying the overwhelming bulk of urban commuters, and having lived through a Warsaw winter, I understand why — nobody in their right mind is cycling through minus twelve degrees for fun.

The tram network itself is a genuinely elegant piece of urban design, criss-crossing the city with the sort of efficiency that makes you suspicious. Ticket inspectors appear with the theatrical suddenness of a magic trick, and heaven help you if you haven't validated — the fine is substantial and the shame is worse, because everyone around you will silently judge you as the tourist who didn't know the rules. Poles do not chat on public transport. This isn't rudeness; it's a shared understanding that the commute is a private, contemplative interlude between two more social parts of the day, and interrupting a stranger's silence to ask directions is treated as a minor act of aggression.

What genuinely impresses me is the seat-giving instinct — elderly passengers, pregnant women, anyone visibly struggling gets a seat offered within seconds, no hesitation, no performative reluctance. It's the one moment of warmth in an otherwise stoic system. Warsaw's metro, smaller than you'd expect for a capital, is clean to the point of feeling faintly unused, and the whole apparatus runs with a discipline that would make a Swiss train conductor weep with joy. It is, in short, transport as engineering rather than transport as vibe.

## Colombia: Transport as Full-Contact Sport

Bogotá's TransMilenio is famous, and not for good reasons if you ask anyone who's used it during rush hour. It is a genuinely impressive piece of bus rapid transit infrastructure on paper — dedicated lanes, high-capacity articulated buses, the works — and TransMilenio's own reports show ridership numbers that would make most Western transit authorities faint. In practice, at peak hours, it becomes a masterclass in the physics of compression, where personal space isn't reduced so much as abolished entirely. You will be pressed against people you've never met with an intimacy usually reserved for long-term relationships, and everyone will act as though this is completely normal, because to them, it is.

DANE's mobility surveys reflect what any Bogotano will tell you unprompted: public transport here is a daily negotiation, part logistics, part endurance sport. Ride-hailing apps have become the sensible middle ground for anyone unwilling to test their skeletal structure against a bus at 7:45am, and most expats I've spoken to switch to them within a fortnight of arriving, dignity intact. Medellín does it better, frankly — the Metro and the famous cable cars (Metrocable) connecting hillside barrios to the city centre are a genuine point of national pride, clean and orderly in a way that surprises people who've only experienced Bogotá's chaos.

The thing about Colombian commuting culture that took me longest to understand is that the apparent disorder has its own internal logic. Locals know exactly which door to stand near, which hour to avoid, which route has a reputation. It looks chaotic to outsiders because we haven't learned the choreography yet.

## The Verdict

Poland wins, unambiguously, on reliability, cleanliness, and not being crushed like a sardine. But there's something almost joyless about a commute so silent and efficient it barely registers as a shared human experience. Colombia's system is objectively more stressful and occasionally alarming, but it has a pulse — you arrive at work having actually interacted, however involuntarily, with your city. If efficiency is your god, move to Warsaw. If you want your commute to remind you that you're alive, and possibly slightly claustrophobic, Bogotá delivers.

## Conclusion

Both countries have built transport systems that reflect their national character with almost comic precision: Poland's is rigorous, silent, and slightly cold; Colombia's is chaotic, warm, and occasionally terrifying. Neither is wrong. Poland will get you to work on time with your dignity fully intact and your personal space respected to a fault. Colombia will get you to work eventually, having bonded, unwillingly, with several strangers at close range. Choose your commute, choose your personality.
