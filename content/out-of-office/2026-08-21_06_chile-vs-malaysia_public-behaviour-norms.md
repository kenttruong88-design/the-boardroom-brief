# Queuing Is a Suggestion in One Country and a Near-Sacred Ritual in the Other

🇨🇱 Chile 🇲🇾 Malaysia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Santiago's public behaviour has a controlled, almost formal quality to it — quiet metro carriages, a real if unspoken commitment to personal space, queues that form and hold with a discipline that would impress a Londoner. Kuala Lumpur runs on a completely different social operating system: warmer, louder, more physically close, queues that exist more as a loose consensus than a rule, and an overall tolerance for public noise and proximity that takes real recalibration if you're arriving from somewhere colder and more spatially precious.

I've stood in a Santiago metro carriage so silent I could hear my own heartbeat, surrounded by people who'd clearly agreed, without discussion, that eye contact was a bridge too far. I've also been gently, cheerfully shoved through a KL night market queue by an auntie who decided I was taking too long to decide on satay. Both systems work. Neither is objectively correct. One is considerably more fun to be inside of.

## Do's & Don'ts: Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Queue in an orderly, silent line and wait your turn without commentary | Speak loudly on public transport; low voices and headphones are the unspoken standard |
| Greet with a single cheek kiss in most social contexts, even semi-formal ones | Stand too close in a queue or on the metro; Chileans guard personal space carefully |
| Say "permiso" before squeezing past someone in a crowded space | Cut in line, even accidentally; it will be noticed and quietly judged, not confronted |
| Match the generally reserved public tone, especially in Santiago's downtown core | Assume Chilean warmth in private settings extends to public strangers; it largely doesn't |

## Do's & Don'ts: Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept that queues in markets and hawker centres are looser and more communal than formal lines | Get visibly frustrated by queue-jumping in casual settings; it's rarely meant as disrespect |
| Use your right hand for eating, giving, and receiving — it's a real and widely observed norm | Point directly at people or sacred objects with your finger; use an open hand or nod instead |
| Expect closer physical proximity in crowded public spaces as completely normal, not rude | Show public displays of anger or raised voices; loss of composure ("malu") carries real social cost |
| Remove shoes when entering homes and some smaller shops or places of worship without being asked | Touch someone's head, even a child's, without real familiarity — it carries cultural weight |

## Chile: Quiet, Contained, and Extremely Deliberate

Santiago's public behaviour reads, at first, almost Northern European in its restraint — a striking contrast to the loud, warm, tactile stereotype often (lazily) applied to Latin America generally. The metro is close to silent during commuting hours, an unspoken agreement among strangers that conversation, phone calls, and general noise belong somewhere else. Queues form with real discipline, and cutting one, even accidentally, generates the kind of silent, withering disapproval that's arguably more effective than any confrontation would be.

Personal space is guarded carefully in public, even as private social settings run considerably warmer — the cheek kiss on greeting, the genuine hospitality once you're inside someone's actual social circle. That gap between public reserve and private warmth is, I think, the single most interesting thing about Chilean social norms, and it consistently catches newcomers off guard. You'll spend weeks assuming Santiago is simply a cold, formal city, and then get invited to an asado and discover an entirely different register exists, just gated behind actual relationship, not offered freely to strangers on a bus.

What I respect about the Chilean approach is its consistency — the rules are clear, if unstated, and everyone follows them with real discipline. What I find slightly exhausting, over an extended stay, is the sheer effort required to access the warmth underneath. Public Santiago is a controlled, considerate, occasionally chilly performance of civility. It's a system that clearly works, and works well for the people inside it, but it asks a lot of patience from anyone waiting to be let past the surface.

## Malaysia: Loud, Close, and Genuinely Warm From the Start

Kuala Lumpur's public behaviour runs almost in direct inversion — considerably louder, physically closer, more communally porous, with queue discipline that exists more as a rough shared understanding than a hard rule, especially in markets, hawker centres, and anywhere food is involved. This isn't disorder; it's a different, equally functional social contract, one built around flexibility and communal negotiation rather than rigid individual turn-taking.

What struck me immediately, and what I think genuinely distinguishes Malaysia, is how quickly the warmth is extended to strangers rather than reserved for established relationships. An auntie ushering you through a market queue, a stranger correcting your etiquette with cheerful bluntness rather than silent judgement, the sheer amount of unprompted small talk in situations that would be silent in Santiago — Malaysia's public space defaults to sociability rather than reserve, and it's a genuinely more immediately welcoming register for a newcomer to step into.

The etiquette that does exist carries real weight, though, and ignoring it lands badly — the right-hand rule for eating and giving, not pointing directly at people, the strong cultural emphasis on maintaining composure and avoiding public displays of anger (the concept of malu, social shame from a loss of face, runs deep and shapes public interaction constantly, even if it's rarely spoken about directly to outsiders). Malaysia's public space is loose and warm on the surface, but it sits on a genuinely serious substructure of respect and face-saving that's easy to violate without realising it.

## The Verdict

Malaysia wins, primarily because its public warmth is available immediately, to anyone, rather than gated behind an established relationship. Chile's system is elegant and disciplined, but the emotional distance it maintains in public — even among people who are, in private, genuinely warm — makes it a harder, colder place to simply exist as a newcomer navigating daily life.

That said, Chile deserves real credit for consistency and consideration — a silent, respectful metro carriage is a genuine public good that plenty of louder cultures could stand to learn from. But given the choice between quiet discipline that keeps you at arm's length and loud warmth that pulls you in from day one, I know which queue I'd rather be shoved through.

## Conclusion

Chile has built a public culture of quiet discipline that rewards patience and punishes noise. Malaysia has built one that runs on warmth, proximity, and a looser sense of order that somehow still gets everyone exactly where they're going. Both systems function. Only one of them will talk to you on the way there. Pack accordingly, and in Santiago, for the love of God, lower your voice on the metro.
