# Poland Lifts Heavy and Czechia Lifts a Glass, and Only One of Them Will Admit It

🇵🇱 Poland vs 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Poland takes the gym seriously in the way it takes most things seriously: with a sense of mission and a slight air of suspicion towards anyone treating it as a hobby. Warsaw is full of people lifting, running and swimming in an earnest attempt at self-improvement, and the chains compete for them with the fierce, slightly desperate energy of a country that has decided health is a project.

Czechia approaches fitness the way it approaches everything else: sideways, with a beer in the other hand. Czechs are active, genuinely so, but they do it by hiking, cycling and skiing, not by paying a monthly fee to look at themselves in a mirror. Which makes the gym culture a quirky, patchy, and rather charming thing to explore as an expat.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Poland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Try a trial session before signing a contract | Sign up on the day because of a good sales pitch |
| Compare the main chains with independent studios | Assume the biggest brand is the best value |
| Check peak-hour crowding at your usual time | Join in January without visiting first |
| Ask clearly about cancellation terms | Skim the small print |

**Czechia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your employer about sports benefit schemes | Pay full price without checking |
| Consider outdoor activity as your main workout | Treat the gym as the only option |
| Visit at your usual training time to gauge crowds | Join blind |
| Look at smaller local studios and community halls | Ignore anything that is not a chain |

## Poland

Polish gym culture is big, competitive and commercial. Warsaw alone has a wide range of chains and independent studios, from budget operations to glossy premium clubs with pools and wellness suites. A Talesmag post report on the city, built from reader contributions spanning 2009 to 2021, names Holmes Place and Jatomi among the main chains, and mentions a water park with a fifty-metre Olympic pool. Reported monthly prices ranged from roughly a hundred to a hundred and fifty zloty at the low end, up to around a hundred dollars for the grander clubs.

Those figures are old, and the lesson is the range, not the number. Poland offers a membership for almost every budget, which is excellent news if you want one and bad news if you hate choosing. Expect group classes, a strong culture of personal training, and a social scene that treats the gym as part of the city's professional life. Offices often take part in sports benefit programmes, and lunchtime sessions are normal.

There is also a warning worth repeating. One contributor to the Talesmag report cautioned about heavy personal-training sales pitches, and anyone who has joined a gym in any country will recognise the pattern. A beaming person with a tablet appears, tells you your body fat is a tragedy, and offers a package that costs more than your rent. Poland is not special in this, but the commercial intensity is real. Say no politely and repeat it until they leave.

What distinguishes Polish fitness is ambition. People train hard, run races, pursue strength goals, and treat the results as something to be earned. A newcomer is welcomed more by ambition than warmth, and that works if you are the sort who likes a training partner who is fully committed. If you prefer a gentle approach, you can find it, but you will have to look.

## Czechia

Czechia has gyms, plenty of them, and the expat experience has historically been a patchwork. An Expats.cz guide to Prague gyms, published in 2009 and now plainly out of date, described World Class and Holmes Place as the largest and pricier options, and recommended the useful, universal trick of visiting at the time you actually plan to train to see how crowded it gets. It also pointed out that comparing pay-as-you-go prices with monthly fees can save money, and described smaller specialist places with boxing and dance classes, plus a hotel gym with a pool, squash court and climbing wall.

A forum thread on Expat.com, undated, shows the same texture. A poster near Průhonice wanted a gym with group classes, and replies named a local sports hall, an aqua centre and Prague chains alongside cheaper small studios. The last reply simply recommends the sports hall. That is classic Czech expat advice: not a brand, but a place a neighbour likes.

The Czech workplace sports benefit is a major part of the fitness economy, and one popular scheme lets employees access a large network of sports facilities for a fixed monthly contribution. If your employer offers it, take it, because it turns your fitness life into a menu rather than a contract.

The deeper truth is that Czechs rarely separate fitness from leisure. The weekend hike, the bike ride along the river, the winter ski trip, the swimming pond: these are fitness. A gym is for February, or for a Tuesday after work. This makes the Czech gym scene feel less ideological than the Polish one. People are fit, but they would be embarrassed to be seen trying.

## The Verdict

For pure gym quality and choice, Poland wins. The market is competitive, the facilities are strong, and if you want to lift seriously, you will find a place to do it and a crowd that understands. The culture takes training seriously, and a serious person will feel at home.

For a life in which fitness is simply woven into the week, Czechia wins. The country has an easy, outdoorsy rhythm that gets you moving without a membership card. You may never join a gym at all, and you will still be better off than most.

I am going to give this to Poland, narrowly, because the question was about gym culture and Poland has more of it. But if you asked me where I would actually be fitter after a year, I would say Czechia, for the cheating reason that nobody there ever calls it exercise.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expats.cz gym guide (2009, outdated) — the practical advice has aged better than the prices: visit at your usual workout time to see how crowded the place really is, and compare pay-as-you-go with monthly fees.*

> *Talesmag Warsaw post report (entries 2009 to 2021) — one reader warns about aggressive personal-training sales pitches, a reminder that the pressure to buy a package is common everywhere.*

> *Expat.com forum (undated) — a poster looking for group classes near Pruhonice got answers pointing to a local sports hall, an aqua centre and cheaper small studios, which is the whole Czech approach in one thread.*

## Conclusion

Poland trains to improve. Czechia moves to enjoy. Neither is wrong, and the expat who understands the difference will save a great deal of money and a certain amount of vanity.

If you move to Warsaw, bring discipline and a firm way of saying no to salespeople. If you move to Prague, bring walking boots and a willingness to be fit by accident. And if you move between them, remember that the best gym is the one you actually go to, and the best personal trainer is whoever drags you out of the flat in the rain. Preferably with a sausage waiting at the end.
