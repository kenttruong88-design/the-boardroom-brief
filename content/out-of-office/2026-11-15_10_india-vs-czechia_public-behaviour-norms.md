# India Says Hello to Everyone at Volume. Czechia Says Nothing to Anyone, Politely, and Takes Its Shoes Off Indoors

🇮🇳 India vs 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Public life in India is a participatory sport. People talk, ask, advise, honk, shout across the road and offer opinions on your choice of vegetables. Personal space is a flexible concept, a queue is a living organism and the line between a stranger and a relative is crossed approximately every four minutes. It can be exhausting, and it is rarely lonely.

Public life in Czechia runs on the opposite principle. The default setting is quiet, and the quiet is defended. Voices drop on trams, strangers do not chat and everyone appears to be carrying a private cloud of reserve. The Czechs are not unfriendly; they simply consider a stranger's life to be none of their business. If you are arriving from a country where everyone is involved in everything, this will feel like moving into a library.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect questions from strangers and answer them with good humour | Treat curiosity as an insult |
| Be ready for crowds, noise and flexible queues | Insist on a personal bubble |
| Use titles and "ji" with elders | Skip the respect |
| Accept the cup of tea | Refuse it twice |

**Czechia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet shopkeepers with "dobrý den" and leave with "na shledanou" | Walk in and start talking |
| Keep your voice low on public transport | Take a loud phone call on a tram |
| Stand on the right of the escalator | Block the pass |
| Give up your seat for the elderly, pregnant and very young | Pretend not to see |

## India

A word of honesty: the Indian half of this article is general knowledge, not a fetched source, and it should be read as a broad sketch of a vast and varied country. But some things hold nearly everywhere. Public space in India is a shared room. Strangers speak to each other on trains, shopkeepers know your name by the second visit, and a question that would be intrusive elsewhere, such as where you are from, what you do and whether you are married, is asked as a form of welcome. Noise is background. Crowds are weather.

Queues, where they exist, are negotiated. Personal space is a luxury. Respect shows up in language, in "sir" and "madam", in "uncle" and "aunty" for strangers, in the assumption that a stranger might one day be someone's relative. In a society that dense, politeness is less about distance than about warmth, and the idea of ignoring a person next to you would feel rude.

That is what makes the Czech world feel so odd on arrival. Nobody is being hostile. They are simply operating on a different theory of respect, one that says the best thing you can do for a stranger is leave them in peace.

Even the warmth has a code. A stranger may offer you tea, call you uncle or aunty, ask what you earn and then, ten minutes later, give you directions with a thoroughness that would shame a satnav. Horns are a language, not an insult, and a nod of the head can mean three different things depending on the person doing it. It is a society where the public realm is also a family room, and the rules are learned by joining in, not by reading a sign. Again, this is a broad sketch, and India is many countries stacked on top of each other, but the rhythm of public interaction, from the train carriage to the market, is notably communal. The first week in a Czech tram will feel like turning down the volume on the world.

## Czechia

The Czech rule book is long and quietly enforced. An Expats.cz guide to public behaviour, written by Ryan Scott in 2010, says spitting in public is unacceptable, that blowing your nose is fine if you turn away and loud sniffling is not, that you should give up your seat to the elderly, pregnant women and very young children, and that on escalators you stand on the right and pass on the left, saying "s dovolením" to get through. Phones are tolerated, but not in post offices, banks, hospitals or libraries, where a sign shows a phone in a red circle. Shouting or whistling to friends is frowned on by older people. The author tells of loudly sniffing on a train and being handed a pack of tissues by an irate woman, and says his wife now carries tissues because of it.

That story is the whole country in a handbag. The Czechs will correct you, but they will do it with an object and a stare, not a speech.

Foreigners.cz calls Czechs reserved at first and says that is the norm and not rudeness, and advises keeping your voice low on public transport and using "pan" or "paní" with a surname until invited to do otherwise. A 2006 Expats.cz article adds that the cool distance from strangers is a custom, not unkindness, and that people warm up once they know you. Shoes come off at the door of a Czech home, flowers come in odd numbers and chrysanthemums are for graves.

Not everyone finds this charming. In a January 2025 Expats.cz roundup of newcomers' advice, Fabrizio from Italy describes Czechs as reserved, stand-offish and insular, while Gabriele from Lithuania tells people to be ready for extremely rude Czechs. Chloe, from Andorra, warns that the foreign police and the Immigration Office do not speak English and says bureaucracy is a never-ending story. Theo, from the US, offers the counterpoint: most Czechs are like people anywhere. Most of these voices say the same thing in different words: learn some Czech, say good morning and do not take the coldness personally.

There is a good deal more to the Czech etiquette book, and it is worth knowing before you arrive. The older Expats.cz guides say tipping of around ten per cent is customary in restaurants, bars, taxis and salons, that men traditionally enter a restaurant before women, and that whistling is taken as booing, not applause. Anyone with a doctorate may be addressed as doctor, public toilets may charge about five crowns and a toast, with the words na zdraví, requires eye contact and a clink with everybody at the table. Greet the shopkeeper with dobrý den before about six in the evening and dobrý večer afterwards, and say na shledanou on the way out. None of this is difficult. It is simply a lot of small ceremonies, each one a way of saying that you noticed the other person was there, which is, if you think about it, a quiet version of the Indian welcome.

## The Verdict

India wins on warmth, and Czechia wins on calm. If you value being noticed, included and fed, India is the better place to be a person in public. If you value quiet, order and the right to be invisible on a tram, Czechia is hard to beat.

I will say this. A society that expects you to say "dobrý den" to a shopkeeper and "na shledanou" on the way out is not cold. It is formal, which is different. The Czechs build a polite wall and then, if you are patient, open a door in it. It takes longer than a handshake in Delhi, but the door, once open, tends to stay open.

The sting is this. Indian public life asks you to share everything and Czech public life asks you to share nothing. The sensible traveller learns to do both, depending on the room.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Ryan Scott, Expats.cz (2010) — sniffled loudly on a train, was handed a pack of tissues by an irate woman, and now his wife carries tissues in case he forgets.*

> *Fabrizio, Italian expat, and Gabriele, Lithuanian expat, via Expats.cz (2025) — Czechs can be reserved and stand-offish, and newcomers should be ready for some extremely rude encounters.*

> *Theo, American expat, via Expats.cz (2025) — locals vary widely, and most Czechs are like locals anywhere.*

## Conclusion

Public behaviour is the grammar of a society. India speaks in a loud, generous chorus. Czechia speaks in short, precise sentences, usually with a hat.

If you cross from one to the other, do not lose your warmth. Just learn the quiet. Take your shoes off, lower your voice, say "dobrý den" and carry tissues.

And when a Czech stranger finally smiles at you on a tram, treat it like the rare thing it is.
