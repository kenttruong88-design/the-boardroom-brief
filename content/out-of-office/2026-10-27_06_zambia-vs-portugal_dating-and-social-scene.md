# Zambia Will Marry You Off By Sunday Lunch. Portugal Will Just Leave You On Read.

🇿🇲 Zambia vs 🇵🇹 Portugal

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

People love to tell me that Africa is "exotic" and Europe is "civilised," as though one continent is a theme park and the other is a finishing school. I have dated, failed to date, and watched other fools attempt to date on both, and I can report that neither adjective survives contact with reality. Zambia will fold you into a family before you've learned anyone's surname. Portugal will let you circle each other for eighteen months before admitting, very quietly, over a third glass of vinho verde, that yes, this might be something.

Both countries are, in their own ways, allergic to the breezy, no-strings Western dating-app shuffle. Zambia simply skips it and goes straight to the in-laws. Portugal performs the shuffle beautifully, with excellent lighting and a great deal of saudade, and then refuses to actually move forward. If you are the kind of person who needs clarity by date three, I regret to inform you that you have chosen the wrong hemisphere twice.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Zambia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet every elder in the room before you greet your date | Walk in and head straight for the person you fancy |
| Expect to be vetted by aunties within the first month | Assume this is "moving fast" — it's just due diligence |
| Bring something for the family on a first proper visit | Show up empty-handed and call it casual |
| Treat weekend church and family lunches as the real social calendar | Try to build a social life entirely around bars and clubs |

**Portugal**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept that "let's grab a coffee" can mean three weeks from now | Chase someone who takes two days to reply to "oi" |
| Let the relationship stay undefined for longer than feels sane | Ask "so what are we?" before month four |
| Learn to enjoy a four-hour dinner as the actual date | Expect a quick, efficient evening out |
| Respect that meeting the family is a milestone, not a formality | Introduce a new partner to your parents casually, on a whim |

## Zambia

Zambian dating is a team sport, and the team is your entire extended family, whether you consented to their participation or not. I say this with admiration, not horror. Within weeks of seeing someone in Lusaka, I had met two aunts, a cousin who worked in insurance, and a grandmother who asked, with zero irony, when the wedding was. Nobody found this premature. It is simply how courtship works here: the relationship isn't private property between two consenting adults, it's a civic event with stakeholders.

The social scene itself runs on church, family compounds, and weekend lunches that stretch from noon until somebody falls asleep in a plastic chair. Bars and nightlife exist, concentrated in Lusaka's Kabulonga and Sunningdale areas and the lodges around the Livingstone tourist corridor, but they are not where the real relationship work happens. That happens at someone's house, over nshima, with at least four other people listening in and offering opinions you did not ask for. Expat forums will tell you, correctly, that cracking into local social circles takes real patience — Zambians are warm but assume you already have your people, so the onus is on you to show up repeatedly before you're folded in, not the other way around.

What nobody tells sweet, deluded foreigners is that gender roles here are not aspirational throwbacks you get to ironically enjoy for a season. Courtship is openly transactional in ways polite Western dating pretends it isn't — lobola negotiations, family approval, and a timeline toward marriage that moves faster than anything you've experienced unless you grew up somewhere equally direct. There is very little ambiguity, which is either refreshing or terrifying depending on your relationship with commitment. The flip side is a loneliness problem for anyone who doesn't want marriage on the table: casual dating as a lifestyle, rather than a phase, barely exists as a concept, and the expat and NGO social circuit can start to feel like the same forty people rotating through the same three venues indefinitely.

## Portugal

Portugal has the opposite problem entirely: an abundance of ambiguity, served slowly, over an exceptional meal. The Portuguese do not rush anything, least of all feelings. The word saudade — that untranslatable ache of longing — isn't just a lyric in a fado song, it's the operating system. People fall into situationships with the structural integrity of a four-year relationship and the official status of nothing at all, and both parties seem completely at peace with this.

Lisbon and Porto have thriving nightlife — Bairro Alto's bar-crawl chaos, Príncipe Real's slower, wine-bar seduction — and a visible, confident queer scene, especially in Lisbon, which genuinely outpaces most of Southern Europe. Dating apps are everywhere among the under-35s, but they function more as an introduction service than an engine of speed; Portuguese daters routinely take weeks to reply, not from disinterest but because urgency itself reads as slightly undignified. Expat guides describe this exact trap: foreigners mistake the slow pace for rejection and bail just as things were quietly, glacially progressing.

Meeting the family is the real threshold here, and it arrives late. Where a Zambian date might produce an aunt by week two, a Portuguese partner might wait a year before risking Sunday lunch with the parents — because that introduction means something, and the Portuguese do not deploy meaning casually. Add in a culture that still runs heavily through friend groups and neighbourhood cafés rather than apps, and you get a dating scene that rewards patience and punishes anyone used to the clarity of a DTR conversation. Lovely people. Infuriating pace.

## The Verdict

If you want to know where you stand, go to Zambia. You will know by the second family lunch whether this is heading toward marriage, and you will know because someone's grandmother will simply tell you. If you want to be charmed, strung along gorgeously, and slowly starved of a straight answer for the better part of a year, book a flight to Lisbon.

I am calling this for Zambia, and I will not be issuing a correction. Clarity, even aggressive, auntie-enforced clarity, beats ambiguity dressed up as romance every single time. Portugal's dating culture is gorgeous theatre — all candlelight, fado, and lingering eye contact — but theatre is still theatre, and at some point you want to know if the show is actually going anywhere or if you're just a recurring extra. Zambia tells you on day one that you're either cast in the lead role or not auditioning at all.

The honourable exception: if you genuinely want to stay unlabelled and unbothered for a few years while eating extremely well, Portugal will treat you kindly and feed you constantly. Just don't mistake the warmth for momentum. One of these countries will put a ring on the agenda. The other will put another glass of wine in your hand and change the subject, beautifully, forever.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com forum, Zambia — paraphrased: a newcomer asking how to make friends in Lusaka was told that locals assume outsiders already have their own social circle, so breaking in takes repeated, deliberate effort rather than waiting to be invited.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Lusaka — paraphrased: a diplomatic spouse described the expat social calendar as a tight, recycled loop of the same embassy functions and NGO gatherings, meaning you'll see the same faces for the entire length of your posting.*

> *Portugalist — paraphrased: the site's guide to dating in Portugal warns that courtships move at a near-geological pace, and foreigners used to faster signals routinely misread patient, unhurried interest as outright rejection.*

## Conclusion

Here is the uncomfortable truth both countries would rather you didn't notice: neither one is optimised for you, the breezy foreign dater who wants options, speed, and an exit clause. Zambia will absorb you into a family structure before you've finished unpacking. Portugal will let you drift in a beautifully lit holding pattern until you either commit or quietly give up and go home. Pick your discomfort. I'd rather know exactly where I stand, even if where I stand is "engaged by Christmas, apparently," than spend another year interpreting the romantic Morse code of a Lisbon text reply. Suki out.
