# One Country Still Expects You to Bring Flowers, the Other Expects You to Bring Stamina

🇵🇱 Poland vs 🇰🇪 Kenya
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Poland has held onto a courtship script that much of Western Europe abandoned decades ago — flowers in odd numbers, hand-kisses from older gentlemen who mean it sincerely, a genuine expectation that the man pays and opens doors, all delivered with a straight face and zero irony. Nairobi, meanwhile, has built one of the most relentlessly social nightlife scenes on the continent, where dating happens less on scheduled evenings and more as an ongoing rolling event across rooftop bars, nyama choma joints, and WhatsApp groups that never sleep.

I've been handed a bouquet by a Polish date who counted the stems in front of me to make sure it wasn't an even number (even numbers are for funerals — get this wrong and you've accidentally delivered a very bad omen). I've also been swept from a rooftop in Westlands to a nyama choma spot to a third location I never got the name of, by a Nairobi social circle that treated "one drink" as a rhetorical opening bid. Both cultures take socialising seriously. They just disagree completely on what seriousness looks like.

## Do's & Don'ts: Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring an odd number of flowers if you're the one gifting them — even numbers are reserved for funerals | Split the bill on a first date if you're a man; it will genuinely be read as a bad sign |
| Expect a slower, more deliberate build to a relationship than "casual dating" implies elsewhere | Rush physical affection in public; Polish dating culture skews more reserved and private |
| Take chivalry gestures (door-holding, coat-offering) at face value — they're sincere, not performative | Assume texting culture is casual; ghosting is taken as a real social offence, not a shrug-worthy norm |

## Do's & Don'ts: Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a night out to have at least three locations — pre-drinks, main venue, and an unplanned final stop | Assume "let's meet for a drink" means one drink or one hour — build in the whole evening |
| Join the group chat culture — plans form and shift constantly via WhatsApp | Show up to Nairobi's rooftop scene underdressed; there's a genuine, well-understood style code |
| Be generous when it's your round — reciprocal generosity is a real social currency | Rush intimacy conversations; Kenyan dating culture often blends group socialising with one-on-one interest for a while before it's official |

## Poland: Courtship as a Preserved Tradition

Polish dating culture reads, to a lot of Western visitors, like a costume drama that never got the memo it's supposed to be ironic. It isn't. The gestures — flowers (odd-numbered, always, because even numbers are for graves and getting this backwards has genuinely ruined first dates), hand-kisses from older men as a formal greeting, the near-universal expectation that a man pays on a date and that this isn't up for negotiation — are sincerely held, not performed for effect. This isn't nostalgia; it's simply the operating norm, especially outside the most internationalised pockets of Warsaw and Kraków.

What surprises newcomers most is the pacing. Where a lot of dating-app culture elsewhere treats a first date as low-stakes and disposable, Polish dating tends to move more deliberately — several dates before anything is officially a relationship, more caution around public displays of affection, and a real social cost attached to ghosting. Disappearing on someone mid-conversation isn't treated as a mild inconvenience; it's treated as a genuine breach of manners, the kind of thing that gets discussed disapprovingly among mutual friends.

The friction point for outsiders, especially those from more casually-dating cultures, is mistaking formality for coldness. It isn't coldness. It's a courtship system that still believes gestures should mean something specific, and that specificity — the flowers, the payment, the patience — is the whole point, not an obstacle to get past on the way to something more "normal."

## Kenya: Socialising as a Rolling, Communal Event

Nairobi's social scene, particularly among the young, urban, and increasingly internationally-connected crowd, operates on an entirely different rhythm: dating rarely happens as an isolated, scheduled event. It happens embedded within a much larger, looser social flow — group hangouts at rooftop bars in Westlands or Kilimani, nyama choma (grilled meat) spots that function as the city's real social infrastructure, and WhatsApp groups that keep plans fluid right up until, and sometimes after, they've technically started.

A "date" in this context often begins as a group outing before narrowing into one-on-one interest, and the line between "we're hanging out" and "we're dating" can stay genuinely blurry for a while — which frustrates newcomers used to more explicit relationship-labeling cultures, and which locals navigate with an ease that suggests the ambiguity is a feature, not an oversight. Generosity matters enormously: buying a round, covering a friend's entry fee, being visibly open-handed within your social circle, functions as real currency, signalling both means and character.

The trap for outsiders is underestimating stamina requirements. A "quick drink" invitation in Nairobi's social scene is rarely quick and rarely stays at one location — the evening accretes venues the way a snowball accretes mass, and trying to leave after the first stop, while technically possible, marks you as someone who didn't understand the assignment.

## The Verdict

Poland offers precision — every gesture means something specific, every stage of courtship is legible if you know the script. Kenya offers momentum — the social scene simply carries you along, and dating happens as a byproduct of a much bigger, warmer collective energy. I respect the Polish flowers. I have more fun at the Nairobi rooftop. If forced to choose an entire evening, give me the group chat that never sleeps.

## Conclusion

Poland's dating culture is a script written decades ago and still performed with total sincerity; Kenya's is an improvisation that somehow never falls apart despite having no fixed plan at all. Show up in Warsaw expecting casual and you'll seem careless. Show up in Nairobi expecting one venue and one hour and you'll simply be left behind by the group chat. Learn the local rhythm before you try to set the tempo yourself — in both places, the tempo was decided long before you arrived.
