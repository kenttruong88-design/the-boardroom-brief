# Chinese Hotels Perfected Surveillance. Montenegrin Guesthouses Perfected Guilt.

🇨🇳 China vs 🇲🇪 Montenegro

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

China has spent thirty years building hotels so vast and relentlessly efficient that checking in feels like clearing customs at an airport that also sells foot massages. Every guest is photographed, scanned, and cross-referenced with a database before anyone hands you a key card, and the lobby chandelier is roughly the size of a small European nation. It's hospitality as infrastructure project, and it works beautifully, provided you enjoy being logged.

Montenegro, meanwhile, has a population smaller than Sheffield and a coastline Instagram discovered around 2015, so its accommodation scene runs almost entirely on family goodwill, cash under the table, and a landlady named Vesna who will decide, unilaterally, that you are not eating enough. There is no chandelier. There is, however, a very good chance she will bring you rakija at 9 a.m. and consider the matter closed.

## Do's & Don'ts: China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry your passport everywhere — every guest, foreign or domestic, gets scanned at check-in by law | Don't expect a double bed by default; twins are the national standard even for couples |
| Do use the electric kettle and tea set; hot water is a civic religion, not a suggestion | Don't bring an unregistered guest to your room — the front desk will know, and will care |
| Do exploit the 24-hour front desk for literally anything, including laundry emergencies at 2 a.m. | Don't expect a top sheet in cheaper hotels — duvet-in-a-cover is the default, no negotiating |
| Do try the hotel's in-house restaurant; business hotels often have the best regional food around | Don't assume "4-star" means what it does elsewhere — China's stars are self-graded and optimistic |

## Do's & Don'ts: Montenegro

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Do carry cash — many "sobe" and apartmani hosts don't take cards, and tourist tax is often collected in person | Don't book everything through an app; messaging the host directly on WhatsApp is standard and cheaper |
| Do accept the coffee, the rakija, and the unsolicited breakfast — refusing is a diplomatic incident | Don't expect a schedule outside peak season; whole coastal towns effectively close in November |
| Do ask about parking before you arrive; old town buildings predate the SUV | Don't flush toilet paper in older properties — check for a bin by the toilet first |
| Do negotiate the nightly rate directly with the owner, especially shoulder season | Don't confuse a "boutique hotel" listing with an actual hotel; it's often a converted family house |

## China: The Hotel as State Apparatus

Book a room in China and you are not merely a guest, you are a data point. Real-name registration is mandatory nationwide: hand over your passport, get photographed by a webcam clipped to a monitor that's seen better decades, and only then does anyone discuss key cards. This isn't paranoia — it's the law, enforced by the Ministry of Public Security, and it applies to citizens and foreigners alike, right down to the domestic tourist trying to sneak a friend into a twin room. Try bringing an unregistered visitor upstairs and the floor attendant, who exists specifically for this purpose, will materialise with the quiet disappointment of an aunt who expected better.

Past the surveillance theatre, the hardware is often spectacular. China builds hotels the way other countries build motorways: constantly, enormously, slightly ahead of demand. Mid-range chains clustered around every train station offer immaculate, cheap rooms with power showers and thermoses of boiling water refreshed daily, because nobody trusts a kettle that isn't visibly working overtime. Five-star properties in Shanghai or Chengdu compete on scale rather than intimacy: helicopter-sized lobbies, spas with more treatment rooms than some countries have hospitals, and breakfast buffets serving congee, dim sum, and a full Western fry-up simultaneously, because why choose.

What nobody warns you about is the mattress. Chinese hotel beds are famously, uncompromisingly firm — sleeping on one the first time feels less like rest and more like being reprimanded by a plank. Expect twin beds by default even for couples; a "big bed room" (大床房) must usually be requested specifically. Airbnb-style homestays exist but occupy a regulatory grey zone Beijing keeps tightening, so the vast, gleaming hotel — not the cosy rental — remains the backbone of Chinese travel.

## Montenegro: The Hotel as Extended Family

Montenegro's accommodation industry runs on a currency more valuable than the euro it technically uses: personal relationships. The backbone of the coast, from Herceg Novi to Ulcinj, is the "soba" — a private room rented directly from a family, usually a grandmother-figure who lives downstairs, monitors your comings and goings like a customs officer, and will notice if you didn't eat the breakfast she left out. Booking one often bypasses platforms entirely; you message a number scrawled on a sign near the bus station and negotiate a price that flexes depending on season and how much she likes you.

This informality is charming until it collides with bureaucracy: municipalities levy a nightly residence tax on tourists, and technically every host must register guests and remit it. In practice this ranges from a scrupulous ledger to a shrug and a request for cash. Budva and Kotor, groaning under their own popularity in July and August, have tightened enforcement, but venture inland to Cetinje or up near Žabljak and you're back in an economy running on trust and word of mouth.

The proper hotels split into two eras: the crumbling Yugoslav-era concrete giant, all sea views and Brutalist ambition, now either renovated or left to decay picturesquely; and the new wave of glossy resorts bankrolled by Gulf and Russian capital along the Budva Riviera, chasing a polish the old socialist hotels never attempted. Between the two sits the soul of Montenegrin hospitality — the family-run guesthouse — which offers no loyalty programme, no spa credit, and no chance of leaving without being fed.

## The Verdict

China wins on competence. The Wi-Fi works, the water is hot, the sheets are clean, and the entire system runs with an efficiency that would make a Swiss train blush — you're simply also a line item in a government database, a trade-off you make peace with by day three. Montenegro wins on something harder to manufacture: it makes you feel like a person rather than a booking reference, mostly because your host genuinely, aggressively wants to know if you're warm enough.

If I need to be somewhere at 6 a.m. with a functioning shower and zero small talk, China's hotel machine delivers without complaint. If I want to remember the trip in ten years, it's Montenegro — specifically, the exact moment Vesna refused to accept that I'd already eaten. Efficiency is admirable. Being fussed over by someone who owes you nothing is unbeatable. Montenegro takes it, on guilt alone.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/expats — paraphrased: a poster describes the shock of discovering that every hotel stay in China, even for a domestic weekend trip, requires a full passport scan and facial photo at check-in, and that skipping a night's registration can mean a call from the local police station the next morning.*

> *Internations Podgorica — paraphrased: a member explains that the best coastal apartments are never on booking platforms at all, and that the trick is finding a local contact who can vouch for you to a host directly, at which point the price drops and the coffee appears immediately.*

> *expat.com Montenegro forum — paraphrased: a long-term resident warns newcomers that many private guesthouses genuinely only take cash, that the tourist tax is sometimes collected as an afterthought at checkout, and that asking for a receipt can occasionally be met with visible confusion.*

## The Parting Shot

Neither country is trying to impress you, which is precisely why both are worth the trip. China isn't apologising for the passport scanner, and Montenegro isn't apologising for the fact that your accommodation doubles as someone's actual home. What unites them is a total absence of the anodyne, mid-Atlantic hotel blandness that has colonised most of the world — no beige lobby jazz, no scented candle called "Serenity." One offers a system so vast it borders on science fiction; the other offers a woman named Vesna who will remember your face for years and mention you to her neighbours. Pick your discomfort. Just don't call either of them "exotic" — that word belongs to people who've never negotiated a mattress firmness level or a breakfast portion size, and frankly, both countries deserve better than your tourist-brochure vocabulary.
