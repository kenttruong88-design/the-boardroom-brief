# One Country Bargains for Everything. The Other Won't Even Make Eye Contact at the Checkout.

🇱🇰 Sri Lanka vs 🇵🇱 Poland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Grocery shopping reveals more about a culture than almost any other mundane act, and Sri Lanka and Poland sit at opposite ends of the spectrum in a way I find endlessly entertaining. In Sri Lanka, buying vegetables is a social event involving negotiation, relationship-building, and the vendor's genuine opinion of your character. In Poland, buying groceries is a silent, efficient transaction conducted at speed, where the cashier will scan your items faster than you can bag them and will judge you — silently, permanently — if you slow down the queue.

I've haggled over pineapples in a Colombo market with a woman who ultimately gave me a free mango "for the effort," and I've been visibly tutted at in a Warsaw Biedronka for fumbling my reusable bags while an elderly woman behind me radiated the specific fury of someone who has somewhere far more important to be. Both are correct on their own terms. Neither will apologise.

## Do's and Don'ts: Sri Lanka

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bargain at local markets — it's expected and even appreciated as a form of respect | Bargain in a supermarket or chain store; prices there are fixed and haggling looks foolish |
| Bring your own bag; plastic bag restrictions are taken seriously | Squeeze or handle produce excessively before buying — ask the vendor instead |
| Build a relationship with one or two regular vendors — loyalty gets you better prices | Expect refrigerated aisles stocked like a Western supermarket outside major cities |
| Shop early morning for the freshest fish and produce | Assume all vendors take cards; carry cash for markets |

## Do's and Don'ts: Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bag your own groceries fast — the cashier will already be scanning the next customer's items | Chat with the cashier expecting a friendly exchange; efficiency, not warmth, is the goal |
| Bring your own bags or coins for a cart deposit at discount chains like Biedronka or Lidl | Block the aisle with your cart while deciding — Poles shop with purpose and expect you to too |
| Learn the difference between a "sklep" (small shop) and a hypermarket for different needs | Expect Sunday shopping — most large stores are closed on non-trading Sundays by law |
| Try the pickle and cold cuts aisle — genuinely excellent, deeply underrated | Dawdle at self-checkout; there is an unspoken time limit and everyone is watching |

## Sri Lanka: Shopping as Relationship Management

The Sri Lankan grocery run is not a task you complete; it's a relationship you maintain. Local markets — the pola, held on fixed weekly days in most towns — are less about the transaction than the theatre surrounding it. You don't simply buy tomatoes. You discuss the tomatoes, you hear about the rain affecting the harvest, you get an unsolicited but confidently delivered opinion on whether you're cooking them correctly, and eventually, having built enough rapport, you get a price that reflects the relationship rather than a fixed number on a sign.

Supermarkets exist — Cargills and Keells have real presence in Colombo and other cities — and they're where you go for imported goods, packaged items, or when you simply don't have the energy for the performance of the market. But anyone who tells you the supermarket is where "real" Sri Lankan grocery shopping happens has never stood in a pola at 6am watching a fishmonger command an entire crowd's attention while gutting the day's catch with theatrical flair. The market is where the culture actually lives.

What trips up foreigners is the assumption that bargaining is aggressive or rude. It isn't — it's a conversational dance with its own etiquette, and refusing to participate (just paying the first price offered) is often seen as odd, even slightly insulting, like you couldn't be bothered to engage. Equally, overdoing it — bargaining too hard over genuinely small amounts — reads as graceless. The sweet spot is somewhere in the middle: push back once, expect a small concession, and accept it with good humour. Do it right and you'll walk away not just with vegetables but with a vendor who remembers your face next week and saves you the good mangoes before anyone else gets a look at them.

## Poland: Shopping as a Timed Event

Poland has weaponised grocery shopping into a speed sport, and nowhere is this clearer than at the checkout of a Biedronka or Lidl during peak hours. Polish cashiers scan items with a velocity that borders on aggressive, and the unstated social contract is that you will bag your own groceries at matching speed, no exceptions, no mercy for tourists still figuring out the coin-deposit trolley system. Hesitate, and you will feel the queue's collective irritation radiating at your back like a physical force.

This efficiency isn't coldness so much as a cultural value placed on not wasting other people's time — small talk at the till is viewed as an imposition, not a courtesy, and Poles generally find the American-style "how's your day going" checkout chat baffling at best, performative at worst. The discount chains (Biedronka, Lidl, Netto) dominate daily shopping, while smaller neighbourhood "sklep" shops still exist for quick top-ups, and larger hypermarkets like Auchan or Carrefour handle the big weekly haul, usually on a Saturday, since Poland's trading-Sunday restrictions mean most large stores are shut on non-designated Sundays — a genuinely disorienting discovery for newcomers who plan a Sunday grocery run and arrive to locked doors.

Poland's produce and deli sections deserve more credit than they get internationally — the pickle selection alone could fill an aisle, and the cold cuts counter is a genuinely serious culinary institution, staffed by people who will silently judge your order if it's too small or too indecisive. But don't expect warmth while you shop for it. Polish retail courtesy is functional, not performative: you'll get efficient, competent service, delivered with the emotional warmth of a tax audit, and that's simply the deal.

## The Verdict

Sri Lanka wins on humanity — nowhere else will a stranger give you a free mango because you tried to bargain with enthusiasm. Poland wins on sheer operational efficiency — you will never once wonder if your grocery run will take longer than fifteen minutes. If you want shopping to feel like a small daily act of community, go local-market Sri Lanka. If you want to get in, get out, and never make eye contact with another human being in the process, Poland has built an entire retail infrastructure just for you. I know which one I'd rather do on a rainy Tuesday, and it isn't the one that ends in silence.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/SriLanka — "Foreigners always overpay at the pola because they don't bargain at all. The vendors aren't offended by haggling, they're offended by you not even trying."*

> *Internations Warsaw — "First week in Poland I said 'how are you' to a Żabka cashier and got a blank stare like I'd asked something deeply personal. Just scan, bag, go. That's the whole interaction."*

> *expat.com Colombo — "Nobody told me most big Colombo supermarkets don't have the fresh produce quality of the local market. I was buying sad imported apples for months before someone took pity on me."*

## Conclusion

Sri Lanka turns grocery shopping into a small daily ritual of negotiation and connection; Poland turns it into a logistics exercise you're expected to complete without conversation or delay. Both systems work exactly as intended, and both will silently judge you if you get it wrong — one for being too stiff, the other for being too slow. Learn the local rhythm fast, because nobody in either country is going to slow down to explain it twice.
