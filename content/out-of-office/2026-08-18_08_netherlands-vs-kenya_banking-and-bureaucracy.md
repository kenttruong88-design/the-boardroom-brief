# The Netherlands Needs a Form to Get You a Form. Kenya Just Needs Your Phone Number.

🇳🇱 Netherlands vs 🇰🇪 Kenya — Banking and Bureaucracy

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The Netherlands runs one of the most maddeningly circular bureaucracies I've encountered in fourteen relocations — you need a BSN number to open a bank account, but you often need a Dutch address to get a BSN number, and you frequently need a bank account to secure that address, a loop so perfectly closed it should be studied by mathematicians rather than expats. Kenya, meanwhile, largely skipped traditional banking altogether and built a financial system on M-Pesa, the mobile money platform so dominant that a market vendor, a taxi driver, and a landlord will all happily transact with you the moment you have a SIM card, no branch visit required.

I have spent four genuinely bewildering weeks trying to open a Dutch bank account and I have set up a functioning Kenyan financial life in under a day. I am still not fully over either experience.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register at your local gemeente (municipality) for your BSN number as your very first bureaucratic move | Expect any Dutch bank, employer, or landlord to proceed without a BSN — it unlocks literally everything else |
| Book municipal appointments online weeks in advance; walk-ins are largely a fantasy in bigger cities | Assume all banks welcome new expats equally; some require a Dutch employment contract before they'll even consider you |
| Keep physical and digital copies of every document you're issued; you will be asked for them again, and again | Lose patience publicly at the gemeente desk; Dutch bureaucratic staff are unmoved by frustration and immovable by design |

### 🇰🇪 Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for M-Pesa the day you get a Kenyan SIM card — it is the actual national payment system, not a backup option | Assume a traditional bank account is necessary for daily life; many Kenyans and expats function entirely without one |
| Get your KRA PIN sorted early if you plan to work, rent, or do any formal business locally | Carry large amounts of cash when M-Pesa transfers are instant, trackable, and universally accepted |
| Save your M-Pesa transaction PIN somewhere secure; it's effectively your entire financial identity on the platform | Underestimate how deeply integrated mobile money is — even school fees and hospital bills run through it |

## Netherlands: A Bureaucracy That Eats Its Own Tail

The Dutch administrative system is efficient, digital, and impressively well-organised — right up until you're a new arrival trying to actually enter it, at which point you discover the entire structure is built on a series of prerequisites that quietly require each other. The BSN (burgerservicenummer) is the master key to Dutch life: without it you cannot open a bank account, sign a formal employment contract, register with a doctor, or in some cases even get a phone contract. Obtaining it requires registering at your local gemeente, which in turn typically wants proof of a registered address, which many landlords will only confirm once you can demonstrate financial stability, which usually means a bank account.

Everyone who has navigated this loop agrees it's technically solvable — temporary addresses, employer sponsorship, patient appointment-booking — but nobody agrees on the fastest route through it, and expat forums are essentially oral histories of individually improvised workarounds. The system isn't malicious; it's simply designed around the assumption that most people entering it were born into it, and retrofitting it for newcomers was clearly not the priority.

What nobody warns you about is the appointment scarcity. Gemeente booking slots in Amsterdam, Rotterdam, and Utrecht routinely fill weeks out, turning what should be a fifteen-minute registration into a multi-week waiting game that stalls literally everything else — banking, healthcare, employment paperwork — behind it. Once you're through, the system runs beautifully. Getting through is the entire test.

## Kenya: Skip the Bank, Trust the Phone

Kenya didn't so much modernise its banking system as leapfrog past the version most of the world still uses. M-Pesa, launched by Safaricom, turned every mobile phone into a bank account, and the adoption has been so total that a huge share of the population — expats included — conducts nearly all daily financial life through it: paying rent, splitting a bill, sending money to family upcountry, even settling hospital fees, all via SMS-based transfers that settle in seconds and require nothing more than a SIM card and a PIN.

For a newcomer, this is disarmingly simple compared to Western banking onboarding. Register your SIM, load some cash through an M-Pesa agent — found on nearly every street corner in Nairobi — and you have a functioning financial identity within hours, no proof of address, no employment contract, no appointment booked six weeks out. Formal banks still exist and matter for larger transactions, business accounts, or international transfers, but they're supplementary to daily life rather than the gatekeeper to it.

What nobody prepares you for is how socially embedded this becomes. Splitting a taxi fare, tipping, paying a street vendor, contributing to a "chama" savings group — all of it defaults to M-Pesa first, cash second, and traditional bank transfer a distant third. The bureaucratic friction that does exist in Kenya — KRA PIN registration, business licensing, some property transactions — is real and can be genuinely slow, but it sits apart from the everyday financial plumbing, which runs with a speed and simplicity most European systems still haven't matched.

## The Verdict

The Netherlands wins on institutional trustworthiness — once you're through the gauntlet, Dutch banking is secure, transparent, and about as reliable as financial systems get. Kenya wins on sheer practical accessibility — a financial system built around a phone number rather than a residence permit is, for a newly arrived expat, the difference between weeks of friction and an afternoon of setup. If you want a system that eventually rewards patience with rock-solid infrastructure, brace for the Netherlands. If you want to be transacting like a local by dinner, Kenya's mobile money system will spoil you for traditional banking forever.

## Conclusion

The Netherlands will eventually give you one of the most trustworthy financial systems on earth, provided you survive the onboarding maze that seems designed by someone who forgot new people ever arrive. Kenya will hand you a functioning financial life before your jet lag clears, built on a mobile network rather than a marble-floored branch. I've been defeated by a Dutch appointment calendar and delighted by a Kenyan SIM card, sometimes in the same month of travel. Only one of them made me want to throw my laptop into a canal.
