# Two Countries That Consider Volume a Moral Failing

🇸🇮 Slovenia vs 🇲🇨 Monaco — Public Behaviour Norms

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

If you want to feel like the loudest person alive without raising your voice a single decibel above your normal speaking register, I recommend visiting either Slovenia or Monaco, standing in a queue, and simply existing at a volume Americans would call "library-appropriate." You will still receive a look. Not a rude look — these are both, in their own very different ways, exceptionally polite countries — but a look that communicates, with total clarity, that you have been noticed, and that noticing is itself a kind of quiet punishment.

Slovenia does its restraint through genuine Central European reserve: soft voices, careful queues, a national aversion to drama that borders on the constitutional. Monaco does its restraint through something closer to performance — a discretion so total it starts to feel like its own form of exhibitionism, the flex of a principality that has decided the most sophisticated thing you can do with enormous wealth is act as though you don't have any. I've been quietly judged in both. Only one of them made me feel underdressed while doing it.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇸🇮 Slovenia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep your voice down on public transport and in residential streets | Raise your voice in a disagreement, even mildly — it reads as a real loss of control |
| Queue properly and wait your turn without comment | Litter, anywhere, ever — fines and social disapproval both apply |
| Show up on time; punctuality is read as basic respect | Assume a handshake and eye contact is optional at a first meeting |
| Respect quiet hours and park rules, especially around Bled and Triglav | Get physically demonstrative with people you've just met — personal space is real here |

**🇲🇨 Monaco**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress neatly and formally, especially near churches, restaurants, and events | Photograph strangers, especially anyone who looks vaguely famous, without asking |
| Speak softly and let discretion do the social work | Display wealth loudly — flashy behaviour reads as vulgar, not impressive |
| Use a formal greeting — handshake for men, cheek kiss once familiar | Approach residents for autographs or gossip about the Grimaldi family |
| Respect privacy as the principality's unofficial state religion | Assume formality is optional just because everyone's on holiday nearby |

## Slovenia: Quiet as a National Project

Slovenians have built an entire public culture around not being a bother, and having lived through the opposite in several considerably louder countries, I want to be clear that this is not a criticism, it's closer to admiration with a slight edge of bafflement. Conversations on trams happen at a volume that assumes nobody else wants to hear them, which, correctly, nobody does. Disagreements, when they happen, happen without theatre — no raised voices, no dramatic exits, just a controlled, slightly chilly exchange that somehow communicates displeasure more effectively than shouting ever could.

Queueing is treated less as a system and more as a moral position: you wait, you don't push, you don't make a scene about the person who clearly cut in, because making a scene would be the actual crime, worse than the cutting itself. Littering carries both a legal and social penalty, and Slovenians take genuine, unforced pride in the cleanliness of their public spaces, from Ljubljana's pedestrianised centre to the trails around Lake Bled, in a way that makes you quietly ashamed of every gum wrapper you've ever dropped in a country that didn't care. Punctuality functions the same way — arriving late isn't just inconvenient, it's read as a small, specific disrespect, the kind that gets remembered. And personal space is sacred: a first meeting gets a firm handshake and direct eye contact, not a hug, not a cheek kiss, not anything that assumes an intimacy you haven't earned yet. It is, altogether, a country that has decided the correct public posture is quiet competence, and it mostly delivers on it.

## Monaco: Discretion as Performance Art

Monaco's public behaviour code runs on a similar surface logic — soft voices, no scenes, formal manners — but underneath it is a completely different animal, because Monaco's restraint isn't about modesty, it's about managing one of the highest concentrations of visible wealth on earth by aggressively pretending it isn't there. Loud, rowdy behaviour is frowned upon not because Monégasques are naturally reserved, but because ostentation is considered vulgar in a principality where everyone already has the ostentatious option available and has collectively decided not to use it in public, which is its own kind of flex.

Discretion around privacy is close to a civic religion, and this is not an abstraction — approaching a resident for a photo, an autograph, or gossip about the ruling Grimaldi family is treated as a serious breach, the sort of thing that gets you quietly but permanently filed under "not our sort." Dress codes matter more here than almost anywhere else I've lived: neat, modest, often formal attire is expected in churches, restaurants, and at events, and being underdressed reads less as casual and more as a small act of disrespect toward the principality's self-image. Greetings stay formal — a handshake for men, a cheek kiss once you're sufficiently familiar — and the whole social fabric operates on an unspoken agreement that everyone here is either enormously wealthy or working for someone who is, and the correct response to both situations is the same: composed, quiet, and scrupulously polite, no matter what's actually happening underneath.

## The Verdict

Slovenia's quiet is earned through genuine cultural humility — a small country that has decided not drawing attention to yourself is simply good manners, full stop, no subtext required. Monaco's quiet is a costume, expensive and beautifully tailored, worn by a principality that has weaponised discretion into its own status symbol. I find Slovenia's version more livable, honestly, because it doesn't ask you to perform anything, it just asks you to be considerate, which is a genuinely lower bar than Monaco's demand that you be considerate while also correctly reading a room full of people concealing yacht ownership. Slovenia wins for sincerity. Monaco wins, grudgingly, for sheer commitment to the bit.

## Conclusion

Both countries will let your public missteps pass without a single raised voice, and both will make absolutely sure you know you made one anyway. Slovenia does this out of collective courtesy; Monaco does it out of collective self-preservation dressed up as courtesy. Either way, the lesson for anyone relocating to either is the same: lower your volume, iron your shirt, and understand that in both places, the loudest thing you can do is stand out.
