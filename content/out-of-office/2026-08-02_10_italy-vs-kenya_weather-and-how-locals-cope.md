# Italy Shuts Down for the Heat. Kenya Just Plans Its Whole Year Around the Rain.

🇮🇹 Italy · 🇰🇪 Kenya
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Italy's answer to extreme summer heat is, essentially, collective surrender — an entire country downing tools every August, shuttering shops, evacuating cities for the coast, and treating the concept of "working through a heatwave" as a faintly barbaric idea reserved for other, lesser nations. Kenya's relationship with weather is far more structural: two distinct rainy seasons dictate agriculture, travel, infrastructure planning, and daily routines with a precision that treats the sky not as an inconvenience but as the actual operating system of the country.

I have sweated through a shopless, restaurant-less Roman ferragosto, wandering a city that had simply decided, en masse, to not be open. I have also stood ankle-deep in a flooded Nairobi street during the long rains, watching locals navigate the chaos with the calm efficiency of people who've done this every single year of their lives and have simply built their whole existence around it.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇮🇹 Italy**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan around ferragosto (mid-August) — much of the country genuinely closes | Expect air conditioning as standard — many older buildings simply don't have it |
| Adopt the siesta-adjacent rhythm — early starts, long lunches, quiet afternoons | Schedule anything urgent during the peak heatwave weeks without a backup plan |
| Seek out the coast or mountains if you can — locals evacuate cities for good reason | Underestimate how seriously ISTAT and the health ministry take heatwave warnings |

**🇰🇪 Kenya**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the Kenya Meteorological Department's seasonal forecast before booking travel | Assume "rainy season" means light drizzle — the long rains can be genuinely intense |
| Build in extra time for Nairobi commutes during rains — flooding disrupts traffic badly | Skip proper rain gear — locals invest in it seriously, and so should you |
| Time safari trips around the dry seasons for the best wildlife viewing | Underestimate how much daily life reorganises itself around the rain calendar |

## Italy: A Country That Simply Refuses to Suffer Through Summer

Italy's relationship with extreme heat is less about resilience than it is about strategic avoidance, executed with a national discipline that's almost impressive in its totality. ISTAT's own public health data confirms what any August visitor to Rome or Milan discovers quickly: heatwaves are taken seriously as genuine public health events, with the Ministry of Health issuing formal heat emergency protocols and warnings, particularly for the elderly and vulnerable.

But the real cultural response is ferragosto — the mid-August period when much of the country, seemingly by silent national agreement, simply stops. Shops close. Restaurants close, save for the ones catering to tourists who haven't been warned. Entire neighbourhoods empty as Italians decamp to the coast or the mountains, leaving cities like Rome eerily quiet, hot, and populated mostly by confused visitors wondering why nothing is open. This isn't laziness; it's a considered, generations-old response to a climate that makes working through peak heat genuinely unpleasant and, historically, unhealthy.

The daily rhythm adjusts too, even outside ferragosto — early starts to beat the heat, long unhurried lunches during the hottest hours, life resuming again in the cooler evening. Older buildings, charming as they are, frequently lack air conditioning, a fact that surprises newcomers expecting modern comfort to match the price tag of a Roman apartment. Italians have simply built an entire cultural infrastructure around not fighting the heat directly, but sidestepping it wherever possible — through architecture, scheduling, and, when all else fails, mass seasonal evacuation to somewhere with a sea breeze.

## Kenya: A Nation Built Around the Rain Calendar

Kenya's relationship with weather operates on an entirely different logic, structured around two distinct rainy seasons — the long rains from roughly March to May, and the short rains from October to December — that the Kenya Meteorological Department forecasts and communicates with real precision, because so much of the country's functioning genuinely depends on it. Agriculture, obviously, is the biggest stakeholder, but the ripple effects extend into transport, safari tourism, urban planning, and daily commuting patterns in a way that weather rarely touches in more temperate countries.

Nairobi during the long rains is its own particular experience — roads flood, traffic grinds to a genuine standstill, and the Kenya Red Cross Society's flood preparedness reports reflect just how seriously the infrastructure risk is taken in vulnerable areas. Locals navigate it with a practised calm that comes from lifelong familiarity: proper rain gear, built-in schedule buffers, an intuitive sense of which routes flood first and which stay passable. It's not that the rain is taken lightly — it's that an entire population has developed genuine expertise in coexisting with it.

Safari tourism, meanwhile, bends around the same calendar — the dry seasons bring the best wildlife viewing as animals congregate near diminishing water sources, and seasoned operators plan trips with the rain calendar as a foundational input, not an afterthought. What struck me most, having come from cultures that treat rain as a minor daily inconvenience, is how thoroughly Kenyan life treats it as structural — not something to escape or wait out, like Italy's heat, but something to actively plan an entire year's rhythm around, because it always, reliably, returns.

## The Verdict

Italy wins on sheer comfort-seeking wisdom — there's something admirable about a culture that simply refuses to grind through discomfort when escape is an option. Kenya wins on adaptive sophistication — building genuine structural resilience around a recurring, significant weather pattern rather than just avoiding it. If I had to survive extreme weather somewhere, I'd want Kenya's preparedness. If I just wanted to enjoy myself while the weather does its worst, Italy's approach — leave, relax, come back when it's over — has an obvious, undeniable appeal.

## Conclusion

Italy and Kenya have arrived at completely different philosophies for the same basic problem: weather that dictates how life gets lived. Italy's answer is avoidance — shut the doors, leave town, wait it out somewhere cooler. Kenya's answer is integration — build the rain into the very architecture of daily life until it's no longer a disruption but simply how things work. Both are, in their own ways, a form of respect for a climate that refuses to be ignored. The rest of us, complaining about a bit of drizzle on our commute, could stand to learn something from either.
