# One Country Still Walks Up and Down the Same Street Every Evening on Purpose. The Other Makes You Meet the Whole Family Before the Second Date.

🇭🇷 Croatia 🇪🇬 Egypt
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Dating culture reveals, more honestly than almost any other social arena, how much privacy a society believes two people courting are entitled to. Croatia's answer is: not much, and proudly so — the korzo, the evening promenade along the main street of every coastal town, exists precisely so that everyone can see who's walking with whom, and the lack of privacy is treated as a feature, a form of community oversight dressed up as leisurely strolling. Egypt's answer leans further still toward the collective — courtship there is frequently, openly, a family project from a relatively early stage, with introductions, approvals, and extended-family opinions woven into the process in a way that Western dating culture would find suffocating and Egyptian dating culture considers simply responsible.

I've walked the corso in Split at dusk, watched teenagers pretend not to notice each other while every adult present noticed everything, and I've sat in a Cairo living room being formally served tea while a young couple's families conducted what was unmistakably a vetting process disguised as hospitality. Both scenes were, in their own register, deeply social. Neither looked anything like the algorithm-driven, swipe-first courtship most of the world has settled for.

## Do's & Don'ts: Croatia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the evening korzo (promenade) if you want to actually meet people — it's a genuine social institution, not just exercise | Don't assume dating apps have replaced it; many younger Croatians still meet through friend groups and shared social circles first |
| Expect to be introduced through mutual friends more often than through cold digital matches | Don't rush physical affection in public in smaller towns — community visibility cuts both ways, and gossip travels fast |
| Be prepared for directness; Croatians tend to be blunt about romantic interest rather than coy | Don't mistake coastal tourist-town dating norms for the whole country; inland towns are considerably more conservative |

## Do's & Don'ts: Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect family involvement earlier than Western dating norms would suggest — it's standard, not intrusive | Don't skip the formal introduction process if the relationship is serious; shortcuts here read as a lack of seriousness |
| Understand that public displays of affection are far more restrained than in Western or even Mediterranean contexts | Don't assume urban Cairo and more traditional regions share identical norms — the gap is significant |
| Respect that gender-separated social spaces remain common in many settings | Don't treat family approval as a formality to get through; it's often a genuine, substantive part of the decision |

## Croatia: Courtship as Public Performance

The korzo is the single most revealing institution in Croatian dating culture, and most visitors mistake it for a tourist quirk rather than what it actually is: a centuries-old social mechanism that turns the entire town into a dating marketplace, conducted entirely in public, every single evening. Teenagers and adults alike walk the same stretch of waterfront or main street, dressed with evident care, greeting the same people they greeted yesterday, all under the collective, half-performed pretense that nobody's actually there to see or be seen. Everybody is there to see and be seen. That's the entire point, and pretending otherwise is part of the choreography.

This public, communal visibility shapes the whole texture of Croatian dating. Because everyone sees everything eventually, discretion operates differently than in more anonymous, apartment-dwelling dating cultures — a new relationship becomes communal knowledge quickly, for better or worse, and gossip functions as a genuine social regulator in smaller towns in a way that would feel invasive in a bigger, more atomized city. Younger Croatians increasingly use dating apps alongside this older structure rather than instead of it, but the friend-group-and-shared-social-circle model remains a dominant, trusted route to meeting someone, precisely because it comes pre-vetted by people you already know, which the apps, for all their convenience, can't replicate.

Directness is the other defining trait. Croatians, generally, don't do coy ambiguity well — romantic interest tends to be stated plainly rather than hinted at through weeks of mixed signals, which foreign visitors raised on more indirect dating cultures often find either refreshingly honest or alarmingly blunt, sometimes both in the same conversation. Inland, more conservative towns move at a noticeably slower, more traditional pace than the tourist-heavy coast, where seasonal visitors and a more cosmopolitan population have loosened some of the older norms considerably — a distinction visitors who only experience Dubrovnik or Split in summer frequently miss entirely.

## Egypt: Courtship as a Family Enterprise

Egyptian dating culture treats courtship as something closer to a shared family project than a private two-person negotiation, and understanding this reframing is the single most important adjustment any outsider needs to make. Family involvement isn't a late-stage formality reserved for engagement — it frequently begins early, with parents and extended family weighing in on compatibility, background, and prospects well before a Western dating culture would consider the relationship "serious" at all. This isn't interference in the Western sense of the word; it's regarded, broadly, as responsible due diligence, the family doing its job of protecting a member's long-term interests.

Public displays of affection are considerably more restrained than in Croatia or most of the Mediterranean, and this holds true even in cosmopolitan Cairo, let alone in more traditional regions where gender-separated social spaces remain genuinely common rather than a historical relic. This doesn't mean romance is absent or repressed — it means it's conducted through a different grammar, one where formal visits, chaperoned meetings, and carefully managed introductions carry the emotional weight that a casual coffee date might carry elsewhere.

The gap between urban and rural, and between different social classes, is significant enough that generalizing about "Egyptian dating culture" as a single thing risks real inaccuracy. Younger, urban, more internationally connected Egyptians increasingly navigate a hybrid model — dating apps exist and are used, Western media's influence is visible — layered on top of family expectations that haven't disappeared so much as adapted, often awkwardly, to a generation trying to honour both their own romantic agency and their family's continued involvement. Visitors expecting either a fully "traditional" or a fully "Westernized" dating culture will find instead a negotiation between the two, happening in real time, differently in every household.

## The Verdict

Croatia wins if you want a dating culture that's communal, public, and refreshingly direct, where the whole town functions as an informal matchmaking apparatus whether you consented to that or not. Egypt wins if you want a dating culture that takes the long-term stakes seriously enough to involve the people who'll actually be affected by the outcome — the families — from an early stage, rather than treating courtship as a purely private experiment. Neither model optimizes for the thing Western dating apps optimize for, which is speed. Both, in their own very different ways, optimize for something that looks a great deal more like actual durability.

## Conclusion

Both countries will quietly correct the assumption, imported from wherever you learned to date, that courtship is primarily a private matter between two consenting adults and nobody else's business. Croatia disagrees loudly, via the korzo, every single evening. Egypt disagrees more formally, via the family, from the very first serious conversation. Figure out which collective you're being folded into before you get too far in, because both will fold you in whether you're ready or not, and resisting either process tends to read, correctly, as a lack of seriousness about where things are actually headed.
