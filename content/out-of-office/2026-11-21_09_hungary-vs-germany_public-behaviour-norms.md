# Germany Legislates Silence on Sundays; Hungary Just Judges You Quietly From the Bench

🇭🇺 Hungary vs 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Germany, public behaviour is written down. There is a rule for when you can vacuum, a rule for when you can mow, a rule for how you separate your rubbish into five bins of mounting moral complexity, and a rule, which everyone knows and nobody can find, for what you do at a pedestrian crossing at six in the morning when there is not a car in sight. You stand there. You wait. You are, in the great national project of being decent, a small and obedient cog.

Hungary has rules too, but they are enforced by a more intimate authority: the neighbour, the woman at the tram stop, the waiter with the eyebrow. Public behaviour in Hungary is governed by a thick, unspoken layer of reserve, politeness and mild disapproval, and nobody will tell you what you did wrong. They will simply be a little less warm. I have lived in both, and I can tell you that the German system is easier to follow and the Hungarian one is easier to forgive yourself in.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇭🇺 Hungary ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrange visits in advance and bring a small gift | Turn up unannounced at someone's door |
| Keep your voice low in public, and earphones in on transport | Play music from your phone on the tram |
| Greet older people first and let them lead | Address an elder with casual familiarity |
| Take your shoes off if the host's slippers appear | Wander through a Hungarian home in outdoor shoes |

| 🇩🇪 Germany ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Observe quiet hours, in particular all day Sunday | Drill, vacuum or mow on a Sunday |
| Wait for the green man, even at an empty crossing | Cross against the light in front of a child |
| Sort your rubbish properly and learn the bin colours | Put glass in the wrong container at the wrong time |
| Greet shopkeepers and waiting-room strangers | Enter a doctor's waiting room silently |

## 🇭🇺 Hungary

Hungarian public life is shy at first and generous later. A Daily News Hungary guide to etiquette notes that loud singing, whistling or humming in public are not tolerated, that loud music on public transport is rude and earphones are expected, and that visits are normally arranged in advance rather than sprung on people. Punctuality matters, though about thirty minutes late is acceptable for a more informal party. Men shake hands, women are not expected to, the younger person greets the older first, and a host will personally greet each guest and get them a drink immediately.

Expats on the Expat.com culture-shock thread paint a more nuanced picture. One American poster was reprimanded in a mall for putting her foot on a bench; she also found that restaurant staff let guests linger and must be flagged down, that locals seem grumpy and reserved on the surface but warm once you know them, and that shop staff may not help if you do not speak Hungarian. Another poster, who had first visited under socialism in the 1970s, noted that visitors today attract far fewer stares than they used to, which is perhaps the most hopeful thing I read all week.

There are also thornier encounters. One poster described neighbours who invited him to a barbecue, steered the talk to selling him their house and paying for lawn mowing, then mowed his lawn unasked and demanded money. He framed it in ethnic terms, which I would not repeat, and it was a single anecdote rather than a national pattern. But it captures something: in Hungary, the boundaries of public and private are a little porous, and an outsider has to earn their place. Anonymous answers on Expat Exchange make a similar point about speaking quietly in public. The rule is simple: be modest, be present, and let them come to you.

It is worth saying a word about the tone of daily life in each country. Germany's public space is tidy, silent and slightly formal, and the default setting on a train or in a lift is a respectful, shared quiet. People do not chat with strangers, but they will help you instantly if you ask. In Hungary, the default is reserve with a trapdoor: strangers rarely smile first, yet a single act of courtesy can unlock an astonishing generosity. Both cultures are often described as cold, and both descriptions are wrong in the same way. They are simply not performing warmth for people they have not met.

## 🇩🇪 Germany

Germany's public behaviour is a masterclass in collective agreement. Quiet hours, or Ruhezeiten, are the cornerstone. HousingAnywhere explains that they run from 10pm to 6am on weeknights and Saturdays, and all day on Sundays and public holidays, with Bavaria extending the night to 11pm to 7am. Saturdays are treated as working days. Sunday supermarkets are closed, with only emergency stores at main stations open, and a good rule of thumb for noisy chores such as vacuuming is to keep them to around twenty minutes. It sounds ludicrous. It also means that apartment blocks in Germany are, in practice, nicer places to live than most.

The deeper logic is that rules are a form of respect. When everyone follows the same system, no one has to negotiate. The bottle bank is closed at lunchtime on Saturday not because someone hates you but because the neighbours are asleep. The pedestrian crossing is obeyed because the example you set is seen by the child behind you. A German who tells you off for something is not being aggressive; they are doing you the courtesy of assuming you would want to know.

For an expat the cost is a certain feeling of being observed. Germans will correct you, politely and without malice, and they will expect you to say thank you. The upside is that you always know where you stand, and the city runs like a well-oiled clock. If you come from a country where rules are advisory, the first month will feel like being dressed by a particularly efficient aunt. By the third, you will be glaring at tourists who cross on red. This is the process. It is irreversible.

A final piece of practical advice for the new arrival. In Germany, learn the rules and the vocabulary, and apologise sincerely the first time you are caught out. In Hungary, learn the greeting and the names of your neighbours, and accept a drink when it is offered, even if it is ten in the morning and the drink is made from plums. In both, assume that you are being watched, not with malice but with curiosity. That is a comforting thought, and, depending on the plum, an accurate one.

## The Verdict

Germany wins for clarity, and Hungary wins for warmth, and I will not pretend that is an easy decision. If you are the sort of person who wants to know the rules and keep them, move to Germany and be happy. You will never be confused, though you may be told off. If you are the sort of person who prefers reading a room to reading a statute, Hungary will give you a puzzle and, eventually, a friend.

My honest ranking depends on how much you value being left alone. German society gives you privacy through rules; Hungarian society gives you intimacy through patience. I would suggest that the German system is better for the first six months of an expat's life, because it removes the guesswork, and the Hungarian one is better for the next six years, because it eventually removes the stranger. I was glad to have spent time in both, and I now hold my vacuum cleaner with a certain amount of guilt.

## Conclusion

In Germany you will be corrected by the rules. In Hungary you will be corrected by the silence that follows. Both are forms of love, delivered through a slightly clenched jaw. Learn the quiet hours, learn the greetings, take off your shoes, and keep your music in your headphones. If you do these things, both countries will, over time, make room for you at the table, and then very politely ask you to move your chair.
