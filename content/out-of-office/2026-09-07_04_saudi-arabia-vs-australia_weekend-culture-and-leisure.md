# One Country Reinvented Its Weekend From Scratch. The Other Has Just Been Doing the Same Barbecue Since 1950.

🇸🇦 Saudi Arabia · 🇦🇺 Australia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Saudi Arabia's weekend has changed more in the last decade than most countries' weekends change in a century — cinemas, concerts, and mixed-gender entertainment venues that simply didn't exist ten years ago have turned Riyadh and Jeddah into cities actively inventing a leisure culture in real time, with all the chaotic energy that implies. Australia's weekend, meanwhile, has been essentially the same fixed ritual since the Menzies era: sport, a barbecue, a beach if you're near one, and a beer consumed at a volume calibrated precisely to whether it's a "big weekend" or a "quiet one." One of these countries is improvising an entire concept of leisure from a standing start. The other perfected its formula decades ago and sees no reason to touch it.

I have watched Riyadh build an entertainment district roughly at the speed other cities build a single shopping mall, and I have sat through an Australian Sunday session that started at noon and, through sheer social gravity, did not end until well past dark. Only one of these countries knew exactly what it was doing the entire time.

## Do's & Don'ts: Saudi Arabia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book big-name concert or event tickets well ahead — they sell out fast in a market starved for decades | Assume the weekend still runs Thursday-Friday; it officially shifted to Friday-Saturday years ago to align with global markets |
| Try a desert camping trip (*barr*) — it remains the single most beloved traditional weekend activity | Expect nightlife to resemble anywhere else in the region; alcohol remains prohibited nationwide regardless of venue |
| Visit new entertainment districts like Boulevard Riyadh City for a genuine sense of the cultural shift | Underestimate how much weekend leisure still centers on extended family gatherings over public entertainment |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a "Sunday session" at a beachside pub if invited — it's a genuine cultural institution, not just drinking | Schedule anything important for Saturday afternoon during finals season; live sport takes precedence, always |
| Get outdoors — beaches, national parks, and backyard cricket are treated as near-mandatory weekend use of good weather | Assume shops and restaurants keep the same hours as weekdays; many regional areas still wind down early on Sundays |
| Bring something to a barbecue if invited — showing up empty-handed reads as a minor social failure | Confuse "having a quiet one" with an actual quiet weekend; Australians recalibrate volume, not participation |

## Saudi Arabia: A Weekend Culture Being Built in Real Time

There is no other country on earth where you can watch an entire concept of public leisure get invented within a single decade, and Saudi Arabia is currently the only place doing it. Until relatively recently, public entertainment as most of the world understands it — cinemas, concerts, mixed public venues — simply didn't exist here in any legal, formal sense, and weekend leisure was overwhelmingly private, domestic, and family-centered. Since the General Entertainment Authority was established and cinemas reopened, Saudi cities have been sprinting to build an infrastructure of leisure that other countries assembled gradually over generations, and the resulting energy is genuinely unlike anywhere else — part genuine cultural excitement, part slightly chaotic construction site.

Riyadh's Boulevard City and Jeddah's entertainment districts now host international concerts, esports tournaments, and theme park attractions that would have been unthinkable a decade ago, and young Saudis — a population that skews remarkably young nationwide — have embraced them with an enthusiasm that borders on relief, as if a pressure valve had finally been released. Weekend crowds at these venues are large, mixed, and visibly proud of what's being built, even as the pace of change means some venues feel more like ambitious pilot projects than finished institutions.

None of this has displaced the older, deeper weekend tradition: the desert. *Barr*, the practice of driving out beyond the city to camp, build a fire, and spend the weekend under open sky with extended family, remains the activity Saudis speak about with the most unguarded affection, entertainment complexes notwithstanding. It's the one constant across the rapid change — however elaborate Riyadh's new leisure districts get, the desert weekend isn't going anywhere, and most Saudis I've spoken to seem to want it that way. The new stuff is exciting. The desert is home.

## Australia: A Formula So Perfected It Never Needed to Change

Australia's weekend is, structurally, one of the most stable and unselfconscious leisure cultures on the planet, built almost entirely around two resources the country has in genuine abundance: good weather and open space. The rituals — Saturday sport (played or watched, often both, often the same person doing both in the same day), a barbecue with more sausages than the guest list strictly requires, a beach trip if you're within striking distance of one, and the "Sunday session" at a pub that starts as a casual afternoon drink and ends, through sheer social momentum, well past when anyone intended to leave — have remained essentially unchanged for decades because nobody has found a reason to improve on them.

What outsiders consistently underestimate is how seriously sport structures the entire weekend calendar, not just as entertainment but as social infrastructure. AFL and NRL finals seasons effectively reorganize Saturday plans nationwide; a wedding or a work function scheduled against a grand final is, socially speaking, a declaration of war. Local grassroots sport — junior football, weekend cricket, surf lifesaving carnivals — pulls in entire families as spectators and volunteers every week, functioning as a genuine community glue in a country large enough that other forms of neighbourhood cohesion don't always naturally occur.

The barbecue deserves its own analysis as a piece of social technology: low-stakes, outdoor, egalitarian in a way that a dinner party never quite manages, and flexible enough to absorb anywhere from four people to forty without anyone needing to plan much beyond "bring something and rock up." Combined with beach culture in coastal cities — where a weekend swim before 9am is treated less as exercise and more as basic hygiene — Australia has built a weekend that requires almost no institutional infrastructure at all. Just sun, a grill, and a television tuned to whichever sport is in season.

## The Verdict

Saudi Arabia gets the points for sheer ambition and velocity — building an entire entertainment sector from nothing in under a decade is a genuinely remarkable feat, and the energy in Riyadh right now is something worth witnessing before it inevitably matures into something more polished and less thrilling. But Australia wins on pure functional design: a weekend culture this simple, this cheap, and this durable doesn't need billions in infrastructure investment to work. It just needs a beach, a sausage, and a semi-final.

Give me the barbecue. Some things don't need reinventing, and Australia figured that out generations before anyone else had to.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations Riyadh — a member who'd lived there both before and after the entertainment sector opened described the change as "watching a city discover Saturday night for the first time, badly, wonderfully, all at once."*

> *Quora — a young Saudi respondent noted that despite the new venues, most of his friend group still considers a weekend without at least one desert trip a wasted one.*

> *expat.com Australia forum — a German expat wrote that he didn't understand the true stakes of the Sunday session until he tried to leave one at 4pm and was informed, kindly but firmly, that this was "not how it works."*

## Conclusion

Saudi Arabia is sprinting to build a weekend culture from first principles, and the result is thrilling precisely because it's still unfinished. Australia has no such urgency — its weekend was solved decades ago and simply gets repeated, contentedly, every seven days. If you want to watch a country invent leisure in real time, go to Riyadh. If you just want to eat a sausage on a beach and not think about anything until Monday, Australia has been ready for you this whole time.
