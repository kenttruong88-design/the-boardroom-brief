# One Country Crosses The Road By Faith. The Other Crosses It By Combi Roulette.

🇻🇳 Vietnam + 🇵🇪 Peru

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular kind of fear reserved for the first time you step off a curb in Hanoi, and a distinct, entirely different fear reserved for the first time you board a Lima combi with no discernible stop pattern, driven by a man who appears to be negotiating a hostage situation with the accelerator. I have done both more times than is medically advisable, and I can report that neither country has any interest in making this easier for you. Vietnam has simply decided that traffic law is a set of gentle suggestions offered to nobody in particular. Peru has decided that public transport is a free market experiment nobody remembered to regulate.

What both share is a total absence of the orderly queue-and-wait choreography that visitors from tidier nations expect, and a citizenry so unbothered by the resulting chaos that you start to suspect you're the only person in either country who thinks any of this is remarkable. It isn't remarkable to them. It's Tuesday.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇻🇳 Vietnam**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Cross the road at a slow, steady, predictable pace — the bikes will flow around you | Stop suddenly mid-crossing; that's the one thing that causes accidents |
| Use Grab for bikes and cars; it removes the fare negotiation entirely | Try to out-honk anyone. You will lose and you will look foolish |
| Wear a helmet even for a two-minute Grab bike hop | Assume a "one-way street" sign means what it says at rush hour |
| Download offline maps — GPS drops constantly in the Old Quarter | Get on a bike taxi without agreeing the price first, app or no app |

**🇵🇪 Peru**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Have small coins ready; combi cobradores hate breaking large notes | Expect a combi to stop only at marked stops — you can flag it almost anywhere |
| Use the Metropolitano BRT for anything along its line; it's shockingly efficient | Get in an unmarked taxi off the street at night — use an app |
| Learn the shouted stop names; cobradores speak fast and won't repeat themselves | Assume Lima traffic laws apply the way the signage suggests |
| Sit near the front if you're unsure of your stop | Carry your phone loosely near an open combi window |

## Vietnam: Organised Chaos, Emphasis On Chaos

The first rule of Vietnamese traffic, which no one will explain to you before you arrive and which you will only learn through pure adrenaline, is that the entire system runs on negotiated fluid dynamics rather than actual rules. Motorbikes outnumber cars roughly nine to one in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, and the resulting river of scooters treats red lights as a polite suggestion, one-way streets as directional guidelines, and pavements as an acceptable overflow lane during rush hour. Somehow, astonishingly, it works. Accident rates are real and not to be dismissed lightly, but the sheer volume of bikes moving at low speed in constant negotiation with each other creates something closer to a flock of starlings than a road system. You are meant to become part of the flock. Standing still, hesitating, or making a sudden panicked dash is the single most dangerous thing you can do as a pedestrian, and every tourist learns this the hard way exactly once.

Grab has, mercifully, colonised the entire country and turned bike-taxi chaos into something app-mediated and price-fixed, which removes roughly ninety percent of the stress and all of the haggling. Ho Chi Minh City's new metro line has also started chipping away at the scooter monopoly, though it currently covers about as much of the city as a determined jogger could manage in an afternoon, so don't build your itinerary around it just yet. What nobody prepares you for is the sound — a permanent, layered orchestra of horns that isn't aggressive so much as informational, a constant stream of "I am here, I am here, I am also here" that becomes, after about four days, strangely soothing. You'll leave Vietnam flinching at silence.

## Peru: The Combi Is Not A Bus, It's A Belief System

Lima's combi network is less a public transport system than a loosely federated collection of independent contractors who have all agreed, informally, to drive roughly the same routes. There is no schedule. There is no fixed stop list in any meaningful sense — you flag a combi down anywhere along its route by waving, and a cobrador (the conductor, usually hanging half out the open door) shouts the destination in a rapid-fire verbal shorthand that took me three weeks to decode and that I still occasionally get wrong. Fares are absurdly cheap, the vehicles are frequently held together by what I can only describe as sustained collective optimism, and the driving style suggests every combi driver in Lima is secretly late for something far more important than your journey.

And yet — the Metropolitano, Lima's dedicated bus rapid transit line, is a genuine marvel, gliding down its own protected corridor while the combis duke it out in general traffic beside it, a bit like watching an orderly sibling coast past their chaotic older brother in the family car. Outside the capital, in Cusco or Arequipa, the combi system loosens even further into something closer to shared taxis with extra steps. Peruvians navigate all of this with a shrug that took me an embarrassingly long time to earn myself. Expats on Internations forums spend their first month white-knuckled and their second month explaining the system to newer arrivals with the smug calm of people who've survived something.

## The Verdict

Vietnam wins on sheer choreography — once you understand the flow, crossing a Hanoi street becomes almost meditative, a trust exercise with several thousand strangers on scooters. Peru wins on comedy value; nothing quite matches the theatre of a cobrador shouting your stop over blaring cumbia while the driver treats red lights as a bureaucratic technicality. Both systems will terrify you in week one and feel oddly logical by week three, which is either a testament to human adaptability or proof that we'll normalise almost anything given enough exposure. I suspect it's the latter, and I say that with real affection for both.

## Conclusion

Neither Hanoi nor Lima will hand you a transit map and wish you luck — they'll hand you chaos and expect you to develop instincts, fast. Vietnam rewards you with a strange, wordless choreography once you stop resisting the flow. Peru rewards you with the specific satisfaction of correctly shouting a stop name and watching the combi actually stop. Both will make your home city's transit complaints look laughably tame. Learn the rhythm, keep small change on you at all times, and never, under any circumstances, hesitate mid-crossing — in either country, hesitation is the only real danger on the road.
