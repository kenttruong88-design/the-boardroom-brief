# Nigeria Celebrates Everything. Italy Just Switches Itself Off.

🇳🇬 Nigeria 🇮🇹 Italy

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every culture has a relationship with time off, but only two countries I've worked in have turned it into a personality trait. Nigeria treats a public holiday as a starting gun for the loudest, longest, most generous celebration logistics will allow. Italy treats one the way a sulking teenager treats a Sunday — as an excuse to disappear entirely and resurface when it's good and ready, usually several weeks later with a tan.

I have sat in a Lagos office on the Thursday before Eid-el-Kabir watching half the floor vanish because the moon was sighted a day early and nobody told HR. I have also stood outside a locked pharmacy in a mid-sized Italian town in August, watching a sun-bleached handwritten sign flap in the heat, informing me the owner would return — approximately — in September. Both countries will ruin your project timeline. Only one of them will apologise, and it won't be Italy.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Nigeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep your calendar loose around Eid-el-Fitr and Eid-el-Kabir — the exact date is confirmed on moon-sighting, often a day or two out | Lock a hard client deadline into Eid week and expect it to survive contact with reality |
| Accept the office Independence Day picnic invitation on 1 October — it is a genuine, functioning piece of workplace diplomacy | Assume Nigeria's national holiday list is the whole story — states add their own on top, with zero warning to outsiders |
| Budget extra lead time the Monday after any major holiday, nationally known as "resumption lag" | Expect full attendance the day after Eid-el-Kabir, New Year, or Christmas — civil servants famously treat this as a suggestion |
| Learn what Isese Day (Lagos, Ogun, Osun, Oyo) actually is before you schedule over it | Dismiss a state-specific holiday as a rounding error — it is a full, legally enforced day off for an entire workforce |

**Italy**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Finish anything involving a bank, a notary, or a lawyer before the end of July | Try to open a bank account, sign a lease, or get a document notarised in the first three weeks of August |
| Check the patron saint day for the specific city you're dealing with — Milan, Rome, Naples and Venice all differ | Assume a national holiday calendar applies uniformly — Sant'Ambrogio shuts Milan while the rest of the country works |
| Treat the week around Ferragosto (15 August) as a dead zone, not a slow day | Schedule a meeting, delivery, or site visit for 15 August and expect anyone to show up |
| Note that from 2027 there's a new "ponte" built into early October around St Francis of Assisi Day | Expect 2026 itself to change anything — 4 October 2026 falls on a Sunday, so the new holiday does precisely nothing this year |

## Nigeria: thirteen holidays and a workforce that treats "resumption" as optional

Nigeria's official holiday count looks modest on paper — thirteen national public holidays a year, stitched together from a careful, deliberate balance of Christian and Islamic observance, because a country roughly split between the two faiths does not get to pick a side and expects everyone to show up to work on the other faith's holy day. You get New Year's Day, Workers' Day, Democracy Day and Independence Day as the civic backbone; Good Friday, Easter Monday, Christmas and Boxing Day for Christians; and Eid-el-Fitri, Eid-el-Kabir and the Islamic New Year for Muslims, with the Eid dates confirmed only once the moon has actually been sighted, which means any company that locks its calendar in January is already wrong by March. Layer onto that the state-level additions nobody warns expats about — Lagos, Ogun, Osun and Oyo observe Isese Day in honour of traditional Yoruba religion; Osun grants a day off purely so residents can collect their voter cards; Kano has Takutaha; Borno has a memorial day for Sambisa — and you get a country where the "national" holiday list is really just the opening bid.

Add statutory annual leave on top, and Nigerian employees are formally entitled to somewhere between 41 and 46 days away from their desks annually, according to analysis built on Nairametrics research — not a bad haul for a country whose labour market is routinely, and lazily, described by outsiders as relentless. What that analysis understates is the cultural weight around each date. Christmas, locally rebranded "Detty December," is not a holiday so much as a national homecoming event, with diaspora Nigerians flooding Lagos and Abuja airports and offices running on skeleton staff for the entire fortnight either side of it. Independence Day on 1 October reliably produces the genre of company picnic that actually works as morale-building rather than performative HR theatre — expats on forums describe barbecues co-organised across nationalities, not just tolerated attendance. And the Monday after any big holiday carries its own local vocabulary: "resumption," and the widely reported, entirely real phenomenon of civil servants simply failing to turn up for it, sometimes for days. Nigeria doesn't quietly observe its holidays. It throws them a party and dares you to keep up.

## Italy: the country that closes for business, literally

Italy's holiday calendar is deceptively short on paper — eleven national public holidays, soon to be twelve once 4 October's new St Francis of Assisi Day beds in — and deceptively enormous in practice, because Italy doesn't take days off, it takes a month off, and the month is August. Ferragosto, 15 August, is the fulcrum: banks, law firms, notary offices and most of the bureaucratic apparatus a foreign professional actually needs are gone for most or all of the month, and small, family-run businesses close without so much as updating their Google listing, leaving you standing outside a shuttered shopfront working out whether this is a holiday, a lunch break, or a business that no longer exists. Parliament itself breaks for summer recess on 8 August and doesn't reconvene until September. The one mercy is that strikes are formally banned on trains and planes from late July to early September — the only two weeks of the year Italy's notoriously strike-prone transport sector is contractually forbidden from making your commute worse, mostly because almost nobody is commuting anyway.

What outsiders consistently fail to grasp is that Italy's holiday culture is hyperlocal, not national. Milan shuts for Sant'Ambrogio on 7 December, a day before the whole country stops for the Immaculate Conception, manufacturing a four-day "ponte" bridge weekend that the rest of Europe doesn't get. Rome closes for Saints Peter and Paul on 29 June. Naples has San Gennaro in September; Venice has San Marco, which conveniently lands on the same day as national Liberation Day; Florence, Genoa and Turin all shut together for San Giovanni in June. A foreign company with offices in two Italian cities is, for all practical purposes, operating on two different holiday calendars, and nobody at head office in London or Lagos will have budgeted for that.

## The Verdict

If you are trying to get actual work done, Italy loses, decisively and without appeal. A country that empties itself for the better part of August, then adds a rotating cast of city-specific saint days your HR system has never heard of, is not observing holidays — it is actively hostile to the concept of a functioning calendar year. Nigeria, by comparison, is almost refreshingly honest about it: the list is long, the dates move, and everyone tells you so upfront.

But if you are trying to understand what a holiday is actually *for*, Nigeria wins without breaking a sweat. Detty December is a cultural event with its own gravitational pull; the Independence Day picnic is genuine workplace bonding, not forced fun. Italy's Ferragosto, by contrast, is largely an exercise in national avoidance — an entire country collectively deciding not to be reachable. One culture builds community around its holidays. The other builds an alibi. I know which dinner party I'd rather be invited to, and it isn't the one where everyone's gone quiet because the office is "basically closed."

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Fodor's Travel Talk Forums — one poster insisted August 15 is the single biggest holiday of the Italian year and that, around it, virtually everything in the country will be closed, so plan your trip or your meetings for any other week.*

> *Expat.com Abuja forum — a longtime expat recommended skipping the guesswork entirely and joining InterNations before your first Nigerian public holiday catches you off guard, because that's where you actually learn which picnic, party, or closure is coming next.*

> *Zikoko vox-pop, "I Spent My Christmas at the Airport" — one Nigerian abroad noted that back home, people spend the holidays going out and being loud about it together, a contrast he only noticed once he spent a quiet, indoor Christmas somewhere else entirely.*

## Conclusion

Here is the uncomfortable truth neither HR department wants printed in a handbook: a public holiday tells you more about a country's values than its GDP figures ever will. Nigeria's calendar is long, unpredictable, and built around the idea that a holiday should be noisy, communal, and worth crossing an ocean for. Italy's calendar is shorter on paper and vastly longer in effect, built around the idea that the entire country has collectively earned the right to be unreachable, and will exercise it whether your deadline agrees or not. Neither approach is designed with your project plan in mind, which is, frankly, the point. Stop trying to schedule around festival culture as though it's an inconvenience to be managed. It is the actual culture. The spreadsheet is the inconvenience.
