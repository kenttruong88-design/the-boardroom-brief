# In Nigeria Your Bank Account Is a Relationship. In Britain It Is a Document Scavenger Hunt

🇳🇬 Nigeria vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two ways to open a bank account. In Nigeria, you turn up, you produce a collection of papers and perhaps a person who knows a person, and you leave with the beginnings of a financial identity and a sense that somebody senior has your measure. In Britain, you submit two documents, one of which must be a bill you do not yet have because you have not got an account, and wait for a computer to decide you are real. Both systems are bureaucratic. Only one of them admits it.

A relocating executive going in either direction learns quickly that banking is where a country's attitude to trust gets put into forms. Nigeria builds trust through identity numbers and human intermediaries. Britain builds it through proof of address and the strange faith that a utility bill is a sacred object. Let me take them in turn, noting that the Nigerian forum material I found concerns expats arriving in Nigeria, which is the reverse direction, and the Nigerian diaspora material comes from one partly retrievable page.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Nigeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your employer whether the bank will send a representative to process forms at your office | Assume every bank works at the same speed; experiences differ sharply |
| Carry your passport, work permit documentation and references from existing customers if asked | Turn up with only one form of ID and hope |
| Get your BVN in order; Nigerians abroad now have a remote enrolment route (NRBVN) | Expect the process to be identical for residents and the diaspora |
| Ask about fees up front, since they vary by bank and account type | Assume a quoted opening fee is the final figure |

**United Kingdom**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring two documents: ID plus proof of address | Assume a foreign utility bill will do the job |
| Use a university admissions letter or an HMRC National Insurance letter if you lack a UK address | Wait until you have a tenancy before asking what alternatives exist |
| Pre-apply online with Barclays, then visit a branch with ID and proof of address | Expect to finish everything online if you are newly arrived |
| Keep every bill and statement under three months old | Throw away the first electricity bill; it is gold |

## Nigeria: Identity as a Number, Trust as a Person

Nigeria runs on the Bank Verification Number, the BVN, a biometric identifier tying you to your accounts. For Nigerians abroad, NairaCompare reports, a remote route called NRBVN was launched by the Central Bank and NIBSS in May 2025, using biometric facial recognition and a liveness check. The page says processing takes "within 72 hours" in one place and about four working days in another, which is the sort of contradiction that is, frankly, a comfort. It lists a one-time fee of $50, and documents including a Nigerian passport, proof of residency abroad and a utility bill dated within three months. Those figures come from a page that was only partly retrievable, so check them with a bank before relying on them.

For an expat opening an account in Nigeria, the Expat.com forum paints a vivid picture of how different the experience can be depending on the bank and the way in. One member, sarahlou1979, said GT Bank made it easy: her employer arranged for a bank representative to visit the office, paperwork was a passport, a temporary work permit and references, and the whole thing took under a week. Another, Les Pat, said Zenith Bank was difficult, with lots of forms and fees. A third, writing from the Philippines, asked whether a $300 fee applied to open a private account, which tells you how much confusion surrounds opening charges.

The pattern is that Nigerian banking rewards preparation and connection. When it works, it works through a human being who takes your forms and walks them through. When it does not, you may be stuck in a branch for hours. If you are a company, the employer's relationship with the bank can be worth more than any document you carry. That is not corruption; it is how institutions with variable service levels behave everywhere.

The deeper difference is what each system assumes about a stranger. The British bank assumes a stranger is a stranger until proven otherwise, and the proof is paper. The Nigerian bank, at least in the stories on the forum, assumes a stranger can be vouched for, and the proof is a person. Both are defensible. Both fail in predictable ways: the British system fails the person without paper, and the Nigerian system fails the person without a contact. Anyone who has ever arrived somewhere new with neither will know exactly which failure hurts more.

## United Kingdom: The Kingdom of Proof of Address

The British system is, by contrast, almost aggressively documentary. According to Wise's guide, you will usually need two documents: ID, such as a passport, driving licence or ID card for EU nationals, and proof of address. Acceptable proofs include a UK photocard licence, a bank or credit card statement under three months old, a mortgage statement, an electricity or gas bill under three months old, a council tax bill or an HMRC tax letter. The catch-22 is real, since many people arriving in the country have none of these.

The guide does offer a way out: without a UK address, some banks accept a university admissions letter, an HMRC National Insurance letter, or an existing bank statement sent to the new UK address. Non-residents can open international or expat accounts with Barclays, Lloyds, HSBC or NatWest, though these may require a large initial deposit, a minimum monthly payment, or a monthly fee. Barclays lets you pre-apply online but requires a branch visit with ID and proof of address after arrival, while Lloyds Classic can be opened with only a passport or UK driving licence. Onboarding often includes a selfie upload for identity verification, and the debit card arrives by post.

The British experience is less about who you know and more about what you can prove. The process is mostly polite, nearly always automated, and occasionally maddening. It is also fairly consistent, which is a virtue underappreciated until you have lived somewhere where consistency is optional. The branch counts tell a story of their own: Wise lists Barclays with over 850 branches, Lloyds over 450 and HSBC over 327. The network is shrinking in public life, but it remains a thing you can walk into if the app breaks.

So here is the practical, dull, useful summary. If you are going to Britain, collect your paper before you land and treat the first utility bill like a passport. If you are going to Nigeria, find your intermediary before you land and treat the bank's representative like a colleague. And if you are going to both, which in this line of work is more likely than anyone admits, keep a single folder with every document in both languages of bureaucracy: the digital and the human. It will save you a week of your life.

## The Verdict

The UK wins on predictability; Nigeria wins on flexibility, when it is working. A British bank will treat you like a risk until you have a bill, and a Nigerian bank will treat you like a project until you have a contact. Which is better depends on whether you prefer the machine or the person.

For an executive, I would choose the machine, because a system that tells you what it needs is easier to plan around than one that requires you to guess. But I would not dismiss Nigeria's relationship model. The employer-assisted account, the representative who arrives with forms, is faster than anything a British high-street bank offers a newcomer. The ideal system would combine the two. We have neither, and so we carry both passports and a folder.

## Conclusion

Money is the most honest test of how a country treats a stranger. Nigeria asks who you are and who you know. Britain asks where you live and whether you can prove it. Neither question is unreasonable, and neither is easy to answer on the day you land.

My advice is dull and works: arrive with your documents scanned, your paperwork in order and your first utility bill saved like a relic. Then thank whoever eventually clears you, and do not ask them to rush. They will not, and they are not paid enough to pretend.
