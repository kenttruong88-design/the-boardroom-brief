# South Africa Braais in the Park, America Books a Permit for It

🇿🇦 South Africa vs 🇺🇸 United States

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Show me a country's parks and I will show you what it fears and what it loves. South Africa loves the outdoors with a ferocity that borders on religion, and fears, with equal sincerity, what might happen in it after dark. America loves its great outdoors with a ferocity that has been professionally branded, and fears only that you might leave without buying the mug.

I have walked, sat and been moved along in parks on both sides of this divide, and the thing that strikes me is not scenery. It is attitude. One country treats green space as a shared sitting room. The other treats it as a monument.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇿🇦 South Africa ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit parks in daylight and in company where possible | Wander alone in a quiet corner after dark |
| Bring a braai kit and charcoal where it is permitted | Light a fire where it is not allowed |
| Keep valuables out of sight and your car locked | Leave a bag visible on the back seat |
| Book the big national parks well in advance | Turn up at Kruger in school holidays without a booking |

| 🇺🇸 United States ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the fees, passes and reservation rules before you go | Arrive at a famous park on a summer weekend without a plan |
| Stay on marked trails and follow ranger advice | Wander off for a better view, as wildlife and cliffs do not negotiate |
| Pack out everything you bring in | Leave rubbish for someone else |
| Keep dogs on a leash where required | Assume a park is a free-for-all for pets |

## 🇿🇦 South Africa

South Africans have an extraordinary relationship with the outdoors. The country has some of the most spectacular landscapes on earth, from Table Mountain to the Drakensberg to the wide savannas of the Kruger, and the national parks system, run by SANParks, is a source of great pride. The big parks are well managed and popular, with lodges, guided drives and a booking calendar that fills quickly during school holidays.

In the cities, parks play a more modest but cheerful role. Johannesburg, built on gold and, remarkably, planted with millions of trees, is often described as one of the greenest cities in the world for its size. Places such as Zoo Lake, Delta Park and Emmarentia Dam are weekend favourites, with picnics, joggers, dog walkers and, naturally, braai smoke curling into the sky. A park is where you meet your friends, your cousins and a surprisingly large number of strangers with a speaker.

The braai is the heart of it. Outside the lodges and picnic sites, in places where it is allowed, the smell of burning wood and boerewors is as much a feature of the landscape as the trees. Food, fire and company are the essentials, and anyone who tries to substitute a salad will be treated with kindness and suspicion.

The shadow is safety. Crime is a real concern in South Africa, and many city parks are best enjoyed in daylight, in groups and in the busy hours. Locals know which areas are safe and when, and an expat is wise to ask before assuming. The result is that green space is sometimes a gated or managed one, with security guards at the entrance and rules about when you can come and go.

It is not a perfect picture, but the love is obvious, and the weather is in on the conspiracy.

Wildlife shapes the whole experience in both countries, though in very different ways. In South Africa, the Big Five and the larger reserves attract visitors from every continent, and many local families treat a trip to a game park as a rite of passage. In America, bears, bison and rattlesnakes feature prominently in park advice, and the most dangerous animal is, as ever, the tourist who tries to take a selfie with it.

## 🇺🇸 United States

America treats nature as a national institution, and the National Park Service is its cathedral. Yellowstone, Yosemite, the Grand Canyon and hundreds of other sites offer scenery that justifies every adjective ever applied to it. The parks are carefully managed, staffed by rangers who are polite, informed and devoted to the rules, and visited by millions every year.

The system is organised. There are entry fees, annual passes, reservations for popular campsites and, at the busiest locations, timed entry. Visitors receive maps, safety briefings and strict instructions about wildlife, and the result is a version of wilderness that is both astonishing and curated. You may see a bison. You will certainly see a sign about bison.

City parks are a separate pleasure. Central Park in New York, Golden Gate Park in San Francisco and countless local parks provide sports fields, playgrounds, dog runs, farmers' markets and free concerts. Many cities have invested heavily in trails, waterfronts and community gardens, and the culture of jogging, cycling and walking the dog is part of daily life. Americans will use a park for exercise far more than for a long, lazy braai.

The etiquette is firm. Leash laws are enforced, rubbish is expected to be carried out, fires are limited to designated rings and amplified music is discouraged. Newcomers sometimes find the rules a little fussy, and then notice how clean everything is and quietly change their minds.

The downside is access. America is vast, many parks are far from public transport, and without a car, the great outdoors can be a long way away. Weekend crowds at famous sites are real, and a day at a national park can involve more time in a queue than on a trail.

Access and cost differ more than most people expect. Many South African city parks are free, while the major national parks charge daily conservation fees with discounts for residents. In the United States, an annual pass covers entry to most national parks, and state parks charge their own fees. Planning ahead, buying the right pass and reading the rules will save you more than any packing list.

## The Verdict

South Africa wins on warmth and America wins on stewardship, and I do not think either country would be insulted. A South African park is a place where people gather, cook, argue and laugh, and where the land feels like a shared living room. An American park is a place where people marvel, learn and behave, and where the land feels like a shared inheritance.

If you want to spend a Sunday with friends, a fire and a bag of charcoal, you want South Africa. If you want to spend a week with a trail map, a sunrise and a very patient ranger, you want America.

My own preference is for the wild reserves of South Africa and the city parks of the United States. I would take a safari in Kruger and a morning run in Central Park, which tells you everything about my sense of balance and nothing about my fitness.

The sting is that both countries tell their citizens that nature belongs to everyone, and both then make it complicated to prove it. In South Africa, safety and inequality limit who can enjoy the green. In America, distance, cost and permits do the same. Nature is free, but the parking is not.

Both countries face a quiet challenge in who actually uses their green space. Surveys and local debates in each point to gaps by income, race and distance from urban centres, and public programmes try to widen access with transport, free days and community projects. The park is a great equaliser in theory, but a good expat will notice who is on the lawn and who is not.

## Conclusion

A park is an argument about what a country owes its people. South Africa answers with a fire, a crowd and a gate. America answers with a trail, a fee and a rulebook.

My advice is to take both seriously and neither too seriously. Learn the local safety rules, pack the right food and arrive early. Be generous with the space you have been given, and do not mistake order for emptiness or warmth for carelessness.

And wherever you are, bring a friend. A park is the one place where the second person makes it safer, cheaper and considerably more fun.
