# Germany Fines You for Crossing on Red. India Considers the Red Light a Suggestion and the Queue a Philosophy

🇮🇳 India vs 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Public behaviour is the part of a culture that nobody explains because everybody assumes it is obvious. In Germany, it is obvious that you stand quietly, wait for the green man, sort your rubbish by colour and avoid hoovering on a Sunday. In India it is equally obvious that the street is a shared negotiation, the horn is a form of speech and personal space is a concept for people with spare room.

Both systems are logical. Both are sincere. Both will make you feel like an idiot within a fortnight if you arrive with the wrong assumptions. A source note: the pages I could fetch describe German norms only, so my account of India comes from general knowledge, and I will say so. The German details come from three guides, one of them sponsored, and from the best kind of source, a person who got it wrong first.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect close proximity in queues and crowds | Take physical closeness as aggression |
| Use your horn and your voice with confidence on busy roads | Wait for perfect silence before crossing |
| Accept hospitality as it is meant, with warmth | Decline everything in the name of efficiency |
| Treat asking questions as normal, not rude | Equate directness with hostility |

**Germany**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive five minutes early | Arrive late without sending a message |
| Check your building's house rules for quiet hours | Run the washing machine at midnight |
| Keep an arm's length in a queue | Hover at someone's shoulder |
| Carry cash for bakeries and markets | Assume every shop takes cards |

## India

I am drawing on general knowledge here, not a fetched citation. Public life in India is dense, social and negotiated. Queues exist, but they are flexible organisms, and a gap in front of you may be filled by someone with a more urgent claim than yours. Personal space is a luxury, not a right, and the idea of an arm's length between strangers would strike many people as a slightly sad form of isolation.

Noise is the other pillar. Horns, loudspeakers, festival drums, vendors, conversation: the soundscape is thick, and silence is the exception, not the default. That is not rudeness. It is signal. A horn on a road is a message, not a complaint, and a shout across a market is a conversation, not an argument.

What impresses me is the social fluency. People talk to strangers, ask questions that Germans would consider private and offer help without being asked. A visitor who is lost will find several volunteers within a minute, each with a different opinion on the correct route. The system runs on contact, not distance, and its warmth is real, if loud.

The sting is for the newcomer who values order. Rules exist, but they are applied with a flexibility that can feel arbitrary, and the honest answer to "what is the rule?" is often "it depends who is asking." If you can laugh, India will teach you something. If you cannot, it will teach you more.

One more point on India that Germans in particular should hear: the scale of public space differs. A crowded Indian street, a packed train or a busy market is not a failure of order but a different arrangement of it, in which the unit of public life is the group, not the individual. A German visitor who tries to impose an arm's length will simply be absorbed, politely, into the crowd. The sensible response is to relax your personal-space expectations before you land, and to treat the noise as a kind of background music. People who do this report that the first week is exhausting and the second week is fine, and the third week is when they start to like it.

## Germany

Germany runs on written and unwritten rules, and the unwritten ones are the strictest. A language-school guide advises arriving five minutes early, checking the Hausordnung for quiet hours, learning the rubbish colours (yellow for packaging, blue for paper, brown for organic, black for general waste) and keeping an arm's length in queues. It also reminds you that cash remains king in bakeries, markets and ticket machines.

Quiet hours, or Ruhezeit, are the great German tradition. One guide gives them as ten at night to six in the morning on weekdays and all day Sunday, while its own FAQ says seven in the morning, which is itself a very German inconsistency. Some buildings add a midday quiet from one to three. The sponsored guide in The Local says ten to six, varying by city, and adds that you should wear house shoes when visiting, expect a fine of roughly five to ten euros for jaywalking, and make eye contact when you say "Prost".

Then there are the softer rules. Asking for tap water in a restaurant is seen as stingy, according to that same sponsored piece, and wishing someone a happy birthday early is considered bad luck. A writer who has lived in Germany since 2014 recalled first reading German directness as hostility, until a colleague's blunt feedback turned out to be normal. He still does his shopping on Saturday mornings because shops close on Sunday.

Another writer, after a loud Berlin neighbourhood, found a calmer district's Sunday silence the moment he understood Ruhezeit. Germany's public behaviour is not cold; it is considerate in a way that looks like restraint. Everyone has agreed to share the same quiet, and the agreement is enforced by neighbours.

The sting is the surveillance. A rule broken in Germany is rarely forgiven by a smile, and an offended neighbour may leave a note or a conversation. It is a society where you are expected to know the rules before you break them.

The rubbish rules alone justify a section. The language-school guide lists yellow for packaging, blue for paper, brown for organic and black for general waste, with glass separated by colour, and adds that the bottle deposit is refunded through a reverse vending machine voucher. For an arrival from anywhere with looser habits, this is both a gift and a trap, because a neighbour will notice a misplaced bottle with a speed that borders on the supernatural. The same guide notes that the Sunday shop closures are written into law, and the Bundesland decides the holiday rules. In Germany, the rules are not mysterious. They are simply many, and they are all taken seriously.

## The Verdict

Germany wins on predictability and India on humanity, and I resent that I have to choose. If you want to know exactly what is expected, to sleep in silence and to cross a road without negotiating with a bus, Germany is a joy. If you want warmth, spontaneity and a street that talks back, India has no rival.

For a corporate transferee, the more telling question is how quickly you adapt. Moving from India to Germany asks you to become quieter, earlier and more private. Moving from Germany to India asks you to become louder, later and more open. Both transitions take about three months to stop feeling like a performance, and about a year to feel like yourself.

I will give it to Germany by a whisker for the single reason that sleep is a fundamental human good. But I will admit that the evenings in India are warmer, and no one will ever write you a note about the washing machine.

## Conclusion

A country's public manners are its constitution in daily use. Germany drafted its own with footnotes. India improvises it on the street, with commentary.

Pack your patience for one, your ear plugs for the other, and your sense of humour for both. In Germany, be quiet and on time. In India, be warm and unsurprised. And in either, learn what the horn, the sign and the neighbour are trying to say.
