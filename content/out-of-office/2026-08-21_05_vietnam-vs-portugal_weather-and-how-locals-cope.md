# One Country Just Drives Through the Flood, the Other Just Complains About the Heat and Calls It a Day

🇻🇳 Vietnam 🇵🇹 Portugal
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Vietnam's weather doesn't negotiate. The monsoon arrives, the streets flood to the axles, and life continues at almost exactly the same pace it did the day before — motorbikes wade through water that would shut down entire cities elsewhere, ponchos go on, umbrellas come out, and nobody treats it as an event. Portugal's weather, by contrast, is comparatively gentle and yet the country responds to a 38-degree summer afternoon like a minor civilisational crisis: shutters closed, siesta enforced, half the population fleeing to whatever coastline is nearest.

I've ridden pillion through Hanoi flood water up to the footpegs while the driver calmly checked his phone at a red light. I've also watched an entire Alentejo village functionally shut down because it got "too hot to function," a temperature that, generously, most of Southeast Asia would consider a mild Tuesday. Resilience is relative, apparently, and one of these countries has simply set the bar considerably higher.

## Do's & Don'ts: Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a proper poncho, not a tourist one — locals know which stalls sell the ones that actually work | Panic when streets flood; check depth, follow locals' lead, and keep moving at their pace |
| Plan around the monsoon schedule roughly, but never cancel plans over a forecast alone | Assume air conditioning everywhere; many local spots rely on fans and open airflow instead |
| Drink more water than feels necessary during humid season — heat exhaustion sneaks up fast | Wear anything that can't handle getting soaked; laundry-friendly fabric is a real packing consideration |
| Embrace the midday indoor lull in central and southern regions during peak heat | Schedule anything requiring dry roads during typhoon season in central Vietnam without a backup plan |

## Do's & Don'ts: Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Close shutters during the day in summer — it's the actual, effective cooling method, not decoration | Expect air conditioning as standard in older apartments; many rely on cross-ventilation and shutters alone |
| Adjust your schedule to avoid the 1–4pm heat if you're outdoors in Alentejo or the Algarve in summer | Underestimate how fast wildfire risk rises in dry interior regions during peak summer |
| Carry water and sun protection seriously in the interior — it gets drier and hotter than the coast suggests | Assume coastal Portugal and interior Portugal have the same climate; the difference is significant |
| Respect heatwave advisories; Portugal issues them for genuine public health reasons | Plan strenuous activity for mid-afternoon in July or August in inland towns |

## Vietnam: Weather as a Fact of Life, Not an Interruption

Vietnam's climate is genuinely punishing by most standards — a monsoon season that can dump the equivalent of a month's rainfall in an afternoon, humidity that sits on your chest like a physical weight from May through September, typhoons that regularly batter the central coast with real destructive force. And yet the defining feature of how Vietnamese people handle all of this isn't stoicism exactly, it's something closer to total normalisation. The weather isn't an interruption to daily life. It simply is daily life, folded into the rhythm of the day the same way traffic or meal times are.

Watching Hanoi during a serious downpour is watching a city solve a problem in real time without anyone declaring that a problem exists. Ponchos appear from nowhere. Motorbikes slow but don't stop. Street vendors shift their stalls under awnings with the practiced economy of people who've done this exact maneuver a thousand times. Shops that flood have drainage routines rehearsed to the point of choreography. There's an implicit cultural agreement that the weather doesn't get to dictate whether the day happens — it only gets to dictate how.

What I find slightly maddening, in an admiring way, is the total absence of complaint. Where I'd expect grumbling, Vietnamese friends simply adjust and move on, treating my own astonishment at the flooding with a kind of amused patience, the way you'd humour someone alarmed by rain in general. The typhoon season in central Vietnam is more serious business — genuine evacuations, genuine damage, genuine risk — and that gets treated with appropriate gravity. But the everyday monsoon deluge that would paralyse most Western cities barely registers as noteworthy here, and there's something almost enviable about a culture that's simply decided not to be inconvenienced by something it can't control.

## Portugal: A Mild Climate Treated Like an Emergency

Portugal, by any reasonable global standard, has a pleasant climate — Mediterranean-adjacent, mild winters, warm but rarely extreme summers along most of the coast. And yet the country's cultural response to heat has a theatrical, almost affronted quality to it that amuses me every single time. A 35-degree day in the Alentejo triggers a full civic response: shutters drawn like a siege is underway, siesta-adjacent behaviour reasserting itself even in places that officially abandoned the siesta decades ago, an entire afternoon quietly written off as unlivable.

To be fair, the coping mechanisms are genuinely smart, even if the fuss around deploying them borders on excessive. The shutter-and-shade approach to cooling buildings, refined over centuries, works remarkably well without a single kilowatt of air conditioning, and there's real architectural wisdom in it that more energy-anxious countries would do well to copy rather than mock. The interior regions — the Alentejo especially — do get genuinely, dangerously hot and dry, wildfire risk climbing every summer in a way that's become a serious, recurring national crisis rather than an inconvenience, and Portugal's more recent heatwave advisories reflect real, well-founded public health concern rather than melodrama.

But there's a gap between the interior's legitimate heat emergencies and the coastal, mild-summer grumbling that treats a perfectly pleasant Lisbon afternoon as some kind of ordeal. Where Vietnam has built a culture of quiet, practiced adaptation to genuinely severe weather, Portugal has built a culture of dramatic response to comparatively moderate weather, and the contrast, once you've experienced both, is difficult to unsee.

## The Verdict

Vietnam wins, and it isn't close, though I'll grant it's an unfair fight — you can't easily out-resilience a country that copes daily with monsoons and typhoons by comparison to one dealing with a warm afternoon. But that's precisely the point. Genuine severity met with genuine, practiced calm beats moderate weather met with theatrical alarm every time. Vietnam has built an entire social choreography around weather that would shut down most of the developed world. Portugal has built shutters and a strongly worded heatwave advisory.

Portugal's interior wildfire risk is a real and serious exception that deserves respect rather than mockery — that's Portugal's actual weather emergency, and it's treated, correctly, with gravity. Everything short of that, though, gets a response wildly disproportionate to the actual inconvenience. Vietnam simply doesn't have that luxury, and it shows.

## Conclusion

Vietnam has turned genuinely severe weather into background noise through sheer practiced normalisation — floods, monsoons, and typhoons absorbed into daily life without ceremony. Portugal has turned genuinely mild weather into a national event, shutters drawn against a heat most of the world would consider pleasant. If you want to learn real resilience, watch how Hanoi handles a flood. If you want to watch a country perform inconvenience with real conviction, wait for a Lisbon heatwave and count the closed shops. Both are educational. Only one is actually impressive.
