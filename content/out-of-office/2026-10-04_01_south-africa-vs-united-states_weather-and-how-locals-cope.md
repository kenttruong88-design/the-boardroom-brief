# Weather Doesn't Build Character, It Just Builds Better Excuses

🇿🇦 South Africa 🇺🇸 United States

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every nation has a weather personality, and these two have the loudest ones I've met. South Africa treats a thunderstorm like a minor inconvenience to be waited out on a veranda with a glass of something cold, because the sun is coming back in twenty minutes and everyone knows it. The United States treats a dusting of snow like the opening act of a disaster film, complete with a press conference, a hashtag, and a man in a parka standing meaningfully in front of a closed Walmart.

I have lived through a Highveld hailstorm that dented cars and a Chicago polar vortex that nearly dented my will to live, and I can report with total confidence that neither country has the moral high ground here. One nation under-dramatizes genuine danger. The other turns a light drizzle into content. Buckle up.

## Do's & Don'ts

**South Africa**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry an umbrella in Johannesburg from October to March, no exceptions | Assume a sunny morning means a sunny afternoon — the Highveld flips in an hour |
| Charge every device and keep a torch handy for load-shedding-meets-storm season | Panic when the power cuts mid-storm — your neighbours are already lighting candles and gossiping |
| Respect the Cape Town wind (the "Cape Doctor") enough to secure anything loose outdoors | Wear a hat you actually like near Sea Point in August |
| Treat a hailstorm warning as a genuine "move the car" instruction | Leave your car under a tree during storm season and act shocked later |

**United States**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stock up before a forecast snowstorm — the bread-and-milk ritual is real and sacred | Mock the bread-and-milk ritual until you've been snowed in with neither |
| Learn your region's actual hazard (tornado, hurricane, wildfire, blizzard) before you learn the small talk | Assume "weather" means the same emergency everywhere — Miami and Minneapolis are not having the same year |
| Sign up for local emergency alerts the week you arrive | Treat a tornado siren as background noise because you've "heard it before" |
| Layer clothing properly for genuine four-season climates | Buy one coat and assume it covers October through March |

## South Africa: Sunshine With Commitment Issues

South Africa's weather is magnificent right up until it isn't, and the "isn't" arrives with almost no warning. Johannesburg sits at altitude on the Highveld, which means its summer afternoon thunderstorms are not gentle European drizzles — they are theatrical, hail-flinging, power-line-threatening events that can total a car's windscreen in under a minute. Locals have an almost suspicious calm about this. They watch the sky go the colour of a bruise, note the temperature drop, and simply move the washing in. Nobody evacuates. Nobody panics. They've seen this show before, and they know the second act is always short.

What foreigners never expect is how the storm season collides with load-shedding, South Africa's long-running relationship with scheduled power cuts. A hailstorm knocking out the grid during an already-scheduled outage is not a crisis to most Johannesburg residents — it's Tuesday. The generator hums on, the gas stove gets lit, and conversation continues as though the universe hasn't just demonstrated its complete indifference to your dinner plans. This is the South African weather trick: total acceptance, zero hysteria, and an inverter battery nobody mentions until you ask.

Cape Town plays an entirely different game, dominated by the "Cape Doctor" — the relentless summer southeaster wind that can reach gale force and has personally ruined more hairstyles than any salon fire. Capetonians don't fight it; they build lives around it, scheduling braais for the wind-sheltered side of the garden and accepting that any outdoor hat purchase is a short-term rental. Winter brings the Atlantic storms rolling in off the Cape of Storms — genuinely dramatic, genuinely cold, and genuinely under-insulated against, because South African building standards were clearly drafted by someone who had never been cold in their life. Double glazing is a rumour. Central heating is a personality trait of the wealthy. Everyone else just wears a jersey indoors and complains cheerfully, which is, incidentally, the national pastime regardless of the forecast.

## United States: A Continent Pretending to Be One Climate

The United States has the geographic arrogance to call itself one country while running at least six distinct weather personalities simultaneously, and Americans navigate this by becoming near-obsessive meteorology consumers. The Weather Channel is appointment viewing. Local news opens with the forecast, not the headlines. Phones buzz with alerts for conditions I didn't know were classifiable. This isn't paranoia — it's earned. A single American year can include a hurricane, a tornado outbreak, a derecho, a polar vortex, and a wildfire smoke advisory, often in places that are supposedly nowhere near each other.

What's genuinely admirable, grudgingly, is the infrastructure built to meet this chaos. Storm shelters are architectural features, not afterthoughts. School districts run actual tornado drills with the same seriousness South African schools reserve for fire drills. Snowplow fleets are deployed with military precision in the Midwest and Northeast, while the same six centimetres of snow would shut down an entire Southern city for a week, because nobody there owns a plow and frankly why would they. This regional specialisation is the real story: Americans aren't uniformly weather-prepared, they're hyper-locally weather-prepared, which means a Minnesotan and a Floridian are essentially experts in two unrelated disciplines who happen to share a passport.

The flip side is the sheer performative anxiety around anything mild. A light snow dusting in Atlanta triggers school closures, grocery runs, and a news cycle usually reserved for actual emergencies, while that same dusting in Minneapolis is simply Tuesday with boots on. Americans will tell you, with a straight face, that "it's not the cold, it's the humidity," as though humidity has ever once improved a cold.

## The Verdict

If I had to survive a genuine weather emergency, I'd want American infrastructure — the shelters, the alert systems, the sheer institutional muscle memory of taking disaster seriously. If I had to survive the daily grind of unpredictable skies with my dignity intact, I'd want South African composure — the unbothered washing-line retrieval, the candlelit dinner during a blackout, the refusal to let a dented car ruin an otherwise perfectly good afternoon.

But if we're being honest about which country has correctly calibrated its panic to its actual danger, neither wins outright. America over-reacts to snow and under-reacts to how genuinely terrifying its tornado alley is until you've lived through a watch-to-warning escalation. South Africa under-reacts to hail that can kill and over-reacts to a slightly chilly evening requiring three jerseys and a hot water bottle. Call it a draw, decided on vibes: South Africa wins for grace under pressure, America wins for showing up with an actual plan.

## Conclusion

What both countries share, improbably, is a complete lack of interest in moderation. South Africa either shrugs at the sky or stares it down with a thermos; America either ignores the forecast entirely or builds a national identity around surviving it. Neither approach is wrong, exactly — they're just calibrated to wildly different furniture. Move to Johannesburg and you'll learn to read a sky like a seasoned sailor while complaining about the cold indoors. Move to Chicago and you'll learn to respect a blizzard the way you'd respect a bear: from a distance, with supplies, and without irony. Either way, pack a jacket you actually like, because you'll be wearing it indoors more than you'd think.
