# Two Countries Built Their Identity Around Wilderness

🇧🇼 Botswana vs 🇨🇦 Canada — Parks and Green Space Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Botswana and Canada both decided, decades ago, that untouched wilderness was worth protecting rather than paving, and both built a real chunk of their national identity around that decision. The difference is what "untouched" gets you access to on a random Tuesday. In Botswana, it means elephants wandering within sight of the Chobe riverfront and one of the most successful high-value, low-volume conservation models on the planet. In Canada, it means a free-to-modest-fee national park system so vast and so normalised that locals treat a weekend hike through genuinely spectacular scenery as barely worth mentioning.

I have sat in a boat on the Chobe River close enough to a bull elephant to reconsider several life choices, and I have hiked a trail in Banff so aggressively beautiful it felt almost unfair that Canadians get to do this on a casual Saturday without booking eighteen months ahead. Both countries made wilderness central to their national story. Only one of them made it genuinely accessible to someone without a safari-lodge budget.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Botswana**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book Chobe and Okavango Delta trips well ahead — the low-volume model means limited capacity | Expect budget safari pricing — Botswana deliberately positions itself high-value, low-volume |
| Hire local guides — their wildlife knowledge and tracking skill is genuinely exceptional | Wander off-trail or self-drive into reserves without proper preparation and permits |
| Respect the "high cost, low impact" conservation philosophy — it's the whole point | Compare prices to neighbouring countries' safari markets and expect Botswana to match them |
| Visit during the dry season (May–October) for the best wildlife viewing | Assume the delta is accessible year-round the same way — water levels shift dramatically |

**Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a Parks Canada Discovery Pass if you're visiting multiple parks in a year | Assume popular parks like Banff or Jasper don't need reservations in peak season — they do now |
| Respect wildlife distance rules — bears and elk are not photo props | Underestimate how quickly weather changes at elevation, even in summer |
| Explore beyond the famous parks — the system is enormous and most of it is quiet | Assume "free" or low-cost access means low quality — it very much doesn't |
| Book camping spots months ahead for the well-known parks | Leave food unsecured at a campsite — wildlife encounters are a real, not theoretical, risk |

## Botswana: Wilderness as a Deliberate Scarcity

Botswana made a genuinely unusual bet decades ago and has stuck to it with remarkable discipline: rather than chasing high visitor volumes the way many safari destinations do, it deliberately capped access and pushed prices upward, betting that fewer visitors paying more would produce better conservation outcomes than mass tourism ever could. The Okavango Delta — a UNESCO World Heritage site and one of the few major inland river deltas on the planet that never reaches the sea, instead spreading into a seasonal wetland that transforms the landscape entirely between wet and dry seasons — is the clearest expression of that philosophy in action. Access is genuinely limited, lodges are few and expensive, and the result is an ecosystem that has largely avoided the environmental strain visible in some more heavily trafficked safari destinations elsewhere on the continent.

Chobe National Park, with its famously dense elephant population congregating along the riverfront, delivers some of the most reliable, close-range wildlife viewing anywhere in Africa, precisely because the "low volume" side of the equation has been protected as fiercely as the wildlife itself. What this means practically for anyone actually living in Botswana, rather than passing through on a two-week tourist itinerary, is that the country's flagship natural spaces remain a genuine luxury experience even for residents — this isn't a park system you pop into casually on a free Sunday afternoon. It's a planned, budgeted, sometimes once-or-twice-a-year event, and treated with the corresponding reverence.

What often surprises newcomers is how completely locals and long-term expats alike buy into the underlying conservation logic, even while grumbling about the cost. There's a shared understanding that the high price tag is doing real ecological work, not just extracting tourist revenue, and that buy-in shapes a certain quiet national pride around Botswana's parks that goes well beyond the usual tourism-board talking points.

## Canada: Wilderness as Standard Equipment

Canada took the opposite approach entirely, building a national park system so extensive — stretching from temperate rainforest on the Pacific coast to Arctic tundra in the north — that access became something close to a civic expectation rather than a luxury. Parks Canada's system spans dozens of national parks, and while the marquee names — Banff, Jasper, Algonquin — draw the crowds and increasingly require advance reservations even for day access during peak season, the sheer size of the overall network means genuinely spectacular, much quieter alternatives sit within a few hours' drive of nearly every major Canadian city.

What genuinely startles newcomers, more than the scenery itself, is how unremarkable Canadians find all of it. A weekend hike through mountain scenery that would headline someone else's once-in-a-lifetime trip gets discussed here with the same casual tone reserved for running errands. This normalisation cuts both ways — it makes world-class wilderness feel refreshingly unpretentious and accessible, but it also means newcomers sometimes underestimate the very real risks involved. Bears, sudden alpine weather shifts, and genuinely remote backcountry terrain don't become less dangerous just because locals treat the whole enterprise so casually, and every park ranger has a story about a visitor who found that out the hard way.

The democratic pricing model is the real structural difference from Botswana's approach: a Discovery Pass covering unlimited access to dozens of parks for a year costs a fraction of a single Okavango Delta lodge night, and even the busiest parks remain accessible to anyone willing to plan reasonably ahead and book a campsite or permit in advance rather than showing up expecting to walk straight in during July.

## The Verdict

Canada, for sheer accessibility, though I'll admit it's an unfair fight in one sense — Canada's model works partly because it has the sheer geographic scale to spread demand across dozens of parks, while Botswana's smaller, more concentrated wilderness genuinely can't absorb mass visitation without destroying the very thing that makes it exceptional. Both approaches are defensible, even admirable, on their own terms. But if you're actually living somewhere and want green space as a regular part of your life rather than an annual splurge, Canada's model simply serves ordinary residents better. Botswana gives you something extraordinary, rarely. Canada gives you something extraordinary, often, and doesn't make a fuss about it either way.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a Gaborone-based consultant paraphrased: he said locals treat a Chobe trip the way other cultures treat a milestone birthday — planned, saved for, and talked about for months beforehand.*

> *Expat.com forum, Botswana thread — paraphrased: a longtime resident warned newcomers not to expect neighbouring-country safari pricing, calling the cost gap "the whole point, not a flaw."*

> *TheLocal.ca — paraphrased forum comment: a relocated European described genuine shock at how blasé Canadian coworkers were about a Banff weekend, "like they were describing a trip to the supermarket."*

## Conclusion

Botswana and Canada both understood, decades apart, that wilderness left alone is worth more than wilderness paved over — they just reached wildly different conclusions about who gets to enjoy it and how often. Botswana's model rewards patience, planning, and a healthy travel budget with something genuinely rare. Canada's model rewards nothing more than a bit of foresight with something genuinely vast. Bring money to one. Bring a decent pair of boots and a reservation to the other.
