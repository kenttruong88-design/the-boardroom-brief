# One Country Digitised Your Entire Medical History in 2008. The Other Wants You to Know Your Doctor Also Has a Government Job in the Morning

🇪🇪 Estonia vs Nicaragua 🇳🇮

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Estonia became the first country on Earth to run a nationwide electronic health record system, back when most of the world was still arguing about fax machines in hospitals, and now issues over 99% of its prescriptions digitally without anyone breaking a sweat about it. Nicaragua, meanwhile, runs on a healthcare system where your doctor might see you in a public clinic at 9am and in a private office at 3pm, same person, same medical license, entirely different price tag. Both systems work. Neither one works the way you assume "healthcare" is supposed to work if you've only ever lived somewhere with one tier.

I've had a prescription filled in Tallinn faster than I could finish a coffee, and I've had a wonderfully competent private consultation in Managua for less than the co-pay I'd pay at home. Neither experience prepared me for the other.

## Do's & Don'ts: Estonia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register as a resident and enrol in the public health insurance system (EHIF) if you're staying long term | Assume a European Health Insurance Card covers you indefinitely — it's designed for short-term stays, not residency |
| Use the Terviseportaal digital health portal to check your own records and prescriptions — it's genuinely comprehensive | Expect paper prescriptions — over 99% are issued digitally and pharmacies expect you to already know this |
| Trust the system's efficiency — with data 99% digitised, your history follows you between providers automatically | Be surprised that care is delivered by private providers even though funding is largely public — it's a mixed model, not a contradiction |

## Do's & Don'ts: Nicaragua

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use private hospitals like Vivian Pellas Metropolitan for anything serious — quality and English-language support are strongest there | Rely on the public system for specialised or urgent care if you can avoid it — funding shortages affect service quality |
| Take advantage of how inexpensive private consultations are compared to home — many expats are pleasantly shocked | Assume every doctor works exclusively in one setting — plenty split mornings in public hospitals and afternoons in private practice |
| Ask about English-speaking coordinators at major private hospitals — they exist specifically to help foreign patients | Expect uniform care quality across the country — Managua's private facilities far outpace rural public options |

## Estonia: A Country That Digitised Trust Itself

Estonia's healthcare system makes sense only once you understand that the country built its entire e-Estonia digital infrastructure around a single premise: friction is the enemy, and paperwork is friction. The nationwide Electronic Health Record system launched back in December 2008, making Estonia the first country in the world to run one at that scale, and it wasn't a symbolic gesture — 99% of health data here is now fully digitised, meaning any physician you see, anywhere in the country, has your complete medical history the second you sit down. No faxed referrals, no lost files, no repeating your allergy list for the fourth time this year.

The prescription system takes this further than almost anywhere else on the planet. Since 2010, digital prescriptions have been the nationwide standard, and today over 99% of all prescriptions are issued electronically, instantly available at any pharmacy in the country the moment your doctor sends them through. You genuinely just show up, give your ID, and walk out with medication — the sort of frictionless bureaucracy that makes you irrationally angry at every other healthcare system you've ever dealt with.

For residents, the funding model is a public-private hybrid that manages to avoid most of the worst features of either pure model: largely publicly funded through the EHIF system, but delivered entirely by private providers competing on quality rather than a single monopolistic public system. Expats staying briefly can lean on an EHIC if they're from the EU/EEA or Switzerland, but anyone settling in long-term needs to register as a resident and actually enrol in the public insurance system — skipping this step is the single most common mistake newcomers make, assuming short-term coverage extends indefinitely when it very much doesn't. The Terviseportaal patient portal ties the whole system together, letting you check your own records and prescriptions directly, which is either wonderfully empowering or faintly Orwellian depending on your relationship with government databases. Either way, it works, with a reliability that borders on smug.

## Nicaragua: Two Systems, One Doctor, Wildly Different Prices

Nicaragua's healthcare system runs on a two-tier structure that catches almost every newcomer off guard: a public system available to residents and foreigners alike, sitting alongside a private system of independent physicians, clinics, and hospitals that most expats end up relying on almost exclusively. The public system exists and technically covers you, but underfunding shows up in real, tangible ways — administration issues, resource shortages, and quality that varies dramatically depending on where in the country you are. It's not that the public system doesn't function; it's that it functions unevenly, and expats who can afford the alternative generally take it.

The private system is where the actual expat healthcare experience happens, and it's genuinely good news on the cost front — private consultations here remain remarkably inexpensive by international standards, to the point that many newcomers describe a strange sense of disbelief the first time they pay a private doctor's bill and realise it costs less than a single co-pay back home. Major facilities in the greater Managua area, especially the Vivian Pellas Metropolitan Hospital, Hospital Bautista, and the Military Hospital, form the backbone of serious medical care for both locals with means and the expat community, with Vivian Pellas specifically established as the default first choice for foreigners needing anything beyond routine care.

Here's the detail that genuinely surprises people: a significant number of Nicaraguan doctors split their working day cleanly in two, working for the government system in the morning and holding private office hours in the afternoon. Same physician, same training, same license — just two entirely different institutional contexts and, often, two entirely different price points for essentially the same expertise. Major private hospitals employ English-speaking coordinators specifically to bridge the language gap for international patients, which matters enormously given how thin the safety net gets outside the capital.

## The Verdict

Estonia wins decisively on system design — a genuinely paperless, frictionless, fully digitised healthcare infrastructure is a remarkable achievement that most wealthy nations still haven't matched, let alone a country of 1.3 million people. Nicaragua wins on something less flashy but genuinely valuable to a certain kind of expat: radical affordability paired with surprisingly high-quality private care, provided you're near Managua and can afford to skip the public tier. If you want healthcare that disappears into the background because the system simply works, Estonia has quietly built the closest thing to that on the planet. If you want competent, personal, dramatically cheaper private care and don't mind that your doctor might have started the day at a public clinic across town, Nicaragua's private hospitals will treat you well, for a fraction of what you're used to paying.

## Conclusion

Estonia and Nicaragua sit at opposite ends of what "modern healthcare" even means — one digitised trust itself into the system so thoroughly that inefficiency became almost structurally impossible, the other kept two entirely separate tiers running side by side and let market forces and family budgets sort out who gets which. Both, remarkably, deliver real value to the people who understand how to use them. Just don't expect Nicaragua's system to hand you a Terviseportaal login, and don't expect Estonia's doctors to also see you at a discount in the afternoon. Different tools, same underlying goal: keeping you alive without wrecking your week in the process.
