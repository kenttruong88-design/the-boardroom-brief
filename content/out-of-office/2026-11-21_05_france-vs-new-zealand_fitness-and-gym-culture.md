# The French Treat the Gym Like a Rumour; New Zealanders Treat It Like a Hill

🇫🇷 France vs 🇳🇿 New Zealand

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The French have a complicated relationship with deliberate exercise. They will walk three miles to a bakery, climb six flights of stairs without comment and cycle gracefully in a linen jacket, but the idea of paying monthly to lift heavy things in a mirrored room in lycra strikes many of them as faintly American, and therefore faintly vulgar. The gym exists in France. It simply does not have the evangelical tone.

New Zealand, on the other hand, treats fitness as a birthright and a competition. Walk into a Kiwi gym on a Tuesday evening and you will find a rugby player gently destroying a squat rack, a grandmother on the rowing machine out-pacing him, and a poster for a triathlon that sounds like a threat. The outdoors does most of the work in New Zealand, and the gym is where people go to prepare for being outdoors. I have trained in both countries and can report that one of them wants you to sweat, and the other wants you to look as if you did not.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇫🇷 France ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say bonjour to the room on entering and au revoir on leaving | Stride in with headphones on and ignore everyone |
| Read the contract, especially the cancellation terms | Sign anything on the first visit just to get the free week |
| Wear dark, tidy sportswear | Turn up in a slogan T-shirt and fluorescent trainers |
| Check holiday closures before you commit | Expect a gym to stay open through August |

| 🇳🇿 New Zealand ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a club, a run group or a team, not just a gym | Treat fitness as a solo, indoor activity |
| Give a cheerful nod to strangers on the trail | Pretend you cannot see the hiker coming the other way |
| Train for something with a date, such as a half-marathon | Boast about your lifts to a farmer |
| Respect the weather and carry a jacket | Cancel a run because it is raining |

## 🇫🇷 France

The first thing you notice about a French gym is that it has manners. Joe Collier, writing for Connexion France, found his local gym in Brittany far more sociable than his London one: members say good morning, ask how you are and say goodbye when they leave, whereas his London gym was dominated by silence. He also observed an unofficial dress code of black. A blogger on Oui In France describes a small-town gym that closes at one on Saturdays, is shut on Sundays and, in August, closed for two weeks, during which she was still billed for the full month. The explanation she was given, in effect, was that everybody goes on holiday.

The second thing you notice is the contract. Connexion reports that France's consumer agency, the DGCCRF, inspected 571 gyms in 2022 and 2023 and found that more than 70 per cent had at least one irregularity, and about 46 per cent were using unfair or illegal contract clauses, including reduced cancellation rights and hidden fees in supposedly no-commitment deals. I am not saying that the French gym is a scam. I am saying that you should read the contract the way you would read a dating profile.

The equipment is a more mixed story. A commenter on the same blog found the classes less serious, the instructors less qualified and the machines run down compared to home. And the market is smaller: IHRSA data quoted by NBC News once put French gym membership at 5.4 per cent of the population, against nearly 12 per cent in the UK, and Connexion counts nearly 5,000 gyms against around 7,000 in Britain. What the French do instead is move through daily life: stairs, markets, bicycles, a long lunch followed by a short walk. If you want a purpose-built temple to biceps, you will be disappointed. If you want a polite, quiet room with a rowing machine, you may well fall in love.

Away from the gym, the French treat fitness as a lifestyle rather than a programme. The walk to the boulangerie, the stairs in an apartment block with no lift, the bicycle on the Sunday market route and the swim in the municipal pool at lunchtime all count. There is also a long tradition of thermal baths, hiking clubs and, particularly in smaller towns, a municipal sports association that offers everything from gymnastics to line dancing for a modest annual fee. If you ask a French person whether they exercise they will often say no, and then mention that they walked eight kilometres the day before. It is not modesty. It is a different definition.

## 🇳🇿 New Zealand

New Zealand fitness begins at the front door. The country has a small population and an enormous supply of mountain, bush and coastline, and most of the working population seems to have a sporting hobby with a start line. You will find a surprising number of people training for a marathon, a multi-day tramp, a surf life-saving club event or an obstacle course that involves mud and a certain amount of public shame. The gym is a support act: where people do the squats, so that they do not collapse on the hill.

Participation data from Sport New Zealand consistently shows high levels of regular activity, including a strong skew towards informal recreation, such as walking, running and swimming, alongside club sport. That tallies with every Kiwi I have met, who are either on their way to or from something physical. The social side matters hugely: clubs, school sports, work teams and the Saturday morning parkrun are where friendships are made. For an expat this is the single best way to meet people, and it is considerably better than a bar.

The downside is the pressure. In a country that venerates the outdoors, being unfit is a faintly eccentric choice, and the gym culture can be a little uncomplicated: functional, no-nonsense, plenty of free weights and very little incense. It is also cheaper to join a team than a commercial club, and nobody will tear up your cancellation letter. I did not, in my time there, encounter a single hidden fee. I did encounter a nine-year-old who beat me up a hill and then offered me a sandwich.

And one final observation about the Kiwi version: the gym itself is often an outdoors gym in disguise. Many towns have outdoor fitness parks, free to use, along a waterfront or in a reserve, and the municipal pool is usually excellent. An expat who joins a surf life-saving club or a running group will find that fitness is simply the thing everyone is already doing, and that nobody is selling you anything. It is the most effective wellness industry I have ever seen, largely because it is not an industry at all, but a habit with a thermos.

## The Verdict

New Zealand wins on fitness, and France wins on fitness culture, and I will explain the difference. A Kiwi will get you fit. The country will force you outside, pull you into a club and coax you up a trail you did not think you could manage, and you will leave stronger, browner and slightly smug. The French will not get you fit at all, but they will teach you how to live at a pace where fitness is the incidental result of a day well spent.

If you are an expat who needs a gym membership to stay sane, take New Zealand, and take a friend. If you can survive in a place where the gym is an afterthought and the baguette is a tool of civilisation, take France. The truth is that the French approach, polite, unobtrusive and quietly stylish, ages better. I admire it enormously and have not once been able to replicate it.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Oui In France, a blogger in a small French town — her gym closes at 1pm on Saturdays and all day Sunday, has no air conditioning, and closed for two weeks in August while still charging for the whole month.*

> *Connexion France, a writer who splits his time between London and Brittany — finds his Breton gym far more sociable than his London one, with members greeting each other on arrival and departure and an unofficial dress code of black.*

> *Expat.com France forum, a poster in Rennes — joined a budget chain, found no personal trainers, and noted that everyone else in the gym was French.*

## Conclusion

Join a French gym and you will learn to say bonjour to people who are lifting weights. Join a Kiwi club and you will learn to say yes to a hill you would rather not climb. Both are admirable forms of social education. One produces better manners, and the other better calves. Read the contract in France, pack a jacket in New Zealand, and in either country, never, ever stand in front of the mirror and say that you are only here for the sauna. They will know.
