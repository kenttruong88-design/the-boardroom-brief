# Norwegians Spend Their Weekends Fleeing Society. Nigerians Spend Theirs Building It.

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ask a Norwegian what they did last weekend and you will get a monosyllabic answer involving a cabin, a fjord, and possibly a dead reindeer they field-dressed themselves with the quiet satisfaction of someone who has never once considered brunch. Ask a Nigerian the same question and you will get a wedding, a christening, three phone calls interrupting the story, and an invitation to next weekend's event before you've finished asking about this one.

I have spent enough weekends in both countries to say with total confidence that Norway treats leisure as a form of disappearance and Nigeria treats it as a form of arrival. One country wants to be alone with a tree. The other wants four hundred of its closest relatives in one marquee by Saturday noon. Neither is wrong. Both will judge you for doing it the other way.

## Do's & Don'ts: Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Own or borrow a hytte (cabin) — it is basically compulsory | Suggest a weekend brunch as an alternative to hiking |
| Master the art of "koselig" — cosy, unhurried, blanket-based living | Assume silence on a trail means someone is unhappy |
| Learn to love your own company between Friday and Sunday | Schedule a big social gathering without weeks of notice |

## Do's & Don'ts: Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clear your entire Saturday for "one quick event" (it isn't quick) | Show up to an owambe in anything less than your finest outfit |
| Say yes to the extra plate of jollof rice, always | Expect anyone to leave a party before it has properly peaked |
| Budget for at least one wedding a month during season | Assume a "quiet weekend at home" is a real category of plan |

## Norway: The Republic of Vanishing

Norwegians do not do weekends so much as evacuate from them. Come Friday afternoon, entire cities empty out with the efficiency of a fire drill, and everyone reconvenes at a hytte somewhere in the mountains that has been in the family since roughly the Viking era, decorated in exactly the same pine panelling as every other hytte in the country. There is a word for this — friluftsliv, "open-air living" — and Norwegians deploy it the way other nations deploy religion: constantly, sincerely, and with zero tolerance for people who don't share the faith.

What foreigners misunderstand is that this isn't antisocial. It's just social on Norwegian terms, which means small groups, long silences, and activities that involve weather. A weekend win in Norway is not a great party; it's a good ski, a cold swim, a fire that didn't require matches. Statistics Norway's own leisure surveys back this up — outdoor recreation consistently outranks nightlife, shopping, or "going out" as the preferred use of free time, and this is true across income brackets, which tells you it's cultural bedrock, not a Instagram trend for people with matching Fjällräven jackets.

The flip side is a social calendar that runs on geological time. Try to organise a Norwegian dinner party for "sometime this weekend" and you'll be met with the mild horror of someone who has had their October Saturdays booked since March. Spontaneity is not charming here; it's a scheduling failure. And if you're an expat hoping for a bustling Saturday nightlife scene to match London or Lagos, you will instead find shuttered shops by 4pm, a handful of overpriced bars, and a national assumption that anyone still in the city on a sunny Saturday has made a serious error in life planning. Go find a fjord. Everyone else already has.

## Nigeria: The Republic of the Owambe

If Norway's weekend is a retreat, Nigeria's is an offensive. The owambe — the sprawling, color-coordinated, aso-ebi-clad celebration that can mean a wedding, a birthday, a burial, or, frankly, any excuse — is less an event than a weekend-consuming institution, and it is glorious. Lagos on a Saturday in December ("detty December," as it's affectionately and accurately known) is a city running on generator power and pure celebratory momentum, with multiple parties competing for the same guests, the same caterers, and the same overworked live band.

What's remarkable is the sheer social density of it. Where a Norwegian weekend might involve two or three people and a great deal of nature, a Nigerian weekend can easily involve two or three hundred people and a great deal of jollof rice, and everyone present is somehow a cousin, in-law, or "family friend" whose exact relationship to you nobody can quite explain. The Nigeria Tourism Development Corporation has increasingly leaned into this as an actual tourism pitch — festival and event tourism, not beaches — because the country's real leisure export isn't scenery, it's atmosphere.

The exhausting part, if you're new to it, is the sheer obligation woven through the fun. Weekend leisure in much of Nigeria isn't optional decompression; it's social maintenance, and skipping three weddings in a row has consequences that ripple through extended family for years. There is no "quiet weekend in," not really — quiet weekends are what you have when something has gone wrong. The default setting is togetherness, loud and lavish and utterly relentless, and woe betide the expat who thought they could get away with a polo shirt and an early exit.

## The Verdict

If you want your soul restored, book the Norwegian cabin — silence, snow, and nobody asking you to dance. If you want your soul reminded that it's alive, buy the aso-ebi and go to the wedding. Norway wins on rest. Nigeria wins on joy. My personal, entirely biased ruling: a culture that treats celebration as a civic duty beats one that treats it as an inconvenience to be scheduled around, every single time. Norwegians can keep their fjords. I'll be at the owambe, badly dancing, thoroughly fed, and home well after everyone in Oslo has already gone to bed.

## Conclusion

Norway will teach you to enjoy your own company, which is a useful and slightly humbling skill nobody warns you you're missing until you're standing alone on a mountain at dusk, genuinely content. Nigeria will teach you that community is a full-contact sport with a strict dress code and no early exits. Neither country will apologise for how it does weekends, and neither should. The only real mistake is importing one country's rhythm into the other — don't show up to a Lagos owambe expecting quiet reflection, and don't expect an Oslo hytte to have a dance floor. Pack accordingly, and for heaven's sake, in Nigeria, iron your outfit.
