# Two Post-Soviet Neighbours, Two Very Different Ideas of Efficient

🇦🇿 Azerbaijan 🇬🇪 Georgia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Azerbaijan and Georgia share a border, a Soviet past, and almost nothing about how they've decided bureaucracy should feel. Georgia built the Public Service Hall — glass buildings, single-visit processing, a genuinely startling level of efficiency for a country its size and budget. Azerbaijan built ASAN Xidmet, its own one-stop government service centre, equally polished on the surface, but banking and bureaucracy here still carry a heavier undercurrent of hierarchy, patience, and knowing precisely which window to approach. Both countries clearly decided, at some point, that Soviet-era queuing culture was an embarrassment worth fixing. They just fixed it differently.

I've opened bank accounts in both, and the experience told me everything about each country's broader personality: Tbilisi's was quick, almost anticlimactic in its smoothness; Baku's was smooth too, right up until it wasn't, at which point I needed a local colleague's phone call to get unstuck.

## Do's & Don'ts: Azerbaijan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use ASAN Xidmet centres for most official processes — they're genuinely well organised | Assume every bank branch outside Baku offers the same English-language service |
| Bring more documentation than you think you'll need, including notarised translations | Expect a single visit to resolve anything involving property, residency, or larger transactions |
| Build a local contact who can make a phone call on your behalf when things stall | Get visibly frustrated with a clerk; hierarchy and politeness matter more here than efficiency complaints |

## Do's & Don'ts: Georgia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the Public Service Hall for almost everything — it's designed for exactly this | Assume informal workarounds exist the way they might elsewhere in the region; the system doesn't need them |
| Open a bank account early; Georgia's foreign-friendly banking rules are a genuine draw for expats | Expect the same speed at smaller regional branches outside Tbilisi and Batumi |
| Take advantage of Georgia's notably low corruption levels compared to its neighbours | Assume English is widely spoken outside the capital and major tourist cities |

## Azerbaijan: Polished on the Surface, Personal Underneath

ASAN Xidmet is, by any honest measure, an impressive piece of state design — a one-stop government service model that bundles dozens of previously scattered bureaucratic processes into a single, modern, air-conditioned building with numbered tickets and genuinely helpful staff. For straightforward tasks, it works exactly as advertised, and it's become something of a regional model that other post-Soviet states have studied and partly copied.

Where things get more traditionally Azerbaijani is in anything involving larger transactions, property, or long-term residency — processes that pull you out of ASAN's streamlined bubble and into a system that still runs, underneath the modern interface, on relationships and hierarchy. Banking follows a similar pattern: opening a basic account in Baku is fairly painless, but anything requiring larger transfers, business banking, or dealing with a branch outside the capital tends to slow down considerably, and often benefits enormously from having a local colleague or contact who can make an introductory phone call rather than relying purely on the formal process.

This isn't corruption in the crude sense — it's closer to a lingering cultural expectation that important things get resolved through vouched relationships rather than pure procedure, even inside an otherwise modernised system. Expats who arrive expecting either total Soviet-style obstruction or total Scandinavian efficiency both end up mildly wrong-footed. Azerbaijan sits in a genuinely specific middle: modern infrastructure, traditional undercurrents, and a system that rewards patience and personal warmth over pure insistence on process.

## Georgia: The Bureaucracy That Actually Wants You to Succeed

Georgia's Public Service Hall is, without much competition, one of the more genuinely impressive pieces of government infrastructure I've encountered anywhere, developed or developing. A single building, often architecturally striking, houses dozens of services under one roof, processes that would take weeks elsewhere get handled in a single visit, and the whole system runs on a transparency-first design specifically built to eliminate the kind of under-the-table dealing that plagued the region for decades after Soviet collapse. It largely worked. Georgia's corruption levels, particularly at the everyday bureaucratic level, are strikingly low compared to most of its neighbours, and that shows up immediately in how straightforward daily admin feels.

Banking follows the same pattern: Georgia has actively courted foreign residents and remote workers with some of the more accessible account-opening rules in the region, and Tbilisi's major banks handle foreign clients routinely, efficiently, often with solid English-language support at central branches. It's become a genuine draw for the country's growing population of digital nomads and remote-work expats, precisely because the system doesn't punish you for being new.

The caveat, unsurprisingly, is geography again: step outside Tbilisi and Batumi into smaller towns, and both the speed and the English-language capability drop noticeably, even if the underlying system remains fundamentally the same well-designed structure. Georgia didn't build one efficient bureaucracy in one city and call it done — it built a genuinely national system — but the polish is, understandably, thickest where the government has invested the most.

## The Verdict

Georgia wins, clearly, on pure procedural efficiency and transparency — the Public Service Hall deserves the reputation it's built, and Tbilisi's banking system has genuinely earned its status as a regional favourite for expats and remote workers. Azerbaijan wins on something less quantifiable: once you understand the system's actual logic, relationships over pure process, ASAN's polish plus the right local contact gets things done just as effectively, if less predictably for outsiders. If I had to bank and file paperwork somewhere without a local safety net, Georgia, every time. If I already had that safety net, Azerbaijan wouldn't slow me down much either.

## Conclusion

Georgia proves that a small, resource-limited country can out-design bureaucracies from far wealthier nations if it actually commits to transparency and single-visit processing. Azerbaijan proves that modern infrastructure and traditional social structures can coexist, sometimes uneasily, inside the same polished building. Bank in Tbilisi if you value pure, replicable efficiency. Bank in Baku if you're willing to make a phone call now and then, and don't mind that the real system runs partly on who you know rather than purely on what the ticket number says.
