# Ireland Asks You Out by Not Asking You Out. Britain Asks You Out by Apologising for Everything Else

🇮🇪 Ireland vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The Irish and the British share an island group, a language, a taste for tea and a talent for avoiding direct emotional statements. Beyond that, they have almost nothing in common romantically, which is why a posting between Dublin and London produces more social confusion than a move to a country whose language you do not speak. At least in Tokyo you know you are lost.

Both nations flirt through banter and both treat sincerity as a medical emergency. But the machinery around it, the pub, the app, the pool of strangers, the density of people who know your mother, differs enough to make dating feel like a different sport.

**Ireland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |

| Show interest through banter and teasing | State your feelings on the first drink |
| Suggest a pint, a walk or a coffee | Plan a formal dinner for a first meeting |
| Expect friends and family to know everything | Assume your date is anonymous |

**United Kingdom**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect drinks, coffee or a park walk on a first date | Arrive with flowers |
| Be prepared for the man to pay first | Suggest splitting the bill on date one |
| Try in-person events if the apps wear you out | Rely on one app in a city of millions |
| Use self-deprecating humour | Take compliments without deflecting them |

## Ireland

Irish dating is an exercise in plausible deniability. The Expat.com guide to dating in Ireland says that relationships often begin in pubs, where chatting to strangers at the bar is normal, interest is signalled indirectly and witty banter beats chat-up lines. The typical first date is a pint, a stroll in the park or a coffee, and the national romantic institution is the Lisdoonvarna Matchmaking Festival, which fills a west-coast town for a month each September with, in the guide's words, tens of thousands of visitors. A man named Willie Daly bills himself as Ireland's only matchmaker. Touch his lucky book with both hands, the story goes, and you will be married within six months.

The locals describe the scene less romantically. A reader letter to the Irish Examiner by a man from Shanbally in County Cork argued that Ireland has plenty of cautious texting and messaging but very little actual dating. People avoid phone calls, give noncommittal answers when asked out, and are vetted by friends and family before anything happens. Success, he claimed, depends on being in a clique or drinking.

The more entertaining first-person account is a column in the Irish Times by Peter Flanagan, a comedian from Kildare who lives in Hackney. He compares the experience of using Tinder with a three-kilometre radius in London, which offers hundreds of thousands of options, with Kildare, where the same filter produced neighbours and family friends. His closing line deserves quoting once: if you cannot get a date in Ireland, his humble advice is to leave.

An American student ambassador at UCD Smurfit described the more charming side: an English classmate counted a shared daily run as their first date, and her "I like you" was heard in England as nearly "I love you". Being an international student, she said, made starting conversations easier, because locals were curious.

So the Irish scene is warm, small and watchful. It rewards charm and punishes anyone who thinks a first date can be private.

## United Kingdom

Britain has the opposite problem: too many people and not enough intimacy. Expatica's guide to dating in the UK describes the standard script. First dates are usually drinks at a pub, coffee or a walk in the park. Banter and self-deprecating humour are the norm. The man typically pays on the first date, with the other paying next time, and suggesting a split can read as disinterest. Flowers on a first date, the guide warns, are a faux pas. Couples often live together after three or four years, there is no common-law marriage, and 29.6 percent of UK residents live alone, according to ONS figures it cites.

The apps are everywhere and so is the fatigue. Time Out London's 2022 feature, "How London dumped the dating apps", reports that a Thursday singles night at a venue near Monument drew about 600 people and sold out. It cites an Eventbrite finding that in-person dating events grew 400 percent in four years, and a poll in which 84 percent of millennials said they would rather find love offline. The co-founder of one event company says that app choice causes paralysis. An Irishman in London, Alan O'Donoghue, says he goes to in-person events to make friends, and a twenty-year-old student calls the UK dating scene dead.

Flanagan, in his column, adds the political layer. London profiles, he says, often announce "No Tories" at the top of the bio, which is both an opinion and a filter. He also notes that as an Irish migrant he gets to reinvent himself, because the English do not know his school or class. He plays up the assumption that he comes from a family of simple country folk. In a city that has made anonymity an art form, that is a gift.

Britain's scene is huge, tiring and anonymous. It offers range and demands stamina. You can meet anyone, and you will, and most of them will say "we should do this again" and mean it as a goodbye.

There is a quieter point under the jokes. In the Irish Times column, Flanagan observes that Brits tend to assume rural poverty when they hear an Irish accent, and he plays along. The Expat.com guide adds that Ireland has speed dating in Dublin, Cork and Galway, a Facebook group called Dating Ireland, and volunteering as a way to meet people, which is a tidy summary of how a small country builds a social scene: from the community up. Britain builds it from the market down, with apps, supper clubs and organised events competing for the same tired Thursday crowd. Choose according to whether you want to be found or to be chosen.

## The Verdict

Ireland has the better odds of being known. Britain has the better odds of being anonymous. That is not the same as luck.

If you want a partner who will turn up with a story, a joke and a cousin, go to Ireland. If you want to reinvent yourself and meet strangers for ever, go to London. The Irish scene is intimate and a little claustrophobic. The British scene is vast and a little cold. Both are lonely in their own special way.

My verdict is Ireland for people who want to be seen and Britain for people who want to be left alone. If I were new in either, I would choose Dublin and a pint, because a conversation at a bar is the only app that has never crashed. But I would take a fortnight in London first, just to remember what it feels like to be a stranger.

## Conclusion

Romance between two neighbouring cultures is a mutual translation exercise. The Irish hide feeling inside jokes and community. The British hide it inside politeness and distance. Neither is cold. Both are fluent in a language that does not contain the word "date".

If you move between them, bring a sense of humour and a willingness to be teased. You will be fine.

And if that fails, there is always the matchmaking festival in September. Touch the book with both hands.
