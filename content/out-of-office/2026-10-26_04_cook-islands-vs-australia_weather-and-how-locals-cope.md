# 🇨🇰 Cook Islands vs 🇦🇺 Australia: One Shrugs at Cyclones, the Other Panics About Sunscreen Reapplication

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I want to be extremely clear before anyone accuses me of exoticising a fourteenth relocation: the Cook Islands are not a postcard, they are a working economy with a weather system that would flatten most Western cities, and the fact that Rarotonga handles cyclone season with more composure than Sydney handles a forty-degree Tuesday says everything about the gap between weather you've built your culture around and weather you've simply decided to complain about instead.

I've sat out a cyclone warning in Avarua drinking instant coffee someone's auntie insisted on making for the whole street, and I've watched an entire Melbourne office evacuate because the aircon briefly stopped. Let's talk about the difference.

## Do's & Don'ts: Cook Islands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Respect cyclone season (November to April) by actually tracking the Meteorological Service updates, not just vibes | Schedule anything firm during a cyclone watch — "firm plans" is a foreign concept here for good reason |
| Learn that community labour — clearing drains, securing roofs — happens communally before a storm, and you're expected to show up | Panic visibly; panic is considered mildly rude, like raising your voice at a funeral |
| Keep water and basics stocked permanently, not reactively — locals never "run out" because they never let themselves start | Assume "small island" means "fragile" — these communities have survived cyclones for centuries without you |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take "Catastrophic" fire danger ratings with total seriousness — it is not a drill, it is a specific legal category | Underestimate 40°C+ heat just because the air feels "dry" — dry heat still kills, just more quietly |
| Reapply sunscreen like it's a religious obligation, because UV here is genuinely, measurably more dangerous than in most of the Northern Hemisphere | Go for a lunchtime run in January without checking the UV index first, newcomers do this exactly once |
| Stock a go-bag during bushfire season if you're anywhere near bushland — it's standard practice, not paranoia | Treat a smoke haze day as "just a bit hazy, I'll go for a jog" — check air quality indices, they exist for a reason |

### Cook Islands: weather as a shared, practiced inheritance

Rarotonga and the outer islands sit squarely in the South Pacific cyclone belt, and the Cook Islands Meteorological Service doesn't sugar-coat the season — November through April is treated with the same structural seriousness a Londoner treats a tube strike, except with actual stakes. What struck me, relocating from a country that treats bad weather as a personal inconvenience, was how utterly unpanicked the response is. When a watch is issued, the response is coordinated, communal, and almost bureaucratically calm: roofs get checked, loose items get secured, water gets stored, and everyone simply... proceeds, because this has happened before, repeatedly, across generations, and the systems for handling it — both infrastructural and cultural — are inherited, not improvised.

The adjustment for a corporate transferee isn't really about the storms themselves, which are genuinely dangerous and should be taken that way — it's about recalibrating your emotional register to match the room. Visible anxiety reads, locally, as a kind of self-indulgence; the expectation is that you handle the practical steps with the same unhurried competence as everyone else, save your feelings for later, and under no circumstances make the cyclone about you. I watched a newly arrived IT consultant try to organise an emergency evacuation flight during what turned out to be a fairly routine watch, and the eye-rolling he generated from his Rarotongan colleagues lasted considerably longer than the storm did.

### Australia: a continent that treats extreme as default and still finds new ways to be surprised

Australia's weather isn't more dangerous than the Cook Islands' in any objective sense — it's dangerous in a completely different register, mostly heat and fire rather than wind and surge, and it comes with an entire bureaucratic apparatus (the Bureau of Meteorology's heatwave service, state-level fire danger ratings, a "Catastrophic" category that exists precisely because "severe" wasn't scary enough) that locals have, somewhat remarkably, learned to live beside without constant dread. What trips up newcomers, especially from temperate or genuinely cold countries, is the UV index — Australia's ozone geography means sun exposure here is measurably, scientifically worse for your skin than the same number on a thermometer in Europe, and the sunscreen culture (hats indoors at school, "slip slop slap" lodged permanently in the national psyche since the 1980s) exists because the country has, quite literally, the data to justify the paranoia.

Where Australians genuinely do panic, and panic performatively, is heat-related infrastructure failure — an aircon outage, a train delay on a 42-degree day, a backyard barbecue cancelled because of a "total fire ban." This isn't melodrama so much as learned, justified caution: bushfire seasons have killed people and destroyed towns within living memory, and the collective nervous system hasn't forgotten it, even in years when the season turns out mild. The gap between Cook Islands composure and Australian vigilance isn't about who's braver — it's about which disaster each culture has decided deserves active management versus quiet endurance.

### Insurance, generators, and the unglamorous logistics of staying ready

What separates genuine local resilience from tourist-brochure calm, in both places, is the unglamorous infrastructure sitting quietly behind it. In the Cook Islands, households and businesses treat generator ownership, water storage tanks, and basic structural cyclone-proofing as standard, non-optional household equipment rather than emergency luxuries, because the power grid on outer islands especially simply cannot be relied upon to survive every season without interruption, and nobody wants to be the household caught explaining, again, why they didn't bother. Australia's equivalent is almost entirely financial and administrative rather than physical: bushfire and flood insurance premiums have risen sharply in high-risk zones, some insurers have withdrawn from certain postcodes entirely, and a genuinely important, under-discussed part of relocating to bushland-adjacent or flood-prone Australian suburbs is simply confirming, before you sign a lease, that insurance is still available there at all. Two very different economies of risk — one built on physical self-sufficiency, the other on an increasingly strained insurance market — but both are quietly, constantly managing the same underlying fact: extreme weather here isn't an occasional event, it's a structural feature of living in either place. Corporate transferees relocating into either system make the same early mistake, just in reverse: arriving in the Cook Islands assuming insurance will cover what a generator and a water tank should have handled already, or arriving in Australia assuming a property is insurable simply because it's for sale, without checking the postcode against an insurer's current flood or bushfire maps first. Ask the question before you sign anything. Everyone local already has.

## The Verdict

The Cook Islands win on sheer unbothered competence — a cyclone watch handled with the emotional energy of a grocery run is, frankly, aspirational. Australia wins on institutional seriousness — nowhere else have I seen a government fire-danger category ("Catastrophic") land with the gravity of an actual legal instrument, because it is one. If you want weather handled with total calm, go Pacific. If you want weather handled with total, well-resourced, slightly anxious precision, go Australian. Either way, buy the sunscreen before you land, not after.

## Conclusion

Weather, it turns out, is one of the few things a corporate relocation genuinely cannot negotiate around — you either learn the local register of fear and calm or you spend your entire posting out of sync with everyone around you. The Cook Islands will teach you that calm is a skill, practiced over generations. Australia will teach you that vigilance is also a skill, backed by a terrifying amount of institutional data. Learn both, apply selectively, and for heaven's sake reapply the sunscreen.
