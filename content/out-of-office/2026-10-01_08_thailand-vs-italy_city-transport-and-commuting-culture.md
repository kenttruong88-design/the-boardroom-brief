# One Country Commutes by Clinging to a Stranger on a Motorbike. The Other Commutes by Ignoring the Rules Entirely and Hoping for the Best.

🇹🇭🇮🇹 Thailand & Italy
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Few things reveal a country's actual relationship with rules quite like its daily commute. Bangkok has built an entire semi-formal transport economy around motorbike taxis — ranked, numbered, vest-wearing drivers who will take you through gaps in traffic that shouldn't physically exist, and do so with the casual confidence of someone who's done it ten thousand times. Rome has excellent, genuinely well-designed public transport infrastructure that locals treat as a loose set of suggestions, validated tickets being apparently optional and traffic lights being, at best, a conversation starter.

I have sat sideways on a motorbike in Bangkok traffic holding nothing but a shopping bag and quiet faith in a stranger I'd known for four minutes. I have also watched a Roman bus driver run three consecutive red lights while eating a sandwich, one-handed, with total serenity. Both experiences taught me that "rules" and "order" are not, in fact, the same thing.

## Do's & Don'ts: Thailand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use motorbike taxi ranks (vest-wearing drivers only) rather than unmarked bikes for safety and fair pricing | Don't negotiate fares after the ride has started — agree before you get on, every time |
| Combine BTS/MRT for longer stretches with motorbike taxis for the "last mile" — it's the genuinely efficient local method | Don't expect motorbike drivers to know addresses by name — have the location pinned on your phone |
| Hold on properly and keep your weight centred — passengers who lean or fidget make rides notably more dangerous | Don't assume rush hour timing matches Western patterns — Bangkok's worst congestion windows are their own beast |
| Carry small notes for fares — drivers rarely carry much change | Don't wear flowing scarves or loose bags on the side closest to traffic — more than one has been lost to a passing wheel |

## Do's & Don'ts: Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Validate your ticket at the little yellow machine anyway, even though half the carriage visibly won't | Don't assume an unvalidated ticket is risk-free — random inspections do happen and fines are real |
| Learn the specific chaos pattern of your route — Roman traffic has local logic even when it looks lawless | Don't expect pedestrian crossings to guarantee you right of way in practice, whatever the official rule says |
| Walk confidently when crossing — hesitation is what actually gets pedestrians hit, not boldness | Don't rely on bus schedules as anything more than loose, aspirational guidance |
| Keep small change for the metro and buses — ticket machines are not always reliable | Don't be surprised by scooters on sidewalks; it happens constantly and nobody else reacts to it either |

## Thailand: Organised Chaos With a Vest and a Number

Bangkok's motorbike taxi system looks, from the outside, like pure anarchy — drivers weaving between stationary cars, mounting curbs, threading gaps that seem impossible — but it's actually a remarkably structured informal economy. Drivers operate from recognised ranks (win motorsai), wear numbered orange or other coloured vests denoting their registered zone, and operate under a locally understood, if not always officially codified, system of fares and territorial rights. What looks like chaos to a newcomer is, to a regular commuter, a perfectly legible transit system with its own internal rules.

The genuine skill on display is remarkable once you stop flinching and start watching. These drivers read traffic the way a sailor reads weather, anticipating gaps seconds before they open, adjusting speed and lean angle with an instinctive precision that comes from doing this same commute hundreds of times a week. Combined with Bangkok's BTS Skytrain and MRT metro — clean, efficient, startlingly punctual — the full commuting toolkit becomes: take the train for the long distance, hop on a motorbike for the "last mile" that would otherwise mean standing in gridlocked traffic for twenty minutes.

What catches newcomers out is less the danger, which is real but manageable with the right precautions, and more the etiquette: agreeing a fare before departure, not during; sitting still and centred rather than gripping and leaning dramatically; understanding that your driver's confidence is earned competence, not recklessness, even when every instinct screams otherwise as you clear a gap between two buses with centimetres to spare.

## Italy: Infrastructure That Works Despite Itself

Rome's public transport system is, on paper, perfectly reasonable: an extensive bus network, two metro lines, trams, and a ticketing system that's clear enough on a map. In practice, it operates on a kind of negotiated informality that would baffle a Swiss transport planner. Ticket validation at those little yellow machines is, officially, mandatory; unofficially, a meaningful percentage of any given bus simply doesn't bother, treating the machine as decorative rather than functional, right up until an inspector boards and fines ripple through the carriage with theatrical indignation from everyone caught.

Driving culture compounds the effect. Traffic lights are respected with a looseness that reads, to anyone from a more rule-literal country, as borderline fictional — red lights get a rolling stop at best, scooters treat sidewalks as a perfectly reasonable overflow lane, and pedestrian crossings function more as a suggestion of where crossing might be socially acceptable than any kind of guarantee. And yet, remarkably, the system works. Accidents happen, certainly, but the whole chaotic performance has its own internal logic, built on decades of mutual, unspoken calibration between drivers, pedestrians, and scooter riders who've all silently agreed on how much risk everyone's comfortable sharing.

The real skill a newcomer has to learn isn't following Roman traffic rules — it's learning to read Roman traffic behaviour, which is a different and more useful skill entirely. Hesitating at a crossing, waiting for an official "right of way" that functionally doesn't arrive, is what actually gets pedestrians hit; walking with calm, visible intent is what gets you across safely, because it gives drivers a predictable variable to navigate around.

## The Verdict

Thailand's commuting chaos is a structured informal system with real rules, just not written ones. Italy's commuting chaos is a formal system that locals have quietly agreed to treat as flexible. Both demand the same core skill from a newcomer: stop looking for the rulebook and start reading the actual behaviour of the people around you, because that behaviour, not the posted signage, is the real transit system in either city.

I'm handing this one to Thailand, on the strength of its motorbike taxi drivers alone — a genuinely elegant solution to urban gridlock, executed with a level of skill that deserves to be called a profession rather than a hustle, dressed in a numbered vest nobody in Rome has bothered to invent an equivalent for.

## Conclusion

What both cities ultimately teach you is that order isn't the absence of chaos; it's chaos that everyone involved has quietly agreed to interpret the same way. Bangkok's motorbike drivers and Rome's scooter riders are, in their own fashion, both masters of a system that looks lawless from the outside and runs on a dense, unwritten rulebook from the inside. Arrive expecting Western-style compliance and you'll be perpetually alarmed. Arrive willing to read the room instead of the signage, and you'll commute like a local within a fortnight, slightly terrified the entire time, exactly as intended.
