# One Country Negotiates Its Bus Fare Mid-Ride. The Other Will Silently Judge Your Cycling Posture.

🇰🇬 Kyrgyzstan + 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Commuting is the one part of daily life tourists never see and expats never stop talking about, because it's the purest distillation of how a country actually functions when nobody's performing for visitors. Kyrgyzstan runs its cities on marshrutkas — battered Soviet-era minibuses that operate on a route system best described as "vibes-based" — while the Netherlands has engineered bicycle infrastructure so flawless it makes every other cycling nation on Earth look like it's still figuring out where to put a curb.

I've hung out the open door of a Bishkek marshrutka going what felt like inadvisable speeds down a mountain road while the driver argued with a passenger about correct change, and I've been overtaken by a Dutch grandmother on a bakfiets carrying three children, a dog, and what appeared to be a wardrobe. Both experiences taught me the same lesson: never assume you understand a country's transport system until it has personally humiliated you at least once.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇰🇬 Kyrgyzstan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Have small bills ready — drivers rarely make change for large notes | Expect a fixed schedule; marshrutkas leave when they're full, not on time |
| Shout your stop clearly in advance ("Ostanovka!") | Assume the marked route number means a fixed route — it flexes |
| Hold on. Always hold on | Sit near the door if you get carsick — it's also the exhaust seat |
| Flag it down like a taxi — they stop almost anywhere | Expect personal space; marshrutkas fill well past "capacity" |

**🇳🇱 Netherlands**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Signal with your arm before turning, every single time | Walk in the bike lane — it is not a wide pavement, it is a weapons lane |
| Lock your bike with two locks; theft is endemic | Cycle slowly in the fast lane of a bike path — you will be tutted at, hard |
| Learn the hand signal for stopping | Assume trams will stop for you just because you're looking at your phone |
| Buy a cheap secondhand bike — new ones vanish within weeks | Expect sympathy after your first crash. Everyone's had one. Cycle on |

## Kyrgyzstan: Chaos That Somehow Always Arrives

Bishkek's marshrutka network is, on paper, a public transport disaster. There is no app that reliably tells you when one is coming, because "when it's coming" is a philosophical question rather than a scheduling one. Routes are numbered but negotiable, drivers double as debt collectors when it comes to fares, and the vehicles themselves are frequently older than the concept of Kyrgyzstan as an independent state. And yet — somehow, always — you get where you're going, usually for the equivalent of about twenty pence, usually faster than you expected, and usually having had at least one conversation with a stranger you didn't ask for.

The etiquette here is unwritten but universally understood by locals: you pass your fare forward hand-to-hand through the crowd to the driver, and your change comes back the same way, through however many strangers are between you and the front. Nobody pockets it. Nobody even seems tempted to. It's a small, everyday act of trust that would be unthinkable on a rush-hour tube carriage, and it says more about Kyrgyz civic culture than any museum could.

Taxis exist too, informally — flag down basically any car and, for the right price, it becomes a taxi. This system runs entirely on reputation and mutual risk tolerance, and while it works fine for locals who know the going rate, tourists get charged double on principle. Learn the fare in advance from a local, or accept the tourist tax as the cost of the adventure. Either way, don't expect Uber-level predictability, because that's not what you signed up for by coming here.

## Netherlands: Cycling As Blood Sport

The Netherlands has built the most sophisticated cycling infrastructure on the planet, and it uses that infrastructure to make you feel, within about ninety seconds of arrival, like an incompetent toddler. Separated bike lanes, dedicated bike traffic lights, roundabouts designed around cyclist right-of-way — it's an engineering marvel. It is also a merciless social hierarchy in which cargo-bike mums, teenagers on fixed-gears, and eighty-year-olds on upright omafietsen all move at a velocity and confidence that instantly exposes any tourist who's rented a bike thinking this would be "a leisurely way to see the city."

There is no leisure here. The bike lane is a commuting artery, and dawdling in it while filming a canal on your phone is treated with the same disdain as parking your car in the middle lane of a motorway. Dutch cyclists don't wear helmets, don't signal apologetically, and absolutely will not slow down for you — they expect you to have already worked out where you're going, because they did that themselves around age four.

Bike theft is the other national sport nobody warns you about. Budget for it. Buy an ugly, used bike specifically so its theft doesn't wound you emotionally or financially, lock it with two separate locks to two separate objects, and accept that you will eventually lose it anyway. It's basically a Dutch rite of passage, filed somewhere between learning to say "gezellig" and being tutted at for holding up a queue.

## The Verdict

Kyrgyzstan's transport system runs on human trust and low expectations; the Netherlands' runs on rigid infrastructure and zero patience. One will occasionally strand you on a mountain pass, the other will occasionally clip your ankle with a cargo bike doing 30 km/h. I'd rather be stranded — at least in Bishkek someone will strike up a conversation while you wait. In Amsterdam, they'll just ring their bell at you, twice, and expect you to have already moved.

## Conclusion

There's a version of "efficient" that means everything runs like clockwork, and there's a version that means everyone just quietly knows what to do even when nothing runs like clockwork at all. Kyrgyzstan is the second kind — beautifully improvised chaos held together by shared understanding. The Netherlands is the first kind, engineered within an inch of its life and enforced by mild but unrelenting social pressure. Neither will coddle you. Learn the local rules fast, in both places, or spend your whole trip either stranded on a mountainside or nursing a bruised shin from a bakfiets you never even saw coming.
