# New Zealanders Dress for Winter Indoors; Canadians Dress for Winter Outdoors and Heat the House Like a Sauna

🇳🇿 New Zealand vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two theories of winter. In Canada, winter is an enemy to be defeated with insulation, triple glazing, central heating, a boot room and a stern attitude. In New Zealand, winter is a mild inconvenience to be endured with a jumper, a cup of tea and a stubborn conviction that the house is not that cold, and that you, personally, are the problem.

I have shivered in both, and what astonishes me is that the colder country has the warmer homes. A Canadian January can drop to minus thirty, yet the inside of a Canadian house is a tropical fruit bowl. A New Zealand July might hover at ten degrees above zero outside, and indoors it is the same, plus damp. It is the greatest unsolved scandal of the temperate world, and nobody there seems to mind. In fact, they are almost proud of it.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇳🇿 New Zealand ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a dehumidifier and use it every day | Leave wet washing hanging in a closed room |
| Layer up with merino and wool-lined bedding | Rely on a single small heater and a stoic expression |
| Choose the sunniest rental you can find | Trust a landlord who says it is warm in winter |
| Take a sunny holiday in July if you can | Complain to a Kiwi that it is cold indoors |

| 🇨🇦 Canada ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in a proper winter coat, boots and gloves | Go out in sneakers because the sun is shining |
| Keep the heating on and the humidity right | Leave your car without a block heater at minus thirty |
| Learn to walk like a penguin on ice | Stride confidently across a frozen car park |
| Accept that a winter hobby is mandatory | Turn down a skating invitation out of sheer pride |

## 🇳🇿 New Zealand

New Zealand's climate is gentle, damp and moderate, and its houses are not designed for it. An American poster on the Expat.com New Zealand thread was surprised that homes had little insulation and no central heating, and said that in winter people heated the lounge, usually with a fireplace, and used electric heaters in other rooms. Snow, she added, was extremely rare in the North Island, with perhaps three or four falls in ten years, melting by the next morning. A fellow American north of Auckland found that a couple of electric heaters kept her comfortable, but noticed that many locals preferred coats and layers indoors to running heaters, a habit she described as odd. She runs a dehumidifier, pays high power bills and books a sunny holiday every June or July.

NZ Herald columnist Ben Mack, writing an immigrant's guide to surviving a New Zealand winter, calls the country's homes notoriously cold and damp, advises buying a dehumidifier and says Kiwis complain about the weather as much as they do about Auckland traffic. A Canadian poster on the same Expat.com thread agreed that most housing is uninsulated and that zero to ten degrees feels uncomfortable indoors, and recommended merino layers and wool-lined duvets. These are the voices of people who have lived through the experience and not seen the funny side.

The most entertaining testimony comes from a May 2026 essay in The Spinoff by an American-born man who has lived in New Zealand for thirty years. He turns the heating down before his Kiwi friends visit because they say his house is too warm, and once carried a thermometer into a cinema and recorded 19 degrees. He writes that he shivers below 20 degrees indoors, even in three layers, while locals eat outside in shorts at 8 degrees. The WHO, he notes, has recommended a minimum indoor temperature of 18 degrees for nearly half a century, and Otago research cited in the piece found average New Zealand winter indoor temperatures around 16. It is a nation conducting a long, brave experiment with hypothermia, and it is losing.

There is a cultural footnote to the weather story, which is the way each country talks about it. New Zealanders discuss the weather with a lively, self-deprecating relish, treating a wet week as a shared joke and a fine day as a gift to be enjoyed at once. Canadians discuss it with grim professionalism, comparing windchills as though they were sporting statistics. Both countries are, in their way, bonded by their climate, and the weather is the most reliable conversation starter in either. If you can talk about the forecast with conviction, you can talk to anyone.

## 🇨🇦 Canada

Canada's relationship with winter is serious, practical and quietly proud. The country has a genuine cold: cities in the prairies see long stretches of minus twenty and below, and even Toronto and Vancouver have their moments. The result is a national infrastructure of defiance. Houses are insulated to within an inch of their lives, doors have double entrances to trap the heat, boot rooms exist for the sole purpose of removing snow, and cars have plugs. A Canadian winter is not an experience you enjoy. It is one you manage, like a mortgage.

What I admire most is the competence. You will see a Canadian child in a snowsuit calmly sledging down a hill at minus fifteen, and a Canadian grandmother shovelling a driveway like it is a hobby. The heating is on, the humidity is managed, the pavements are salted, and the buses run. And then the people turn it into a pleasure: skating on frozen canals, cross-country skiing, hot chocolate in a cabin, and the spring thaw as a collective religious experience. The Spinoff essay claims that a Vancouver bylaw says rental living spaces must be kept at 22 degrees, which I cannot verify, but it fits the mood: Canada assumes you should be warm indoors, and builds accordingly.

The price is the extremity. A Canadian winter is long, dark, expensive and exhausting, and a newcomer used to temperate climates may find the first January a shock. The cold is real, and so are the heating bills. But once you have the right coat and the right boots, you realise that the only thing between you and the outdoors is a good layering system and a willing spirit. Canadians are not tougher than anyone else. They just have better equipment and an agreed national position that complaining about the cold is a competitive sport, to be won on stoicism.

The seasons themselves are different in character. New Zealand's year is a long, mild sequence, with no extremes to speak of, in which the main hazard is that summer is cooler than expected and winter is wetter than advertised. Canada's year is a drama in four acts, with a violent winter, a muddy spring, a short, golden summer and an autumn so vivid that it has become a national brand. If you like your weather as a story, with plot twists and a happy ending, Canada will give you one. If you like it as background music, New Zealand has a very pleasant playlist.

## The Verdict

Canada wins, on a technicality, and the technicality is the heating. A cold country that keeps you warm is a better proposition than a mild country that lets you shiver. I would rather endure minus thirty outdoors and plus twenty-two indoors than live in a place where the thermometer reads sixteen over my breakfast, and the person next to me is proud of it.

But New Zealand has its own advantages: no one is going to freeze to death on the way to work, the sun is generous, the winters are short, and nobody has ever needed a block heater. If you want to be comfortable, choose Canada. If you want to be rained on gently in a beautiful place and told to put on another jumper, New Zealand is a charming compromise. I know where I would spend my January, and it has a boot room.

## Conclusion

New Zealand teaches you to wear a jumper indoors. Canada teaches you to wear a coat outdoors and nothing at all in the living room. Either way, you will have strong opinions about heating by the end of your first winter, and a surprising loyalty to a particular brand of thermal underwear. My advice is to rent the sunniest flat you can find, buy a dehumidifier, and never accept the argument that it is not cold. It is cold. Someone just has to say it.
