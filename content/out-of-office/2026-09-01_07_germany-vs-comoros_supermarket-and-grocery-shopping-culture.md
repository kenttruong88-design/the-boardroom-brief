# Two Ways to Buy an Onion

🇩🇪 Germany vs 🇰🇲 Comoros — Supermarket and Grocery Shopping Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Germany has turned grocery shopping into a small, silent test of competence — bag your own items fast enough or feel the quiet judgment of the entire queue behind you. The Comoros has no such test, mostly because grocery shopping there is still, gloriously, a social occasion rather than a logistics exercise. One country optimises for efficiency to the point of minor psychological warfare. The other hasn't fully industrialised the errand yet, and honestly, good for it.

I have been silently but unmistakably judged by a German cashier for fumbling my reusable bags at a Rewe checkout, and I have spent forty unhurried minutes at Moroni's central market being talked into buying spices I didn't need by a vendor who simply refused to let the conversation end. Only one of those experiences left me sweating.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Germany**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your own bags — and a €1 coin for the shopping cart deposit | Expect the cashier to wait patiently while you slowly pack your bags |
| Weigh your own produce at the scale before checkout, where required | Chat with the cashier mid-scan — it disrupts the flow, and they will notice |
| Learn the difference between Aldi/Lidl discount culture and Edeka/Rewe full-service | Assume Sunday shopping is an option — nearly everything is closed |
| Have cash or card ready before your items finish scanning | Dawdle at the conveyor belt — the queue's patience has a hard limit |

**Comoros**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget real time for the central market — haggling is part of the transaction | Expect fixed, posted prices the way you would at a supermarket back home |
| Learn basic Comorian or French market phrases — it smooths every interaction | Assume every island has the same shopping infrastructure as Grande Comore |
| Buy fresh — fish, produce, and spices move fast and locally | Rely entirely on imported supermarket goods — prices and stock vary wildly |
| Build a relationship with a regular vendor — it pays off in trust and quality | Rush the interaction — a hurried shopper reads as a rude one |

## Germany: Speed as a Moral Virtue

German supermarket culture operates on an unspoken but universally enforced code: you will pack your own groceries, you will do it fast, and you will not hold up the line contemplating whether you really need the second block of cheese. Cashiers scan items at a pace that borders on aggressive, and the entire system assumes you've already mentally rehearsed your bagging strategy before your items even hit the belt. Foreigners who haven't been warned about this experience their first proper Rewe or Edeka checkout as something between a pop quiz and a minor public shaming.

The discount chains — Aldi and Lidl chief among them — take this efficiency further still, stripping out branding, packaging flourishes, and customer hand-holding in favour of raw price competitiveness that has made Germany one of Europe's most discount-friendly grocery markets. Full-service chains like Edeka and Rewe exist a notch up in comfort and selection, but the underlying cultural expectation — move quickly, know what you want, don't chat unnecessarily at checkout — holds across the board. It's not rudeness. It's simply that efficiency has been quietly enshrined as a form of mutual respect: wasting the next person's time is the actual social violation here, not skipping small talk.

Then there's the trolley deposit coin, the Sunday closures that catch every newcomer at least once, and the produce scales scattered through the fresh section that you're expected to use yourself before checkout, correctly, without assistance. None of it is explained anywhere. All of it is assumed. Get it wrong once — forget your coin, hold up the queue, show up on a Sunday craving a specific ingredient — and you'll learn the rules the way every expat in Germany eventually does: through mild, silent, extremely effective social pressure.

## Comoros: Groceries as a Conversation

Grocery shopping in the Comoros runs on an entirely different logic, one where the transaction is inseparable from the relationship around it. Moroni's central market remains the genuine heart of daily food shopping for most residents — an immersive sprawl of spices, tropical fruit, fresh fish, and colourful fabric stalls where haggling isn't an awkward negotiation tactic but simply how prices get set, full stop. Fixed pricing exists mostly at the handful of supermarkets serving the wealthier and expat population, and even there, stock can vary considerably depending on what's recently arrived by ship.

What surprises newcomers most is the sheer sociability baked into the errand. A trip to buy fish or produce is rarely a fast, transactional affair — vendors chat, negotiate with visible enjoyment, and treat a returning customer with a warmth that quickly tips into genuine familiarity. Massiwa Bazar and similar local grocery spots in Moroni offer something closer to a cultural immersion than a shopping trip, staples kept affordable through market pricing that responds to local conditions rather than a corporate pricing algorithm somewhere in Frankfurt.

The trade-off, and it's a real one, is convenience. Imported goods can be inconsistent, prices for anything shipped in from abroad fluctuate with what's actually made it to the islands that month, and outside Grande Comore, the shopping infrastructure thins out considerably. But what the Comoros lacks in supermarket predictability, it makes up for in something Germany's system has deliberately engineered out entirely: the errand as a genuine, unhurried human interaction, where rushing the exchange is the actual faux pas.

## The Verdict

Depends entirely on what you're optimising for, and I refuse to pretend otherwise. If your life runs on a tight schedule and you resent errands eating into your day, Germany's ruthless efficiency is a genuine gift once you learn the unwritten rules. But if grocery shopping has become, for you, just another soulless chore to minimise, spend one unhurried afternoon at Moroni's market and watch that assumption quietly fall apart. I'll take the conversation over the conveyor belt, every time — though I'll admit, I've never once been publicly judged for slow bagging in Moroni, and that alone counts for something.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *TheLocal.de — paraphrased forum comment: a newly arrived American described the specific dread of hearing the beeps speed up while she was still fumbling with reusable bags, "like the scanner itself was judging me."*

> *Quora — an expat in Moroni paraphrased: he said his first market trip took nearly two hours because he kept getting pulled into conversations he didn't know how to politely exit, and eventually stopped trying to.*

> *Expat.com forum, Comoros thread — paraphrased: a longtime resident advised building a relationship with one or two regular vendors early on, saying "the prices get fairer the moment they recognise your face."*

## Conclusion

Two islands, two philosophies, one onion. Germany has engineered the errand down to its most efficient possible form and expects you to keep up without complaint. The Comoros hasn't industrialised it at all, and treats that as a feature rather than a gap. Learn the rules of whichever one you land in — fast or slow, silent or chatty — because showing up with the wrong instincts will mark you as an outsider faster than any accent ever could.
