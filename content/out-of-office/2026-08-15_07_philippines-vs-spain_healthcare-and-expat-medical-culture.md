# In Manila You Pay Cash Upfront or You Don't Get Seen, and in Madrid You Wait Six Months for an Appointment You'll Barely Remember Booking

🇵🇭 Philippines 🇪🇸 Spain
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Healthcare abroad is the moment every expat's romantic notions about "adventure" collide with the much less romantic reality of needing a doctor at 2am in a country whose system you don't understand. The Philippines runs largely on a cash-and-insurance model where private hospitals are excellent, fast, and require a card on file before they'll even glance at your chart. Spain runs on a public system so comprehensive and so free at the point of use that Spaniards genuinely cannot comprehend why anyone would ever go bankrupt over a hospital bill — right up until you try to book a non-urgent specialist appointment and discover "free" and "fast" were never the same promise.

I've been hospitalised in both countries, for entirely unglamorous reasons I won't detail here, and the experiences could not have been more different. Manila got me seen, scanned, and medicated within ninety minutes, for a price that made me wince before I'd even left the building. Madrid got me the same care, eventually, for nothing at all — but "eventually" did a tremendous amount of heavy lifting in that sentence.

## Do's & Don'ts: Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comprehensive private health insurance before you need it | Assume PhilHealth alone covers a private hospital stay in full |
| Carry cash or a card for hospital deposits — they're requested upfront | Expect a public hospital to move at private hospital speed |
| Research hospital accreditation in your specific city beforehand | Wait until an emergency to learn which hospital is actually good |

## Do's & Don'ts: Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for your tarjeta sanitaria (health card) as soon as you're a resident | Expect a routine specialist referral to move quickly through the public system |
| Consider private insurance (seguro privado) for speed on non-urgent issues | Skip your padrón (local registration) — it's required to access public healthcare |
| Use urgencias (A&E) for genuine emergencies — it's fast and excellent | Assume every region's public wait times are identical — they vary hugely |

## Philippines: Excellent Care, As Long As You Can Pay for It Immediately

Philippine private healthcare, particularly in Manila's better hospitals — St. Luke's, Makati Medical Center, Asian Hospital — is genuinely excellent, staffed by doctors trained to a standard that rivals anywhere I've been treated, many of them fluent in English and trained partly abroad. What catches newcomers off guard isn't the quality, it's the transaction: private hospitals here routinely require a cash or card deposit before treatment begins, even in urgent situations, and settling the final bill before discharge is standard practice, not an exception reserved for elective procedures.

PhilHealth, the national insurance scheme, exists and covers a meaningful portion of costs for both citizens and eligible foreign residents, but it was never designed to make private hospital care free — it caps reimbursements well below what private facilities actually charge, meaning most expats layer PhilHealth under a private international insurance policy just to avoid a bill that could genuinely bankrupt them. I learned this the expensive way, assuming government coverage would behave the way it does in countries with fuller universal systems. It does not.

Public hospitals exist as the actual safety net for the majority of Filipinos, and they carry the burden accordingly — overcrowding, long waits, and resource strain are real and well documented, especially in Metro Manila. What's remarkable, and what nobody quite prepares you for, is the family-centred nature of Filipino hospital culture: patients are rarely alone, ever, with extended family members camping in waiting rooms, bringing food, and taking rotating shifts at the bedside in a display of collective care that puts most Western hospital visiting-hour policies to shame. Get sick in the Philippines and you will, structurally, never be alone in it, which is more than I can say for the sterile solo hospital stays I've had elsewhere.

## Spain: Free, Excellent, and Not in Any Particular Hurry

Spain's Sistema Nacional de Salud is, on paper and largely in practice, one of the best public healthcare systems in the world — universal, free at the point of use for registered residents, and staffed by doctors as skilled as any private alternative. Register your padrón, get your tarjeta sanitaria, and you have access to a GP, a local health centre, and full hospital care without ever seeing a bill. For genuine emergencies, urgencias delivers fast, high-quality care — I've been triaged and treated for an urgent issue within the hour, at zero cost, and left genuinely stunned by the efficiency.

The catch, and it is a significant one, arrives the moment your need is non-urgent. Referrals to specialists through the public system can take months — routine dermatology or orthopaedic appointments stretching to four, five, six months in some regions isn't a horror story, it's a documented and widely tolerated reality, one Spaniards themselves grumble about constantly while remaining fiercely, almost patriotically defensive of the system's overall value. Wait times vary dramatically by region too, with Madrid and Catalonia generally faster than more rural autonomous communities, a fact that surprises newcomers who assume "national" health service means uniform national wait times.

What most expats eventually do, and what locals with the means also increasingly do, is take out relatively affordable private insurance — seguro privado — specifically to bypass the public system's queue for non-urgent care while keeping the public system as a free, excellent backstop for anything serious. This dual-track approach isn't seen as opting out of solidarity, it's seen as pragmatic, and even ardent defenders of the public system often carry a private policy quietly in their back pocket. Spain's healthcare will never bankrupt you. It will, occasionally, test the outer limits of your patience.

## The Verdict

The Philippines wins on speed and personal attention, provided your wallet or your insurer can keep up — you'll be seen fast, cared for warmly, and billed accordingly. Spain wins on universal access and long-term financial peace of mind, provided you're not in a hurry for anything short of an actual emergency. If you need care today and can pay for it, Manila delivers. If you need care eventually and would rather never see an invoice, Madrid delivers — just don't ask when.

## Conclusion

Healthcare abroad forces a very unromantic question onto every expat eventually: would you rather pay and wait less, or pay nothing and wait more? The Philippines answers with cash-forward efficiency and a family gathered at your bedside. Spain answers with universal coverage and a patience the entire country seems to have collectively agreed to practice. Get insurance appropriate to wherever you land, understand the system before you need it in an emergency, and stop assuming your home country's model — whatever it was — travels with you. It never does.
