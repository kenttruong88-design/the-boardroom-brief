# One Country Queues So Quietly You'll Wonder If Anyone's Actually There. The Other Treats a Queue as a Suggestion, Not a Rule.

🇷🇴 Romania · 🇧🇪 Belgium

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Public behaviour is a country's unwritten constitution, the set of rules nobody teaches you and everybody enforces, usually with a look rather than a word, and few pairs illustrate the range quite like Belgium's near-monastic restraint and Romania's far more elastic relationship with personal space, volume, and the sanctity of the queue.

Belgium will make you feel, within about four days, that you are being slightly too loud simply by existing. Romania will make you feel, within about four minutes of attempting to board a tram politely, that queueing is a belief system you hold alone, and nobody else in Bucharest has signed up for it.

## Do's & Don'ts: Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Lower your voice on public transport by about 30% from what feels natural | Have a loud phone call on the train; it will earn you real, visible disapproval |
| Queue properly and silently; it is treated as a genuine social contract | Skip a queue even by accident — Belgians notice and remember, quietly |
| Greet shopkeepers with a proper "bonjour" or "goedemorgen" before anything else | Assume French works everywhere; Flanders will expect Dutch, politely but firmly |
| Give wide personal space on escalators, platforms, and pavements alike | Stand too close in a queue; Belgian personal space runs noticeably larger than you'd guess |

## Do's & Don'ts: Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stay alert and claim your space firmly when boarding crowded trams or buses | Expect a formal, single-file queue to hold on its own without active participation |
| Match the conversational volume around you rather than apologising for it | Take mild shoving on public transport personally; it's rarely meant that way |
| Hold doors and give up seats for elders — this norm is taken seriously | Assume a "line" at a kiosk or pharmacy is strictly first-come, first-served |
| Speak directly and expect directness back; it's efficient, not rude | Mistake Romanian bluntness for hostility; it almost never is |

## Belgium: Quiet as a Civic Virtue

Belgium's public behaviour code is subtle enough that most tourists miss it entirely and most expats only learn it through the specific, mortifying experience of being the loudest person on a quiet train carriage and realising it three stops too late. The volume baseline on Belgian public transport sits remarkably low — conversations are hushed, phone calls are rare and apologetic when they do happen, and the collective silence isn't awkward so much as actively maintained, a shared agreement that public space is not the place for your personal noise.

Queueing here isn't merely practiced, it's treated with something close to moral seriousness. Jump a queue in Belgium, even accidentally, even as a confused newcomer who genuinely didn't see where the line started, and you'll get a reaction — not aggressive, Belgians rarely raise their voice over this, but a very pointed, very visible silence, a look, perhaps a quiet word, that communicates precisely how out of order your behaviour was. The linguistic divide between Flanders, Wallonia, and Brussels adds its own layer of social navigation: greeting someone in the wrong language isn't catastrophic, but defaulting to French in a Flemish town, assuming it'll be fine because it usually is in Brussels, reads as a specific kind of carelessness that locals absolutely register.

Personal space runs generously large by most European standards — Belgians will position themselves on an escalator, a platform, a pavement with a noticeable buffer that feels almost standoffish until you realise it's simply the agreed-upon comfort radius here, consistently applied regardless of how crowded the space actually is. The overall effect is a public realm that runs on restraint: speak less, stand further apart, wait your proper turn, and the city rewards you with a kind of calm, orderly predictability that, once you've adjusted to it, makes almost everywhere else feel faintly feral by comparison.

## Romania: Directness, Elbows, and a Looser Relationship With the Line

Romania's public behaviour norms run on an entirely different set of assumptions, chief among them that a queue is a description of where people currently are, not a binding contract about the order in which they'll be served. Board a crowded tram in Bucharest during rush hour and you will understand within seconds that the polite British instinct to wait your turn, hang back, let others go first, simply does not function here — not because Romanians are rude, but because the system assumes active participation, a bit of decisive elbow and forward motion, as the actual mechanism by which a crowded bus gets boarded at all. Stand back too politely and you'll simply watch the bus fill without you, repeatedly, while wondering what you're doing wrong.

Conversational volume runs noticeably higher than in Western Europe generally, and this isn't rudeness either — it's simply a more expressive, more direct culture, where disagreement gets voiced rather than suppressed, and a loud conversation on a tram is just two people having a normal conversation at a normal Romanian volume, not an imposition on anyone else's peace. Directness extends to feedback and criticism too; a Romanian colleague will tell you plainly that your idea has a problem, without the cushioning and caveats a Western European or North American office might expect, and newcomers who read this bluntness as hostility are consistently, pleasantly surprised to discover it almost never is — it's efficiency, not animosity.

Where Romania does hold a real, firmly enforced social code is around respect for elders — giving up a seat, holding a door, letting an older person go first at a till, these aren't optional niceties here, they're taken seriously and visibly enforced by onlookers if someone fails to observe them. It's a useful corrective for anyone tempted to read the tram-boarding chaos as a culture with no rules at all: Romania has plenty of rules, they're simply not the rules a Belgian newcomer would expect, and they're weighted toward different values entirely — respect for age over orderly process, directness over diplomatic cushioning.

## The Verdict

Belgium wins on predictability and calm — if you value quiet, order, and a reliably enforced queue, nowhere in this comparison beats it. Romania wins on honesty and warmth underneath the chaos — the bluntness is real, the elbow-throwing on the tram is real, but so is the seriousness with which an elderly stranger is looked after, every single time, without exception.

Give me Romania's loud, direct, occasionally elbow-first honesty over Belgium's beautifully maintained silence. At least in Bucharest, nobody's quietly judging you behind a polite, unreadable Belgian face while saying nothing at all.

## Conclusion

Belgium and Romania sit at genuinely opposite ends of the same spectrum — one built around restraint, quiet, and procedural fairness, the other around directness, active participation, and loudly expressed warmth once you're inside the right relationship. Arrive in Brussels expecting Bucharest's energy and you'll be the problem on the train. Arrive in Bucharest expecting Brussels' orderly queue and you'll simply never board the tram. Learn the actual local rhythm, not the one you brought with you, and both cities will make considerably more sense within a fortnight.
