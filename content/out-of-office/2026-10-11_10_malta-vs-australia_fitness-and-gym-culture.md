# Malta Will Get You Fit By Accident, Mostly Via Swimming and Stairs; Australia Will Get You Fit on Purpose, Loudly, Before 6am

🇲🇹 Malta vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Malta's approach to fitness is almost accidental — a small, hilly, sun-soaked island where walking to the shop involves actual elevation gain, and swimming in genuinely spectacular water is simply what you do on a hot afternoon, not a scheduled workout. Australia's approach to fitness is the opposite: deliberate, early, loud, and organised into an entire subculture of bootcamps, ocean swims, and group training sessions that start at an hour most of humanity considers a personal attack.

I have gotten a better leg workout climbing Valletta's steps to buy bread than I have in several actual gyms. I have also been personally, aggressively recruited into a 5:45am beach bootcamp in Bondi by a stranger named Shaz who would not, under any circumstances, accept "no" as a complete sentence.

## Do's & Don'ts: Malta

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Embrace swimming as the default fitness activity — the water here is clean, warm, and genuinely gorgeous | Expect a huge 24-hour gym scene; Malta's gym culture is real but far smaller and more boutique than you'd assume |
| Accept walking the island's hills and steps as legitimate daily exercise; Valletta alone is a workout | Underestimate the summer heat during outdoor training; shift sessions earlier or later, like locals do |
| Try a CrossFit box if that's your thing — Malta's scene is small, tight-knit, and surprisingly serious | Assume fitness culture here mirrors big-city gym chains; much of it is smaller, personal, word-of-mouth based |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Try an outdoor group bootcamp or beach run club — it's a genuinely major way locals socialise and train | Be shocked by the early start times; 5:30-6am outdoor sessions beating the heat are completely normal here |
| Learn to swim properly if you don't already — ocean swimming and surf lifesaving culture run deep here | Swim between the flags carelessly or ignore surf conditions; Australian beaches are genuinely more dangerous than they look |
| Join a local gym chain or community sports club; it's one of the fastest ways to build a real social circle | Assume all training happens indoors; a huge proportion of Australian fitness culture is deliberately, defiantly outdoors |

## Malta: Fitness Woven Into the Island, Not Scheduled On Top of It

Malta's relationship with fitness is shaped entirely by its geography and size — a small, dense, hilly archipelago where daily life already involves a level of incidental physical activity that bigger, flatter countries have to manufacture deliberately. Walking Valletta's steep, stepped streets, cycling or walking between tightly packed towns, and swimming in some of the clearest water in the Mediterranean aren't fitness choices here so much as simply how life on the island works, which produces a population that's reasonably active without necessarily thinking of itself as "into fitness" in the structured, branded sense.

Formal gym culture exists, but it's notably smaller-scale and more personal than what newcomers from bigger countries expect — fewer giant 24-hour chain gyms, more boutique studios and genuinely tight-knit communities where the same faces show up to the same sessions, and owners actually know your name. Malta's small but surprisingly serious CrossFit scene is a good example — CrossFit Games' own coverage of "the first box on the island" describes a community that's modest in size but notably committed, the kind of scene where everyone trains together because there simply aren't enough alternative boxes to spread people thin.

Swimming deserves special mention as the island's actual default fitness activity. The water is warm enough for a huge portion of the year to make open-water swimming a genuinely viable, pleasant daily habit rather than a cold-tolerance challenge, and plenty of Maltese residents who'd never set foot in a gym swim regularly, simply because the sea is right there and it's wonderful.

## Australia: Fitness as National Identity, Delivered Loudly at Dawn

Australian fitness culture operates on an entirely different scale and philosophy: deliberate, organised, intensely social, and overwhelmingly oriented toward getting outdoors before the heat makes it miserable. The early-morning bootcamp — groups of people doing burpees on a beach or in a park at 5:30am, often led by an instructor with a headset microphone and a frightening amount of enthusiasm for that hour — is a genuine, widespread cultural institution, not a niche fitness trend, and it functions as much as a social and dating scene as an actual workout.

Ocean swimming and surf culture run genuinely deep here, tied closely to Australia's broader national identity and its (entirely justified) pride in its coastline. Surf lifesaving clubs are serious, longstanding community institutions, not just safety services, and learning to read surf conditions and swim confidently between the flags is treated as close to a basic life skill, especially for anyone planning to live near a coast. SBS's own Cultural Atlas notes on Maltese-Australian communities reflect an interesting cross-pollination here too — Maltese migrants to Australia, already comfortable swimmers from home, often integrate easily into this beach-and-ocean-centred fitness culture, finding it less foreign than other aspects of Australian life.

What's most striking to newcomers, though, is the sheer social weight fitness carries in Australia. Gym memberships, running clubs, and group training sessions function as primary social infrastructure in a way that rivals or exceeds the pub or the workplace — turning up to the same 6am session three times a week is, genuinely, one of the fastest ways to build an actual friend group in a new Australian city, which explains why Shaz from Bondi recruits so aggressively. She's not just building fitness. She's building a community, one reluctant burpee at a time.

Even the vocabulary differs tellingly. Maltese residents rarely describe swimming or walking the island's hills as "working out" at all — it's simply called "going to the sea" or "popping to the shop," framed as errand rather than exercise. Australians, by contrast, have an entire specific vocabulary for organised training — "doing a session," "smashing a PB," "getting amongst it" — language that frames fitness explicitly as achievement, performance, and identity, rarely as mere incidental movement through the day.

## The Verdict

Malta wins for anyone who wants fitness without the performance — the island simply builds activity into daily life, no alarm clock, no headset microphone, no stranger named Shaz required. Australia wins for anyone who wants fitness as genuine social infrastructure, with the explicit trade-off that said infrastructure runs on a schedule that begins while most of the civilised world is still asleep.

I respect Malta's quiet competence. I fear Australia's enthusiasm. Both, somehow, work.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/australia — a recurring thread theme: newcomers are warned, repeatedly and emphatically, to always swim between the flags and respect posted surf conditions, with multiple threads citing it as the single most important safety advice for anyone new to Australian beaches.*

> *ExpatExchange Malta — members note that joining a small CrossFit box or swim group is a far faster way to build a social circle on the island than relying on a conventional gym membership alone, given how tight-knit and small those communities are.*

## Conclusion

Neither country actually built a fitness culture on purpose — they both built it as a response to geography, and the response simply calcified into identity over time. Malta's hills and sea made activity incidental. Australia's heat and coastline made activity social and scheduled, loudly, around sunrise. Move between them and you'll trade an accidental island workout for a mandatory beach bootcamp with a stranger who already knows your name by week two. Pack running shoes either way. You'll need them for entirely different reasons.
