# One Country's Weekend Starts With a Nap and Ends at 4am. The Other's Weekend Starts With Closed Shutters and Ends With a Quiet Walk.

🇧🇪 Belgium · 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A country's weekend is its honest self-portrait, stripped of the office persona, and Belgium and Spain paint themselves in almost comically opposite palettes. Belgium's weekend is a hushed, deliberate exhale — shutters down, streets quiet, a long lunch, a walk, an early night, the whole country essentially agreeing to pause. Spain's weekend doesn't pause anything; it simply relocates the entire population outdoors, stretches dinner until midnight, and treats 2am as a perfectly reasonable hour to still be deciding where to go next.

I've spent a Belgian Sunday so still I could hear my own anxiety about having nothing urgent to do, and a Spanish Saturday so long I genuinely lost track of which meal I was currently eating. Both, somehow, count as rest.

## Do's & Don'ts: Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan your grocery run before Sunday — most small shops close completely | Expect lively nightlife outside Brussels, Antwerp, or Ghent on a weeknight |
| Embrace the long, unhurried Sunday lunch as the actual social centerpiece | Schedule anything requiring errands or admin for a Sunday; the country shuts down |
| Use the quiet weekends for cycling, walking, or a proper countryside day trip | Mistake the calm for boredom; Belgians genuinely value the stillness itself |
| Book restaurants ahead for Sunday lunch — it's the one meal everyone goes out for | Assume shops reopen early Monday either; some ease back in gradually |

## Do's & Don'ts: Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept that dinner plans starting at 9 or 10pm are completely normal, not late | Plan an early night on a Friday or Saturday; the real plans haven't started yet |
| Join the evening paseo — the walk-and-socialize ritual locals treat as essential | Expect quiet residential streets after dark; outdoor socializing runs very late |
| Pace your drinking and eating across a long night — it's a marathon, not a sprint | Try to out-schedule Spanish spontaneity; plans solidify late and shift constantly |
| Let the siesta-adjusted afternoon lull become part of your own rhythm | Assume Sunday is a "rest day" in the Belgian sense; it's often the loudest family day |

## Belgium: The Art of a Properly Protected Pause

Belgium's weekend operates on a principle that would baffle a lot of expats before they've lived it: rest is not the absence of activity, it's a deliberately, almost civically protected state, and the country organises its entire Sunday around preserving it. Small shops close, often completely, across much of the country outside major cities — no quick top-up grocery run, no casual errand-running, because Sunday simply isn't the day for that kind of motion. The first time this catches you out, forgetting bread on a Saturday and discovering every bakery within walking distance is shuttered until Monday, it feels almost punitive. By your second year, you've learned to plan around it entirely, and the forced stillness starts to feel less like inconvenience and more like a genuinely well-designed boundary against the exact kind of errand-creep that eats everyone else's weekend alive.

The actual centerpiece of the Belgian weekend is the long Sunday lunch — not a quick bite, a proper multi-course, multi-hour affair, often extending family gatherings well into the afternoon, treated with a seriousness that rivals any Spanish dinner in duration if not in volume. Outside this, the weekend rhythm leans heavily toward quiet, outdoor, low-key activity: cycling clubs are a genuine social institution here, not a niche hobby, and a Sunday countryside ride with a group, followed by a beer at a village café, is about as close to a national weekend pastime as Belgium has.

What surprises a lot of newcomers, especially those coming from louder cultures, is how genuinely content Belgians seem within this quiet — there's no FOMO radiating off a closed Sunday high street, no sense that something more exciting must be happening somewhere else. The calm is the point, not a fallback. Expats who arrive expecting Brussels' EU-quarter buzz to extend into the weekend are often disappointed; expats who instead lean into the long lunches, the cycling clubs, and the deliberately unhurried pace tend to describe Belgian weekends, eventually, as one of the most restorative things about living here.

## Spain: Rest by Relocating Outdoors and Stretching the Clock

Spain's weekend doesn't believe in Belgium's version of stillness at all; it believes in motion, specifically outdoor, specifically social, specifically stretched across hours that would be considered deeply antisocial almost anywhere else. The paseo — the evening walk, typically after dinner's had a chance to settle, through the main streets and plazas, greeting neighbours, stopping for one more drink — is a genuine, near-universal institution, performed by every generation simultaneously, from toddlers in strollers to grandparents on benches, in a way that makes the street itself feel like the actual living room of the town.

Timing here runs on a clock that takes real adjustment: lunch at 2pm, dinner at 9 or 10pm, and a Saturday night that doesn't meaningfully start until close to midnight, with bars and clubs expecting their busiest hours between 1 and 4am. This isn't recklessness; it's a deeply embedded, centuries-old rhythm that the entire culture — work hours, school schedules, even television programming — has organised itself around, and newcomers who try to impose an earlier, more "sensible" schedule onto it end up eating alone at restaurants that haven't properly opened yet, or going out just as everyone else is going home for a pre-dinner nap.

Sunday, crucially, is not Belgium's quiet pause here — it's often the loudest, most crowded family day of the week, long lunches spilling across entire afternoons with three generations at one table, followed by more walking, more socialising, more noise, right through the evening. The Spanish weekend essentially refuses the very premise of a protected quiet day; rest, when it comes, is woven into the rhythm itself — the long lunch, the afternoon lull, the late start — rather than carved out as a separate, silent block the way Belgium insists on. Expats who need genuine, enforced silence to recover from their week often find Spain's version of rest exhausting rather than restorative; expats who thrive on constant, low-stakes social contact find it's the best weekend format they've ever lived inside.

## The Verdict

Belgium wins if what you actually need from a weekend is genuine, protected, guilt-free stillness — nowhere in this comparison does quiet better, or defends it more seriously. Spain wins if you measure a good weekend by how connected, fed, and socially alive you felt by Sunday night, even if you're exhausted in a way that has nothing to do with work.

Given the choice, I'll take Spain's exhausting aliveness over Belgium's protected calm. Rest is lovely. Being properly, loudly, deliciously alive until 2am on a Tuesday-adjacent Saturday is better.

## Conclusion

Neither Belgium nor Spain is wrong about what a weekend is for; they've simply landed on opposite, equally sincere answers. Belgium protects stillness like a civic resource, Spain spends its weekend hours generously on noise, food, and company, treating exhaustion as an acceptable price for connection. Show up expecting Brussels to party like Barcelona and you'll find a locked bakery and a silent street. Show up expecting Barcelona to rest like Brussels and you'll be asleep two hours before the real plans even start. Learn which weekend you've actually landed in, and stop trying to import the one you left behind.
