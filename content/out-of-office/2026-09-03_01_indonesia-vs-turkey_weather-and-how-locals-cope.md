# One Country Floods Every Wet Season, the Other Just Pretends Winter Isn't Coming

🇮🇩 Indonesia 🇹🇷 Turkey

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every culture has a relationship with its own weather that ranges from denial to full theatrical surrender, and nowhere is that more entertaining to watch from the outside than in Jakarta and Istanbul. One city treats an entirely predictable, calendar-scheduled deluge as a fresh personal betrayal every single year. The other treats a Bosphorus wind that will slice through three layers of merino wool as a minor inconvenience worth complaining about for four months straight, indoors, over tea, without ever turning the heating up.

I have lived through one Jakarta wet season and two Istanbul winters, and I can report with complete confidence that neither country has made peace with its own climate, they've just developed increasingly elaborate rituals to avoid admitting that. Indonesians build entire informal economies around flood season. Turks build entire personalities around how much colder it is this year than last year, a claim that is never once backed by the meteorological service.

## Do's & Don'ts: Indonesia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep a spare pair of sandals by the door for wading through ankle-deep water | Wear anything with laces during rainy season, you will regret it by the third puddle |
| Track BMKG flood warnings the way locals do, seriously, they're accurate | Assume "rainy season" means light drizzle, it means the sky opens like a tap |
| Elevate valuables in ground-floor apartments from October onward | Park your motorbike in a low-lying street and expect to find it dry the next morning |
| Befriend a neighbour with a boat, genuinely, some streets need one | Panic when the office closes for a "banjir day," it happens, roll with it |

## Do's & Don'ts: Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in a proper coat, the Bosphorus wind is not a myth, it is a weapon | Trust an Istanbul flat's "central heating" claim without asking which decade it was installed |
| Learn to love a glass of tea by a window watching the rain, it's the national coping mechanism | Expect consistent public transport during the first real storm of the season, everything grinds |
| Layer up for indoor spaces too, insulation is more of a suggestion than a standard | Complain about the cold to an Anatolian villager, you will lose that conversation badly |

## Indonesia: The Flood Is Not a Surprise, Stop Acting Surprised

Jakarta floods. It floods every year, on a schedule so reliable you could set a calendar by it, and yet every single wet season the city collectively behaves as though this is the first time water has ever fallen from the sky. The rainy season runs roughly October through March, and by December large parts of North and East Jakarta are functioning less like a metropolis and more like a very determined Venice tribute act, minus the gondolas and the romance.

What I find genuinely admirable, once you get past the infrastructure despair, is how quickly ordinary life adapts around it. Warungs stay open on stilts of stacked bricks. Motorbike taxi drivers develop an encyclopedic mental map of which streets are passable and which have quietly become rivers. Office workers keep a go-bag of dry clothes at their desks as standard practice, the same way a Londoner keeps an umbrella, except the stakes are considerably higher than slightly damp shoulders.

The real skill isn't avoiding the flooding, because you cannot avoid the flooding, the real skill is reading the specific texture of Jakarta rain. Locals distinguish between the manageable afternoon downpour that clears in twenty minutes and the ominous, sustained, grey-sky-for-days pattern that means BMKG will be issuing advisories and your commute is about to become a logistical operation involving at least one ojek driver who charges triple and deserves every rupiah of it.

Outside the capital, the story shifts. Bali handles its wet season with tourist-facing shrugs and slightly discounted villa rates. Yogyakarta gets lush and green and genuinely gorgeous, if you don't mind your washing never quite drying. But it's Jakarta that turns weather into a full civic event, complete with a governor who has to publicly explain, every year, why the drainage system built for a much smaller, much less paved-over city in the 1970s still hasn't been comprehensively fixed. Spoiler: it still hasn't.

## Turkey: The National Sport of Underdressing and Overcomplaining

Istanbul's climate is, by any honest global standard, mild. It rarely drops far below freezing in the city centre, snow shuts things down for at most a handful of days a year, and the Mediterranean sun makes a proper effort for a solid six months. And yet Istanbullus discuss the arrival of autumn with the grim resignation of people bracing for a Siberian winter, because the actual enemy here isn't temperature, it's the wind coming off the Bosphorus and the fact that half the city's older apartment buildings were insulated, generously, never.

I spent two winters in a flat with radiators that functioned as more of a symbolic gesture than a heat source, and I learned quickly that everyone around me was doing the same silent calculation: is it colder to be outside walking briskly, or inside sitting still under three blankets pretending the central heating is "just warming up." The tea culture makes complete sense once you understand it isn't really about the tea, it's about having something hot in your hands at all times because the ambient indoor temperature has quietly opted out of the conversation.

The wind itself deserves its own paragraph. It comes down the strait with a specific, targeted cruelty, funnelling between buildings in Karaköy and Beyoğlu in a way that turns a five-minute walk into a genuine ordeal. Locals know exactly which streets to avoid on a windy day and which ones offer shelter, an entire mental geography built purely around not getting flattened by a gust while carrying your simit.

And then, without fail, the moment there's an actual snowfall, the entire city seizes up. Bridges close, ferries get cancelled, and Istanbullus who spent all December performing hardened winter stoicism suddenly discover they own precisely zero pairs of appropriate footwear. It's the most Turkish contradiction going: endless complaining about the cold, paired with a complete structural refusal to actually prepare for it.

## The Verdict

Jakarta wins on sheer competence under genuinely severe conditions, its residents have built real, functional, if unglamorous systems for living around an annual disaster. Istanbul wins on drama, producing four months of magnificently theatrical suffering over weather that a Norwegian would call "a bit fresh." If you want practical resilience, move to Jakarta and buy sandals. If you want to participate in an ongoing, city-wide performance of stoic misery over a chilly breeze, Istanbul is waiting, tea in hand, radiator lukewarm, complaining beautifully. Neither city has solved its weather. Both have simply gotten extremely good at narrating it.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — "How do Jakarta residents deal with constant flooding?" paraphrased: a longtime resident describes keeping a permanent "flood bag" by the door with dry socks, a phone charger, and instant noodles, because you never know if you're getting home tonight or sleeping at the office.*

> *expat.com Jakarta forum, paraphrased: a new arrival was stunned that colleagues just quietly switched to sandals and rolled-up trousers the moment the sky darkened, with zero commentary, like it was as routine as opening an umbrella.*

> *InterNations Istanbul, paraphrased: an expat vented that their landlord insisted the flat was "fully heated" while they could see their own breath in the kitchen every January, and that this is apparently just how Istanbul rental listings work.*

## Conclusion

Weather doesn't care about your national pride, but apparently national pride cares deeply about weather, because that's the only explanation for how two countries with such wildly different actual conditions have both convinced themselves they're uniquely burdened. Jakarta has earned its complaints, the flooding is real, disruptive, and annual. Istanbul's complaints are, frankly, a bit rich given the thermometer readings, but I respect the commitment to the bit. If you're moving to either, pack accordingly: sandals and a flood bag for one, a proper coat and low expectations for landlord-provided heating for the other. Nature doesn't grade on effort, but if it did, Istanbul would still be getting marked down for exaggeration.
