🇷🇺 Russia vs Austria 🇦🇹 — *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two nations that have perfected the art of silent disapproval, and yet they go about it so differently you'd think they'd never met. Russians queue like it's a contact sport nobody agreed to the rules of, and Austrians queue like it's a liturgy — hushed, orderly, and faintly resentful of anyone who breathes too loudly while waiting their turn.

I have stood in both lines. I have been elbowed in one and glared at in the other for daring to check my phone. Only one of these experiences left a bruise. The other left a lingering, low-grade sense of moral failure that took three Melanges to shake.

## The Do's & Don'ts

### 🇷🇺 Russia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Hold the door, even for strangers three metres back | Smile at people on the metro for no reason — they will assume something is wrong with you |
| Bring your own bags to the shop, bring exact change if you can | Expect a queue to self-organise without at least one dedicated shouting match |
| Dress up to go anywhere, including the bins | Raise your voice indoors — loudness is for drama, not daily life |
| Learn the phrase for "who's last in line" (кто последний) — it's a real system | Assume silence on the metro means people aren't watching you; they are, intensely |

### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take a number at the Amt and wait in total, monk-like silence | Cross at a red light even when there is visibly no car for a kilometre |
| Observe Ruhezeit (quiet hours) like it's federal law, because it basically is | Make noise after 10pm, including — allegedly — flushing in some older buildings |
| Queue in a straight, evenly-spaced, almost architectural line | Jump a queue, even by accident; you will be informed, at length, of your error |
| Greet shopkeepers with "Grüß Gott" and mean it | Assume Viennese reserve is coldness — it's just Habsburg-grade self-containment |

## Russia: Chaos With a System Nobody Wrote Down

Moscow doesn't queue so much as it negotiates. There is a line, technically, the way there is technically a speed limit — a concept everyone is aware of and few feel bound by. You will be told, with total sincerity, that there is in fact a very precise system: you ask "кто последний?" — who's last? — the person behind the actual last person claims the title, and from then on an invisible ledger exists in everyone's head of exactly where they stand. It works. It works brilliantly, in fact, right up until someone's cousin shows up and the ledger gets quietly revised.

What foreigners mistake for rudeness is usually just efficiency stripped of padding. Nobody is going to ask how your day is at the pharmacy. You will not receive a smile with your bread. This isn't hostility — it's a culture that reserves warmth for people who've earned it, which, give it six months, will absolutely include you, delivered with a ferocity that makes Western small talk look like a weather report. Once a Muscovite decides you're "theirs," you will be fed, defended, and told uncomfortable truths about your haircut, often in the same conversation.

Noise is a different matter entirely. Public space is loud, private space is loud, arguments are performed at volume and with gusto, and the person yelling at their phone on the bus is not having a crisis — they're just talking. Personal space, meanwhile, barely exists on public transport, where the rules of the metro seem to be: get as close as physically possible without making eye contact, which you will never, ever make, because eye contact on the Moscow metro is practically an act of aggression. Your first week, this will feel like being slowly crushed by a crowd that's somehow also ignoring you. By month three, you'll be doing it to the newcomers yourself, guilt-free, because you have become one of them.

## Austria: A Nation That Agreed, Collectively, to Shut Up

Vienna's queues are a thing of beauty, in the way a Swiss watch is a thing of beauty — precise, slightly cold, and faintly judgmental of anything less precise than itself. You take your Wartenummer, you sit, you wait your exact numeric turn, and the social contract holds with a rigidity that would make a Bundesbank auditor weep with joy. Nobody shouts. Nobody even really speaks. The silence in an Austrian post office at 9am on a Tuesday has a texture to it, dense and total, broken only by the occasional tut when someone's phone rings at a volume above "funeral."

This translates, inevitably, into Ruhezeit — the quiet hours, usually from around 10pm to 6am and again over the Mittagsruhe at lunch, during which an entire nation apparently agrees not to vacuum, mow, hammer, or in some buildings even shower too enthusiastically. It is not strictly enforced by police in most cases, and yet it is enforced with a social force that makes legal enforcement look optional by comparison — a note under your door, a pointed cough in the stairwell, the kind of passive-aggressive silence that somehow conveys an entire paragraph of disapproval.

Personal space, by contrast, is sacred and generously proportioned. Austrians will stand a full, deliberate arm's length away from you in any queue, on any platform, in any lift, as though proximity itself were a faux pas requiring an apology. Jaywalking, meanwhile, is treated with an almost theological seriousness — I have watched a man stand at an empty crossing in the rain at 11pm, waiting for a green light that was not coming for anyone, because the principle mattered more than the puddle. That's not caution. That's civic devotion.

## The Verdict

If you want a society that runs on unspoken rules everyone somehow knows and nobody will explain to you, pick Russia — just know the rules bend for family, charm, and sheer force of personality, and once you're in, you're properly in. If you want a society that runs on *written* rules everyone follows to the letter and will absolutely explain to you, at length, pick Austria — just know the rules do not bend, not for charm, not for jet lag, not for your heartfelt insistence that it's "basically" quiet hours.

Austria wins on predictability. Russia wins on warmth, eventually, violently, deliciously. But for sheer unbothered personal space on a crowded tram, Vienna takes it by a full, dignified arm's length — and honestly, after a Moscow rush hour, you'll weep with gratitude for the room to breathe.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/expats — paraphrased: "I thought Moscow traffic was the hard part. No. The hard part is the silent death-stare you get from a Viennese pensioner for standing one centimetre too close to them at the bakery counter."*

> *Internations Vienna — paraphrased: a longtime member described calling a plumber after 8pm as "a diplomatic incident," and recommended simply not having emergencies after dinner.*

> *expat.com Moscow forum — paraphrased: a Canadian transplant wrote that she didn't understand the "who's last" queue system for two years, assumed she was being skipped constantly, and was deeply relieved to learn it was a system and not a conspiracy against her specifically.*

## Conclusion

Here's the uncomfortable truth neither city wants printed on a tourism leaflet: both of these places are, underneath the noise and the silence respectively, deeply rule-bound societies that have simply chosen different rules to be fanatical about. Russia will shout at you in the queue and then invite you to a four-hour dinner the same week. Austria will never raise its voice at you once, and will also never, under any circumstances, let you flush after 10pm with impunity.

Pick your poison. Pick your decibel level. Just don't move to either expecting Western-style small talk to smooth things over — in Moscow it'll get you nowhere, and in Vienna it'll get you a very long, very quiet look.
