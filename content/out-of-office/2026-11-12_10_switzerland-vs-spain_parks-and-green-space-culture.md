# Switzerland Mows the Lawn Before You Sit on It. Spain Gives You a Bench and a Reason to Stay Out Past Midnight

🇨🇭 Switzerland vs 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Swiss green space is a curated experience. Every picnic site has a grill, a stack of wood and a laminated sign explaining what you may not do with either. The grass is mown with a discipline normally associated with surgery. Spanish green space is looser: a plaza with a few trees, a park where families colonise a corner until midnight, and a mountain trail that begins a bus ride from the beach.

You could argue the Swiss do nature better and the Spanish do outdoors better, and you would be mostly right. The first is a place you visit. The second is a place you live in. Moving from one to the other changes not just your landscape but your rules for what a park is for.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Switzerland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the equipped picnic areas and clean up completely | Leave anything behind, including ash |
| Respect quiet hours and Sunday rest | Play loud music in a park |
| Take advantage of lakes for swimming and strolling | Assume every lake allows every activity |
| Check the rules posted at the entrance | Improvise |

**Spain**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the plaza as an extension of your living room | Expect it to be quiet in the evening |
| Walk or hike early in summer, avoid midday heat | Start a long trail at noon |
| Bring a jacket for mountain evenings | Dress for the coast in the Sierra |
| Join the neighbours, a bench is an invitation | Sit with headphones and ignore everyone |

## Switzerland

Switzerland treats green space as national infrastructure. The Expat.com leisure guide lists the Swiss National Park, the Beverin and Chasseral nature parks and the Entlebuch biosphere, notes that many parks offer specially equipped picnic areas, and describes the Kurpark in Bad Zurzach as having extensive green spaces. Lake Zurich, the guide says, is popular for swimming and strolling. That is true, and also an understatement: on a hot day, the lake becomes a municipal living room where half of Zurich eats lunch in a swimsuit.

The Swiss approach is organisation. Paths are signposted down to the minute, public transport reaches trailheads, and the rules are posted at every entry. The upside is that you can walk anywhere without getting lost, bring children without anxiety and expect a clean bench at the end. The downside is that spontaneity is regulated. The grill is there; so is the sign about when to use it.

I could not find a first-person voice on Swiss parks, and the one anonymous answer I read was too generic to trust. But a former Swiss resident who spent about a decade there, quoted in a Spanish expat publication, said that in Switzerland things work more predictably. That sentence is the whole philosophy of Swiss green space in six words. The mountain will be there, the path will be marked, the hut will be open.

Predictability has a cost. A Swiss park rarely surprises you, and it rarely encourages you to linger after dark. It rewards routine, not improvisation. If you are the kind of person who likes to plan a hike by Tuesday, you will love it. If you want to wander into a square and see what happens, you may find it too polite.

The Swiss relationship with green space is also tied to a broader outdoor ethos. Walking, hiking, swimming in the lake and skiing in winter are treated as normal parts of adult life, and the infrastructure supports them. The leisure guide's note that many parks provide equipped picnic areas is typical: the state does not merely preserve nature, it furnishes it. For a newcomer this is wonderful and slightly overwhelming. You are never short of somewhere to go, but you are also never far from a rule about how to behave there, and the quiet social pressure to do it properly is part of the price.

## Spain

Spain makes its parks work harder. An insurer's expat video from Estepona, where a golfer who has lived there for eighteen years talks about outdoor life, calls Spain a paradise for sports and outdoor lovers, with greens outdoors and gyms indoors in plentiful supply. That is a commercial summary and not a direct quote, but it fits what I have seen.

The more interesting account comes from a Granada expat on Expat Exchange. The writer said they hike and ski in the Sierra Nevada, about an hour by bus from the coast, and live near a tennis park, though the tennis there is limited. An hour from beach to snow is a very Spanish idea: not elaborate, simply available.

Then there is the green north. An Austrian woman who lived nine years in Spain chose Cantabria for its landscape, calling it green, not unbearably hot, and with snow in winter. She described her stone house on about four and a half hectares as beautiful, calm and very grounding. She left Spain to move back to Switzerland, but the reasons she gave were economic and bureaucratic strain, not the scenery.

That is a vital point. Spain's green space is not about manicure. It is about life. The plaza is where people gather, the park is where families spend evenings, and the countryside is a short drive away for people who didn't plan it in advance. The weakness is maintenance and heat: the south dries out in summer, and municipal budgets sometimes show. But the culture of using outdoor space, late, loudly and with all generations, is something Switzerland rarely matches.

Spain's natural variety is also easy to underestimate. The country has a green north, a snowy Sierra, long beaches and a sunbaked interior, which means an expat can pick a landscape the way others pick a flat. The Austrian resident chose the green of Cantabria and found it calm and grounding, while the Granada expat relies on mountains an hour from the coast. What unites these accounts is that Spanish nature is not pre-packaged. You find it, adapt to it and share it with neighbours, and that unpolished quality is exactly what some people leave Switzerland to find.

## The Verdict

Switzerland has the better parks. Spain has the better use of them. If you judge by upkeep, signage, safety and the quality of a mountain trail, Switzerland wins with a clean sweep. Nobody does an alpine meadow like the Swiss.

But if you judge by how deeply green space is woven into daily life, Spain pulls ahead. A Spanish plaza at ten at night, full of grandparents, toddlers and teenagers, is a better advertisement for the public realm than any national park. It is noisy, imperfect and alive.

My verdict is a split decision with a lean: Switzerland for nature, Spain for community. If I were bringing up children, I would pick Switzerland for the safety and the order. If I were old and wanted company, I would pick Spain without hesitation. The best answer, as the Austrian resident proved, may be to live in one and long for the other.

## Conclusion

Parks reveal what a society trusts. Switzerland trusts rules, so its green spaces are clean, ordered and a pleasure to visit. Spain trusts people, so its green spaces are noisy, social and full of life.

Take the best of both. Do your serious walking in the Alps, where the paths are perfect and the huts are open. Do your evening wandering in a Spanish plaza, where nobody will tell you to leave.

And whichever you choose, bring a jacket. Mountains do not care which country they are in.
