# Two Bureaucracies Walk Into a Bank. Only One of Them Lets You Leave With Money.

🇹🇿 Tanzania 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

People love to tell you that moving somewhere "exotic" means adapting to chaos, while moving somewhere "developed" means everything just works. I have opened bank accounts on four continents and I can tell you this binary is a lie dressed up as a travel brochure. Tanzania will make you produce two passport photographs and a letter from an employer who may or may not exist yet, then hand you a debit card within the week. Canada will tell you, in writing, that you have a legal right to a bank account — and then quietly let a teller who's never heard of that law turn you away anyway.

This is the actual difference between the two: Tanzania's bureaucracy is honest about being an obstacle course. Canada's bureaucracy is dressed in the soft, reassuring language of a human-rights pamphlet while doing roughly the same thing with a smile and a referral to "another branch."

## Do's & Don'ts: Tanzania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Sort your residence or work permit before you go anywhere near a bank counter | Walk in without one and expect a teller to feel sorry for you |
| Carry a stack of shillings everywhere, cash is still king | Assume the card machine works just because the shop has one |
| Build a relationship with one trustworthy bureau de change | Change money at your hotel desk and call it convenient |
| Keep spare passport photos on you permanently | Lose your temper over the "minimum deposit" theatre — it's non-negotiable |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for your SIN the moment you land | Assume you need it just to open a basic chequing account |
| Bring two pieces of government ID to every appointment | Expect every teller to have memorized the Bank Act |
| Ask explicitly for the "newcomer package" by name | Accept the first product offered without checking the fine print |
| Get a refusal confirmed in writing if it happens | Assume "we don't do that here" is actually true |

## Tanzania: The Honesty of Obstacle Courses

Tanzanian banking bureaucracy does you the enormous favour of never pretending to be anything other than a hurdle. To open a personal account as a foreigner, you need a valid work or residence permit, two passport-sized photographs, a letter of appointment from your employer, and a minimum opening deposit — TSH 50,000, or the equivalent in US dollars or euros, which in practice usually means a cool $1,000. Company accounts escalate from there into incorporation documents, a business licence, a Tax Identification Number and a board resolution, which is bureaucracy's way of telling you it respects you enough to make you suffer properly.

Once you clear that gate, though, something almost refreshing happens: the system mostly leaves you alone. Banks correspond in English and Swahili, you can move money abroad freely once you have sufficient funds, and the restrictions that remain are more about currency conversion mechanics than suspicion of your existence. Bank of Tanzania's 2022 foreign exchange regulations tightened the rules around bureaux de change rather than punishing ordinary account holders, which is more than can be said for plenty of "developed" systems that treat every incoming wire as a potential crime scene.

The daily friction is elsewhere, and it's real. Banking hours run roughly 8:30 a.m. to 3:30 p.m. on weekdays and a brief Saturday morning window, so you plan your life around the bank, not the other way round. Try arriving at 3:29 p.m. with a queue ticket and a problem; you will be politely, immovably told to come back tomorrow, and you will learn to respect that answer the way you respect weather. ATMs cap withdrawals at TSH 1,000,000 a pop, card machines fail routinely because the mobile network underneath them fails routinely, and upscale restaurants that do accept cards will often tax you an extra 5% for the privilege. Cash remains not a backup plan but the actual plan. The genuinely clever move — and the one most newcomers take embarrassingly long to make — is mobile money. M-Pesa and its competitors move shillings between ordinary people faster and more reliably than most bank transfers, permit or no permit, photograph or no photograph. Tanzania's formal banking bureaucracy is a toll booth; its informal mobile economy is the open road running right alongside it.

## Canada: Bureaucracy With Excellent PR

Canada's pitch is seductive: you have a legal right to a basic bank account, full stop, regardless of employment status, credit history, or how recently your plane landed. The Bank Act obliges Schedule I and II banks to open one for anyone who shows acceptable identification — a foreign passport plus one supporting document will usually do — and valid refusal grounds are narrow: prior fraud against that specific institution, missing ID, or a genuine regulatory risk. "You just got here" is not, legally, an acceptable reason to say no. Fees on qualifying basic accounts are capped at roughly $4 a month, and you don't even need a Social Insurance Number unless you want your money to earn interest.

On paper, this is the most dignified system a newcomer could ask for. In the branch, it's a different story, and the Globe and Mail has reported exactly why: staff unfamiliarity with the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada's own rules routinely results in newcomers being turned away or quietly steered toward costlier commercial "newcomer packages" instead of the basic account they're actually entitled to. Those newcomer packages have their own eligibility fences — often limited to permanent residents within their first two or three years, frequently excluding international students and temporary workers entirely — so the friendliest-sounding product on the shelf may not be available to the person standing in front of it.

Then there's the in-person requirement. Despite Canada's digital-banking reputation, most traditional institutions still insist on seeing you and your documents face to face before they'll verify your identity, which rather undercuts the national self-image of effortless efficiency. Digital-only banks will do it all online, true, but only if you're comfortable handing a stranger's app your passport scan and trusting an algorithm more than you'd trust the polite person at the counter who just told you no for the wrong reason. Monthly fees elsewhere in the system range from under $5 to over $30, international transfers remain clunky enough to push diaspora communities toward informal remittance channels anyway, and the SIN — while not required to open an account — becomes essential the moment you want your savings to actually behave like savings. Canada has built a genuinely humane legal framework and then staffed its execution with people who never read it.

## The Verdict

If you're grading on paper rights, Canada wins by a landslide; nobody in Tanzania is legally entitled to anything resembling a basic account, and the deposit minimums alone would exclude most newly arrived Canadians from Canadian banking if the roles were reversed. But I don't grade on paper, because paper doesn't get you cash on a Sunday.

Tanzania tells you exactly what it wants — permit, photos, deposit, patience — and then largely steps back. Canada tells you that you're protected, legislates that protection into existence, and then lets a chronically under-briefed teller undo it at the counter while you stand there holding two pieces of valid ID and a sinking feeling. One system is upfront about its toll booth. The other hides its toll booth behind a government pamphlet and calls it customer service.

Advantage: Tanzania, on honesty alone. Canada, take notes.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *TalesMag, real post report from Dar es Salaam — paraphrased: credit cards are "not widely accepted, not always safe to try," and the only ATMs most expats fully trust are the ones clustered at the shopping centres other foreigners already use.*

> *Expatexchange.com forum, Dar es Salaam thread — paraphrased: one member described the real adjustment as "learning to be more patient," because, in their words, things simply don't happen on the timeline you're used to.*

> *Newcomers interviewed by The Globe and Mail — paraphrased: several described being turned away from a basic account or pushed toward a costlier "newcomer" product, not because the law required it, but because the person behind the counter didn't know the rules they were supposed to be enforcing.*

## Conclusion

Here's the sting nobody wants: both of these systems will cost you something, and neither will tell you upfront what it is. Tanzania charges you in patience, cash, and passport photographs before it lets you in. Canada charges you in the gap between what the law promises and what the branch actually delivers, and then lets you discover that gap alone, at the counter, holding documents that are supposedly more than enough. If you want predictable friction, go to Dar es Salaam. If you want friction with a smile and a pamphlet about your rights, go to Toronto. Either way, keep cash on you and your expectations lower than the brochure.
