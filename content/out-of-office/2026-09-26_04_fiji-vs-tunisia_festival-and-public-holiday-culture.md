# Fiji Gave You a Bonus Religion's Holidays; Tunisia Gave You Two Calendars at Once

🇫🇯 Fiji 🇹🇳 Tunisia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two countries, two entirely different ways of telling you the year has more public holidays in it than you were led to believe. Fiji quietly stacks Christian, Hindu, and Muslim holy days into one national calendar like it's no big deal. Tunisia runs on both the Gregorian and Islamic lunar calendars simultaneously, which means your public holidays don't even land on the same date two years running. I have shown up to closed banks in both countries for entirely unrelated reasons, and I regret nothing.

## Do's & Don'ts: Fiji

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join Diwali celebrations even if you're not Indo-Fijian — it's genuinely communal | Assume Fiji's festival calendar is "just" a Pacific Islander one |
| Expect Christian, Hindu, and Muslim holidays all gazetted nationally | Plan a business trip around the same week every year — dates shift |
| Dress modestly and bring an appetite to any Diwali open-house invite | Skip local sevusevu (kava ceremony) customs at community festivals |
| Check the government holiday list yearly — it updates with lunar dates | Assume "public holiday" means everything's actually closed |

## Do's & Don'ts: Tunisia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Track both the Gregorian and Hijri calendars — Tunisia runs on both | Expect Eid or Ramadan to land on the same date as last year |
| Expect a genuinely festive, family-forward Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha | Schedule anything urgent in the days immediately around Eid |
| Accept food invitations during Ramadan evenings (iftar) — it's an honor | Eat or drink visibly in public during Ramadan daylight hours |
| Budget for closures around Eid al-Adha — sheep prices spike, so does spending | Assume French colonial-era holidays still carry the same weight they once did |

## Fiji: A National Calendar That Respects Everyone's God

Fiji's public holiday list reads like a country that decided, sensibly, to just include everyone rather than pick a team. Christmas and Easter sit on the calendar next to Diwali and, in recent years, Eid — not as multicultural gestures, but as fully gazetted national public holidays that shut down banks, schools, and government offices the same way Christmas does anywhere else. ABC Pacific's coverage of Fiji's Diwali holiday is matter-of-fact about it in a way that would surprise visitors from more religiously homogenous countries: this isn't a minority community quietly marking a holiday among themselves, it's the whole nation observing it together, lights and all.

The Fiji Pocket Guide's rundown of the annual calendar makes clear just how layered this gets: because Diwali and Eid both follow lunar calendars, their dates shift relative to the Gregorian calendar every single year, which means Fiji's "fixed" public holiday list actually requires an annual government gazette update just to confirm the actual dates. Locals treat this as entirely unremarkable. Visitors, particularly the ones trying to book flights a year in advance, do not.

What makes Fiji's festival culture distinct isn't just the diversity of holidays observed — it's the genuine cross-community participation. Diwali open-houses in Suva welcome neighbors of every background; indigenous iTaukei communities show up to Indo-Fijian celebrations and vice versa, in a rhythm that feels less like tolerance and more like actual shared ownership of each other's calendars. It helps that Fijian social life already runs on hospitality and communal eating as a default setting, so adding "more religious festivals, more open invitations" onto that foundation barely required cultural adjustment — it just gave everyone more excuses to feed each other, which nobody in Fiji has ever needed convincing to do.

## Tunisia: Two Calendars, One Very Punctual Country

Tunisia's public holiday culture runs on a genuinely dual-track system: the Gregorian calendar handles secular national holidays (Independence Day, Republic Day, Revolution and Youth Day), while the Islamic Hijri lunar calendar governs the religious ones — Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha, and the Islamic New Year — which drift roughly eleven days earlier each Gregorian year. Carthage Magazine's own holiday calendar breakdown is essentially required reading for anyone trying to plan around it, because "Eid is in April this year" becomes "Eid is in March" within a few years flat, on a schedule no Western calendar app tracks correctly by default.

Ramadan itself isn't a public holiday in the Christmas-day sense — offices technically stay open — but functions as one anyway: hours shorten, productivity visibly drops in the emptiest, hungriest hours before iftar, and the entire rhythm of the day reorganizes itself around the sunset meal. The Arab Weekly's coverage of Eid in Tunisia captures something genuinely moving about the holiday's texture: new clothes, family gatherings that stretch across days, and a level of communal warmth that outpaces most Western Christmases in sheer duration, if not commercial spectacle.

Eid al-Adha carries its own very specific, very Tunisian economic subplot: the ritual sheep purchase, which Carthage Magazine has covered as an actual household budgeting event, with sheep prices tracked and complained about with the same intensity Americans reserve for Thanksgiving turkey inflation. It's a striking reminder that a religious holiday and a household economic event can be the exact same day, and Tunisian families navigate both with a shrug that reads as pure lived experience rather than performance.

## The Verdict

Fiji wins on sheer inclusivity — a national calendar that treats Diwali, Eid, and Christmas as equally, officially worth the day off is a genuinely rare civic achievement, and the cross-community participation makes it feel earned rather than merely legislated. Tunisia wins on depth and intensity — nobody does Eid with the layered, calendar-defying commitment Tunisians bring to it, sheep prices and all. If you want your public holidays to feel like a shared national hug, book Fiji. If you want to actually understand what a religious festival means when an entire country organizes its year, its economy, and its family structure around it, go to Tunis during Eid and don't make plans for four days either side.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a respondent comparing Diwali in Fiji to Diwali in India said the Fijian version felt "smaller in scale but somehow warmer, because the whole neighborhood shows up regardless of background."*

> *expat.com Tunisia forum — a European expat admitted to badly misjudging when Ramadan would fall two years running and said "you cannot mentally calendar this the Western way — track the Hijri dates or get caught out every single year."*

> *Internations Suva community — a longtime foreign resident described being invited to a Diwali open-house by neighbors she'd barely spoken to, calling it "the fastest I've ever gone from stranger to guest of honor."*

## Conclusion

Fiji and Tunisia are both, in their own ways, calendars stitched together from more than one tradition — Fiji by official multicultural design, Tunisia by the simple fact of running two calendar systems that refuse to sync. Both will humble anyone who assumes a public holiday list is a fixed, boring administrative document. It isn't. It's a running record of who a country decided was worth stopping everything for, and both of these countries have decided, generously, that the answer is: quite a lot of people, actually. Check the gazette before you book flights. Both times.
