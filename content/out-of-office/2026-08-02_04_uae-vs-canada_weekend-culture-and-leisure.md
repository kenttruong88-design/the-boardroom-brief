# The UAE Invented the Bottomless Brunch. Canada Invented Disappearing to a Lake and Not Explaining Why.

🇦🇪 UAE · 🇨🇦 Canada
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The UAE's weekend is an engineered spectacle — poolside brunches with unlimited champagne starting at noon, desert convoys at sunset, malls the size of small nations offering air-conditioned refuge from a sun actively trying to kill you. Canada's weekend, by contrast, is a quiet vanishing act: everyone you know simply disappears "to the cottage" on Friday afternoon and reappears Monday, sunburnt, mosquito-bitten, and unbothered, having achieved a kind of leisure so understated it barely registers as an activity at all.

I have been handed a glass of prosecco at 11:58am in a Dubai hotel brunch that would not end until well past sunset. I have also sat in near-total silence on a Canadian dock at 7am, coffee in hand, being informed this was, in fact, "the whole point." Both experiences left me exhausted. Only one left me with a headache.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇦🇪 UAE**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book brunch reservations well in advance — the good ones sell out weeks ahead | Expect a Saturday-Sunday weekend — Friday-Saturday is standard |
| Dress smartly even for "casual" outings — the bar is higher than you think | Underestimate the heat during outdoor leisure — plan around the hours, not the whole day |
| Try a desert safari at least once — it's genuinely spectacular, not just for tourists | Assume alcohol is available everywhere — licensing rules vary by emirate and venue |

**🇨🇦 Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say yes if invited "to the cottage" — it's a real cultural institution, not just a house | Expect fast, structured plans — Canadian leisure is deliberately unstructured |
| Embrace the long weekend camping trip, bugs and all | Book anything requiring precision timing near a holiday Monday — everyone's gone north |
| Learn to enjoy comfortable silence — not every outing needs conversation | Assume "brunch" means the UAE version — here it's eggs, coffee, and modest quiet |

## UAE: Leisure as a Production Value

The UAE's weekend runs Friday to Saturday, a scheduling quirk that trips up nearly every newcomer at least once, per the UAE Government Portal's own official calendar, and it recalibrates your entire sense of time in a way that's oddly disorienting for the first month. But the bigger adjustment is the sheer scale of ambition applied to leisure. Dubai's Department of Economy and Tourism reports staggering growth in hospitality and leisure spend, and you feel it the second you attempt brunch — not a modest plate of eggs, but a four-hour, multi-venue, unlimited-beverage marathon that starts at noon and ends somewhere around your own regret.

Brunch culture here isn't a meal, it's an institution with its own unwritten etiquette: book weeks ahead for the popular spots, dress to the theme, pace yourself or don't, but understand that "just popping in for an hour" isn't really how this works. The heat dictates everything else — outdoor leisure exists in a tight morning or evening window, and the middle of the day belongs entirely to air-conditioned malls that function as genuine social infrastructure, not just shopping destinations. The Dubai Mall on a Friday afternoon is less a retail experience than a climate-controlled town square.

What surprised me most was the desert itself — dune bashing, camel treks, sunset camps with shisha and fire pits, all of which sound like tourist-brochure clichés until you're actually watching the sun drop behind a dune and realise the spectacle is entirely earned. The UAE's weekend leisure economy is unapologetically maximalist: bigger brunch, bigger view, bigger production. Subtlety is not the point. The point is that you should leave every weekend outing slightly overwhelmed, and by that metric, it delivers spectacularly.

## Canada: Leisure as Strategic Disappearance

Canada's weekend culture is the precise inverse: minimalist, deeply private, and organised almost entirely around the cottage — a word that undersells what is, functionally, a national religion. Statistics Canada's leisure time survey confirms what anyone who's lived through a Canadian summer already knows instinctively: outdoor recreation dominates leisure time, and Destination Canada's own data on the scale of lake and forest tourism only confirms how central this is to the national identity, not a niche pursuit for the wealthy.

The ritual is almost identical across the country: Friday afternoon, city empties out, everyone heads north to a lake, and the entire point of the exercise is to do remarkably little. Fishing, if you're inclined. Canoeing, if you're feeling ambitious. Otherwise, mostly sitting, mostly in comfortable silence, mostly with a coffee or a beer, watching water do nothing in particular. I found this genuinely disorienting the first time — I kept waiting for the "activity" to start, and eventually realised the sitting was the activity. Canadians do not perform their leisure for an audience. There's no brunch to be photographed, no production value to maintain. The absence of spectacle is, itself, the entire appeal.

Long weekends compound this into a national migration — book anything requiring precision timing on a holiday Monday and you'll find half the country has simply left, will not respond to messages, and will return Tuesday faintly sunburnt with a story about a loon they saw. It's an entirely internal, undocumented form of leisure, resistant to Instagram in a way that feels almost defiant in 2026. Canadians aren't relaxing to prove anything. They're relaxing because the lake is there and, frankly, why wouldn't you.

## The Verdict

The UAE wins if your idea of a good weekend involves genuine spectacle, effort, and the sense that leisure should be earned through sheer production value. Canada wins if you believe rest should require absolutely no performance whatsoever, just water, quiet, and the tacit understanding that nobody's watching. I lean Canadian on this one, mostly because my liver has opinions about four-hour brunches, but I'll concede the UAE throws a better sunset.

## Conclusion

The UAE turned the weekend into an event; Canada turned it into an absence. Neither is more correct, though I'll admit the emotional recovery time differs wildly — Dubai's brunch culture leaves you needing Monday to recover from Saturday, while Canada's lake culture leaves you needing Monday simply because you don't want to go back. Choose spectacle or choose silence. Just don't expect either country to apologise for taking their version of rest as seriously as they do.
