# Canada Fights Winter With Boots and Hong Kong Fights Summer With a Shopping Mall

🇨🇦 Canada vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country has weather. Canada has a *relationship* with its weather, one of those long, abusive marriages in which both partners claim to be fine. Hong Kong's relationship is more transactional: it is hot and wet for half the year, and the residents have built a vast network of air-conditioned corridors to avoid ever having to feel it.

Neither is sensible, though both are impressive. A Canadian will tell you cheerfully that minus twenty is "actually fine if there's no wind". A Hong Konger will tell you that they have not stepped outdoors since May, and they are not lying. They simply walk from one chilled mall to another via a footbridge.

## Canada

The key to understanding the Canadian climate is that it is predictable. The country knows precisely when the cold arrives, and so it has legislation, equipment, garages, tire-swap appointments and a national vocabulary for it. The Montreal guide notes a tire swap costs roughly sixty to a hundred and twenty dollars and that garages fill up in November, which tells you everything: in Canada, winter is a booking you make in advance. Add boots rated for minus twenty, towing for cars left in orange-sign snow-clearing zones and black ice during the March thaw, and the winter is less a season than a logistics project.

Canada's winter is not weather, it is infrastructure. The Quebec government requires winter tires on Quebec-registered vehicles, including rentals, from 1 December to 15 March, with fines of $200 to $300, and tires must carry the winter pictogram or studs. Studs are permitted from 15 October to 1 May. In Ontario winter tires are not legally required, though they can earn an insurance discount of a few per cent, and a Toronto guide reports sidewalks must be cleared within twelve hours of snowfall. Montreal's guide reports January averages around minus 14 degrees, wind chill of minus 25 to minus 30, roughly 210 centimetres of snow a year and a December sunset about 4:10 in the afternoon. Heating bills of three to four hundred dollars from December to March are cited for Toronto. These are secondary-source figures, so treat them as approximate.

Then there is the underground. Toronto's PATH runs about thirty kilometres between Union Station and City Hall, and has been called a notoriously confusing maze. Montreal's RESO tunnel network is about the same length, give or take a kilometre depending on the source. Both exist because people decided it was more reasonable to build a city beneath the city than to leave the house.

A contributor on Expat.com has simple advice: keep two wardrobes, buy the winter one after you arrive and treat it as an investment, because whatever the weather, there are clothes for it. Cold, sunny, calm days beat cold, wet, windy ones, the same poster adds, and Canadians discuss the weather constantly. Snow days are common everywhere except Vancouver and Victoria. That conversation is the real coping mechanism. Canadians do not suffer in silence; they suffer in company, which makes it social.

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong's climate is subtropical. Expat Arrivals describes summers from May to September that are hot and humid, peaking around 33 degrees, with thunderstorms. October to early December is considered the best stretch, with low humidity and clear skies. Winter, December to February, averages around 15 degrees, cloudy, dry and with no snow or frost. Typhoon season runs from May to November, with heavy rain, flooding and landslides, and a ranked signal system that tells the city whether to carry on, go home or lock the windows.

The coping strategy is architectural. A seven-year resident on Expat.com recommends light tailored summer suits, routing walks through air-conditioned malls and keeping a warm overcoat for the winter. Employers, the poster says, warn staff about typhoons and may release them early, and ferries are disrupted. Residents tape windows in a crisscross pattern. A Talesmag post report says winters are wonderful, with highs in the sixties Fahrenheit and low humidity, while summers hit the nineties and the humidity can cause mould in some apartments. The answer to damp, in a city of tiny flats, is a dehumidifier the size of a small refrigerator.

The typhoon system is the most civilised part of it. Rather than a vague warning, Hong Kong has a ranked signal system, and the whole city knows what each level means. When the signal rises, ferries are disrupted, employers decide whether to send people home, and a shopping district empties in an orderly fashion. It is government by traffic light, and it works, because nobody wants to be the person who argued with a signal and lost. Landslides and flooding are not abstractions on those hillsides.

A caution on the evidence: there is a gap in my research. I did not find a first-person account by a Canadian living in Hong Kong, so I cannot tell you how a Winnipeg native copes with an August humidity of that kind, though I suspect with a great deal of complaining and a rapid conversion to the air-conditioned lifestyle. What I can say is that both climates reward the same virtue, which is respect. Ignore the winter and you freeze; ignore the typhoon signal and you flood.

Hong Kong's weather also distorts the calendar. The best weather arrives just as the working year accelerates, and nobody has time to enjoy it. October is a golden month and everyone spends it indoors.

## The Verdict

Hong Kong wins on drama, and Canada wins on honesty. A typhoon signal is a thrilling thing, a bureaucratic promise that the sky is serious and you are excused. But a Canadian winter simply asks you to survive it, without ceremony.

If I had to live in one for a year, I would choose Hong Kong. Humidity is miserable, but it is miserable in a warm way. It makes you sweat, not shatter. Canadians will say winter builds character. So does a long walk to a bus stop at minus twenty-five, and I would like my character without the frostbite, thanks.

There is also a social difference. In Canada, bad weather is a shared enemy, and strangers will help you push a car out of a snowbank. In Hong Kong, bad weather is a scheduling problem, and strangers will help you by getting out of your way. Both are forms of consideration, but only one of them involves a stranger with a shovel.

But I cannot deny Canada's advantage in one regard: the winter ends, and when it does, an entire nation emerges blinking onto patios in shorts. Hong Kong's weather never really ends. It just changes brand.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com forum, seven-year Hong Kong resident — wear light tailored summer suits, walk through air-conditioned malls and keep a warm overcoat for winter; employers warn staff about typhoons and may let them go early.*

> *Expat.com forum, Canada — buy winter clothing after you arrive because "whatever the weather, there are clothes for it and it's worth the investment"; cold, sunny, calm days beat cold, wet, windy ones, and Canadians talk about the weather constantly.*

> *Expat Arrivals, Hong Kong — a British expat, Alison, says the outdoor life and good weather are a real improvement over the UK, a reminder that the verdict depends on where you came from.*

## Conclusion

Weather is the topic every new arrival thinks is small talk. Then the first typhoon signal rises, or the first January wind-chill arrives, and you realise it was always the main subject.

Canada teaches you to layer, to plan and to chat about the forecast with strangers. Hong Kong teaches you to dress light, to hide from the sun and to take warnings seriously. Either way, buy the right clothes, pay attention to the locals and never say "it can't get that bad". It always can.
