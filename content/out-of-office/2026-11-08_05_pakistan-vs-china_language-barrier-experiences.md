# Karachi Will Argue With You In Better English Than You Have; Shanghai Will Just Smile And Switch To WeChat

🇵🇰 🇨🇳 *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular humiliation reserved for the monolingual Westerner who lands in a country assuming the whole world has been quietly learning their language in preparation. Pakistan will disabuse you of that notion gently, mostly by being better at English than you expected, occasionally better than you are. China will disabuse you of it completely, immediately, and without the faintest flicker of apology.

I have had an argument about Pakistani cricket statistics in English more grammatically precise than my own. I have also stood in a Shanghai pharmacy pointing at my own throat making a noise like a dying seal, which, in fairness, did eventually work.

## 🇵🇰 Do's and Don'ts: Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect English fluency at a genuinely high level in business, media, and educated urban circles — Pakistan has consistently ranked above regional peers on global English proficiency measures | Assume this extends uniformly to every rickshaw driver, market vendor, or small-town office — English competence drops off hard outside the urban middle and upper classes |
| Learn a handful of Urdu courtesy phrases anyway — it costs nothing, buys enormous goodwill, and signals effort in a country that notices effort | Treat English fluency as proof of Western cultural alignment; it's a colonial-era legacy and a class marker here, not an invitation to assume shared values |
| Use English confidently in professional and business settings — contracts, meetings, and official correspondence routinely run in English without friction | Forget that English carries real class baggage here; fluency is tied to private-school access and urban privilege in ways that can make locals touchy about the subject |
| Pick up basic Urdu script recognition for signage, especially outside Karachi, Lahore and Islamabad | Assume regional languages (Punjabi, Pashto, Sindhi, Balochi) are "just dialects" of Urdu — they're distinct languages with their own serious literary traditions |

## 🇨🇳 Do's and Don'ts: China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Install a translation app and a VPN before you land — Pleco, DeepL and WeChat's built-in translator are basically load-bearing infrastructure for your daily life | Expect English to work reliably anywhere outside international hotels, top-tier offices, and a handful of expat-dense neighbourhoods in Shanghai or Beijing |
| Learn to read a menu phonetically, even if you can't speak a word — recognising characters for meat, spice level and "no" will save you from some genuinely regrettable meals | Assume Mandarin fluency is optional if you're staying more than a year; multiple expat surveys consistently rank language as the single biggest daily-life obstacle in China |
| Get comfortable communicating entirely through WeChat, including payments, translation, and even ordering food — it is the actual national interface, not an app among apps | Expect pointing and gesturing to reliably work; tonal misunderstandings and cultural gesture gaps cause more confusion than they solve |
| Hire or befriend a bilingual colleague early and lean on them hard for the first six months — nearly every long-term expat says the same thing in hindsight | Underestimate how isolating the language gap gets socially, not just logistically — expat forums consistently flag loneliness, not paperwork, as the real cost |

## Pakistan: English As Inheritance, Not Import

Pakistan's relationship with English is a direct, unresolved hangover from the British Raj, and it has calcified into something genuinely distinctive: a country where fluent English isn't a tourist-facing performance, it's load-bearing national infrastructure. English remains an official language alongside Urdu, dominates higher education, courts, and most serious business, and — per recent comparative reporting — Pakistan has actually out-ranked India on several global English-proficiency measures in recent years, a fact that gets repeated locally with no small amount of pride.

What this means practically for an expat is that the language barrier, where it exists, is really a class barrier wearing a linguistic disguise. In Karachi boardrooms, Lahore universities, and Islamabad's diplomatic circuit, you will conduct entire working relationships in English without friction, occasionally feeling slightly outclassed by the precision of it. Step outside that stratum — into a rural market, a small-town government office, a rickshaw at 11pm — and English evaporates almost entirely, replaced by Urdu or one of the country's genuinely distinct regional languages: Punjabi, Pashto, Sindhi, Balochi, each with real literary weight behind it, not footnotes to Urdu.

The friction expats actually report isn't comprehension, it's the social subtext. English fluency here is tangled up with private education, urban privilege, and a colonial legacy that plenty of Pakistanis have complicated feelings about — so leaning entirely on your English, without even a token Urdu phrase, can read as obliviousness rather than convenience, however fluent the room actually is.

The Friday Times has written at length about English functioning here as a de facto class credential rather than a neutral communication tool — fluency sorts job applicants, marks out which school someone attended, and signals a particular kind of urban, often elite, upbringing in a way that has very little to do with the language's actual usefulness. The EF English Proficiency Index's own regional fact sheets consistently place Pakistan's major cities well ahead of the national average, which only reinforces the pattern: this is a country where language proficiency maps almost perfectly onto geography and income, and an expat who only ever interacts with the fluent, urban upper tier can walk away with a badly skewed sense of how universal that fluency actually is.

## China: Fluency Or Isolation, No Middle Ground

China offers no such safety net. Outside the most international pockets of Shanghai, Beijing and Shenzhen, English simply isn't a working assumption anyone makes, and expat forums — ikkyinchina's blunt "China expat challenges guide" chief among them — consistently name language as the single most persistent daily obstacle, ranking above visas, pollution, or homesickness. This isn't a comprehension gap so much as a totally different operating system: tonal Mandarin, a character-based writing system with zero overlap with the Latin alphabet, and a cultural tendency to avoid the awkwardness of a failed English exchange by simply not attempting one.

What makes modern China survivable without fluency, where it wouldn't have been a decade ago, is WeChat. The app has absorbed translation, payment, food ordering, ride-hailing and most social coordination into one interface so completely that expat guides increasingly describe it less as an app and more as a parallel nervous system for daily life. Combined with Pleco (the expat Mandarin dictionary that's reached near-cult status) and a decent VPN, a determined newcomer can function — shop, eat, commute — almost entirely without speaking the language, which simply wasn't true even five years ago.

But function isn't connection. Multiple long-term expat accounts converge on the same quiet admission: the real cost of the language gap in China isn't logistical, it's social. You can order food and pay rent through an app forever. You cannot build a genuine friendship, office camaraderie, or local reputation through one, and that isolation compounds in a way Pakistan's English-fluent professional class simply never forces on you.

There's a specific professional flavour to this too. Academic research on expatriate adjustment in China, including work out of Tilburg University specifically examining language and culture for incoming expatriates, has found that Mandarin ability — even partial, workplace-functional ability rather than fluency — correlates strongly with how effectively foreign managers are actually able to lead local teams, as opposed to simply directing them through a translator. The expats who report genuine professional success tend to be the ones who treated their first six months as a deliberate, almost embarrassing immersion period — bad tones, mangled tenses, visible effort — rather than the ones who held out for fluency before ever attempting a sentence.

## The Verdict

Pakistan wins on pure accessibility — if you're Anglophone, you will never be functionally stranded, only occasionally class-flagged. China wins on honesty — it never pretends the barrier isn't there, forcing a real decision: learn it, or accept a WeChat-mediated half-life. If you want ease, go to Karachi. If you want to actually be changed by the experience of learning a language from zero, against your will, under genuine social pressure, China will do that to you whether you consent or not.

There's an uncomfortable mirror here worth naming plainly. Pakistan's English fluency is a colonial inheritance, and leaning on it uncritically means quietly benefiting from a historical imposition you had nothing to do with and everything to gain from. China's language wall owes nothing to anyone else's history, and demands the actual effort English speakers have, for generations, been globally excused from making. One barrier is a gift you didn't earn; the other is a bill nobody else is going to pay for you.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *"I thought I could coast on English in Shanghai for a year. I lasted about six weeks before I started genuinely resenting myself for not learning any Mandarin before I came." — ikkyinchina.com, paraphrased*

> *"People here are shocked how good the English is in professional settings, then shocked again two hours later in a taxi when nobody understands a word." — Expat.com China forum commenter discussing a different but comparable Anglophone-assumption gap, paraphrased*

> *"The isolation isn't about ordering food, WeChat solves that. It's not being able to make a joke land with your coworkers for the first year." — Reddit r/Shanghai, paraphrased*

## The Parting Shot

Pakistan will let you coast on English until the moment it quietly judges you for coasting. China will never let you coast at all, and will instead hand you an app that does everything except let you actually belong. Learn ten words of whichever language you land in — not because you'll need them to survive, but because the ten words are the only part of either culture that's actually asking something of you.
