🇩🇴 Dominican Republic 🇨🇭 Switzerland
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In the Dominican Republic, grocery shopping is a relationship, not a transaction — you have a colmado on your corner, you have a guy, and that guy will extend you credit until payday without a word of paperwork because he's known your mother since before you were born. In Switzerland, grocery shopping is a closely timed military operation against a clock that stops entirely on Sundays, when the entire concept of buying bread becomes, by law, someone else's problem until Monday.

I have been the person frantically running through a Swiss Migros at 6:58pm doing arithmetic on whether I have time for cheese. I have also been the person standing at a Dominican colmado window at 11pm buying exactly one egg and a cigarette, on credit, with zero judgment offered in either direction. Both systems work. Neither system would survive being swapped into the other country for even a week.

## Do's & Don'ts: Dominican Republic

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a relationship with your neighbourhood colmado — small daily purchases are the norm, not bulk shopping | Assume a weekly big-box run replaces the colmado; locals use both, for very different purposes |
| Expect to buy "fiao" (on credit) at a trusted colmado, settled informally later | Try this at a supermarket chain; credit culture is strictly a small-shop, relationship-based thing |
| Haggle lightly at informal markets (mercados) for produce; it's expected, not rude | Haggle at a chain supermarket's fixed-price register; that will just confuse the cashier |
| Shop late — many colmados are effectively open into the night | Panic if you forgot something after 8pm; something, somewhere, is still open |

## Do's & Don'ts: Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Finish your weekly shop before 6-7pm on weekdays, earlier on Saturdays (often 4-5pm) | Plan a Sunday grocery run; nearly everything is closed nationwide by law, with rare train-station exceptions |
| Bring your own bags and bag your own groceries quickly — the belt keeps moving | Dawdle at checkout; the cashier and the queue behind you will both silently judge the pace |
| Buy a Migros Cumulus or Coop Supercard and actually use the loyalty discounts | Ignore loyalty programs; they meaningfully affect prices in an already expensive market |
| Stock up deliberately on Saturday for the Sunday-Monday gap | Get caught needing milk on a Sunday; your only real options are a train-station Coop or a gas station markup |

## Dominican Republic: the colmado is the system

The colmado — a small, often family-run corner shop — is the actual backbone of Dominican grocery life, more than any supermarket chain. It sells everything in small, affordable units: a single egg, a cigarette, a splash of cooking oil poured from a larger container, because the colmado understands that many customers shop daily, in small amounts, based on what today's money allows. This isn't a quaint inefficiency. It's a credit system, a social safety net, and a convenience store operating on trust, all at once — "fiao" (buying on credit, settled whenever you're able) runs on reputation rather than paperwork, and a good colmado owner knows exactly who's reliable and who needs a gentler leash.

Supermarkets exist and are used for bulk shopping, imported goods, and the kind of big monthly stock-up that doesn't suit a corner shop's shelf space — but they coexist with the colmado rather than replacing it. An expat who only shops at the supermarket misses an entire parallel economy of credit, neighbourhood gossip, and late-night emergency purchases that locals rely on constantly.

Market haggling, at informal mercados for fruit and vegetables, is a separate skill again — mild, good-natured negotiation is expected, and a price quoted first is rarely the final price. Newcomers who pay the first number named aren't admired for their straightforwardness; they're just quietly understood to be new.

## Switzerland: the clock is the law

Switzerland's grocery culture is shaped almost entirely by its shop-closing laws, which are genuinely strict, cantonally variable, and non-negotiable. Most shops close by 6:30-7pm on weekdays, earlier on Saturdays, and are shut entirely on Sundays nationwide, with the narrow exception of shops inside major train stations (which charge a visible premium for the privilege of existing on a day of rest). This isn't a lack of demand for Sunday shopping — it's a deliberate policy choice, rooted in labour protections and a cultural commitment to a shared day off, and it is enforced with a consistency that surprises almost every newcomer exactly once, expensively.

The result is a nationwide Saturday-afternoon ritual: a slightly tense, time-pressured stock-up run, because getting it wrong means a Sunday spent either going without or paying inflated prices at a petrol station. Checkout itself is brisk and self-service-oriented — you bag your own groceries, quickly, because the belt does not slow down for you and the person behind you is visibly calculating how long you're taking.

Swiss groceries are also simply expensive by most standards, which has turned loyalty-card culture (Migros's Cumulus, Coop's Supercard) into something closer to a serious household budgeting tool than a gimmick. Locals take the discounts seriously because the baseline prices make it worth taking seriously.

Delivery culture is the newest fault line between the two. In the Dominican Republic, grocery delivery has exploded in popularity, but it's layered onto the colmado system rather than replacing it — apps now let you order from your own trusted neighbourhood colmado, meaning the relationship-based model simply gained a digital front door rather than being disrupted by it, and the delivery rider is often, charmingly, someone the colmado owner already knows personally. Switzerland has been slower and more cautious here, partly because its strict labour protections make gig-economy delivery work more regulated and expensive to scale than in many other markets, and partly because Swiss consumers, accustomed to a dependable weekly supermarket routine, haven't needed the convenience as urgently as more time-pressed, car-free urban populations elsewhere. The practical result is that a Dominican expat in Zürich who assumes they can simply order groceries at 9pm on a Tuesday the way they would back home finds the local delivery ecosystem thinner, slower, and considerably more expensive than expected — one more small way the Sunday-closure mindset quietly shapes the rest of the week too — a single cultural rule about rest ends up rippling outward into logistics, pricing, and even how quickly a new market is allowed to grow.

## The Verdict

The Dominican Republic wins, decisively, on humanity — nowhere else have I been extended credit for a single egg based purely on vibes and my landlady vouching for me. Switzerland wins, just as decisively, on predictability — you will never once wonder whether the supermarket is open, because the rule is simple and absolute: not on Sundays, ever, anywhere, no exceptions, don't even ask. One system trusts people. The other trusts the clock. If forced to choose which one I'd want running my actual life, it's the colmado, every time — but I'd want Switzerland's punctuality running my flights.

## Conclusion

What's actually being compared here isn't convenience — it's what each culture decided grocery shopping is *for*. In the Dominican Republic, it's for maintaining relationships and surviving month to month with dignity intact. In Switzerland, it's for efficient provisioning within a system that has decided, as a matter of policy, that rest matters more than retail. Both are coherent. Both are, in their own register, quietly moral positions about how people should live. Just don't try to buy milk in Zürich on a Sunday evening expecting Dominican flexibility to magically appear. It won't. The shop is closed, the law says so, and no amount of charm has ever once changed a Swiss cantonal statute.
