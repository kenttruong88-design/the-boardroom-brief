# Ghana Parties for Its Ancestors and Britain Takes a Bank Holiday to Wait in a Garden Centre

🇬🇭 Ghana vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ghana celebrates like a country that has been waiting all year for the excuse. Festivals have drums, chiefs under enormous umbrellas, kente cloth, ancestors invoked with ceremonial food, and communities that treat remembering where they come from as a full-contact sport. A Ghanaian festival is loud, colourful, deeply serious and delightful in roughly equal measure.

The United Kingdom has bank holidays. These are days on which the nation, granted an official opportunity to celebrate its heritage, queues for a garden centre, argues about parking and watches the weather ruin a barbecue. There is genuine charm in this, and a genuine absence of drums. Moving from one to the other is like going from a carnival to a very polite traffic jam.

**United Kingdom**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|

## Ghana

A Ghanaian festival is a living history lesson with a party attached. An article carried by the Daily Post, credited to GhanaWeb, describes one such event: Ga communities from the UK and Ireland held a joint Homowo celebration in London, under the theme of peace and stability. Homowo, which marks the end of a famine in Accra, involves the ritual sprinkling of a traditional food to invoke the ancestors. The mere fact that this happened in a British venue says something: the tradition does not need Accra, it travels.

At home, the calendar is full. A guide published by Expat Arrivals lists the statutory holidays, among them New Year's Day, Independence Day on the sixth of March, Labour Day, Founder's Day on the fourth of August, Christmas and Boxing Day, with Eid also observed. It adds a lovely administrative detail: when a holiday falls on a weekend, it moves to the following Monday. Ghana, a country that understands the human right to a lie-in, refuses to waste a holiday on a Saturday.

Alongside the formal calendar lies the festival season of the regions, with durbars, processions and harvest ceremonies that blend ancestry, spirituality and spectacle. Christmas is also enormous: church, family, food, and a general expectation that you will return home if you have the means. The sense of obligation is part of the pleasure. Festivals are not optional leisure. They are how a community checks that it still exists.

What the expat leaves behind, therefore, is not just a few days off. It is a calendar of belonging. Ghanaians abroad often describe a persistent homesickness around these dates, and they respond by creating them again, in church halls, community centres and rented rooms. Which is exactly what the London Homowo did.

## The United Kingdom

The British calendar is a fascinating contradiction. It holds some of the world's most beloved celebrations, Christmas and Bonfire Night chief among them, and then administers them with a restrained, slightly embarrassed air. There are bank holidays, which give most workers a long weekend, and there is the national habit of using them for DIY, garden centres and complaining about traffic.

Christmas is the major event. It is intensely domestic and intensely commercial, with shops decorated in October and the country shutting down almost entirely on the twenty-fifth. For a Ghanaian arriving from a Christmas full of church music and extended family, the British version can feel like a lovely, quiet, somewhat lonely meal. You will need to build your own crowd.

Another important point is that religious festivals outside the Christian calendar do not generally come with a day off. A HuffPost UK piece on why Eid is not a public holiday explains the issue: Eid's date depends on the moon, so staff must take leave or work, and some employers have introduced flexible systems that let staff swap standard public holidays for the festival they actually observe. It is a Muslim-focused example, but the principle applies to anyone whose calendar sits outside the national default. The UK is formally secular and practically Christian in its rhythm, and you will need to plan your own.

There is, however, a real strength: the diaspora infrastructure. The 2005 Homowo report noted that a Ghana High Commission official urged Ghanaians in the UK to obey British law, which is the kind of gentle bureaucratic reminder that makes everyone smile. But the community organisations, churches and associations are deep and active, and in cities with large Ghanaian populations you can find a real festival if you look.

## The Verdict

Ghana wins this one in a walk, and I will not pretend there is a contest. A culture that builds festivals from ancestry, hunger, history and drums beats a culture that builds holidays from sandwiches and a vague sense of obligation. The emotional charge is simply different.

The UK deserves credit for a few things. It is efficient, it is orderly, and its holiday calendar is stable and predictable. Christmas is magical when it works, and bonfire night is gloriously anarchic for a nation that otherwise queues. There is also a growing multicultural scene that brings real festivals to British streets. Carnival, Diwali lights and Eid gatherings are part of the national picture now.

But if you want a country that treats a festival as a sacred and joyful duty, Ghana is the answer. The UK gives you the days off. Ghana gives you the reason. And on balance, a reason is worth more than a day.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Daily Post Ghana (2005), on a London Homowo — Ga communities from across the UK and Ireland held a joint celebration under a peace and stability theme, with a chief sprinkling a traditional food to call on the ancestors, in a British hall rather than an Accra street.*

> *Daily Post Ghana (2005) — a Ghana High Commission official used the occasion to urge Ghanaians in the UK to obey British law, a reminder that even a sacred festival comes with a licence from the council.*

> *Expat Arrivals, Ghana holidays guide — a holiday that lands on a weekend is moved to the following Monday, which a UK-based Ghanaian may find either deeply civilised or deeply unfair, depending on which side of the border they are standing.*

## Conclusion

A holiday is a calendar entry. A festival is a promise. Ghana keeps its promises with food, drums and elders, and the UK keeps its with a long weekend and a garden hose. Both are sincere. Only one will make you dance.

If you are a Ghanaian in Britain, build your own calendar and stick to it. Find the community hall, the church group, the association that still sprinkles the ritual food in September. And if you are a Briton moving to Ghana, bring a decent outfit and an open mind. Someone will hand you a plate. Do not refuse it. That is not a holiday. It is an invitation to belong.
