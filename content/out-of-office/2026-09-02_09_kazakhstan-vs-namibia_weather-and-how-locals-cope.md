# One Country Drinks Hot Tea at Minus Thirty, the Other Just Waits for the Sun to Lose Interest

🇰🇿 Kazakhstan vs 🇳🇦 Namibia — weather and how locals cope

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Almaty in January will hand you -30°C on its worst days and a population that responds by drinking more tea, wrapping up in fur, and going skiing anyway. Windhoek in summer will hand you temperatures pushing 40°C, sometimes touching 50, and a population that has simply, quietly rearranged its entire day around avoiding the sun rather than fighting it. Neither country negotiates with its climate. Both have simply built lives around not being surprised by it anymore.

I've stood on Almaty ice with my eyelashes freezing together mid-blink, and I've felt Windhoek's midday heat press down like a physical weight the moment I stepped off an air-conditioned bus. Extreme weather, it turns out, doesn't humble everyone the same way — some places armour up, some places simply slow down.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇰🇿 Kazakhstan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in genuine fur-lined winter gear — the cold here is dry but relentless, dipping toward -30°C on the worst Almaty days | Underestimate the dry cold; bring lip balm and moisturiser, or your skin will make you regret it within days |
| Use underground walkways and heated public transport in winter — cities are genuinely built for the cold | Assume winter shuts everything down; Shymbulak ski resort and the Medeu ice rink run full winter seasons and draw locals out, not in |
| Lean into hot tea and winter menu items like sea buckthorn or honey-based soups — it's a real coping ritual, not a tourist gimmick | Expect a mild winter; snowfall peaks at around 11 days each in December and January, and it is not decorative |

**🇳🇦 Namibia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan outdoor activity around early morning or evening — midday sun in the hot season is not to be underestimated | Expect uniform heat nationwide; the coast is moderated by fog and cloud while Windhoek's central plateau runs hotter and drier |
| Stay hydrated aggressively during the December–March hot, rainy season | Assume warm days mean warm nights; temperatures can drop below 0°C overnight even after a 40°C day |
| Respect the day-night temperature swing when packing — layers matter more than raw heat tolerance | Forget that "winter" in Namibia (May–October) still reaches a pleasant 20–25°C by day; don't overpack for cold you won't get |

## Kazakhstan: Cities Built to Outlast the Cold

Almaty doesn't so much cope with winter as absorb it into the basic architecture of daily life. Average winter temperatures hover around a relatively survivable -6°C, but the outlying days — and there are always outlying days — can plunge to -30°C, cold enough to genuinely reshape what a normal day looks like. The response isn't to hide from it; it's to build infrastructure that simply refuses to notice. Underground walkways connect key parts of the city so pedestrians never have to fully surface into the worst of it. Public buses and trams run heated as standard, not as a premium feature. Powerful central heating keeps interiors genuinely warm rather than merely tolerable, and locals move through the season in full fur-lined gear with the kind of unbothered practicality that comes from doing this every single year of your life.

What surprised me most, researching this, was how specific the coping mechanisms get. The cold in Almaty is a dry cold, and that dryness is apparently worse for skin than the raw temperature — lip balm and moisturiser get mentioned again and again as genuinely essential winter kit, not an afterthought. Hot tea, chai, becomes a near-constant companion, and cafés lean into seasonal menus built around warming ingredients like sea buckthorn and honey, treating the cold as something to actively counter through diet and ritual rather than simply endure.

And crucially, winter in Kazakhstan isn't something the country waits out indoors. Snowfall peaks at around 11 days each in December and January, and rather than treat that as a reason to stay home, Almaty locals head for Shymbulak ski resort or the Medeu ice rink, both of which draw serious crowds through the entire snow season, December to April. It's a genuinely impressive cultural trick: turning an objectively brutal climate into a source of recreation rather than dread, purely through infrastructure, ritual, and sheer collective stubbornness.

## Namibia: A Country That Rearranges Its Whole Day Around the Sun

Windhoek runs on the opposite problem entirely — not too little heat but far, far too much of it, layered onto one of the more dramatic day-night temperature swings you'll encounter anywhere. Sitting at 1,700 metres on Namibia's semi-arid central plateau, the city experiences a genuinely subtropical arid climate: a hot, rainy season from December through March where temperatures can climb above 40°C and, on the worst days, push toward 50°C, followed by a dry season from May to October that still holds a pleasant 20-25°C by day — except at night, when temperatures can drop below freezing even after a scorching afternoon.

That swing is the detail that trips up newcomers hardest. You cannot simply dress for "hot" in Namibia; you have to dress for a single day that might demand both sun protection and a jacket, sometimes within the same twelve hours. The coast fares somewhat differently, moderated by fog and low cloud rolling in off the Atlantic, but Windhoek and the central highlands get neither that relief nor much mercy.

What genuinely impresses me, even without a definitive published playbook on daily coping rituals, is the structural response baked into how life here operates: outdoor activity clusters around early morning and evening, midday becomes a deliberately quieter stretch, and hydration isn't treated as optional advice but as basic survival logistics during the hot season. It's a slower, more deliberate relationship with the clock than most Western visitors arrive expecting — the day isn't uniform, it's shaped entirely around when the sun is tolerable and when it isn't, and locals have simply internalised that shape so completely it barely registers as an accommodation anymore.

## The Verdict

Kazakhstan fights its climate with infrastructure, engineering an entire city that barely has to slow down for -30°C. Namibia negotiates with its climate, restructuring the rhythm of the day rather than trying to out-build it. I have more respect, honestly, for Almaty's sheer defiance — heated trams and ski resorts in weather that would shut down most Western cities entirely. But Windhoek's quiet acceptance, the willingness to simply let the sun dictate the schedule rather than fight it, might be the more sustainable long-term strategy. Extreme cold, it turns out, is more fun to conquer. Extreme heat is wiser to simply outwait.

## Conclusion

Extreme weather has a way of exposing exactly how a country sees resilience. Kazakhstan sees it as something you build — heated infrastructure, warming rituals, ski resorts that turn brutal cold into a weekend plan. Namibia sees it as something you schedule around — a day reshaped entirely by when the sun is bearable and when it isn't. Visit either expecting to control the weather and you'll lose. Visit either willing to adapt on its terms — hot tea and fur in one, shade and hydration in the other — and you'll find both climates are considerably more livable than the raw numbers suggest. Just pack for both extremes in Namibia. The night has different plans than the day.
