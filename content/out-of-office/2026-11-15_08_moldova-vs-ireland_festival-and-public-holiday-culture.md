# Moldova Ties a Red and White String Around March. Ireland Paints the World Green and Calls It Culture

🇲🇩 Moldova vs 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Moldova celebrates the arrival of spring with a piece of thread. Specifically, a red and white one, with a small trinket, which you pin to your coat on the first of March and wear for the month like a promise to the weather. It is not a day off. It is not a parade. It is a private, almost shy gesture that millions of people understand without a word.

Ireland celebrates its patron saint by turning the planet green, closing the pubs on the way into town and reopening them within the hour. St Patrick's Day is the most successful piece of national branding in history, a holiday so effective that politicians fly around the globe to attend it. One country's biggest March ritual is a bow on a lapel. The other's is a worldwide marketing campaign with a hangover. Both are about the same thing: a nation saying, we are still here.

## Moldova

Moldova's festival calendar runs on tradition, church and kinship. According to OfficeHolidays, Martisor falls on 1 March every year and is not a public holiday, although it is widely observed in Moldova and Romania. The token is a red and white thread tied in a bow with a small trinket, red said to stand for summer or heat and white for winter or cold. They are sold in every town and village, bought as gifts for female relatives and worn through March for strength and health. At the end of the month, the threads are tied to a fruit tree branch, which is supposed to bring wealth, and the page notes that the custom may predate the Roman name, tracing it back to the Dacians, who it says began their year on 1 March.

That is the entire festival. A thread, a tree, a wish. I find it extraordinary how much meaning a nation can pack into something you could lose in a coat pocket.

Elsewhere the calendar is richer. Orthodox Easter is the great domestic feast, village patron saint days are a reason to feed forty relatives at once, and Independence Day on 27 August and Limba Noastră, the language day, on 31 August keep the country's identity in public view. I am drawing on general knowledge here, not a fetched source, but the pattern is consistent: Moldovan holidays are about people, food, memory and a certain stubborn pride.

The diaspora keeps them going. The IPN news agency reports Moldovan communities marking Martisor abroad, with an evening at the Palace of Nations in Geneva, events in Bologna and a poetry and folk-song night in Mestre organised by an association called Dacia. In Portugal, the Moldovan embassy and cultural centre hosted a band from the Chișinău Philharmonic. This is the Moldovan festival culture in miniature: wherever there is a diaspora, someone will find a hall, a piece of thread and a reason to sing.

The diaspora events are a useful measure of how seriously the tradition is taken. IPN says similar Martisor events were planned for March in Padova, Bologna and Trento, that the Moldovan consulate general in Bologna was involved, and that the embassy in Armenia and schools in the capital there also marked the day, although the page names the wrong capital, which is a reminder to read news items from small agencies with a pencil. The pattern, though, is plain. Moldovans abroad do not merely remember Martisor. They schedule it, and they do so in places with no obvious connection to Moldova except the people who moved there.

## Ireland

Ireland's calendar is just as reliant on the sacred, but it has learned how to export it. The official Irish Department of Foreign Affairs release from March 2026 shows the Minister of State, Thomas Byrne, ending his St Patrick's Day programme with a visit to Moldova, where he was to meet the president and senior officials and attend a reception in Chișinău for MPs, diplomats and what the release calls the small Irish community in Moldova. It also says the visit marked the establishment of Ireland's embassy there, and links Moldova's EU accession talks to Ireland's EU Council presidency later in 2026.

That is the whole logic of St Patrick's Day in a paragraph. A saint's feast becomes a diplomatic season, a ministerial itinerary and a global greeting card. It is the only holiday where a government sends its politicians abroad to be photographed in a good mood.

At home, Irish holiday culture is more modest and more layered. Bank holidays punctuate the year and move the whole country into long weekends. Halloween, which has Irish roots, is taken more seriously than anywhere else I have eaten a barmbrack. Christmas is a week of family and weather, and the local parade, fair or festival is a standard part of the social diary. What strikes outsiders is how participatory it all is: people march, dance, sing and turn up, even in the rain, especially in the rain.

There is a lesson in the comparison. Ireland took a religious feast and made it a national brand. Moldova kept a thread and let it stay a ritual. Neither is wrong. One is just louder.

The Irish release gives the political side of the same impulse. Thomas Byrne was due to meet President Maia Sandu and Cristina Gherasimov, the deputy prime minister for European integration, whom he had hosted in Dublin the month before, and State Secretary Carolina Perebinos of the Moldovan Foreign Ministry. The message was that Ireland's embassy in Chișinău marked a new relationship, and that Ireland's EU Council presidency later in 2026 would overlap with Moldova's accession talks. A holiday that started as a saint's day is now an instrument of foreign policy. A thread on a coat, by contrast, has never lobbied anyone, which is perhaps why it has lasted so long.

## The Verdict

Ireland wins on spectacle, and I will concede it grudgingly. No country has done more with a saint and a shamrock, and a St Patrick's Day parade in a wet Irish town has a warmth that no marketing department can manufacture. The Irish understand that a holiday is a performance, and they perform it well.

But Moldova wins on intimacy. A piece of red and white thread tied to a tree, a village feast with enough food for an army and an evening of poetry in an Italian hall carry a quiet seriousness that spectacle cannot buy. It is a culture that remembers what it is celebrating.

If I could keep one, I would take the Moldovan thread and the Irish pub. You need both: something small to believe in and somewhere large to say so.

## Conclusion

A holiday is a way of telling a country's story in a day. Moldova tells it in a thread. Ireland tells it in a parade.

If you move between the two, bring both: a ribbon for the lapel and a taste for company. And do not confuse quiet with small.

Some of the biggest festivals fit in your pocket.
