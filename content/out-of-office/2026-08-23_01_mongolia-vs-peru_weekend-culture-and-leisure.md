# A Day Off Is Either a Horse Race Across a Thousand Miles of Grass or Lunch That Refuses to End

🇲🇳 Mongolia vs 🇵🇪 Peru
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have now had my weekend plans hijacked on two continents by people who consider "relaxing" to mean either galloping toward the horizon on an animal that could kill me, or sitting at a table for the entire daylight span while a grandmother refuses to let me leave until I have eaten a fourth helping of causa. Neither culture has heard of a nap. Both consider the very idea of "me time" faintly pathological.

Mongolia treats the weekend as an excuse to remember it is still, underneath the Ulaanbaatar apartment blocks, a nation of herders — everyone with a functioning set of legs seems to disappear to a ger camp the moment Friday hits. Peru treats the weekend as a scheduling problem, because you cannot possibly visit every branch of the family, eat every dish, and still make it to the market by Sunday morning, and yet somehow they manage all three before anyone else has finished breakfast.

## Do's & Don'ts: Mongolia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept an invitation to a countryside ger, even a spontaneous one | Refuse the fermented mare's milk out of squeamishness — it's rude, not optional |
| Bring a small gift if you're visiting a family's ger | Point your feet at the hearth or step on the threshold |
| Try archery or ankle-bone shooting if offered at a gathering | Assume "weekend" means Ulaanbaatar stays put — half the city empties out |
| Dress for genuinely unpredictable weather, even in July | Expect wifi, or indeed much of anything electronic, once you're past the ring road |

## Do's & Don'ts: Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clear your entire Sunday for a family lunch — it is not a suggestion | Try to leave a Sunday almuerzo before the third course, or the second bottle |
| Visit a Sunday market like Chinchero's for the real texture of the week | Treat street food or market stalls as beneath a proper weekend outing |
| Learn at least one traditional dance step before a fiesta finds you | Assume every weekend is quiet — festival season turns entire towns into parades |
| Budget a full afternoon for the Magic Water Circuit or a plaza stroll | Rush a Peruvian host who is mid-story about a cousin's wedding |

## Mongolia: The Country Where the City Is Just Where You Keep Your Coat

Ulaanbaatar during the week is a smog-choked, traffic-strangled Soviet fever dream with better coffee than it has any right to have. Ulaanbaatar on a Friday evening is a ghost town, because everyone who can manage it — and Mongolians manage it with an ease that shames the rest of us — has driven, or been driven, several hours into genuinely nowhere to sit in a canvas tent their family has used for generations.

This is not glamping. Nobody here is doing this for the aesthetic, though god knows the aesthetic is unbeatable — grassland running flat to a horizon that makes you feel, correctly, insignificant. This is the actual continuation of a nomadic culture that never fully agreed to be urbanised, no matter how many high-rises went up around the Chinggis Khaan statue. The Naadam Festival, held nationally in July but echoed in miniature at countryside gatherings all summer, is essentially the physical manifestation of what Mongolians think a good time looks like: wrestling, archery, and horse racing, performed with total sincerity by children as young as five who will outride you, outshoot you, and then hand you a bowl of milk tea with better manners than you have ever managed in your life.

What nobody tells you is how quickly the pace changes. City Mongolia is transactional and brisk. Countryside Mongolia, the weekend Mongolia, operates on an entirely different clock — you arrive, you are fed, you are given a place to sleep, and the conversation happens whenever it happens, usually late, usually over more milk tea than any human digestive system was built for. There is no rushing a ger visit. I have tried. I lost.

## Peru: Where Sunday Lunch Is a Competitive Sport With No Winners, Only Survivors

Peruvians will tell you, with total confidence, that they invented the concept of relaxing. They have not — nobody who considers a six-hour lunch "relaxing" gets to claim that title — but I understand the impulse, because the Peruvian weekend is genuinely one of the more joyful pieces of choreography I've witnessed on this beat. Saturday is market day, errands, maybe a walk through whichever plaza your neighbourhood has been arguing about renovating for a decade. Sunday belongs, entirely and without negotiation, to family.

The almuerzo dominguero starts around one and does not have a stated end time, because stating an end time would suggest the meal is finite, and Peruvian hospitality does not deal in finite. Multiple courses arrive whether or not you have finished the previous one. Someone's cousin will show up uninvited and be seated without comment, because that is simply how many chairs a Peruvian household keeps on standby. Leaving early is technically possible but socially catastrophic, and I say this as someone who once tried to catch a flight and was very nearly re-routed by force of aunt.

Outside the family orbit, weekend Peru is a country that takes its outdoor leisure seriously in a way that has nothing to do with performative wellness. Lima's Magic Water Circuit turns an entire park into a nightly light show families genuinely queue for. Cusco's San Blas quarter fills with people just walking, looking, existing in public space together, which is a more radical act than it sounds once you've lived somewhere that doesn't do it. And should your weekend collide with one of the country's absurd number of regional festivals — and it will, because Peru appears to have a festival for every possible occasion including several I could not get anyone to properly explain — you will simply be swept into a parade you did not know was happening.

## The Verdict

If your idea of a weekend is doing something, Mongolia wins in a walk — or rather a gallop. There is a purity to disappearing into grassland with no phone signal and no obligation beyond being fed by strangers who are now, for the weekend, your family. It resets something in you that a spa day never will.

But if your idea of a weekend is being with people, endlessly, past the point of comfort and somehow out the other side into something like joy, Peru will not let go of you, and you will not particularly want it to. The Sunday almuerzo is exhausting in the way only genuine belonging is exhausting.

I refuse to crown a winner, because that is a coward's move dressed up as diplomacy, so here is my actual verdict: Mongolia gives you space. Peru gives you people. Choose based on which one you're currently starved of, and stop asking me to make the decision for you.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — "What do Mongolians actually do on their days off?" — paraphrased: people underestimate how normal it is for an entire office to just vanish on a Friday afternoon in summer because everyone's driving to a relative's countryside ger, and nobody thinks this is remarkable.*

## Conclusion

Somewhere between a Mongolian horizon and a Peruvian dining table is probably the correct amount of weekend, but neither country is interested in "probably correct" — they are interested in doing the thing properly, all the way, without half-measures. Mongolia will hand you a horse and trust you not to die. Peru will hand you a plate and trust you to eat everything on it, then a bit more. Both are, in their own entirely unreasonable ways, a masterclass in taking leisure seriously enough to make you feel guilty about how you spend yours. Go home and cancel your brunch reservation. It was never going to compare.
