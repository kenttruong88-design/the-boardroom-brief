# One Country Will Charge You a Small Fortune for a Bag of Peppers. The Other Will Sell You a Perfect Single-Serving Dinner From a Vending Machine.

🇳🇴🇯🇵 By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office

Norway's grocery bills exist to remind you, weekly, that you are living in one of the most expensive countries on Earth — a basic shop that would cost thirty euros in southern Europe routinely clears seventy here, and nobody so much as blinks. Japan's supermarkets and convenience stores, by contrast, have turned grocery shopping into a small masterclass in precision, freshness, and portion control, where even a single onigiri is arranged with more care than most countries put into a wedding cake.

I have wept, quietly, internally, at an Oslo checkout over a receipt for what amounted to eggs, bread, and a modest chicken. I have also stood in a Tokyo Lawson at midnight, genuinely moved by the quality of a 400-yen bento box. Only one of these experiences was expensive.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇳🇴 Norway**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shop at budget chains like Rema 1000 or Kiwi to soften the shock | Expect wine or spirits at a regular supermarket — that's Vinmonopolet's job |
| Bring your own bags — plastic ones cost extra, on principle | Be surprised by weekend closures; Sunday trading is still heavily restricted |
| Buy local produce in season — imported items carry a brutal markup | Assume "cheap" exists here — budget mentally before you shop, not after |
| Use self-checkout — it's standard and genuinely faster | Linger chatting at the till; efficiency is the unspoken social contract |

**🇯🇵 Japan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the discount sticker timing in the evening for reduced bento prices | Squeeze the fruit — pre-selected quality means it's already been done for you |
| Use a konbini for a genuinely good, fast meal, any hour, any day | Expect huge portions — Japanese packaging favours small, precise servings |
| Bring cash as backup — some smaller supermarkets remain cash-preferred | Assume all seafood is impossibly expensive; local, in-season fish is reasonable |
| Marvel at the seasonal fruit gift boxes — but budget accordingly if buying one | Bag your own groceries hurriedly; take the time others clearly do |

## Norway: Grocery Shopping as a Financial Event

Norwegian grocery shopping requires a level of mental preparation most countries reserve for larger purchases. Statistics Norway's own consumer data confirms what every resident already feels acutely: Norway is consistently ranked among the most expensive countries in Europe for basic groceries, and the gap between local, seasonal produce and anything imported is stark enough to reshape your entire shopping list around what's currently in season, purely to survive the receipt.

Budget chains like Rema 1000, Kiwi, and Coop Extra exist specifically to soften this blow, and locals genuinely shop around between them for marginal savings that would seem absurd to chase anywhere cheaper — driving an extra ten minutes to save the equivalent of a couple of euros on a weekly shop is treated as basic financial common sense here, not excessive frugality. Alcohol adds its own layer of complexity: anything above a modest strength can only be purchased at Vinmonopolet, the state-run monopoly, which keeps limited hours and closes notably early, catching visitors off guard who assumed a Friday evening bottle of wine would be a simple supermarket errand.

What genuinely surprised me was how normalised the expense has become among Norwegians themselves — there's little of the outraged commentary you'd expect; instead, a kind of resigned, practical acceptance, paired with genuinely impressive home cooking skills born of necessity, since eating out compounds the expense further still. Self-checkout is near-universal and used briskly, efficiently, without small talk, reflecting a broader Norwegian preference for functional, unfussy transactions. Sunday closures remain law for most retail, a holdover that still catches newcomers unprepared on a weekend with an empty fridge and nowhere open to fix it.

## Japan: Grocery Shopping as a Study in Precision

Japanese grocery culture operates on entirely different principles: freshness, portion precision, and presentation, executed with a consistency that borders on obsessive. Supermarkets stock produce individually inspected and often shrink-wrapped to preserve exact freshness, fruit is frequently sold as a genuine luxury item — a perfect melon can run the equivalent of fifty dollars as a gift item — while everyday seasonal produce remains genuinely reasonable, a contrast that confuses newcomers who assume all Japanese grocery shopping is expensive based on the gift-box exceptions alone.

The convenience store, or konbini, deserves recognition as one of Japan's genuine retail triumphs. JETRO's own retail data reflects just how central these stores are to daily Japanese life — not the sad, overpriced afterthought convenience stores represent elsewhere, but genuinely reliable sources for fresh, well-made meals at any hour. A midnight bento box from 7-Eleven, Lawson, or FamilyMart is prepared fresh daily, portioned with real care, and priced reasonably enough that many Japanese workers treat konbini meals as a legitimate, unremarkable dinner option rather than a last resort.

Best-by date culture runs deep here too — Japanese consumers are famously precise about freshness dating, and supermarkets respond with a rotating discount sticker system, typically appearing in the evening, that drops prices meaningfully on items approaching their sell-by window. Locals time evening shopping trips specifically around these discount windows, turning what would be waste elsewhere into a genuinely smart, normalised savings strategy. Bagging your own groceries is standard, done unhurriedly and with visible care, mirroring the same attentiveness staff bring to arranging displayed produce — nothing about Japanese grocery shopping is rushed, even at its most routine.

## The Verdict

Norway wins on nothing related to price, and everyone here would readily agree — this is a country whose grocery culture is defined entirely by managing genuine, structural expense, and it does so with impressive resourcefulness rather than complaint. Japan wins decisively on quality-to-price ratio, precision, and the sheer, quietly beautiful care put into even a convenience store bento.

If you want grocery shopping that teaches you real financial discipline, Norway will teach it whether you want the lesson or not. If you want grocery shopping that consistently, quietly impresses you, Japan delivers that at every single price point, gift melons excluded. I have never budgeted so carefully as I did in Oslo, and I have never been more delighted by a vending machine dinner than I was in Tokyo.

## Conclusion

Norway's supermarkets will teach you, firmly and repeatedly, the true cost of living somewhere beautiful, stable, and structurally expensive — a lesson best absorbed with a Rema 1000 loyalty card and low expectations. Japan's supermarkets and konbini will teach you that even routine, everyday food can be handled with genuine craft and care, no gift-box price tag required. Bring a healthy grocery budget to Norway, and bring an appetite for genuinely excellent convenience food to Japan. Only one of these countries will make peace with your wallet.
