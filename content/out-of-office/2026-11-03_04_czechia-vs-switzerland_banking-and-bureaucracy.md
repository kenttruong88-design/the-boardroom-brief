# Prague Will Stamp It. Zurich Will Means-Test It. Bring Snacks Either Way.

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular flavour of European bureaucracy that doesn't announce itself as hostile — it's far too polite for that — and simply ensures you lose an entire Tuesday to a queue, a stamp, and a form that required a document you didn't know existed. Czechia and Switzerland have both perfected this art form, and they've done it in almost opposite directions, which is the sort of detail that makes relocating genuinely entertaining rather than merely exhausting.

One country will make you collect physical rubber-stamp approvals like a scavenger hunt designed by a sadist. The other will charge you a wildly different tax rate depending on which of its twenty-six cantons you happened to pick an apartment in, sometimes without telling you until the bill arrives. Both will test your patience. Only one will test your math.

## Do's and Don'ts: Czechia 🇨🇿

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Open an account at Fio Banka or Česká spořitelna if you want the least painful onboarding | Expect officials at the foreign police outpost to switch to English; budget patience and a translator app |
| Get your rodné číslo (national ID number) sorted early; some digital account openings require it | Assume one bank's "2-3 day setup" story is universal; account freezes and delays are reported just as often |
| Keep every stamped document; Czech bureaucracy runs on paper trails, not systems that talk to each other | Underestimate the admin burden; Czech firms report spending close to 930 hours a year on tax-related paperwork |

## Do's and Don'ts: Switzerland 🇨🇭

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your canton's tax rate before signing a lease; Zug and Zurich are not the same country, financially | Assume you'll ever personally file a tax return; most foreign employees are taxed automatically at source |
| Budget for a first tax bill that is an *estimate* — it can swing by thousands once properly assessed | Expect your Swiss bank account to survive a move abroad unquestioned; closures over "domicile abroad" are common |
| Get comfortable with German or French paperwork; translation help is often a paid, separate errand | Relax just because you're an EU-adjacent country; a C-permit changes your entire filing obligation overnight |

Czechia's bureaucracy has a folk villain, and its name is the razítko — the rubber stamp. It is not an exaggeration or an expat myth; Czech-language business press runs regular pieces on entrepreneurs complaining that "bureaucracy scares off investors" and that stamp-collecting is actively "destroying" small business formation. A PwC/World Bank assessment across 178 countries ranked Czechia 113th globally for tax-system efficiency and a genuinely startling 168th for administrative burden — the worst in the entire EU — with the average firm burning close to 930 hours a year on tax admin alone. The foreign-residence process compounds this: one widely cited account of the Koněvova Street foreign-police outpost in Prague describes "staff shortages, endless queues, omnipresent arrogance," with officials rarely willing to engage in anything but Czech. Nearly 390,000 foreigners hold long-term or permanent residence in Czechia, a jump of over 100,000 in roughly two decades, and every one of them has their own stamp story. The genuinely useful intelligence, courtesy of actual residents comparing notes online: Česká spořitelna will open an account with just a passport; Air Bank wants your residence-permit card as primary ID if you're non-EU; Fio Banka gets the most consistent praise for a lenient, English-friendly setup, though its app has been called "outdated"; Raiffeisenbank and ČSOB both draw a split verdict, with some users reporting smooth two-to-three-day openings and others reporting arbitrary account freezes and month-long delays demanding the same proof of residence repeatedly. Bureaucracy here, in short, is a known, documented, almost folkloric inconvenience — slow, paper-heavy, occasionally absurd, but rarely mysterious once you've heard the warnings.

Switzerland's problem is subtler and, I'd argue, more insidious, because it doesn't look like bureaucracy at first — it looks like federalism. There is no single "Swiss tax rate." There are twenty-six cantons, each setting its own rate on top of a federal cap of 11.5%, which produces a genuinely absurd outcome: an effective combined rate of roughly 40% in Zurich versus just 22% in Zug, for the same income, in the same country, a forty-minute train ride apart. New arrivals routinely discover their first tax bill is only an estimate — one that can later swing by as much as twenty thousand Swiss francs once properly assessed, a correction that arrives with no particular urgency about warning you first. There's a genuine mercy buried in here, though: tax residency only triggers after thirty days of employment or ninety days without economic activity, and most foreign employees never file their own return at all — tax is deducted invisibly at source, and only C-permit holders or those married to Swiss nationals handle the full cantonal paperwork directly. Where Switzerland's bureaucracy turns genuinely hostile is banking, and specifically banking for Swiss people who've had the audacity to move abroad themselves: a SwissInfo investigation gathered a string of first-person accounts of banks closing accounts over "domicile abroad," one Zurich bank demanding an extra CHF 960 a year to keep an account open, another charging CHF 240 annually "just for every tiny service, like transferring money," and a PostFinance compliance letter that interrogated one expat over a single rent payment and her entire "connection to Indonesia." One account-holder's verdict, which I'm simply going to let stand on its own: "I feel messed around by Swiss banks and let down by the state."

One more Czech wrinkle worth knowing before you sign anything: a handful of banks have built genuinely foreigner-friendly onboarding specifically to compete for the expat market — Raiffeisenbank and Komerční banka are both flagged by local guides as offering fully English-language apps, a small but real relief after a morning spent at the foreign-police outpost. And on the Swiss side, the permit category you hold quietly decides your entire bureaucratic experience: a B-permit holder on standard payroll may go years without filing a single form, while a C-permit holder or a newly self-employed arrival inherits the full, unforgiving cantonal paperwork apparatus overnight, with no transitional grace period to speak of.

The real contrast, then, isn't efficiency versus inefficiency — it's visible friction versus invisible friction. Czechia tells you exactly how annoyed to be, upfront, with a stamp in your hand as proof. Switzerland tells you everything is fine, deducts your tax silently, and then closes your account eleven years later over a postal code it didn't like the look of.

## The Verdict

If you want bureaucracy that announces itself honestly and lets you plan around the inconvenience, Czechia wins on sheer transparency, even if the transparency is mostly a queue. If you want a system that feels effortless for years and then turns on you without warning over a technicality buried in cantonal tax code or a compliance algorithm's mood, Switzerland is your country, and I say that as someone who still hasn't forgiven a Zurich bank for a letter it sent me about a transfer to my own mother. I'm calling this one for Czechia — at least the razítko tells you, in advance, exactly how much of your week it intends to ruin.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/Prague (accessible mirror), paraphrased: Fio Banka is the most recommended account for English speakers, but ČSOB "repeatedly froze" one user's account asking for proof of residence "even after showing the same documents each time."*

> *SwissInfo witness accounts, Marianne Senften, paraphrased: paying CHF 240 a year just to keep a Swiss account open is, in her words, "a disgrace" for what amounts to basic, routine banking.*

> *SwissInfo witness accounts, Irene Naumann, paraphrased: a PostFinance compliance letter demanding an explanation for a single rent payment and her "connection to Indonesia" left "a very bitter aftertaste" and a lasting loss of trust in the bank.*

## Conclusion

Both countries will, eventually, make you photocopy something you were certain you'd already provided. Czechia will at least have the decency to make the inconvenience visible, physical, and stamped in triplicate, so you know precisely what you're up against. Switzerland will let you coast for years on autopilot taxation and then quietly reassess your entire financial relationship with the state via a letter you didn't see coming. Choose your bureaucracy the way you'd choose a bad weather system: not whether it rains, but whether you'd rather see the clouds coming.
