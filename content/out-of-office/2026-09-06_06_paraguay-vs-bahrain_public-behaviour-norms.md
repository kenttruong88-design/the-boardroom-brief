# One Country Will Tell You Exactly What It Thinks of Your Outfit, the Other Would Rather You Just Covered It Up

🇵🇾 Paraguay × 🇧🇭 Bahrain
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Paraguay is a country where a stranger at a bus stop will happily comment on your haircut, your weight, or the fact that you look tired, all delivered with such warmth that taking offence feels almost churlish. Bahrain is a country where the far more useful skill is knowing exactly how much shoulder, knee, or decolletage is socially acceptable in any given public space, because nobody is going to comment on it directly — you're simply expected to already know.

Public behaviour in Paraguay runs on an informal, unhurried directness that treats personal remarks as a form of warmth rather than intrusion. Public behaviour in Bahrain runs on a much more codified sense of modesty and decorum, where the comfort of not being personally commented on is balanced against a much clearer, quieter set of expectations about how to present yourself. Both systems reward the same thing in the end — paying close attention to the room you're in.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇵🇾 Paraguay**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take personal comments as friendly warmth, not rudeness | Take offence at direct remarks about your appearance or habits |
| Expect a relaxed, unhurried pace to most public interactions | Rush a greeting or skip small talk before getting to business |
| Greet with a cheek kiss even among new acquaintances | Assume a handshake alone covers a proper social greeting |
| Embrace long, meandering conversations as the social norm | Expect brief, efficient exchanges in casual settings |

**🇧🇭 Bahrain**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress modestly in public spaces, especially outside malls and hotels | Wear revealing clothing in public areas outside tourist zones |
| Observe quieter, more reserved public conduct generally | Assume Gulf public norms mirror Western casualness |
| Be mindful during Ramadan of eating and drinking in public | Eat or drink openly in public during fasting hours |
| Respect gender-specific customs in more traditional settings | Assume all social customs apply identically regardless of context |

## Paraguay: Warmth Delivered Without a Filter

Public life in Paraguay runs at a pace and with a directness that can genuinely surprise visitors used to more reserved cultures. Personal remarks — about weight, tiredness, a new haircut, how long it's been since you visited — are delivered constantly and without any of the social hedging that would soften the same comment elsewhere. Crucially, this isn't received as rude by Paraguayans themselves; it's understood as a form of attentiveness, proof that someone actually noticed you, and taking offence at it marks a visitor as not quite understanding the social contract yet.

This directness pairs with an unhurried, deeply informal approach to time and social interaction generally. Conversations rarely rush straight to business; a proper greeting, complete with a cheek kiss even among fairly new acquaintances, and a stretch of genuinely unhurried small talk, function as the necessary social warm-up before anything transactional happens. Visitors who try to skip straight to the point, in the efficient manner common in more time-pressured cultures, often find themselves quietly read as cold or overly formal, regardless of intent.

What makes Paraguayan public behaviour distinct even within South America is just how consistently this warmth extends to strangers, not just acquaintances. A queue, a market stall, a bus ride — all become opportunities for genuine, personal small talk rather than being treated as neutral, transactional spaces to get through quickly. It's a public culture built on the assumption that a stranger today is simply an acquaintance you haven't properly met yet.

## Bahrain: Decorum as the Default Public Language

Bahrain's public behaviour norms run on a very different logic, one shaped substantially by modesty expectations and a generally more reserved public demeanour, particularly outside the more cosmopolitan, tourist-oriented pockets of Manama. Dress code matters here in a way it simply doesn't in Paraguay — modest coverage of shoulders and knees in public spaces isn't strictly law in most everyday settings the way it might be in some neighbouring Gulf states, but it's a strongly observed social expectation, and visitors who ignore it, particularly outside malls and international hotels, will feel the social friction quickly even without anyone saying a word.

Public conduct generally trends quieter and more reserved than Paraguay's boisterous informality — raised voices, overt physical affection, and loud personal remarks about a stranger's appearance would all read as a genuine breach of decorum rather than warmth. This extends to specific seasonal sensitivities too: during Ramadan, eating or drinking in public during daylight fasting hours is a real social — and in some contexts legal — line that residents and visitors alike are expected to respect, regardless of their own religious observance.

What's easy to misjudge from the outside is assuming this reserve reflects coldness. It doesn't — Bahraini hospitality, especially once you're inside a home or invited into a more personal setting, can be remarkably warm and generous. The public/private distinction is simply drawn in a very different place than in Paraguay: warmth is reserved for closer, more personal contexts, while public spaces are governed by a quieter, more codified sense of what's appropriate to display at all.

## The Verdict

Paraguay treats public space as an extension of personal warmth, where directness about a stranger's appearance is a form of connection, not an intrusion. Bahrain treats public space as a place governed by modesty and quiet decorum, saving warmth for more private, personal contexts. One culture wears its personality on its sleeve in public; the other keeps its warmth carefully behind closed doors. Neither is more polite than the other — they've simply drawn the line between public and private in different places.

If forced to pick, I find Paraguay's unfiltered warmth genuinely disarming in a way I didn't expect to enjoy as much as I did — but I've also come to respect how much more comfortable Bahrain's quieter public norms make it to simply exist in a crowd without becoming the subject of anyone's running commentary.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora, "How direct is too direct when talking with people in Paraguay?" — paraphrased: a respondent said visitors should expect comments on their appearance within minutes of meeting someone, and that the correct response is to laugh and comment right back, not take offence.*

> *expat.com Asunción — paraphrased: a resident described how long it took to stop feeling rushed by the unhurried pace of daily errands, eventually realising the slowness was the entire point of the interaction, not an inefficiency.*

> *Internations Manama — paraphrased: an expat said nobody ever directly told them their outfit was inappropriate for a given neighbourhood, they simply noticed the stares and adjusted after the fact, which took some getting used to.*

## Conclusion

Paraguay and Bahrain have built two entirely different public cultures around the same basic question — how much of yourself do you show in public, and to whom. Paraguay says: everything, to everyone, immediately, and call it warmth. Bahrain says: very little, carefully, and save the real warmth for those who've earned closer access. Import Paraguay's directness into a Bahraini public space and you'll cause quiet offence you won't even register. Import Bahrain's public reserve into a Paraguayan bus stop and you'll be gently, persistently interrogated about why you seem so cold.
