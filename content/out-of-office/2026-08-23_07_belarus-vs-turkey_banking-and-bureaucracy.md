# One Country Needs Your Address Within Ten Days, the Other Needs a Tax Number Before It'll Even Talk to You

🇧🇾 Belarus vs 🇹🇷 Turkey
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a special circle of relocation hell reserved specifically for the first month of paperwork, and Belarus and Turkey both design their bureaucratic gauntlets with what I can only assume is deliberate, almost artistic cruelty. Belarus wants you registered, documented, and cross-referenced within ten days of arrival, sanctions and international banking friction be damned. Turkey wants a tax identification number before it will so much as glance at your passport for a bank account, and then wants that account tied to a residence permit it takes its sweet time issuing.

Both systems technically work. Both will make you cry gently into a form at least once.

## Do's & Don'ts: Belarus

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register your address within 10 days of arrival, without exception | Assume international transfers will process smoothly — sanctions create real friction |
| Use a specialised lawyer or consultant for account-opening paperwork | Expect foreign banks to reliably process transfers to or from Belarusian accounts |
| Check whether you're eligible for the newer e-visa system before travel | Update your address late — it must be re-filed every time you move |
| Budget extra time for any banking transaction touching international borders | Expect the same account-opening speed you'd get in a more banked-up economy |

## Do's & Don'ts: Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your Turkish Tax Identification Number before anything else — it's the real first step | Try to open a bank account without a passport, tax number, and proof of address ready |
| Apply for your residence permit (ikamet) within 30 days of arrival | Let your residence permit lapse — your bank account status depends on it staying valid |
| Look for banks offering multilingual or dedicated expat services — they now exist widely | Assume every bank requires a residence permit — some don't, but documentation still varies |
| Use online pre-registration where available to speed up in-branch visits | Expect one bank's process to mirror another's exactly — requirements still vary bank to bank |

## Belarus: A Bureaucracy Built on Registration, Complicated Further by Geopolitics

Belarus runs one of the more tightly regulated banking systems in the region, sitting under the direct, dense oversight of the National Bank of the Republic of Belarus, and foreign arrivals consistently underestimate exactly how dense that regulation gets in practice. Opening an account as a foreigner is rated, generously, as medium difficulty — which in bureaucratic-speak usually means "possible, but bring a translator, a lawyer, and an afternoon you weren't planning to lose."

The mandatory registration requirement is the first and most persistent headache: address registration within ten days of arrival, full stop, and then re-registration every single time you move, with real consequences for letting it lapse. It's not a formality treated loosely, the way some countries quietly let residency admin slide for expats who mean well. Belarus tracks this closely, and newcomers who assume otherwise learn fast that the system does not forgive an honest oversight.

Layered on top of the domestic bureaucracy is the genuinely distinctive Belarusian problem: international banking friction driven by the broader geopolitical situation and associated sanctions. This isn't a minor inconvenience — it's the single daily frustration that comes up most often in expat accounts, with foreign banks routinely refusing to process transfers to or from Belarusian accounts at all, leaving residents scrambling for workarounds just to move money across a border most other expats would consider a non-issue. There are, to be fair, genuine signs of modernisation — a new e-visa system launched in 2025 has streamlined entry and registration for arrivals from dozens of eligible countries — but the core banking friction remains a structural fact of life here, not a temporary hiccup waiting to resolve itself.

## Turkey: A System That Wants Your Paperwork in a Very Specific Order, or Not at All

Turkey's bureaucracy operates less on the "register within days" urgency of Belarus and more on a strict sequencing problem — get the steps in the wrong order, and you'll simply be turned away and sent back a stage. The Turkish Tax Identification Number is the actual starting point for nearly everything, banking included, and it's obtained quickly and for free at a local tax office, but skip it or assume your passport alone will suffice at the bank counter, and you'll be quietly, politely redirected right back to square one.

The residence permit — ikamet tezkeresi — is the second load-bearing document in the sequence, and it comes with its own thirty-day registration clock from the moment you land, mirroring Belarus's urgency in spirit if not in exact mechanics. What makes Turkey's system genuinely distinct is how tightly banking access is tied to that permit's ongoing validity: personalised debit cards, in particular, are issued specifically to holders of a valid residence permit, permanent residency, or citizenship, meaning your day-to-day banking convenience is directly hostage to keeping your immigration paperwork current and renewed.

The encouraging part — and Belarus offers comparatively little of this — is that Turkey's banks have genuinely modernised their expat onboarding in recent years, with multilingual support, online pre-registration, and dedicated service desks for foreigners now common at major institutions. It doesn't remove the bureaucratic sequencing entirely, and different banks still apply different documentation requirements in maddeningly inconsistent ways, but it does mean that once you have the tax number and the residence permit in hand, the actual account-opening process is considerably smoother than the reputation of Turkish bureaucracy might lead you to fear.

## The Verdict

Belarus's bureaucracy is a domestic system doing exactly what it's designed to do, undermined entirely by an external geopolitical situation no amount of paperwork discipline can fix — you can file every form correctly and still find your money stuck at an international border it can't cross. Turkey's bureaucracy is a sequencing puzzle: frustrating, rigid, occasionally redundant, but ultimately solvable with the right documents in the right order and, increasingly, genuine institutional effort to make foreigners' lives easier.

Turkey wins, not because its rules are gentler, but because they're at least fixable through your own competence. Belarus asks you to be competent and then hands the outcome to forces entirely outside your control.

## Conclusion

Neither country makes it easy, and neither is particularly sorry about that. Belarus will get your paperwork perfectly in order and then let a sanctions regime undo half the benefit anyway. Turkey will make you jump through a specific, occasionally illogical sequence of hoops, but reward you with a system that, once conquered, actually functions the way a modern bank account should. If you're choosing where to fight this particular battle, choose the one where winning is actually possible.
