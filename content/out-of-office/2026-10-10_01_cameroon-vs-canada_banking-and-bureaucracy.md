# Bureaucracy Doesn't Slow You Down To Punish You. It Slows You Down Because It Can.

🇨🇲 Cameroon 🇨🇦 Canada
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Everyone arrives in a new country assuming bureaucracy is a problem to be solved, like a crossword, or a toddler. It is not. It is a weather system. You do not solve weather. You dress for it, you complain about it to anyone who'll listen, and then one day you simply stop checking the forecast because you've accepted that the forecast is, structurally, "inconvenient, with a chance of worse."

Cameroon and Canada have built two entirely different weather systems around the same basic human need — a bank account, a stamped form, a signature from someone who has gone to lunch. One runs on relationships and the right kind of patience. The other runs on forms that generate other forms, like a photocopier having a breakdown in slow motion. I have opened accounts in both. I have cried, politely, in both. Here's the forecast.

## Do's & Don'ts: Cameroon

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a local contact or colleague to the bank with you the first time | Walk in expecting the website to reflect what the branch actually requires |
| Carry far more photocopies of your passport than seems reasonable | Assume one branch's rules apply at the branch across town |
| Build a real relationship with your specific teller or account officer | Expect email follow-up to replace a physical visit, ever |
| Budget two to three visits minimum for anything involving a wire transfer | Lose your temper — it resets the clock to zero, socially and literally |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book your bank appointment before you've even found a flat | Expect anyone to care that you're "just visiting to sort this out" |
| Get a SIN number sorted immediately — everything downstream depends on it | Call to check on your application's status; it will not speed anything up |
| Keep every reference number issued to you, forever, in a dedicated folder | Assume "processing" means someone is doing anything at all |
| Accept that "a few weeks" is an optimistic translation of "a season" | Mistake politeness on the phone for actual progress on your file |

## Cameroon: The Relationship Is The System

In Cameroon, bureaucracy isn't a machine you feed documents into. It's a person, and the documents are more like a gift you bring when visiting someone's home — necessary, expected, but not actually the point. The point is the person. Walk into a branch of Afriland First Bank or any of the major commercial banks in Douala or Yaoundé with a folder of photocopies and a transactional attitude, and you will be processed at the speed of a glacier that has personally decided it doesn't like you. Walk in having been introduced by a colleague, having learned the name of the account officer, having asked after their week — and the same stack of paperwork moves through in an afternoon.

This is not corruption, whatever the lazier expat forums will tell you. It's a relationship-first system operating in a country where institutions have, for entirely legitimate historical reasons, earned a healthy distrust, and where personal vouching fills the gap that a credit-scoring algorithm fills elsewhere. The World Bank's Doing Business metrics have Cameroon ranked poorly on "ease of" anything administrative, and that ranking is measuring something real: the paperwork requirements are genuinely heavier than they need to be, and the BEAC's regional banking regulations add a layer of regional-currency-zone complexity that a Canadian walking in cold will find baffling. But the ranking misses the workaround, which is that none of it is actually about the paperwork. It's about whether the person behind the counter has decided you're worth helping quickly.

The visa and residency bureaucracy runs on the identical logic, which is either maddening or liberating depending entirely on whether you've made a friend at the préfecture yet. I watched one American colleague spend six weeks failing to get a document stamped through pure transactional persistence, and another expat get the same document stamped in two days because she'd spent an hour drinking tea with the clerk's supervisor the week before. Neither of them was doing anything wrong. They were playing different games on the same board.

## Canada: The Machine Doesn't Care, And That's The Point

Canada's bureaucracy is the opposite kind of beast entirely: impersonal to a degree that initially feels cold and eventually reveals itself as a strange kind of fairness. Nobody is going to fast-track your SIN application because you bought them a coffee, and nobody is going to slow it down because they had a bad morning. It moves at exactly the same glacial pace for everyone, which sounds like faint praise until you've lived somewhere where it very much does not.

The catch is that "the same glacial pace for everyone" is still glacial. The Canada Revenue Agency's own published processing-time estimates for anything involving a Social Insurance Number cross-check, a tax file, or a benefits application routinely run into weeks, and that's the optimistic case where you've filled in every form correctly the first time, which you have not, because the forms are designed by people who have never had to fill one in themselves. Banks will not open a full-service account without that SIN in hand, so the entire financial-onboarding sequence becomes a dependency chain: you wait for the government to confirm you exist on paper before the bank will confirm you exist in its ledger, and every step in between runs through a call centre that will cheerfully inform you your call is important to them while providing zero information that advances your situation by a single day.

What Canada does offer, and what Cameroon's system structurally cannot, is predictability without favouritism. Nobody is getting ahead of you because they know someone. The rules genuinely are the rules, applied with the flat, unblinking consistency of a Service Canada representative who has read the same script to four hundred people this week and will read it to four hundred more. It is maddening in the moment and, I'll grudgingly admit, something close to just in the aggregate. The forms are not designed to punish you specifically. They were simply never designed with any individual human in mind at all, which is a different and in some ways gentler kind of indifference.

## The Verdict

If you need something done today, you want Cameroon's system, provided you've invested in the relationships that make "today" possible — and provided you accept that without those relationships, today becomes never. If you need something done fairly, eventually, with a paper trail you can actually audit later, you want Canada's, provided you have the patience of a geological formation and nothing time-sensitive riding on the outcome.

Neither is "better." Cameroon's system rewards social capital and punishes the friendless transactional foreigner who thinks a stack of photocopies should simply speak for itself. Canada's system rewards patience and punishes anyone who assumes that politeness on a phone line is the same thing as urgency. The honest expat move is to stop asking which system is more "efficient" — a word that only makes sense once you've already decided what you're optimising for — and start asking which kind of frustration you'd rather spend your Tuesday afternoon on. I know which one gives me a better story at dinner. I also know which one I'd want handling my mortgage.

## Conclusion

Here is the thing both systems agree on, quietly, without either admitting it: bureaucracy was never actually about the forms. Cameroon's version makes that obvious by routing everything through a human relationship the forms merely decorate. Canada's version hides it better, dressing indifference up as fairness and delay up as due process, but underneath the hold music and the reference numbers, it's the same basic transaction — a system deciding, by whatever logic it's built on, whether you're worth its time today. Learn which logic you're dealing with before you walk in. Everything else is just photocopies.
