# One Country Shops Where the Fish Is Still Moving, the Other Where the Tomatoes Are Still Warm From the Truck

🇻🇳 Vietnam vs 🇲🇽 Mexico

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Hanoi, I did my grocery shopping at six in the morning surrounded by live fish in buckets, women calling out prices in rapid Vietnamese, and a general assumption that if you wanted anything resembling freshness, you showed up before the good produce disappeared to someone faster than you. In Mexico City, I did mine at a Sunday tianguis, a temporary street market that materialises out of nowhere on a specific day of the week and vanishes by evening, leaving no trace it was ever there, taking its extraordinary chile selection with it until next Sunday.

Vietnam's wet markets run daily, permanent fixtures of urban life so essential that the supermarket, when used at all, is treated as the backup option for things the market simply doesn't carry. Mexico's tianguis and mercados run on rotating schedules and permanent locations both, existing alongside supermarkets in a genuine, functioning parallel economy that most locals navigate fluently and most newcomers find bewildering for roughly the first month.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go early — the best produce and seafood disappear by mid-morning | Expect fixed prices at a wet market stall |
| Bring small bills and exact change where possible | Haggle aggressively over genuinely small amounts |
| Learn basic numbers in Vietnamese — it changes your prices | Poke or handle produce excessively before buying |
| Build a relationship with a regular vendor for better prices | Assume the supermarket is cheaper — it usually isn't |

### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn your neighbourhood's tianguis day and plan around it | Assume every mercado stall takes cards — bring cash |
| Ask vendors for recommendations — they expect and enjoy it | Rush through a mercado visit, it's a social occasion too |
| Buy in the quantities locals do — items are often sold loose | Bag your own produce without checking stall custom first |
| Greet the vendor before browsing — it's expected courtesy | Compare tianguis prices loudly against supermarket prices |

## Vietnam: The Wet Market as Non-Negotiable Daily Ritual

Vietnamese wet markets aren't a cultural experience laid on for visitors, they're the actual, unglamorous, entirely functional backbone of how most households eat. Fish, still swimming or freshly killed to order, herbs cut so recently they're still faintly wet with morning dew, produce that arrived hours, not days, before it reached the stall. The supermarket exists, stocked and air-conditioned, and is used for packaged goods, imported items, the odd convenience purchase — but for anything that actually needs to taste good, the market wins by a margin nobody seriously disputes.

Pricing at these markets is fluid, personal, and genuinely responsive to who's asking. Vendors quote differently depending on whether you're a regular, a stranger, or, unmistakably, a foreigner who hasn't yet learned to ask prices in Vietnamese numbers rather than pointing and nodding. This isn't malicious, it's simply how the market has always worked — prices flex, relationships matter, and the vendor who sees you every week for a month will, without being asked, start setting aside the good produce before it disappears.

The pace demands adjustment too. Markets are genuinely busiest and best stocked shockingly early, often before six, and by mid-morning the finest cuts, the freshest catch, the most vibrant greens are simply gone, sold to people who understood the assignment sooner than you did. I learned this the hard way, repeatedly, arriving at what I considered a perfectly reasonable eight a.m. to find the good stalls already down to scraps. Vietnamese grocery shopping rewards discipline in a way that took real, humbling repetition to absorb.

## Mexico: A Rotating Economy of Mercados and Tianguis

Mexican grocery culture operates on two parallel, genuinely distinct systems: the permanent mercado, a fixed indoor market open most days, and the tianguis, a temporary street market that appears on a specific weekday in a specific neighbourhood and simply isn't there any other time. Learning your local tianguis schedule isn't optional trivia, it's essential knowledge that shapes an entire week's shopping and cooking rhythm.

What distinguishes the Mexican mercado experience is how social it remains, even for routine shopping. Vendors expect greeting, conversation, genuine rapport before a transaction even begins — walking in silently, grabbing items, and leaving reads as faintly odd, even mildly rude, in a way that took me a while to internalise coming from more transactional grocery cultures. Ask a chile vendor which pepper suits your dish and you'll get a genuine, often lengthy, opinionated answer, usually accompanied by an unsolicited recipe suggestion.

The tianguis in particular rewards a kind of loyalty and pattern recognition — the same vendors return to the same spot weekly, building relationships with regular customers that translate into better cuts, better prices, first pick of whatever came in freshest that morning. Cards are rarely accepted at either mercado or tianguis stalls, so cash discipline matters, and produce is frequently sold loose or by informal weight rather than pre-packaged, requiring a comfort with slightly imprecise quantities that supermarket shopping never demands.

## The Verdict

Vietnam's wet markets demand early mornings and quick decisions; Mexico's mercados and tianguis demand a working knowledge of the weekly calendar and genuine conversational investment. Both reward the shopper who treats grocery buying as relationship-building rather than transaction.

I ate better in both countries once I stopped defaulting to the supermarket out of convenience and started actually learning the rhythm each system demanded. Suki's verdict: Vietnam wins on sheer freshness and urgency — nothing beats fish that was swimming an hour ago — but Mexico wins on human warmth, because no wet market vendor I met anywhere gave me a chile recommendation with quite that much genuine, unprompted enthusiasm.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/vietnam — a newcomer admitted showing up to their local wet market at nine a.m. for weeks before a neighbour gently informed them "the good stuff's gone by seven, always."*

> *Reddit r/mexicocity — someone described their tianguis vendor remembering their usual order after just three visits, calling it "more customer loyalty than I've ever gotten from an actual bank."*

> *expat.com Mexico City — a longtime resident warned that skipping the greeting before browsing a mercado stall "won't get you thrown out, but it will get you a noticeably worse avocado."*

## Conclusion

Vietnam and Mexico have both built grocery cultures that refuse to be reduced to convenience, each demanding a kind of attentiveness the supermarket never asks for. Move to Hanoi and set an alarm you'll resent. Move to Mexico City and learn your tianguis day like it's a religious observance, because in a meaningful sense, it is one. Either way, the supermarket will still be there for toilet paper. Everything worth eating lives elsewhere.
