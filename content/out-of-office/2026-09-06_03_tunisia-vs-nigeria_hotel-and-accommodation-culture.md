# One Country Hides Its Best Hotels Behind an Unmarked Medina Door, the Other Judges a Hotel by Its Generator

🇹🇳 Tunisia × 🇳🇬 Nigeria
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Tunis, the best place I ever stayed had no sign, no visible entrance worth mentioning, and a door that looked identical to the twenty other doors on the same medina alley — until it opened onto a tiled courtyard so beautiful I nearly forgot I'd been lost for twenty minutes finding it. In Lagos, the first question any sensible traveller asks about a hotel isn't about the thread count, it's about the generator: does it have one, how big, and how many hours a day is it actually running.

These are two completely different hospitality philosophies, and both make perfect sense once you understand what each country is actually solving for. Tunisia hides its charm behind unassuming exteriors because the interior courtyard, not the street-facing wall, has always been where the real architecture lives. Nigeria has built an entire hotel-rating instinct around infrastructure reliability, because a beautiful room means nothing at 9pm during a power cut.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇹🇳 Tunisia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book a medina riad or dar for the real architectural experience | Judge a guesthouse's quality by its street-facing exterior |
| Negotiate directly with owner-run guesthouses; it's expected | Assume listed online rates are always final |
| Ask about rooftop terrace access; many riads have stunning ones | Expect modern hotel-chain amenities in a historic medina property |
| Confirm exact directions in advance; medina addresses are tricky | Rely solely on GPS pins in the old city; they're often imprecise |

**🇳🇬 Nigeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask specifically about generator capacity and running hours | Assume "24-hour power" claims without confirming the backup setup |
| Check for a functioning water storage tank, not just taps | Assume running water is guaranteed at all hours |
| Read recent guest reviews for real infrastructure reliability | Trust star ratings alone as a proxy for consistent amenities |
| Book well-reviewed serviced apartments for longer stays | Assume all listed "luxury" hotels deliver equally on essentials |

## Tunisia: Beauty Behind an Unmarked Wall

Tunisian hospitality architecture inherits directly from centuries of medina design, where a home or guesthouse's real character was never meant to be visible from the street — privacy and modesty on the exterior, ornamentation and life concentrated entirely around an interior courtyard. This isn't a quirky design choice for tourists to admire, it's the actual logic of how these buildings were constructed, and it means a riad's plain, unmarked door tells you nothing at all about what's behind it.

Finding these places is genuinely part of the experience, for better or worse. Medina addresses are notoriously difficult to navigate by GPS alone — narrow alleys, similar-looking doorways, and a numbering system that predates any modern mapping convention combine to make "just follow your phone" a surprisingly unreliable strategy. Guests who've stayed at a well-run riad before know to call ahead for precise directions, or arrange to be met at a recognisable landmark rather than the building itself. It's an inconvenience that, somehow, only adds to the sense of having found somewhere genuinely special once you finally arrive.

The ownership model matters too — many of the best riads and guesthouses are small, family-run operations where negotiating directly with the owner over price, breakfast inclusion, or an extra night isn't rude, it's simply how business is done. This personal, relationship-driven approach extends to the whole stay: owners who live on-site or nearby tend to treat guests less like a transaction and more like a temporary addition to the household, complete with recommendations, adjustments, and the kind of hospitality that a chain hotel simply can't replicate at scale.

## Nigeria: Judging a Hotel by What It Can Actually Guarantee

Nigerian accommodation culture, particularly in Lagos, runs on a completely different set of priorities, shaped by an infrastructure reality that no traveller can safely ignore: grid power is unreliable enough that a hotel's true quality is measured less by its décor than by its ability to keep the lights and water running regardless of what the national grid is doing that day. Asking about the generator isn't a strange question to a Lagos hotel manager, it's the single most sensible question a guest can ask, and any reputable property will answer it specifically — capacity, fuel arrangements, hours of coverage — rather than vaguely.

This extends naturally to water. A hotel can look immaculate and still leave guests without running water for stretches of the day if its storage tank setup isn't sufficient, so seasoned travellers and expats alike learn to ask about water storage capacity with the same seriousness they'd apply to checking a fire exit. Star ratings and glossy photos matter far less here than recent, specific guest reviews describing whether the essentials actually held up during their stay, because a beautifully marketed listing says nothing about what happens during the inevitable outage.

For longer stays, well-reviewed serviced apartments have become the default choice for many expats and business travellers precisely because they tend to be more transparent about their infrastructure setup than traditional hotels marketing themselves on ambiance alone. Nigeria's hospitality instinct, in short, has been shaped by a practical, unsentimental question that most countries' hotel guests never have to think about: not "is it beautiful," but "will it actually work."

## The Verdict

Tunisia hides its finest hospitality behind plain, unmarked doors, trusting that the interior courtyard will do all the talking once you finally find it. Nigeria judges a hotel by its infrastructure resilience, treating a working generator as a more meaningful signal of quality than any star rating. One culture is organised around architectural discretion; the other around practical reliability. Both, in their own way, teach visitors to look past the surface before judging anything.

Given the choice, I'll always chase the romance of a Tunisian riad courtyard revealing itself behind an anonymous door — but I've also sat in enough Lagos hotel lobbies during a power cut to know which question actually matters more when you just need a shower and the lights to work.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora, "What should first-time visitors know about staying in a Tunisian riad?" — paraphrased: a respondent advised always calling ahead for exact directions, since even a correct GPS pin can leave you standing in front of the wrong identical-looking door.*

> *expat.com Tunis — paraphrased: a long-term resident described negotiating an extra night and breakfast directly with a riad owner over mint tea, something that would never happen at a standard hotel chain reception desk.*

> *Internations Lagos — paraphrased: an expat said they now ask about generator wattage before asking about anything else on a hotel listing, having learned the hard way that a five-star rating means nothing during a multi-hour outage.*

## Conclusion

Tunisia and Nigeria have each built a hospitality culture shaped by what actually matters in their context — one by centuries of architectural modesty concealing genuine beauty, the other by an infrastructure reality that rewards blunt, practical questions over glossy marketing. Bring Tunisia's trust in appearances to a Lagos hotel search and you'll get burned by a pretty listing with no water. Bring Nigeria's infrastructure interrogation to a Tunisian medina and you'll miss the entire point of a door that isn't supposed to tell you anything at all.
