# The Japanese Will Fold Your Towel Into a Swan. The Croatians Will Just Hand You a Key and a Shrug.

🇯🇵 Japan 🇭🇷 Croatia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular kind of hospitality that requires a twelve-step bathing ritual, a kimono you will definitely put on backwards, and a member of staff bowing so precisely you start to wonder if you're the one being rude. Then there is the kind of hospitality that involves a woman named Ivana meeting you outside a stone building, unlocking a door with a key roughly the size of a paperback, and telling you the Wi-Fi password is "sunčano123" before disappearing on a scooter. Both, extraordinarily, count as five-star experiences. I have stayed in both. I have wept, gently, in both — once from gratitude, once from the sheer will of a Dalmatian summer with no air conditioning.

I've relocated fourteen times and slept in stranger's beds — professionally, I mean — in sixty countries, and nowhere does the gap between *engineered* hospitality and *inherited* hospitality show up more starkly than between a Kyoto ryokan and a Split apartment. One country has turned lodging into a precision instrument. The other has turned it into a family obligation with a price tag. Neither is wrong. One of them will make you cry with joy over a breakfast tray. The other will make you cry over a landlord who "forgot" the deposit.

## Japan: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take your shoes off the second you see a step-up genkan, no exceptions | Wear the yukata outside the building, ever, however photogenic you feel |
| Follow the bathing order at an onsen ryokan — wash first, soak after | Bring your regular-sized Western suitcase into a tatami room and drag it everywhere |
| Tip with a quiet, sincere thank-you — never cash | Speak loudly in the hallway after 9pm; ryokan walls are made of paper and good intentions |

## Croatia: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm the check-in time by phone the morning of — listings lie | Expect the air conditioning to be included, working, or mentioned honestly |
| Bring cash for the tourist tax the host collects separately | Assume the "sea view" isn't a sliver visible only if you climb onto the balcony railing |
| Say hvala and mean it — the family running the place usually built it themselves | Complain about stairs. There are always stairs. It is always four floors. There is never a lift |

## Japan: A System Designed to Anticipate You Before You've Finished Wanting It

The genius of Japanese hospitality — *omotenashi*, a word travel writers deploy so relentlessly it's practically load-bearing at this point — is that it removes the need for you to ask for anything, because someone has already thought of it. Your slippers are by the door. Your yukata is folded, by size, before you arrive. The onsen schedule is timed so families and solo travellers never collide awkwardly in the corridor. This isn't performative; it's structural. A traditional ryokan runs on a rhythm — check-in at 3pm, kaiseki dinner served at a precise hour, breakfast delivered to your room or a dining hall with the same quiet choreography — and once you surrender to that rhythm, something in your nervous system actually relaxes. I've stayed in ryokan in Hakone where the staff bowed goodbye until my taxi turned the corner. I checked. I looked back three times. They were still bowing.

The catch, and there is always a catch with systems this refined, is that the system does not bend for you. Arrive late for kaiseki and you've disrupted a machine that took decades to calibrate. Business hotels in Tokyo are the opposite extreme — capsule-adjacent efficiency, rooms the size of a generous coffin, everything you need and nothing you don't — which tells you something about the national talent for making constraint feel intentional rather than sad. Airbnb exists in Japan but operates under regulations so tight (the minpaku law caps operating days, requires registration, neighbours can and do report you) that it never became the free-for-all it is elsewhere. Which means when you book a hotel in Japan, you are booking a promise, backed by a genuinely terrifying amount of institutional pride. Nobody in the history of Japanese hospitality has ever said "not my problem." They will solve your problem even if your problem is your own poor planning, and they will do it without once making you feel stupid for having a problem in the first place.

## Croatia: Hospitality as a Family Business, Whether the Family Meant to Go Into Hospitality or Not

Croatian accommodation runs on an entirely different operating system, and that system is called *sobe* — literally "rooms" — a tradition of private, family-run lodging that predates the country's tourism boom by generations and has scaled, chaotically, to meet it. Along the Dalmatian coast every third house seems to have a discreet sign advertising rooms, and behind that sign is very often an actual grandmother who built the extension herself, or paid a nephew in beer to do it, and now runs the bookings from a phone she answers on the third ring while frying something extremely good-smelling. This is hospitality as inherited enterprise rather than trained discipline, and the warmth is real — you will be brought rakija you didn't order, told which beach the tourists haven't found yet, and quietly upgraded to the room with the better view because the host liked your face.

What it isn't is standardised, and Croatia's accommodation sector has been visibly straining under its own tourism success for a decade now — Dubrovnik's Old Town books out entire buildings to short-let platforms, driving actual residents out, and Split's apartment stock increasingly caters to a market of scooter-riding twenty-somethings rather than families, which shows up in everything from thinner walls to later, louder nights. Air conditioning is billed as standard and functions as aspiration. Reviews cluster around two poles: transcendent, tearful gratitude for a host who treated you like family, or seething fury about a "confirmed" booking that evaporated the day before arrival because someone offered more. There is very little middle ground, because there is very little institutional buffer between you and the actual human who owns the building.

## The Verdict

If you want to be cared for by a system, go to Japan. If you want to be cared for by a person, gamble on Croatia. Japan will never surprise you, in the best and occasionally most suffocating sense of that sentence — you know exactly what you're getting, down to the precise angle of the bow. Croatia will surprise you constantly, and roughly half the surprises are rakija and half are a WhatsApp message at 11pm saying the booking's "had a mix-up." I'll admit a soft spot for the chaos. A country that runs on family trust and vibes shouldn't work as well as it does, and yet I've had my best nights of the entire Balkans on a terrace some uncle built without permits. Japan wins on reliability by a landslide. Croatia wins on the story you'll actually tell at dinner parties for the next decade.

## Conclusion

Somewhere between a bow held three seconds too long and a rakija poured four fingers too generous, you'll work out which kind of traveller you actually are. The truth is you need both registers in a life well spent Out of Office — the reassurance of a system that has thought of everything, and the reminder that some of the best nights come from a system that's thought of nothing at all and is winging it magnificently. Book the ryokan for your sanity. Book the sobe for your soul. Confirm the air conditioning either way, and never, under any circumstances, trust a Dalmatian host who tells you it's "basically sea view."
