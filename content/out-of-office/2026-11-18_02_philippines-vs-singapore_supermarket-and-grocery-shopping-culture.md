# Singapore Sells You Groceries and Manila Sells You an Experience, Preferably at the Wrong Price

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A Singapore supermarket is an air-conditioned act of faith. The prices are marked, the aisles are labelled, the cold chain is unbroken, and the only unpredictable element is whether your cereal has been flown in from somewhere that charges you thirty per cent extra for the privilege. You walk in, you scan, you leave. It is the sort of efficiency that makes visitors weep gently into the dairy section.

A Philippine shop is a negotiation with several variables. There is the mall supermarket, which resembles Singapore with extra queues. There is the palengke, the wet market, which resembles a theatre. And there is the sari-sari store on the corner, which resembles a very small, very friendly black market for biscuits. If you want groceries in Manila, you do not shop. You *navigate*.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Philippines**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go to the palengke early, before the heat and the crowds | Turn up at noon and expect the best catch |
| Ask the price before you pick anything up | Assume an unmarked item is priced fairly |
| Keep small notes and coins for local stores | Expect every provincial shop to take a card |
| Ask permission before photographing vendors | Treat the market as a safari |

**Singapore**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry small cash to the wet markets | Wave a credit card at a fishmonger |
| Compare chains: FairPrice, Sheng Siong, Giant and Cold Storage | Do the entire shop at the priciest one |
| Bring your own bag | Expect a free stack of plastic |
| Use online delivery for heavy items | Haul rice and water home on the MRT |

## Philippines

The Filipino grocery landscape runs on three layers. The first is the mall supermarket, where one retired expat's published budget for two shows about ₱25,000 a month spent on mall groceries alone, with imported meats and artisan foods layered on top. The second is the palengke. The ASTIG Vegan guide to palengke manners describes markets that open around 5am and run until about 7pm, are busiest between 6 and 9 in the morning, and reward a get-in-get-out attitude. Ask before you photograph anyone, leave the flashy watch at home, and expect prices lower than the farmers' market a white-collar neighbourhood has invented for itself.

The third layer is the sari-sari store, the home-based shop selling snacks, canned goods, rice and toiletries. It is also the home of *tingi*, the sale of tiny portions: a single sachet of shampoo, a few cigarettes, one egg. It is a retail model built around people who live day to day, and it is far more sophisticated than any supermarket loyalty scheme.

The expat voice here is sharp. A long-time forum contributor writes that smaller stores often have no price on anything, so a foreigner can find sardines worth about ₱12 quoted at ₱30. Stores lack clear aisles, shopping is largely done by women and is thoroughly social, and many households without a fridge shop several times a day. His advice is to let a partner or housekeeper do the buying at first. Another thread is divided on markets: one poster dislikes uncovered, unrefrigerated meat, while another buys produce at local markets at about half the price and makes a monthly trip to a membership store for the imports. Preference for Robinsons' meat and SM's foreign foods is a perfectly Manila sort of tribal loyalty.

There is also the question of rhythm. Filipino grocery shopping is not a weekly event but a continuous one. If a household has no fridge, and the forum evidence says many do not, then the shop happens three times a day, in small portions, with small talk. It is an entire social economy built around what you can carry and what will not spoil by teatime. Singapore, by contrast, has solved storage by building a refrigerator the size of a country, and so it shops in bulk and in silence, with earphones in.

The practical rule is that provinces are cash territory, Manila mostly takes cards, and imported goods are priced as if each tin had personally booked a seat on the plane. I love the markets, but I am not going to pretend they are comfortable. They are hot, loud and occasionally slippery, and I would not trade a single minute of it.

## Singapore

Singapore has chosen a different religion. The chains are tidy and tiered. A 2018 expat guide lists NTUC FairPrice as the biggest, with a premium Finest range and a bulk Xtra range, Cold Storage as the posher international option, Giant as the Malaysian-owned hypermarket and Sheng Siong as the heartland local. Delivery and click-and-collect thresholds were published back then; do not trust the numbers, but note the pattern, which is that almost everyone delivers and the system rewards people who plan.

Singapore also keeps its wet markets, and they are not museum pieces. Most open early, around 6am, and wind down around 2pm. Most stalls do not take cards, bargaining is normal especially in bulk, and the guide's list of examples, Tekka Centre, Chinatown Complex and Tiong Bahru Market, will give you a map of the country's real appetite. Post-report voices from expat families say much the same thing: online ordering works well, and the cheaper shops, Sheng Siong, Giant and the markets, rescue the budget from the expat-oriented grocers.

The Singaporean system also hides a small joy: the wet market is where the country quietly admits it is still Asian. Between the chilled aisles and the app-based delivery, the old aunties at the fish stall still weigh your prawns by eye and tell you which ones are not worth buying. Bring cash, learn two phrases and do not ask for a bag. You will be treated better than the Cold Storage manager treats a stranger.

The ugly truth is cost. A Talesmag post report from 2015 notes cereal at around US$12 at a one-stop import shop, and an older one puts US brands about thirty per cent higher. A Mumsnet thread from 2012, dated but revealing, notes a poster comparing FairPrice with Cold Storage and finding the difference striking, with avocados costing a noticeable multiple at the posh end. The lesson is permanent: Singapore is cheap if you eat as Singaporeans eat and ruinous if you insist on the cereal from home.

## The Verdict

Singapore wins for convenience, and Manila wins for soul, which is another way of saying that I would rather shop in Singapore and eat in Manila.

If your priority is a predictable, labelled, safe, delivered weekly shop, Singapore wins by a margin I find faintly insulting to the rest of Asia. You will never be overcharged for sardines in a supermarket where the price is on the tin. The only thing you will pay for is your own sentimentality.

But Manila rewards the curious. The palengke is cheaper, fresher and a good deal more fun, and the corner store knows your name by week two. Singapore will give you consistency, and consistency is what most corporate relocations are secretly paying for. A guaranteed ingredient list, a cashless basket, a delivery window that actually opens. Manila will give you stories. Choose according to how many stories you need before the contract ends.

A Singapore supermarket will never ask after your mother. A sari-sari store will, loudly, and then sell you one egg.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com forum, long-time Philippines expat — in small shops nothing is price-marked, so foreigners get overcharged; sardines worth about ₱12 were quoted at ₱30, and the writer suggests letting a partner or housekeeper shop at first.*

> *Philippines-expats.com thread — one poster says Robinsons has the better meat while SM has better foreign food; another buys local-market produce at about half the price and makes a monthly trip to a membership store for imports.*

> *Mumsnet, Singapore food shopping thread (2012, dated) — a mother found FairPrice Finest far cheaper than Cold Storage, and other posters pointed her to wet markets for fresh produce and fish.*

## Conclusion

Groceries are the first test of whether you have actually moved or merely relocated. In Singapore you will pass it by lunchtime, with a delivery slot and a loyalty card. In the Philippines you will fail it twice, overpay once and then, around week three, find a vendor who saves you the good mangoes.

Take Singapore for efficiency and the Philippines for education. Bring cash, learn the price of an egg, and be gracious when you are fleeced. It is the cheapest tuition fee you will ever pay for a culture.
