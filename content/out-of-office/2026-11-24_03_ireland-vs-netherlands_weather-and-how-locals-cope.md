# Ireland Has a Relationship With the Rain. The Dutch Have a Raincoat.

🇮🇪 Ireland vs 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have been rained on in sixty countries, and I can tell you that rain is not one thing. In Ireland it is a mood, a houseguest, a topic of conversation that outranks politics and possibly God. In the Netherlands it is an inconvenience on the way to somewhere, like a red light, and nobody has ever stood in a doorway discussing a red light for twenty minutes.

Both countries sit on the edge of the same grey Atlantic and North Sea soup, and both will cheerfully tell you the weather is "changeable". Only one of them has turned that fact into a personality disorder. The other has simply bought better trousers. If you are moving to either, the difference between the two coping strategies will decide whether your first winter is a story or a diagnosis.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇮🇪 Ireland ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a proper waterproof jacket with a hood and wear it everywhere | Trust an umbrella; the wind will turn it inside out before you reach the corner |
| Say "grand day" when the sun appears and leave immediately to enjoy it | Postpone your outdoor plans until a "better" day, because it may be an hour away or never |
| Layer up, because four seasons in one day is a genuine forecast | Leave the house in a single thin layer because the morning looked sunny |
| Make the pub, the fire and the kettle your winter infrastructure | Expect a hot summer to be anything other than a national emergency |

| 🇳🇱 Netherlands ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Own a full rain set: jacket, trousers, and something for your bag | Cancel a bike ride because it is drizzling; the locals will assume you are ill |
| Learn the vocabulary, from drizzle to downpour, so you can complain accurately | Underestimate the wind, which is the real villain here |
| Rush outside the moment the sun appears, ideally in a T-shirt | Forget lights on your bike in the dark months |
| Plan for dark, damp afternoons with something warm and a candle | Expect the Dutch to romanticise any of it |

## 🇮🇪 Ireland

Ireland's weather is not extreme, and that is exactly the problem. A poster on an Expat.com thread about adapting to the Irish climate lays out the facts with the weariness of a survivor: temperatures rarely wander far below freezing or much above twenty, there are no hurricanes or blizzards worth the name, and you can get all four seasons in one afternoon. Nothing dramatic ever happens. It simply goes on, damply, forever. Another poster notes that the humidity makes a modest winter day feel colder than the thermometer admits, and I can confirm this from several wet Februaries in Cork, when my fingers felt like cold sausages.

So how do the Irish cope? First, by talking about it. This is not small talk, it is meteorology as social contract. "Soft day" is a legal category, and you will hear it said with genuine tenderness about weather that would send a Spaniard to bed. Second, by being completely, almost smugly, prepared for nothing. The advice on that forum is to ditch the umbrella, because the wind makes it a fantasy, and put your money into a good waterproof or waxed jacket. They are right. The Irish do not fight the rain, they simply put a hood up and walk through it as if it were a mildly rude colleague.

Third, and this is the part I admire, they have a patience for waiting. The consensus among posters is that a downpour usually passes within the hour, so you wait it out in a pub, a café, a shop doorway, with no sense of failure. Weather is not an obstacle to be defeated but a queue to stand in.

There is even a writer, Sarah Moss, who moved to Ireland and wrote in The Irish Times that she actually likes the rain, and finds the locals oddly suspicious of her for it. She points to a report suggesting that young Irish people are more likely than their grandparents to stay inside when it pours. Interesting. A nation famous for its weather is quietly retreating from it, and the only person defending the rain is a foreigner.

The sting in the tail is the sun. When it appears, one poster claims, offices let staff go early to enjoy it. Whether or not your employer has that much heart, the principle holds: Irish weather has trained a whole population to treat blue sky as a limited-time offer.

## 🇳🇱 Netherlands

The Dutch have a different relationship with weather entirely: they have a bicycle. IamExpat is honest that the Dutch talk about the weather constantly, which is fair when it can change sharply and the rain can run for days. There are specific words for it, from miezeren for drizzle to pijpestelen for the kind of downpour that looks like stair rods. A language that precise about wetness is a language that has stopped being surprised by it.

But talking is where the similarity with Ireland ends. A columnist for DutchNews, an American living in The Hague, described the local approach as something close to a national shrug. Dutch rain, he noted, falls quietly, almost daily, without the thunder he knew back in Iowa, and the sandy ground and canals swallow it. He also learned the maternal wisdom that most of the rain falls next to you, delivered with a perfectly straight face. A stylish Dutch woman told him she does not mind getting wet; she dries off and wipes the mascara. That, to me, is the entire culture in one sentence. No drama, no negotiation, no pub.

The practical kit is serious. Rain suits, rain trousers, ponchos and wellies are wardrobe staples, according to IamExpat, and they are worn without any visible embarrassment. Cycling does not stop for weather, it simply adds waterproofs. Nobody here would consider drizzle a reason to take the car, the tram, or a day off.

The real enemy, as the same DutchNews piece reminds us through a windmill miller, is wind rather than rain. A miller quoted there says it is the breeze after the storm that matters to the sails. Anyone who has cycled into a North Sea headwind on a flat road knows this is not a joke. Flat land has no mercy.

Then there is the winter gloom. Average winter temperatures hover around three degrees, according to IamExpat, with the occasional hard frost that sets the nation skating and dreaming of the Elfstedentocht, last held in 1997. The Dutch do not complain about the dark. They light candles, call it gezellig, and carry on pedalling.

## The Verdict

I will say it plainly: the Dutch win, and not by a close margin. Ireland has the poetry; the Netherlands has the plan. Waiting out a shower is charming for about three months. By the fourth, you realise you have built your entire social calendar around a cloud.

The Dutch treat bad weather as a logistics problem, which means it gets solved rather than discussed. Rain gear, bike lights, lunch indoors, home by dark. It is boring, and it works. The Irish approach is lovelier to sit through, because a pub with a fire and a stranger who wants to talk about the clouds is a fine place to be wet. But it is also an approach that makes the weather the main event of your life.

If your mental health depends on getting outside, choose the Netherlands and buy the trousers. If your mental health depends on being understood while you complain, choose Ireland, where the complaint itself is a form of friendship. I know which one saved my sanity during a long grey November, and it was not the one with the better stories.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com forum poster — Skip the umbrella entirely, because the wind makes it pointless; spend the money on a good waxed or waterproof coat, and treat sunny spells as something to use at once, since the rain is rarely gone for long.*

> *Sarah Moss, The Irish Times — Having moved to Ireland, she genuinely enjoys the rain and the way it changes the landscape, yet finds it strange that a country so defined by wet weather treats it as a problem instead of something to celebrate.*

> *DutchNews columnist — Dutch cyclists keep riding in the rain without fuss, and the local saying that cycling in the rain is easy is passed down with a straight face, even if the soaked newcomer remains unconvinced.*

## Conclusion

So here is what I have learned from sixty countries and a depressing number of damp socks. Weather is never really about weather. In Ireland it is a language, a way to say hello, to complain, to hold on to a bit of community when everything else is grey. In the Netherlands it is a dress code, and the dress code is "carry on".

Neither country will ever give you the sun you secretly want. Both will teach you to live without it, one with a story and the other with a bicycle. If you move to Ireland, buy the hooded coat and learn to love the wait. If you move to the Netherlands, buy the rain trousers and learn to love the wind, because it is coming either way.

And whichever you choose, do not make the mistake of bringing an umbrella. It will not survive the first week, and neither will your dignity.
