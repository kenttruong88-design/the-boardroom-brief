# Moldova Introduces You to Someone and Ireland Introduces You to a Pint

🇲🇩 Moldova vs 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Moldova and Ireland have more in common than either would admit. Both are small, both have a famous history of people leaving, both have a deep national relationship with wine or whiskey, and both believe that a good evening starts at eight and ends when somebody's grandmother calls. What they do not share is a method for meeting a human being. In Moldova you are introduced. In Ireland you are encountered, usually near a bar, usually by accident.

I have been introduced to a man in Chișinău by a cousin of a friend of a neighbour, and I have been chatted up in Dublin by a person who, forty minutes later, I realised had never actually asked me a question. These are two completely different systems for romance and they deserve to be taken seriously.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇲🇩 Moldova ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the invitation to the family table | Arrive empty-handed |
| Join a local club or group to meet people | Wait for strangers to come to you |
| Try the wine, especially if it was made by the host | Refuse a glass without a reason |
| Learn a few words of Romanian or Russian | Assume English will carry you through |

| 🇮🇪 Ireland ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go to the pub and stay for more than one drink | Leave when you finish your pint |
| Buy your round when it is your turn | Slip off before it is your shout |
| Take banter as friendliness | Take it as an insult |
| Be patient with slow, indirect romance | Expect a clear signal of interest |

## 🇲🇩 Moldova

Moldova's social scene is smaller than you expect and warmer than you fear. Expat Exchange, in a post attributed to an anonymous expat, says that expats in Moldova typically have an active social life, joining local clubs and mostly befriending other expats, retirees and digital nomads. Another contributor mentions hiking, biking, skiing, clubs and bars. It is not a big-city menu, but it is a full one, and the capital has a way of making a modest list feel like a plan.

The crucial thing is that it is relational. Social life in Moldova runs on introductions, on friends of friends, on a cousin who knows a person with a flat near the park. Dating follows the same rule. You are less likely to meet someone at a bar than at a birthday dinner, and the dinner will be longer, louder and better fed than anything in a dating app. There is wine, because there is always wine, and there are toasts, because there are always toasts, and a wise guest knows that the glass in front of them is a promise.

Family matters too. A new partner is not a private matter, and the whole table will have an opinion. That can feel intrusive to an outsider and strangely comforting to anyone who has spent a winter alone. In a small country, everyone knows someone, and that cuts both ways. It means help is close at hand. It also means a bad first date is common knowledge by Sunday.

Language is a real factor. Between Romanian and Russian, and a patchwork of ages and regions, the question of which to speak is itself a social negotiation. An expat who learns a handful of phrases in either will be rewarded far out of proportion to the effort.

The pace of a Moldovan relationship is also slower and more serious than many expats expect. People tend to treat an introduction as a step toward something, not a casual pastime, and a partner is likely to be introduced to relatives sooner than a Western European would consider reasonable. That clarity can be refreshing. Nobody has to wonder whether they are dating. The family will have decided already, and will have made a cake.

## 🇮🇪 Ireland

Ireland is a different animal. The social scene is the pub, the match, the club night and the walk home, in roughly that order of importance. The Irish Times ran a profile in 2017 of a Moldovan woman, then 25, from Nisporeni, who arrived in Ireland in October 2016. She lived first in Athy, then in a Dún Laoghaire house with 14 other people, most of them from Ukraine, learned English from YouTube tutorials and washed dishes in a Dublin coffee shop. Her friends included waitresses from Argentina, Australia, France and Poland. In her words, paraphrased, the main reason she learned English so fast was that she felt so alone, and she had to make friends.

That story contains the whole Irish social scene. It is warm, open and surprisingly easy to join once you are in the room, but it is also easy to miss if you are not. She said Dublin was better than Athy because she went out and did more things. Small-town Ireland can feel closed to a newcomer, while the city hands you a cosmopolitan crowd and a hundred pubs.

Dating works through banter. Directness is rare. An Irish person who likes you will tease you, buy you a drink and then deny everything. The pub is the venue, the conversation is the courtship, and the signals are so oblique that a visitor from a more literal culture can spend months wondering whether anyone has been flirting at all. That is the joke, and the delight, and the frustration.

The round system is the other piece of etiquette. When you are out in a group, you take your turn. Skip your shout and you will hear about it for a year. It sounds like a drinking rule and it is really a loyalty test.

The Expat.com threads on dating and friendship in Ireland are thin on data, with one reply apiece, but they point in the same direction as the Irish Times profile: newcomers are often unsure where to start, and the answers come from clubs, classes, sports teams and the pub rather than any organised system. A person planning a move to Cork or Galway, as one poster was, is really asking where the communities are. The answer in Ireland is almost always the nearest group that meets on a regular evening.

## The Verdict

I am going with Ireland, and I say it as someone who has a soft spot for Moldovan hospitality. The Irish scene is bigger, more varied and far more welcoming to a stranger with no family to introduce them. If you arrive alone, as our Moldovan woman did, Ireland gives you a pub, a crowd and a mountain of people who will talk to you.

Moldova's strength is intimacy. A system built on introductions is slower, but it is warmer, and a person who has been introduced has been vouched for. That is a luxury in a world of strangers on apps. If you are lucky enough to land in a circle, you are in for life.

But I cannot pretend that an expat landing in Chișinău has the same range of options as one landing in Dublin. The Irish scene is easier to enter, noisier and more forgiving of mistakes. It is also, I should add, deeply confusing. If you are dating in Ireland and think someone is interested, they may be. They may also be asking for the salt.

## Conclusion

Both countries will welcome you, but on very different terms. In Moldova you are brought into the family, handed a glass and expected to stay. In Ireland you are handed a pint, ignored politely and slowly adopted. Neither is rude. Both are slow. Both reward patience.

So arrive with a bottle in one case and a round in the other. Learn a little of the language, show up to the next thing you are invited to and say yes more than feels comfortable. And remember that the lonely first months are the ones that teach you the most, which is annoying, and true.
