# Two Countries That Share A Flag Joke And Almost Nothing Else About How To Behave In Public

🇳🇿 New Zealand 🇦🇺 Australia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nothing delights an outsider quite like watching a New Zealander and an Australian insist, in the same breath, that they're basically the same and that the other one has got it all wrong. They share a flag so similar that tourists mix it up constantly, an accent that outsiders can't reliably distinguish, and a decades-long sporting rivalry conducted with the grim seriousness of a border dispute. What they do not share, it turns out, is a single working theory of how a person is supposed to behave in a queue, on a beach, or within earshot of a stranger.

I've lived the quiet-apologetic version and the loud-affectionate-insult version back to back, and the whiplash was considerable. One country treats public space as something you tiptoe through, apologising to inanimate objects along the way. The other treats it as a stage, and everyone on it is fair game for a joke you're expected to laugh at.

## Do's & Don'ts: New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Queue without being told to, and stand a genuinely generous distance apart | Raise your voice in public spaces — it reads as aggression far faster than at home |
| Apologise reflexively, even when someone else bumped into you | Cut in line, ever, for any reason; it is treated as close to a moral failing |
| Keep opinions and achievements understated, even when they're objectively impressive | Boast, even mildly — "tall poppy syndrome" will clip you down fast |
| Respect personal space on public transport as near-sacred | Strike up loud conversation with strangers unprompted; a quiet nod is the norm |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect and return a bit of friendly mockery — it's how affection gets expressed | Take "sledging" personally; silence in response reads worse than playing along |
| Join the beach's unspoken rules on space and noise within reason | Be precious about queue order at a casual setting like a kiosk or a pub |
| Speak at a conversational volume that would register as loud almost anywhere else | Assume directness is rudeness; it's usually just efficiency |
| Embrace a looser, louder version of "mate" culture in everyday interactions | Mistake the national sense of humour for permission to be genuinely unkind |

## New Zealand: The Quiet Discomfort Of Being Noticed At All

New Zealand's public-behaviour code runs almost entirely on a single underlying value: don't make yourself the main character of a shared space. Stats NZ's community-wellbeing survey work consistently finds high reported value placed on social harmony and low tolerance for public friction, and living there makes that statistic feel less like data and more like a constant, gentle, ambient pressure to simply take up less room than you're inclined to.

"Tall poppy syndrome" — the cultural reflex to cut down anyone perceived as boasting or standing out — gets mentioned in every guidebook, but the lived version is subtler than the phrase suggests. It's not hostility toward success. It's a near-physical discomfort with anyone drawing attention to themselves in public, success or otherwise, and it shapes everything from how loudly people speak on a bus to how insistently a queue self-polices without a single word being exchanged. Cut in line in Auckland and you will not be shouted at. You will be met with a silence so pointed it does more damage than shouting ever could.

The upside of all this understatement is a public sphere that feels, almost uniquely among English-speaking countries, genuinely calm. Personal space on public transport is respected to a degree that borders on reverent. Strangers don't strike up conversation uninvited. Apologies flow constantly, directed at furniture, at doorways, at the general concept of existing near another person. It took me months to stop reading this as coldness. It isn't coldness. It's an entire culture that has collectively agreed the kindest thing you can do for a stranger is leave them alone, and once you accept the premise, the quiet becomes something closer to restful.

## Australia: Affection Delivered At Volume, With Insults Attached

Cross the Tasman and the entire operating system flips. Australian public life runs at a volume and directness that New Zealand would flag as borderline confrontational, and the Australian Bureau of Statistics' general social survey work on community interaction bears out what any beach, pub, or servo queue will teach you within a week: Australians are simply, structurally, more comfortable being loud near strangers, and more comfortable having strangers be loud near them.

"Sledging" — the ritualised, affectionate insult-trading that shows up everywhere from backyard cricket to checkout-line small talk — is the single hardest thing for outsiders to calibrate correctly. Get called a drongo by someone you've just met at a barbecue and the correct response is not offence. It is an insult right back, ideally a sharper one, delivered with a grin. Fail to play along and you haven't avoided conflict — you've signalled, somehow, that you don't belong in the conversation at all, which is its own small social cost.

The beach deserves its own paragraph, because it functions as something close to Australia's real town square. The Department of Home Affairs' own settlement guidance for new arrivals spends real space on beach etiquette — not because the rules are complicated, but because the volume and physical closeness of a crowded Bondi or Cottesloe weekend will feel, to anyone arriving from a more reserved culture, like an aggressive invasion of personal space rather than the relaxed, communal norm locals genuinely experience it as. Directness carries through everywhere else too: the blunt feedback, the loud opinion offered unprompted, the total absence of New Zealand's tiptoeing instinct. It isn't rudeness, mostly. It's a culture that has decided the kindest thing you can do for a stranger is include them loudly in the joke, rather than politely leave them out of it.

## The Verdict

New Zealand optimises for a public sphere where nobody is embarrassed. Australia optimises for one where nobody is excluded, even if inclusion arrives as a mild insult about your footwear. Both are, in their own register, genuinely warm cultures — they've just built opposite theories of what warmth in public is supposed to sound like. If quiet, considerate, low-friction public space is your currency, New Zealand pays out generously. If you'd rather be roasted within an inch of your dignity by someone who likes you, Australia's the better bet. I'm giving this one to New Zealand, purely because nobody there has ever once called me a drongo before I'd finished my coffee.

## Conclusion

The flag joke writes itself and so does the deeper one: two countries that look nearly identical from a distance and operate on almost opposite social physics up close. New Zealand's public behaviour says "I will not make this moment about me." Australia's says "this moment is better if we're all in on the joke together." Confuse the two on arrival — stay too quiet in Sydney, too loud in Auckland — and you will spend your first few months slightly, persistently out of step with everyone around you, for reasons nobody will bother explaining, because to them, it was never a rule. It was just how a decent person behaves.
