# 🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire vs 🇨🇦 Canada: One Guards Its Green Space With a Rainforest, the Other Abandons It Every January

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Abidjan has an actual, intact patch of primary rainforest sitting inside city limits, complete with pygmy hippos nobody mentions until you nearly step on evidence of one, and treats it as a slightly inconvenient but non-negotiable fact of urban life. Canada has, per capita, some of the most generous public park acreage on the planet, and spends roughly five months of the year acting as though parks are a theoretical concept best discussed indoors, by a fire, with mitten in hand.

Both countries are, technically, extremely good at green space. Only one of them actually uses it year-round, and it isn't the one with the national park inside the capital.

## Do's & Don'ts: Côte d'Ivoire

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit Parc National du Banco respectfully — it's an actual protected rainforest ecosystem inside Abidjan, not a landscaped amenity | Wander off marked paths looking for wildlife "photo opportunities" — the pygmy hippos are shy, protected, and not interested in your Instagram |
| Use neighbourhood green spaces communally — picnics here are multi-family, multi-generational affairs, not solo laptop sessions | Expect manicured, mown-every-week park lawns everywhere; maintenance budgets vary wildly by commune |
| Go early if you want shade and quiet — midday heat empties most green spaces reliably by 11am | Assume a "park" on a map is maintained the same way the famous ones are — some are aspirational more than actual |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Embrace the summer park frenzy — slackline, frisbee, impromptu barbecues, entire friend groups colonising a patch of grass for six hours | Expect any of that same energy in February; most urban parks go functionally dormant under snow |
| Leash your dog where signage says leash, and respect off-leash hours scrupulously — Canadians police this socially, politely, relentlessly | Assume a park bench is empty because it's free — in winter it might simply be buried |
| Learn your city's specific park-alcohol rules; some allow a permitted picnic drink, most don't, and enforcement varies by neighbourhood vibe, not just by law | Picnic directly after a off-leash hour ends in the same unfenced field — the timing overlap is a known hazard, ask any local dog owner |

### Côte d'Ivoire: the rainforest as a civic fact, not a feature

Parc National du Banco sits roughly three kilometres from downtown Abidjan, a genuine remnant of primary tropical rainforest with pygmy hippos, endemic bird species, and canopy cover that drops the temperature by several degrees the moment you step under it — and the city has built its relationship with it with a kind of matter-of-fact pride that never tips into tourist-brochure sentimentality. Locals don't talk about Banco the way a Londoner talks about Hyde Park; they talk about it the way you'd mention a slightly eccentric relative who happens to live down the road and occasionally causes a stir. It's there, it's protected, you don't mess with it, and you certainly don't go wandering off-trail looking for hippos to film.

Beyond Banco, Abidjan's neighbourhood green spaces are a study in resourcefulness rather than municipal polish — maintenance budgets are uneven, mown grass is not guaranteed, and "park" can mean anything from a genuinely pleasant communal lawn to a patch of shaded ground that functions as one through sheer collective agreement that it should. What corporate transferees consistently underestimate is how deeply communal the actual use of green space is here: a Sunday picnic isn't two people and a blanket, it's three families, a folding table, several pots that definitely weren't meant to travel, and a level of shared, loud, joyful occupation of public land that would get side-eyed as "a lot" in most European cities and is simply Tuesday-normal here. The heat dictates the rhythm more than any sign ever could — go early, or go at dusk, and the park will be yours; go at noon, and you'll have it to yourself for reasons that will become extremely obvious within four minutes.

### Canada: paradise with a seasonal closing notice nobody posted

Canada's urban parks are, by almost any global metric, extraordinary — Vancouver's Stanley Park, Toronto's ravine system, Montreal's Mont-Royal, all genuinely world-class, genuinely well-funded, genuinely beloved. For roughly five months of the year. The other seven, give or take, the relationship between Canadians and their own parks becomes a seasonal romance that cools dramatically every October and doesn't properly warm back up until the May long weekend, at which point the entire population behaves as though green space has just been invented, colonising every patch of grass with a ferocity that makes Abidjan's communal picnics look restrained by comparison.

The off-leash dog culture deserves its own paragraph, because it is bafflingly specific and intensely, socially enforced: designated hours, designated zones, an entire unwritten code of conduct around which dogs get to roam free and when, policed not by any visible authority but by a kind of collective Canadian passive-aggression that will absolutely let you know, politely, that you've brought your unleashed dog into a leash-only hour. Winter changes everything structurally — many park amenities (public washrooms, drinking fountains, some trail sections) simply close, snow removal prioritises roads over park paths, and the parks that were, in July, packed with slackliners and barbecues become eerily, beautifully empty, used mainly by the small, hardy subset of the population who cross-country ski or walk dogs in minus-twenty weather out of what can only be described as principle. New arrivals from year-round-green-space cultures find this baffling; Canadians find it so normal they barely discuss it, the way you don't discuss the fact that your kitchen has a stove.

### Where the children actually go tells you everything

Watch where families send their kids to play and the two cultures' relationship with green space becomes even clearer. In Abidjan, formal playground infrastructure is genuinely patchy — purpose-built play equipment exists in wealthier neighbourhoods and international-school-adjacent areas, but in most communes, children play in the same multi-use green spaces as everyone else, improvising games across shared ground that also hosts picnics, football matches, and the occasional goat. There's a flexibility to this that Canadian playgrounds, by contrast, have engineered almost entirely out of existence: Canadian municipal playgrounds are rigorously standardised, safety-certified down to the rubber matting and the exact fall height of every piece of equipment, age-segregated, and typically empty for a solid third of the year under snow, at which point the city's considerable investment in play infrastructure simply goes dormant until spring. Ivorian children get less equipment but more continuous, cross-generational access; Canadian children get vastly superior, safer equipment for considerably fewer usable months. Neither country would describe this trade-off as a trade-off. Both are simply doing what their climate and budget allow, and calling it normal.

## The Verdict

Côte d'Ivoire wins on consistency — green space here is used, communally and constantly, regardless of heat, because the alternative (staying inside, alone, in that heat) is worse. Canada wins on sheer acreage and quality, but loses significant points for essentially mothballing a genuine civic treasure for a third of the calendar year. If I had to choose where to actually spend my Sunday afternoons, I'd take Abidjan's crowded, joyful, slightly chaotic green spaces over Toronto's gorgeous-but-empty winter ravines every single time. A park nobody's using isn't really a park. It's a very well-maintained field.

## Conclusion

Green space tells you a lot about a country's actual relationship with comfort versus climate — Abidjan simply refuses to let heat dictate whether people gather outdoors, while Canada lets winter dictate almost everything, and both choices are, in their own way, completely rational responses to the hand each country was dealt. Go to Banco respectfully. Go to a Toronto ravine in July, not February, unless you enjoy solitude and the specific dignity of being the only idiot on the trail.
