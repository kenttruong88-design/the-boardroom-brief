🇸🇬 **Singapore** vs 🇭🇰 **Hong Kong**
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Singapore and Hong Kong get filed together constantly — two gleaming Asian financial hubs, both obsessed with efficiency, both allergic to jaywalking fines going unpaid. Then you try to buy a chicken in each city and realise they couldn't be less alike. Singapore has engineered the wet market into a sanitised, air-conditioned, Michelin-adjacent experience with a hawker centre built in for your trouble. Hong Kong has kept its wet markets loud, wet, slightly terrifying, and absolutely non-negotiable on freshness, because the alternative — a supermarket chicken — is treated by most older residents as an insult to the concept of dinner.

I have shopped for groceries in both, and both have made me feel, at various points, like an incompetent tourist who doesn't know what a "good" fish looks like. The difference is that Singapore will forgive you for it with a loyalty card. Hong Kong will simply let the auntie behind you tut audibly and move on with her life.

## Do's & Don'ts: Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a FairPrice or Cold Storage loyalty card immediately — the points add up fast | Expect wet markets to disappear just because the supermarkets are everywhere |
| Try a proper wet market at least once, ideally with a local who knows the good stalls | Assume the air-conditioned supermarket version is automatically fresher — it often isn't |
| Bring your own bags — the plastic bag charge is real and mildly judgmental | Dawdle in the checkout queue sorting your wallet; Singapore shoppers move with purpose |
| Shop the hawker-adjacent wet markets early for the best seafood | Expect weekend crowds at Tekka or Tiong Bahru to be anything but relentless |

## Do's & Don'ts: Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go to the wet market before 9am if you want the good stuff, not the leftovers | Poke or squeeze produce without intending to buy — vendors will call you out |
| Learn basic Cantonese numbers — it speeds up every single transaction | Expect stallholders to haggle happily over small amounts; it's not that kind of market |
| Bring cash and a trolley bag — card payment is still patchy at many stalls | Assume ParknShop or Wellcome is a like-for-like substitute for live seafood |
| Watch how locals choose produce before copying their picks | Buy the first fish you see — regulars circle the whole market before committing |

## Singapore: Efficiency, Even in the Produce Aisle

Singapore did to grocery shopping what it does to everything else: it audited the chaos, kept the parts that worked, and engineered the rest into a system. The wet market survives, but increasingly as a curated experience rather than a default — Tekka Centre and Tiong Bahru Market are genuinely excellent, but they coexist with NTUC FairPrice, Cold Storage, and a dozen home-delivery apps that have quietly become where most actual households do their weekly shop. The result is a city where you can buy a perfectly good fish from an air-conditioned supermarket at 11pm on a Tuesday, something that would be unthinkable, and slightly suspicious, in most of the region.

What surprises newcomers is how seriously Singaporeans still take the wet market ritual even as supermarkets dominate volume. Older residents will drive past three supermarkets to buy their greens from a specific stall they've used for twenty years, because a relationship with a vendor is still considered a form of quality control no barcode can replicate. Expats who only ever shop at FairPrice get a perfectly convenient, perfectly adequate grocery life — and miss entirely the layer of the culture that treats knowing your fishmonger as basic competence, not eccentricity. The efficiency Singapore is famous for shows up here too: queues move fast, self-checkout is everywhere, and nobody is shopping for entertainment. You're in, you're out, and the produce is reliably fine, if rarely thrilling.

The hawker centre complicates this picture in an interesting way, functioning as a kind of social compromise between the wet market's tradition and the supermarket's convenience — cooked food bought fresh daily, from vendors many families have used for years, eaten communally rather than prepared at home. It's not groceries in the literal sense, but it absorbs a huge amount of the function groceries serve elsewhere, which is part of why Singaporean kitchens are, on average, used far less intensively than their counterparts across the causeway. Newcomers who commit to actually cooking at home sometimes find this slightly isolating, since the social infrastructure around food assumes you'll be eating out, cheaply and well, rather than shopping and cooking the way you might have back home.

## Hong Kong: The Wet Market Is Not Going Anywhere

Hong Kong's supermarkets — ParknShop, Wellcome — are perfectly functional and almost entirely beside the point for anyone who grew up here. The wet market remains the actual heart of daily food shopping, not as nostalgia but as genuine preference: live or freshly killed seafood and meat, vegetables bought that morning, and a level of freshness scrutiny that treats a supermarket's pre-packaged chicken as a last resort for the desperate or the foreign. This isn't sentimentality. Older Hong Kongers will tell you, bluntly, that the texture and taste difference is real, and that a culture built on fresh seafood simply never developed the habit of trusting something that's been refrigerated for three days.

This extends into genuine superstition around freshness that outsiders often mistake for mere preference. Fish bought still swimming is considered meaningfully different, not just marginally fresher, than fish bought dead on ice — and plenty of home cooks will tell you the dish simply tastes wrong otherwise, a claim that's hard to argue with once you've actually tasted the difference. Seasonal produce gets the same treatment: a specific green only available for a few weeks a year will draw queues specifically for it, with locals timing their shopping trips around a harvest window the way other cultures might time theirs around a sale. The wet market, in other words, isn't simply where Hong Kong buys food. It's where a fairly exacting set of inherited standards about what food should even be gets enforced, stall by stall, every single morning.

For an outsider, the wet market is a genuine sensory event — fish tanks, the smell, vendors gutting things at a speed that borders on violence, and absolutely zero patience for anyone blocking the narrow aisle to take a photo. The etiquette is unspoken but strict: you watch, you learn, you don't touch what you're not buying, and you move with the flow of regulars who've been doing this same loop for decades. Academic research has tracked wet markets slowly shrinking under redevelopment pressure, and the anxiety around that is real — this isn't a quaint tourist backdrop, it's genuinely how a huge portion of the city still eats, and its decline is treated as a cultural loss, not an inevitability to shrug at.

## The Verdict

Singapore wins on convenience, hygiene, and not having to confront a live eel before 8am. Hong Kong wins on everything that actually matters to someone who takes food seriously: freshness, ritual, and a level of quality control enforced by decades of neighbourhood reputation rather than a corporate supply chain. If you want grocery shopping to be fast and forgettable, move to Singapore. If you want grocery shopping to be an actual event that occasionally terrifies you, Hong Kong's wet markets are undefeated.

Given the choice, I'll take the terror. A fish that's still looking at you is a fish you can trust.

## Conclusion

Both cities will teach you the same lesson eventually: convenience and quality are not the same axis, and a society gets to choose which one it optimises daily life around. Singapore chose convenience and kept a tasteful amount of tradition on the side, like a garnish. Hong Kong kept the tradition load-bearing and let the convenience be optional. Neither is wrong. But only one of them will make you watch a man gut a grouper six inches from your face before 9am, and honestly, that one's more memorable.
