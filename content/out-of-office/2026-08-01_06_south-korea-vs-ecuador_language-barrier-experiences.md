# Seoul Will Translate Everything For You Except The One Thing You Need. Quito Just Expects You To Learn Spanish.

🇰🇷 South Korea + 🇪🇨 Ecuador

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

South Korea has poured extraordinary resources into English signage, translation apps, and tourist infrastructure, and yet somehow the exact phrase you need at 11pm outside a pharmacy is never the one covered. Ecuador has poured comparatively little into accommodating non-Spanish speakers and, refreshingly, doesn't pretend otherwise — you either learn functional Spanish reasonably fast or you spend your trip gesturing at menus like a contestant on a very stressful game show.

I've relied on Papago translating a pharmacist's rapid-fire Korean at midnight, and I've relied on a patient Quito shopkeeper slowly repeating a word four different ways until it clicked. Both experiences were humbling. Only one of them left me with an actual, transferable skill by the time I left.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇰🇷 South Korea**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Download Papago or Naver's translator before arrival — Google Translate underperforms here | Rely on English signage outside major tourist zones like Myeongdong or Gangnam |
| Learn to read Hangul basics; it takes about an hour and unlocks huge amounts | Assume younger Koreans are fluent in English just because they studied it in school |
| Use translation apps' voice/conversation mode for real-time back-and-forth | Speak loudly or slowly at someone as if that bridges the gap — it doesn't |
| Carry your destination written in Korean for taxis; drivers rarely speak English | Expect restaurant staff to have English menus outside heavily touristed areas |

**🇪🇨 Ecuador**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Spanish before arriving — English is genuinely rare outside Quito's tourist core | Expect Andean Spanish to sound like textbook Spanish; the accent and pace differ |
| Pick up a few Quechua-derived words used regionally — locals notice and appreciate the effort | Assume everyone speaks the "standard" Spanish you learned in school |
| Use hand gestures and patience in markets — locals are generally warm about the struggle | Get frustrated audibly; it's considered poor form and won't speed things up |
| Take a short local Spanish class in your first week if staying longer-term | Expect widespread translation apps to work well offline in rural Andean areas |

## South Korea: A Fortress Of Infrastructure With Surprising Gaps

Seoul has genuinely impressive multilingual infrastructure by global standards — subway announcements in four languages, English menus in most tourist-adjacent restaurants, and signage that would put most Western capitals to shame. And yet the actual lived experience of a language barrier here is oddly specific: it's not that nobody can help you, it's that the help evaporates the moment you step half a block outside the designated tourist infrastructure. Ask for directions in Myeongdong and you'll likely get fluent English back. Ask the same question two neighbourhoods over and you'll get a kind, apologetic head shake and, if you're lucky, a phone thrust toward you with a translation app already open.

Konglish — the Korean adaptation of English loanwords, often altered just enough in pronunciation or meaning to throw off native speakers completely — is its own special trap. You'll hear a word that sounds almost exactly like the English original and assume you've understood, only to discover it means something subtly, sometimes hilariously different. Papago, developed specifically for Korean, consistently outperforms Google Translate here, and its real-time conversation mode saved me more than once at a pharmacy counter trying to explain a very specific ailment through gestures and increasing desperation. Koreans themselves are, in my experience, incredibly patient with the struggle — there's little of the impatience you might fear, just a genuine, slightly apologetic effort to bridge the gap using whatever tools are at hand.

## Ecuador: No Safety Net, But No Pretence Either

Ecuador offers none of Seoul's translation infrastructure, and it doesn't pretend to — outside Quito's historic centre and the touristed streets of Cuenca or Baños, English drops off sharply, and you're left leaning entirely on whatever Spanish you've managed to accumulate. What makes this less intimidating than it sounds is the almost universal patience Ecuadorians extend to visibly struggling foreigners; I've had shopkeepers slow down, rephrase, and repeat a word four separate ways with genuine warmth rather than the faint irritation you sometimes get elsewhere when you fumble the local language.

The Andean Spanish spoken across the highlands carries its own distinct rhythm and vocabulary, peppered with Quechua loanwords for everything from food to family terms, which means even reasonably confident Spanish speakers arriving from Spain or Mexico can find themselves briefly thrown by regional expressions. Learning even a handful of these — "guagua" for baby, "chuchaqui" for hangover — earns an outsized amount of goodwill, a small effort that gets noticed and rewarded far more than perfect grammar would. There's no real substitute for putting in the work here; translation apps struggle with patchy rural connectivity in the Andes and the Amazon basin, and you'll find yourself relying on old-fashioned gesturing, patience, and a phrasebook far more than in Seoul's tech-saturated streets.

## The Verdict

Seoul gives you an impressive safety net that occasionally, maddeningly, has a hole in exactly the spot you need it most. Ecuador gives you no safety net at all, but compensates with a warmth and patience that makes the struggle genuinely bearable, even enjoyable once you stop fighting it. If you want the comfort of infrastructure with occasional gaps, Seoul wins. If you want to actually improve your Spanish out of sheer necessity, Ecuador will do it for you whether you intended that or not — and honestly, that forced growth is the better travel outcome, even if it's the less comfortable one in the moment.

## Conclusion

South Korea will make you feel technologically supported right up until the exact moment you need help most, at which point you're improvising with an app and a lot of hand gestures anyway. Ecuador skips the illusion of a safety net entirely and just hands you the challenge directly, softened by a patience and warmth that makes the learning curve genuinely enjoyable rather than punishing. Download Papago before Seoul. Learn actual Spanish, not just textbook Spanish, before Quito. And in both places, accept that the language barrier is not a problem to be solved before you arrive — it's a part of the trip you'll spend the whole time working through, badly at first, and that's exactly the point.
