# One Country Has Twenty-Two Languages and Somehow Still Understands You, The Other Has One and Refuses To

🇮🇳 India vs 🇵🇱 Poland — Language Barrier Experiences

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular arrogance to assuming English will save you anywhere, and both India and Poland will disabuse you of it within days, for entirely opposite reasons. India has twenty-two officially recognized languages, hundreds more spoken regionally, and yet somehow produces enough functional English — inherited, adapted, remixed into something wonderfully its own — that a determined foreigner can usually stumble through. Poland has one dominant language, Polish, spoken with a consonant density that seems designed as a deterrent, and a population split cleanly by age on whether they'll bother meeting you halfway in English at all.

I have been gloriously misunderstood in six different Indian states in six different ways, and I have stood in a Kraków pharmacy in total silence while a pharmacist and I simply stared at each other, both certain the other should be trying harder. Only one of these experiences ended with a stranger inviting me to their cousin's wedding.

## Do's & Don'ts

**India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn a handful of Hindi phrases even in English-heavy cities — it earns instant goodwill | Assume the same language works everywhere; Tamil Nadu, Bengal, and Punjab are entirely different worlds |
| Embrace "Hinglish" — mixed English-Hindi conversation is normal, not a compromise | Correct someone's English grammar; Indian English has its own valid conventions, not errors |
| Use hand gestures and head movements freely — the head wobble is a genuine communication tool | Expect written signage to match spoken language; regional scripts vary hugely by state |

**Poland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Polish greetings before arriving — even minimal effort changes how you're received | Assume older Poles speak English; many were schooled in Russian, not English, under communism |
| Use a translation app proactively in pharmacies, government offices, and smaller towns | Speak loudly and slowly in English expecting comprehension to improve — it doesn't work, ever |
| Lean on younger Poles and cities like Warsaw and Kraków, where English is genuinely widespread | Take brusque, minimal responses personally; directness isn't rudeness, it's just Polish |

## India: Chaos That Somehow Communicates Anyway

India's relationship with English is one of history's stranger inheritances — colonial in origin, thoroughly indigenized in practice, and now spoken by more people than in the UK itself, albeit in a form that bears about as much resemblance to British English as a remix bears to the original track. "Prepone" is a word. "Do the needful" is a complete, if faintly menacing, sentence. Indian English isn't broken English, whatever a smug tourist might assume; it's its own fully formed dialect with its own grammar logic, and treating it as an error to be corrected is one of the fastest ways to mark yourself as an insufferable foreigner.

The real complexity isn't English at all — it's the sheer linguistic density beneath it. Twenty-two languages hold official status, hundreds more exist regionally, and a taxi ride from Chennai to Kolkata takes you through several entirely distinct linguistic worlds, each with its own script, its own idioms, its own relationship to Hindi (which, contrary to outside assumption, is not universally spoken or welcomed, especially in the south, where suggesting Hindi as a lingua franca can land you in a genuinely tense conversation about linguistic politics).

What makes India navigable for outsiders despite this is a kind of communicative generosity — a willingness to meet confusion with gestures, patience, and the famous head wobble, which manages to convey agreement, acknowledgment, and polite disagreement all with the same movement, decoded only through years of immersion or a very good local friend. Signage complicates things further: Devanagari in the north, Tamil script in the south, Bengali in the east, each requiring its own recalibration. And yet, somehow, foreigners muddle through constantly, aided by an overall culture that finds a struggling visitor endearing rather than embarrassing. It's chaotic. It's also, against all odds, one of the more forgiving places on earth to not speak the language.

## Poland: One Language, Zero Patience For Guessing

Poland presents the opposite problem entirely: a single dominant language, spoken by nearly the entire population, structurally unforgiving to outsiders in a way few European languages manage. Polish has seven grammatical cases, consonant clusters that look typo'd even to native readers of other Slavic languages, and a pronunciation system that punishes confident mispronunciation with a swiftness Indian English never would. There's no friendly hybrid dialect to lean on here, no generous linguistic middle ground — you either learn actual Polish, or you rely entirely on whoever happens to speak English in the room.

And that reliance is generationally uneven in a way that catches many expats off guard. Younger Poles, especially in Warsaw, Kraków, and Wrocław, often speak excellent English, learned through school, media, and international exposure post-1989. Older Poles, particularly outside major cities, frequently don't — many were educated under a system that prioritized Russian, not English, and simply never had reason or opportunity to pick it up later. This creates a strange generational language cliff: fluent conversation with a twenty-five-year-old barista, total silence with the sixty-year-old pharmacist two doors down, and no obvious way to predict which you'll encounter until you're already mid-sentence.

Poles are also, culturally, direct to the point of appearing curt, which compounds the language barrier for anyone expecting warmth to smooth over confusion. A shrug and a one-word answer isn't hostility, it's simply the Polish register for "I don't have the English for this and I'm not going to perform enthusiasm about it." It takes a while to stop taking that personally. Once you do, and once you've learned perhaps twenty functional Polish phrases, the country becomes dramatically easier — not because anyone's meeting you halfway, but because you've finally started meeting them.

## The Verdict

India wins on sheer communicative generosity — a linguistic obstacle course somehow rendered navigable by warmth, gesture, and a genuinely global dialect of English that meets you more than halfway even at its most bewildering. Poland wins on precision and consistency once you clear the initial wall — Polish itself is a single, learnable target, unlike India's shifting regional mosaic, it's just an unusually tall wall to clear.

If you'd rather struggle warmly across twenty-two languages than struggle coldly across one, book the flight to Delhi. If you'd rather invest properly in learning one difficult language and be rewarded with total fluency eventually, Warsaw is your project. Either way, arrive humble. Both countries have, quite reasonably, stopped being impressed by foreigners who assume the world owes them English.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com India forum — a new arrival described being completely lost trying to use Hindi phrases in Chennai, only to be gently informed that Tamil was the only language that would actually get them anywhere locally.*

> *Internations Warsaw — a member recounted an entire pharmacy transaction conducted via Google Translate held up on both sides of the counter, calling it "the most efficient silent conversation of my life."*

> *Quora — someone asked if English alone was enough to live in India, and the top answer read simply: "Enough to survive, not enough to be understood the way you think you are."*

## Conclusion

India and Poland both refuse to let you coast on English, just via opposite mechanisms — one drowns you in linguistic variety until you find a raft, the other hands you a single, difficult raft and expects you to build your own oars. Learn a few words in either place, badly, sincerely, and watch how quickly the door opens. Refuse to, and you'll spend your entire stay narrating your own confusion to strangers who have already, correctly, stopped listening.
