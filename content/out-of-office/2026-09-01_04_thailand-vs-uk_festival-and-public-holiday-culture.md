# One Country Throws Water, the Other Throws Shade

🇹🇭 Thailand vs 🇬🇧 UK — Festival and Public Holiday Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Thailand celebrates its New Year by nationally, enthusiastically, and unapologetically soaking every single person within range for three consecutive days. The UK celebrates its bank holidays by having a barbecue that gets rained on and complaining about it with real, sustained passion until the following bank holiday arrives to disappoint everyone again. Both countries take their days off extremely seriously. Only one of them has weaponised the concept of celebration into actual crowd-sourced warfare.

I have been drenched to the bone on Bangla Road during Songkran by a delighted eight-year-old with a industrial-grade water gun, and I have stood in a British beer garden in June wearing a coat while everyone insisted, through chattering teeth, that this counted as summer. Public holidays reveal more about a national character than almost anything else, mostly because they show you what people do the second nobody's making them work.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Thailand**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a proper waterproof phone pouch before Songkran, not during it | Wear anything you actually care about on the streets in mid-April |
| Visit a temple and pour scented water over Buddha images if invited | Assume Songkran is just a street party — the merit-making side matters too |
| Book travel and hotels months ahead for the New Year period | Expect to get anything done April 13–15 — the whole country stops |
| Join the water fights with good humour, even if you didn't sign up for it | Take a soaking personally — everyone gets hit, that's the entire point |

**United Kingdom**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the actual date every year — bank holidays move and nobody remembers | Plan an outdoor event without a rain contingency, ever, regardless of forecast |
| Book trains and hotels early — bank holiday weekend prices spike hard | Assume shops and transport run normal hours — many scale back |
| Embrace the barbecue-in-drizzle tradition; it is culturally load-bearing | Expect enthusiasm to match the actual number of days off — there aren't many |
| Learn which holidays are UK-wide versus nation-specific | Assume Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland share England's exact calendar |

## Thailand: A National Holiday That Fights Back

Songkran is, on paper, a water festival marking the traditional Thai New Year. In practice, for three days every April, it is the closest thing to a nationally sanctioned street war that any country runs with a straight face and a UNESCO endorsement to match. What starts as a gentle, symbolic pouring of water over the hands of elders and Buddha images — rod nam dam hua, a genuinely tender ritual of respect and purification — has, in cities like Bangkok, Chiang Mai, and especially Phuket's Bangla Road, evolved into an all-ages, all-comers water gun free-for-all that treats "bystander" as a concept the entire country has collectively agreed to ignore.

The genius of it is how completely it flattens hierarchy for three days. Your taxi driver will soak you. Your landlord will soak you. A monk-adjacent uncle you've never met will soak you with what can only be described as professional-grade equipment. Nobody is exempt, nobody is spared, and the correct response — the only response that doesn't mark you instantly as a miserable foreigner — is to laugh, get soaked back, and understand that you have just been fully, if damply, welcomed into the neighbourhood.

What surprises newcomers isn't the water fights themselves but how seriously the country still treats the ritual underneath them. Temple visits, sand chedi building, releasing fish and birds for merit — these happen quietly, often at dawn, before the streets turn into a battlefield, and skipping straight to the water gun without understanding any of that is a bit like showing up to a wedding reception and missing the ceremony. The holiday works precisely because it holds both registers — sacred and absolutely unhinged — at once, without either one undercutting the other.

## UK: The Art of Getting Rained On With Dignity

British public holidays operate on an entirely different emotional register: managed expectation. There are eight bank holidays a year in England and Wales, which is a genuinely modest allowance by European standards, and the entire national culture around them has calcified into a specific, beloved ritual of optimism followed swiftly by disappointment. You will plan a barbecue. It will rain. You will have the barbecue anyway, under an umbrella, and describe it afterwards as "actually really nice, in the end," and you will mean it, mostly.

What expats consistently underestimate is the sheer logistical chaos a bank holiday triggers. Trains get replaced by buses for engineering works nobody scheduled around the holiday, hotel prices near the coast triple overnight, and half of Britain simultaneously decides to drive to the same three seaside towns, creating traffic that will genuinely test your relationship with whoever's in the passenger seat. The other trap is assuming the calendar is uniform: Scotland observes different bank holidays than England, St Andrew's Day matters north of the border and not south of it, and Northern Ireland has its own additions entirely. Get this wrong once, book a flight home on what you assumed was a normal Tuesday, and you'll understand why locals check the actual date every single year rather than trusting memory.

The emotional core of British holiday culture, though, is the gap between the modest number of days off and the wildly disproportionate anticipation each one generates. A long weekend in May gets discussed with the reverence other cultures reserve for actual festivals. It's less about the holiday itself and more about the collective, slightly performative relief of having one at all.

## The Verdict

Thailand wins on pure spectacle, no contest, but the UK wins on something quieter: predictability wrapped in self-deprecation. Songkran will give you a genuinely unforgettable three days and possibly a waterlogged phone. British bank holidays will give you a soggy barbecue and a story about the M5 that you'll tell for years. If I had to pick one culture's relationship with celebration to actually live inside, I'd take Thailand's full-hearted chaos over Britain's managed melancholy — but I'd bring a raincoat to both, because apparently nobody, anywhere, controls the weather on the days that matter most.

## Conclusion

Public holidays are the purest window into a country's actual personality, stripped of the working-week performance everyone puts on the other 361 days. Thailand's is loud, communal, and drenching. Britain's is understated, weather-defiant, and secretly quite moving once you understand the barbecue was never really about the weather. Show up for either with the right attitude, and you'll leave with a story. Show up with your good shoes on, and honestly, that's on you.
