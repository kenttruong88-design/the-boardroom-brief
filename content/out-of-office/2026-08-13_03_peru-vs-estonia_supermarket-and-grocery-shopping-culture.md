# One Country Will Haggle You Into Submission Over a Bag of Onions. The Other Won't Make Eye Contact Long Enough to Let You Try.

🇵🇪 🇪🇪 Peru vs Estonia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Grocery shopping in Peru is a full-contact sport played in the open air, at volume, with strangers who will insult your onions to your face before selling them to you anyway. Grocery shopping in Estonia is a hushed, frictionless transaction conducted almost entirely through a self-checkout screen, in which the ideal outcome is that no human being acknowledges your existence at any point. Both are efficient in their own way. Only one of them will leave you slightly emotionally exhausted and clutching a free extra avocado you didn't ask for.

I have haggled in a Lima mercado over three soles' worth of aji peppers with a woman who called me "hija" while absolutely fleecing me, and I have stood in a Tallinn Rimi in total silence for eleven minutes while my card, my app, and a robot voice negotiated my payment without a single human word exchanged. Both are, in their own way, deeply culturally honest. Neither prepares you for the other.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Haggle at the mercado — it's expected, even enjoyed | Haggle at a supermarket chain like Wong or Plaza Vea — fixed prices are fixed |
| Bring small bills; vendors rarely have change for large notes | Squeeze the fruit unless you intend to buy it — vendors notice and mind |
| Greet the vendor before asking prices — courtesy comes first | Rush the transaction; a little conversation is part of the deal |
| Bring your own bag; plastic bags often cost extra or aren't offered | Expect refrigerated produce sections everywhere — much shopping is same-day fresh |

### 🇪🇪 Estonia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the self-checkout; it's faster and culturally preferred | Attempt small talk with the cashier — it will be met with polite confusion |
| Download the Rimi or Selver app for real discounts | Expect a bagger or any help packing — you're on your own |
| Recycle bottles at the pandipakid (deposit machines) for cash back | Dawdle in the checkout queue; efficiency is a shared expectation |
| Shop early or late to avoid the after-work rush | Assume Sunday hours match the rest of the week — some stores trim them |

## Peru: Commerce as Conversation

Shopping for groceries in Peru, at least outside the international supermarket chains, is inseparable from negotiation, and negotiation is inseparable from relationship-building. At a mercado like Surquillo or San Camilo, the transaction doesn't start with a price, it starts with a greeting, a bit of banter, maybe an inquiry about your family or your day, and only then does the actual haggling begin — a ritual both parties understand is partly theatre and partly serious business. Vendors call regular customers "casera" or "caserita," a term of genuine warmth that signals you've graduated from tourist to recognised local, and that graduation comes with real discounts and real extra handfuls of cilantro thrown in for free.

The produce itself is staggering — Peru's biodiversity means markets stock varieties of potato and corn that don't exist anywhere else on Earth, and vendors take real pride in explaining which purple potato is best for which dish, unprompted and at length. Refrigeration is inconsistent by design; much of what's sold was harvested or caught that morning, which means the freshness is extraordinary but also means you shop more often, in smaller quantities, because nothing is built to last a week in your fridge.

What catches newcomers off guard is the sheer sensory intensity — the noise, the smell of fish stalls next to flower stalls next to spice stalls, vendors calling out prices and specials over each other, and the expectation that you'll actually engage rather than silently browse. Turning up with headphones in and eyes down isn't just antisocial, it's read as faintly rude, a refusal of the relationship the whole system is built on. Once you lean into it — learn a few vendors' names, accept the haggling as a form of respect rather than confrontation — Peruvian grocery shopping becomes one of the most genuinely human parts of the week, not an errand to survive but a small daily ritual of belonging.

## Estonia: Commerce as Frictionless Silence

Estonian grocery shopping operates on the opposite premise entirely: the best interaction is the one that barely happens. This is, after all, the country that digitised its entire government before most of Europe had figured out online banking, and that same instinct for removing unnecessary friction applies directly to buying milk. Self-checkouts dominate. Apps handle loyalty discounts with genuine, meaningful savings rather than the token gestures you get elsewhere. Cashiers, where they exist, will process your items with brisk, silent efficiency, and attempting warm small talk will generally be met with a polite but faintly baffled response, not from unfriendliness but from a real cultural conviction that you're both there to complete a task, not perform a relationship.

The stores themselves are immaculate, logically laid out, and stocked with a genuinely excellent range for a country this size — Estonia's proximity to Finland and Scandinavia shows in the quality of the dairy and rye bread sections particularly. The pandipakk deposit-return machines for bottles and cans are everywhere and used religiously; recycling isn't a moral stance here so much as a basic assumed baseline of competence, the way locking your front door is elsewhere.

What surprises newcomers most is the sheer absence of friction as a design philosophy extending even to social interaction — nobody hovers, nobody chats, nobody notices what's in your basket, and the entire experience can be completed without saying a single word to another human being, from entry to receipt. For some this reads as cold. Living there, I found it closer to a kind of respect: the assumption that you're a competent adult who doesn't need managing, guiding, or engaging, just left alone to get your shopping done and get on with your day.

## The Verdict

Peru wins if you believe a grocery run should double as a small daily dose of human connection, haggling included, chaos included. Estonia wins if you believe the ideal shopping trip is the one you barely remember happening, frictionless and silent and over in four minutes flat. I've come to genuinely love both, for opposite reasons — Peru because it refuses to let commerce become impersonal, Estonia because it refuses to let commerce become inefficient. If forced to choose a permanent home for my weekly shop, though, my knees are Peruvian and my patience is Estonian, and that tension has never fully resolved.

## Conclusion

Neither country is wrong about what a grocery run should feel like — they've simply optimised for entirely different things. Peru optimises for relationship, texture, and the small daily theatre of being known by name at a market stall. Estonia optimises for time, silence, and the dignity of being left alone to do a mundane task without performance. Bring cash and patience to Lima. Bring an app and zero expectation of conversation to Tallinn. Confuse the two systems and you'll either offend a vendor in Peru by rushing her, or unsettle a cashier in Estonia by trying to befriend her over a bag of rye bread.
