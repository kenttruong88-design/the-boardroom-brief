# One Country's Language Barrier Is Grammar, the Other's Is an Entirely Different Alphabet

🇵🇱 Poland vs 🇯🇵 Japan

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Polish, at least, uses the Latin alphabet, which gave me the false, short-lived confidence that I might sound out a menu correctly on my first attempt. I did not. Seven consonants in a row is not a typo, it's a word, and Polish grammar cases rearrange word endings with a ruthlessness that makes memorising vocabulary feel like the easy part. Japanese offered no such false confidence from the outset — three separate writing systems, one of which contains thousands of characters, meant I couldn't even attempt to sound out a menu, let alone get it wrong.

Poland's barrier is fundamentally auditory and grammatical — you can eventually read the words, you just can't reliably predict how they'll bend. Japan's barrier starts earlier, at the level of basic literacy itself, before grammar even enters the conversation. Both countries, despite this, are far more navigable than the barrier initially suggests, provided you accept a specific kind of humility neither will let you skip.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic polite phrases — effort is genuinely noticed | Assume English works reliably outside major cities |
| Expect younger Poles to speak English more comfortably | Get discouraged by the seven-case grammar system — everyone struggles |
| Use translation apps openly in shops and offices | Assume older generations are comfortable switching to English |
| Practice pronunciation out loud, even badly | Expect consistent English signage outside Warsaw or Kraków |

### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn hiragana and katakana early — it unlocks far more than expected | Assume polite formality means the other person understood you |
| Use translation apps for kanji you can't read | Speak loudly or slowly as if that solves the comprehension gap |
| Carry your address written in Japanese for taxis and deliveries | Expect casual spoken fluency to transfer to reading signage |
| Appreciate that many locals understand more English than they speak | Assume city fluency reflects rural or older-generation fluency |

## Poland: A Grammar System That Punishes Confidence

Polish looks, deceptively, like it should be crackable through sheer persistence — familiar alphabet, some recognisable loanwords, cognates scattered here and there that offer brief, misleading hope. Then the seven grammatical cases arrive, reshaping noun endings depending on their role in a sentence, and any confidence built on recognising individual words collapses the moment those words start actually functioning in a sentence together.

What helped, eventually, wasn't mastering the grammar — I never did, not properly — but accepting that visible effort mattered more than accuracy. Poles I met responded warmly to mangled, grammatically incorrect attempts at Polish in a way that made the humiliation worthwhile, switching to patient correction rather than dismissal. English proficiency skews heavily generational and geographic: younger Poles in Warsaw or Kraków often speak comfortably, while older residents, particularly outside major cities, may have little to none, making a phrasebook or translation app genuinely essential rather than a backup.

Bureaucracy delivered the sharpest test. Official offices, forms, and processes rarely offer English accommodation, and navigating anything administrative without either fluent Polish or a patient, bilingual friend became, for a stretch, a genuine source of stress. I eventually learned that showing up with a translated document already prepared, rather than hoping to communicate live, saved both parties considerable frustration.

## Japan: Literacy Before Fluency

Japan's barrier begins earlier and more fundamentally than Poland's. Hiragana and katakana, the two phonetic syllabaries, are learnable within weeks with genuine effort, and doing so unlocks a surprising amount — menus, basic signage, train information. Kanji, the thousands of logographic characters borrowed and adapted from Chinese, is a different, much longer undertaking entirely, and most newcomers, myself included, never approach real literacy in it within any reasonable expat timeframe.

What complicated things further was Japan's particular relationship between politeness and comprehension. Formal, courteous exchanges continue smoothly even when actual understanding has broken down entirely — nods, polite acknowledgment, careful phrasing can all continue uninterrupted while neither party has actually understood the other, something I discovered only when instructions I thought I'd confirmed turned out to have been completely missed. Directness about confusion, oddly, works better than persistent polite nodding, though it took real trial and error to learn when and how to interject without being rude.

Spoken English comprehension among Japanese people is often considerably higher than spoken English confidence — many understand far more than they're willing to attempt producing, a gap rooted partly in education systems emphasising reading over speaking, and partly in a cultural discomfort with imperfect performance. Translation apps, particularly for reading kanji on packaging or signage, became indispensable in a way they never quite were in Poland, where at least I could attempt to sound words out, however badly.

## The Verdict

Poland's barrier is grammatical and auditory, punishing but ultimately crackable with enough repetition and a thick skin. Japan's barrier begins at literacy itself, requiring a separate, earlier investment before grammar even becomes the primary obstacle.

I found Polish more frustrating day to day and Japanese more humbling structurally. Suki's verdict: Japan wins for sheer intellectual respect — nothing recalibrates your ego like realising you cannot read a cereal box — but Poland wins for eventual payoff, because the moment a Polish grammar case finally clicked felt like defeating a genuinely worthy opponent rather than simply memorising more characters.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Poland — a newcomer described spending three months mispronouncing a street name so badly that taxi drivers stopped recognising it entirely, "even written down."*

> *Reddit r/japanlife — someone recounted nodding politely through an entire conversation only to realise afterward they'd agreed to something completely different than intended.*

> *Internations Warsaw — a longtime expat warned that showing up to any government office "without a pre-translated document is optimism you will regret."*

## Conclusion

Poland and Japan both punish the assumption that a language barrier is a single, uniform wall rather than several distinct ones stacked on top of each other. Poland's grammar rewards persistence eventually. Japan's writing system rewards a completely different, earlier kind of humility. Move to Warsaw and brace for seven grammatical cases that will never fully make sense. Move to Tokyo and learn to read before you learn to speak. Neither barrier disappears quickly. Both, eventually, become part of the appeal.
