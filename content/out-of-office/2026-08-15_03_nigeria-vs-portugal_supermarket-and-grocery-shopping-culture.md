# In Lagos You Negotiate for Your Tomatoes, and in Lisbon You Just Trust the Price Tag Like a Coward

🇳🇬 Nigeria 🇵🇹 Portugal
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Grocery shopping should be one of the least eventful parts of living abroad, and then you move to Lagos, where buying pepper involves theatre, opinion, and occasionally a small crowd offering commentary on your negotiating skills. Or you move to Lisbon, where the entire process is so orderly, so quietly transactional, that you start to suspect the Portuguese have never once had a strong feeling about the price of onions in their lives.

I've stood in Balogun Market being told, with total sincerity, that I was "wounding" the vendor by offering a fair price. I've also stood in a Lisbon Pingo Doce in total silence, scanning my own groceries at a self-checkout while nobody made eye contact with anybody, ever, for any reason. Both are valid ways to buy food. Only one of them made me feel alive.

## Do's & Don'ts: Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Always haggle, even when the price seems reasonable | Accept the first price a trader quotes — it's an opening bid, not a fact |
| Bring smaller notes; change is a constant, low-grade battle | Show up to a busy market expecting a quiet, efficient trip |
| Build a relationship with one or two regular traders | Insult a trader's goods to negotiate — charm works, contempt doesn't |

## Do's & Don'ts: Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Weigh your own fruit and veg and print the sticker before checkout | Skip the weighing step — cashiers will send you straight back |
| Visit the local mercado for fish on the morning it lands | Expect Sunday afternoon opening hours outside big malls |
| Bring your own bags — plastic ones cost extra and are frowned upon | Assume small mercearias take card for tiny purchases |

## Nigeria: Where Buying Tomatoes Is a Negotiation, a Relationship, and Occasionally a Performance

Grocery shopping in Nigeria — real grocery shopping, the kind that happens in Balogun, Mile 12, or any of the sprawling open markets that actually feed the country — is not a transaction, it's a conversation with theatrical stakes. Every price is an opening position. Quoting the first number a trader gives you and simply paying it isn't just financially foolish, it's read as slightly rude, as though you couldn't be bothered to engage. Haggling isn't a chore here, it's a social skill, performed loudly, often with an audience of other shoppers who will absolutely weigh in on whether you're getting a fair deal.

What surprised me, moving from markets that treat commerce as a cold formality, was how relational the whole system is. Get a good trader — one you return to, one who remembers your face and your usual order — and prices soften, quality improves, and you start getting the good stuff set aside before it even hits the table. This isn't corruption, it's loyalty economics, and it works better than any loyalty card scheme a Western supermarket chain has ever devised. Supermarkets do exist — Shoprite, SPAR, and local chains have grown fast in Lagos and Abuja — and they're where you go for imported goods, fixed prices, and air conditioning, but they never fully replaced the markets, because the markets are where the actual culture, and frankly the actual value, lives.

The chaos is real, though, and nobody should pretend otherwise. Mile 12 market at 7am is a sensory assault of shouting, generators, motorbikes, and produce piled in quantities that suggest the concept of "too much" doesn't translate. Traffic to get there can eat two hours each way. But once you're in it, once you've learned to hold your ground on a price without causing offence, there's a satisfaction to Nigerian grocery shopping that no scan-and-bag self-checkout has ever given me, and I include actual retail therapy in that comparison.

## Portugal: Where Everyone Weighs Their Own Fruit and Nobody Says a Word

Portugal's grocery culture runs on the precise opposite philosophy: fixed prices, minimal interaction, and an almost sacred respect for queueing that borders on liturgy. You do not haggle in a Portuguese mercearia. You do not haggle in a Continente. You barely make eye contact, and the checkout exchange, if it happens at all, is a clipped "bom dia" and the beep of a scanner. This isn't coldness, exactly — Portuguese people are warm once you know them — it's just that grocery shopping is not considered a social occasion, and treating it as one marks you instantly as a tourist.

The genuinely maddening bit, and one nobody warns you about, is the fruit-and-veg weighing system: in most Portuguese supermarkets you're expected to weigh your own produce at a self-service station and print a barcode sticker before you ever reach the till, and if you forget, the cashier will not weigh it for you — they'll send you, and the entire queue behind you, back to do it yourself. I have caused a queue of eleven people to sigh in unison over an unweighed bag of nectarines. It was humbling.

Where Portugal genuinely earns its stripes is the mercado municipal — the covered municipal markets found in nearly every town, where fish arrives straight off the boat and gets sold within hours, and where the quality genuinely does rival, or beat, anything I've had elsewhere in Europe. It's quieter than Lagos, more orderly, prices are fixed and clearly displayed, but the freshness is non-negotiable in the best sense — you simply cannot get sardines this good from a supermarket shelf, and every Portuguese grandmother in the building will silently judge you for trying.

## The Verdict

Nigeria wins on energy, relationship, and the sheer sport of the thing — you leave with tomatoes and also, somehow, a small sense of triumph. Portugal wins on efficiency, order, and produce that requires zero negotiation to be excellent. If you like your errands to double as social combat, Lagos will never bore you. If you like your errands to be silent, weighed, and over in eleven minutes, Lisbon will never surprise you either. Choose based on how much conflict you want with your courgettes.

## Conclusion

Fourteen relocations in, I've decided that how a country buys groceries tells you more about its soul than almost anything else — its patience, its formality, its appetite for confrontation. Nigeria will make you a better negotiator whether you want to be one or not. Portugal will make you a more patient queuer and a more competent produce-weigher, which sounds unglamorous until you're the person holding up eleven strangers over unstickered peaches. Learn the local rules fast in either place. The alternative is being the tourist everyone quietly judges, and I've been her enough times to know it's not worth it.
