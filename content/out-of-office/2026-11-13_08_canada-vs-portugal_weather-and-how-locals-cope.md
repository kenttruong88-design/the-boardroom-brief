# Canada Is Cold Outside and Warm Inside. Portugal Is Warm Outside and Cold Inside, and Nobody Will Admit It

🇨🇦 Canada vs 🇵🇹 Portugal

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Everyone knows Canada is cold. It is the first fact about the country and the only one most relocating executives can recite. The mistake they make is to assume that the cold is a hardship, when it is in fact a thoroughly solved problem. Canadians have spent two centuries perfecting the art of being warm in a freezer.

Portugal has no such reputation. It has beaches, custard tarts and a golden-coloured sun in every brochure. What the brochure leaves out is that the sun stays outside, and that January indoors in a Lisbon flat can feel colder than January outdoors in Winnipeg.

**Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy boots rated well below the forecast | Trust a fashion boot in -25C |
| Check for an outdoor car plug when renting | Assume your car will start without one |
| Layer and carry gloves everywhere | Go out with wet hair in January |
| Allow extra stopping distance on snow | Drive as if the road is dry |

**Portugal**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a dehumidifier before winter | Believe the flat will stay dry on its own |
| Heat the room you are in, not the whole flat | Expect central heating in an older home |
| Ask about insulation and windows when renting | Trust the energy certificate as a comfort guide |
| Check the electric circuit capacity | Run three heaters at once |

## Canada

Canada's cold is well documented and thoroughly respected. The Expat Arrivals weather guide notes that the country spans six time zones, with long cold winters and short hot summers, and that most expats live within a few hundred miles of the US border. British Columbia's coast and Newfoundland's east coast are milder but wetter, and the Prairies manage extreme heat and extreme cold in one calendar year.

The best evidence on how newcomers cope is a British Expats forum thread, "How's your 1st winter there?", dating from 2007 but timeless in its details. A poster in Stony Plain, Alberta, who had three winters of wildly different character, reported a brown Christmas one year and snow from October to May another, with lows of -38C, and advised renters to check for an outdoor car plug, which is needed at around -20C. A Winnipeg resident reported wind chill of -55C and described the block heater, the plug-ins in car parks and the heated underground parking that make it survivable. A Calgary newcomer, arriving in January 2007, met a low of about -29C with boots rated for -40C, ski gloves and layers. A Vancouver poster said it was mostly rain.

The principle is infrastructure. Canadians do not endure winter, they engineer it. Houses are sealed, double or triple glazed and heated to a stable indoor temperature. Cars have block heaters. Parking garages are underground and warm. A poster on that thread said she had fewer colds since she moved. Another logged fifty-five hours ploughing a 32-acre property before buying a tractor with a loader, which tells you how the country solves problems: with a bigger machine.

The Gamintraveler article offers a useful benchmark for the other side of the Atlantic. American winter guidance suggests a thermostat of 20 to 21 degrees Celsius, against the World Health Organization's minimum of 18. Canada assumes a baseline of indoor comfort. It is not an extra.

## Portugal

Portugal assumes no such thing. A January 2026 Gamintraveler piece reports Eurostat figures for 2023 showing that 20.8 percent of both Spaniards and Portuguese said they could not keep their home adequately warm, and that 38 percent of Portuguese reported their dwelling was not comfortably warm in winter. The article blames thin walls, tile floors that act as heat sinks, room-by-room heating, and expensive electricity. A damp 18 degrees, it adds, feels colder than a dry 18.

A blogger couple, writing as The Cook and the Writer in January 2024, put numbers to it in Lisbon. January lows sit around nine degrees with highs near fifteen and humidity around eighty percent. An unheated flat stays at about eighteen indoors. Condensation runs down the walls, mould arrives between December and March, and laundry takes two to three days to dry. They heat with two electric radiators and small bathroom heaters, and the household gathers in the room with the big radiator. Their cleverest trick was using a portable air conditioner as a dehumidifier, collecting about two gallons of water a night and drying clothes on a rack in its warm exhaust.

Forum residents confirm the picture. On Expat.com a German-based poster living in Portugal described gas central heating, bedrooms above 18C and living areas above 20C, and recommended one or two large dehumidifiers because poorly ventilated areas get mould. An Iranian expat in Lisbon was surprised that even new flats rely on air conditioners rather than radiators. An American warned that electric heating can be limited by circuit capacity, possibly about 2,000 watts for a whole dwelling. A Portuguese expert noted that mid- and low-range homes often lack central heating and that energy certificates, mandatory since 2013 and graded A+ to F, indicate efficiency but not comfort.

On a British Expats thread, a builder explained that uninsulated outer walls cause condensation and mould, and that external foam and render is the popular fix, quoted at about 30 euros a square metre. One poster priced a pellet burner installation at about 6,000 euros. The thread ended with a resident's conclusion: air conditioning, a wood burner and insulation, all three.

The same Gamintraveler article offers the rule of thumb that explains it all. The World Health Organization sets an 18 degree indoor minimum, and in a damp Portuguese flat 18 degrees feels colder than it reads on the thermostat. A Canadian arriving in Lisbon in January, accustomed to a house heated to 21, will walk into a room that is technically within guidelines and feel betrayed. That betrayal is the real culture shock. Canadians fight the weather. Portuguese residents negotiate with it, and the negotiation takes place under a blanket.

## The Verdict

Canada wins, which will astonish anyone who has only looked at the temperature chart. The outdoor cold is brutal, but the indoor comfort is reliable. Portugal's outdoor weather is delightful, but the indoor experience in older housing is a damp, chilly gamble.

That said, the comparison depends on your tolerance for different kinds of misery. I would rather be cold for ten minutes between a warm car and a warm building than shiver for four months in a flat that feels like a cave. Others will take the Portuguese light over a Canadian January in a heartbeat.

My advice: if you value a warm home, choose Canada. If you value a warm street, choose Portugal, but rent a new-build with double glazing and bring a dehumidifier. In either country, the real weather is the one inside your front door.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *British Expats forum, ladymoose in Stony Plain, Alberta — winters vary wildly, from a brown Christmas to snow from October to May, and when renting you should check for an outdoor car plug, which you need at around -20C.*

> *The Cook and the Writer, a couple in Lisbon — an unheated flat sits at about 18C indoors, with condensation on the walls, mould from December and laundry that takes two or three days to dry.*

> *Expat.com, donn25, an American in Portugal — electric heating can be capped by your circuit capacity, possibly around 2,000 watts for the whole home.*

## Conclusion

Weather is not what happens outside. It is what happens to you once you try to get comfortable. Canada has built its whole society around that insight. Portugal has built its postcards around the opposite one.

Move to either and you will learn quickly. In Canada you learn to dress. In Portugal you learn to insulate.

And in both countries, you will learn to complain about it in the same way, with pride, to anyone who will listen.
