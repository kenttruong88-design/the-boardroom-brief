# One Weekend Ends at the Mosque, the Other Ends at the Esky, and Only One of Them Will Judge You for Sleeping In

🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have spent weekends in both of these countries doing the two most diametrically opposed things a human being can do on a Saturday: being gently but firmly reminded, five times a day, that leisure is secondary to devotion, and standing in a public park in the greater Sydney area watching a grown man in thongs guard an electric barbecue like it owes him money. Both experiences, I want to be clear, were deeply moving.

Pakistan treats the weekend as a negotiation between faith, family and whatever energy is left over after both have taken their cut. Australia treats the weekend as a human right, specifically the right to be horizontal near water with a beer in hand by 11 a.m. Neither country is wrong. Neither country would ever admit the other might also be right. That, dear reader, is the whole game.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Pakistan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build your weekend around Friday prayers — plan flexibility, not just politeness, into that afternoon | Don't schedule anything irreversible on Friday midday and act shocked when it doesn't happen |
| Do hike the Margalla Hills or picnic in one of Islamabad's many parks — the city has more green space than anywhere else in the country | Don't assume Islamabad nightlife will rival Lahore or Karachi — the scene is thin and Western-style venues are rare |
| Do dress conservatively even on your day off — covered shoulders and legs aren't optional out of respect for local norms | Don't expect to drink casually; alcohol is restricted to licensed hotels and permit holders, not corner shops |
| Do say yes to a dinner invitation — hospitality runs deep and a home-cooked meal beats anything a restaurant will offer you | Don't take public transport if you can avoid it in high-security postings; hire a driver instead |

**Australia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Do learn to work a public barbecue — they're free, everywhere, even in national parks with no running water, and nobody will stop you using one | Don't try to reserve one. You can't. Turn up early or sulk |
| Do join a sports club, whether it's AFL, cricket or the local swim squad — it's the fastest way into a social circle that isn't just other expats | Don't plan errands for Sunday; plenty of shops shut by 6pm on weeknights and don't reopen at all on weekends |
| Do take the sun seriously, even in what passes for winter — hat, sunscreen, rash guard, no negotiation | Don't skip sunscreen because "it's not that sunny" — it is, it always is, and you will regret it by Tuesday |
| Do use the weekend to actually leave the city — the Great Ocean Road, the Whitsundays and Kangaroo Island are not exaggerations | Don't assume a "quick drink" means a quick drink. A Saturday "arvo" can swallow your entire afternoon and most of your dignity |

**Pakistan: the weekend that has to ask permission first**

Pakistan's weekend has had an identity crisis for decades, and I mean that literally — the country has flip-flopped between a Friday-Saturday weekend (to accommodate Friday prayers) and the more globally conventional Saturday-Sunday setup more than once, a genuinely novel problem for a nation to have. Whichever configuration is currently official, Friday remains the organising principle. Prayer five times a day shapes the rhythm of the whole week, but it's Friday's congregational prayer that reorders a day around itself; plan around it, not through it.

What's left of the weekend, once devotion and family obligation have taken their share, splits into two very different registers depending on where you are and who you are. For the diplomatic and expat set in Islamabad, that often means a near-constant rotation of private dinner parties — one Talesmag contributor described a social calendar that was practically "non-stop," heavy on expat clubs (French, British, Canadian, Australian) and locals who'd studied abroad. For everyone else, Islamabad itself is, by the admission of its own residents on expat forums, "not a very happening city" — gorgeous parks (Jasmine, Rose Garden, the Japanese Garden, Fatima Jinnah Park), genuinely excellent hiking in the Margalla Hills or an hour out in Murree, decent malls and restaurants, and a nightlife scene so modest it could fit in a hotel bar, because it largely does.

Then there's cricket, which isn't leisure in Pakistan so much as a second, parallel national government. It went from an elite colonial pastime to the single unifying obsession of a country that agrees on very little else, and a Saturday without a match on somewhere is a Saturday that feels structurally incomplete. Add security-driven restrictions for foreign residents — no public transport for many missions, compound living that one Talesmag respondent said "severely limits your social life," conservative dress as a baseline rather than a suggestion — and you get a weekend that rewards patience and hospitality over spontaneity. It is warm. It is also, quite deliberately, contained.

The other great weekend institution, particularly across spring and autumn, is the wedding — not a single event but a multi-day production spanning mehndi, baraat and walima, each with its own dress code, guest list and catering budget that would make a corporate events planner weep. If you're invited to one, that's your weekend, in full, and declining is not meaningfully on the table. Malls have also quietly become the default neutral ground for anyone priced out of a private club or wary of the limited public mixed-gender spaces: Centaurus in Islamabad, Packages Mall in Lahore, Dolmen in Karachi — air-conditioned, family-friendly, and the closest thing the country has to a universally acceptable Saturday outing.

**Australia: the weekend as a load-bearing national identity**

Australia's weekend is not a pause in the culture. It is the culture, on full display, sunscreen and all. The barbecue — "the barbie," never anything else — is the national leisure infrastructure, and I mean infrastructure literally: free, council-maintained electric and gas BBQs sit in parks, beaches, even remote campgrounds with no flush toilets or running water, because apparently priorities are priorities. Supermarkets stock a dedicated "BBQ meats" aisle. Groups queue for a free hotplate, cook for thirty or forty minutes, then clean up after themselves without being asked — an act of civic decency that still mildly scandalises me.

Shops, confusingly for a country this relaxed, close early and often don't open weekends at all, which pushes errands onto weekdays and leaves Saturday and Sunday almost entirely unclaimed by obligation. That vacuum gets filled with sport — rugby, AFL, cricket — and the sort of outdoor pursuit that would exhaust a lesser nation: surfing, hiking, diving on the Great Barrier Reef, a weekend dash to Melbourne or a campervan crawl up the coast. One Sydney-based expat noted that at a rugby match, the line for coffee was longer than the line for beer, which tells you everything about a culture that takes its caffeine as seriously as its football.

Joining a sports club is the fastest social shortcut going, and the outdoor obsession is relentless regardless of season — the sun, famously, does not clock off in winter. It's a leisure culture built entirely around access: to water, to green space, to a hotplate that's always, somehow, free.

No ranking of Australian weekend institutions is complete without the Bunnings snag — a sausage sizzle fundraiser parked outside the hardware store's entrance most Saturdays, run by whichever local school or sports club has booked the slot that week. It is, improbably, treated as a genuine civic landmark, the subject of its own tracking apps and minor scandals when a store dares to run out of onions. Farmers' markets fill the same early-morning slot in the inner cities — fresh produce, a coffee van with a queue longer than sense allows, and the general sense that the day hasn't properly started until you've stood in a car park holding a paper bag of heirloom tomatoes.

**The Verdict**

If you're after structure, hospitality and a society that has thought very hard about where devotion ends and downtime begins, Pakistan's weekend has a quiet integrity to it — once you accept its limits. If you're after a weekend that asks nothing of you except turning up with meat and mates, Australia wins, and it isn't close. A country that puts free, clean, communally maintained barbecues in national parks with no water supply has simply out-leisured the room. Islamabad's residents calling their own capital "not very happening" is the kind of honest self-assessment Sydney would never manage about itself, mostly because it's never needed to. Australia takes it — not because its weekend is more meaningful, but because it's engineered for maximum, frictionless pleasure, and frankly, that's the entire point of a weekend.

**What Nobody Warned You About**

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Islamabad — a diplomatic spouse noted that living on an embassy compound severely limits your social life, and that queer life in the city exists but stays strictly under wraps in public.*

> *Expat.com forum, Islamabad weekends thread — a long-term contributor summed the capital up bluntly: plenty of parks and hiking, but it's "not a very happening city," so weekends reward people who plan their own fun rather than expecting it to find them.*

> *Matador Network, Sydney expat essay — the writer observed that shops routinely close by 6pm and stay shut all weekend, so errands get pushed onto weekdays, leaving Saturday and Sunday free for the barbecue and the beach instead.*

**The Parting Shot**

Here's the thing nobody tells you before you relocate for either: the weekend you get is the weekend that country has decided you deserve, and both of these have strong, unshakeable opinions on the matter. Pakistan will hand you a weekend built around reverence and hand-delivered dinner invitations, bounded by rules you didn't write and can't negotiate. Australia will hand you a free barbecue, a strong sunburn and the quiet expectation that you'll clean up after yourself without being asked twice — which, depending on your upbringing, may be the more confronting demand. Pick your discomfort. Just don't call either one "exotic" for having rules you've simply never had to follow before. That's not culture shock. That's you, underprepared, in someone else's Saturday.
