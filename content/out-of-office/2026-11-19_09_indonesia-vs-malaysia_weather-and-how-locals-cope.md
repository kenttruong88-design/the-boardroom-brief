# Jakarta Floods With Dignity and Malaysia Sweats With Style

🇮🇩 Indonesia · 🇲🇾 Malaysia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

British people believe they invented talking about the weather. They did not. They merely invented the habit of doing it without consequence. Visit the equator in rainy season and you will meet people for whom the weather is not conversation but logistics, planning and, in some postcodes, a matter of whether the ground floor is still there. Indonesia and Malaysia share a climate that sounds identical on a brochure and feels entirely different in the commute.

Indonesia has the sharper drama: a country whose rainy season is an event with an agency, a press release and an official forecast of how early it will arrive. Malaysia has the humidity: a long, even, relentless warmth that you do not so much cope with as negotiate. One country asks you to be a hydrologist. The other asks you to be a thermostat.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇮🇩 Indonesia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Schedule meetings for mid or late morning; storms typically hit late afternoon or evening | Plan an afternoon commute during peak rain and expect it to take 30 minutes |
| Get a flood map and check the area's flood history before renting | Assume floods are citywide; some areas are badly hit and others untouched |
| Use dehumidifiers or moisture absorbers; open windows and curtains after rain | Leave leather and closets unventilated |
| Take dengue seriously when standing water appears | Ignore mosquito risk in the wet season |

**🇲🇾 Malaysia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Choose a high-floor, coastal condo for breezes | Rent a west-facing unit without checking the afternoon sun |
| Visit a property in the afternoon to judge the heat | Judge a flat only by its morning light |
| Let your body adapt; fans can help | Fight the heat by hiding in the air conditioning forever |
| Check the highlands' reputation for flash floods and slope collapses | Assume the hill station is a weather escape without drawbacks |

## 🇮🇩 Indonesia: The Seasonal Spectacle

The rainy season in Indonesia runs from roughly November to March, with the heaviest rain from December to February; the dry season runs April to October. Storms usually hit in the late afternoon or evening and last one to three hours, so the practical advice is to schedule meetings for mid or late morning. In peak downpours a 30-minute drive can stretch to 90 minutes. The country's meteorological agency, BMKG, says the transitional pancaroba periods are increasingly hard to forecast, and that significant rain in the dry season now has a name, "wet drought", which is the most gloriously bureaucratic oxymoron I have ever read.

BMKG's 12 September 2025 press release laid out the 2025/26 rainy season in numbers. The season ran from August 2025 to April 2026 and began earlier than normal: 294 of 699 rainy-season zones, or 42.1 per cent, started early, with Java and Bali onset mostly in October 2025. Peak rain was expected in January and February 2026 on Java and November and December 2025 in Sumatra and Kalimantan, with 193 zones, or 27.6 per cent, expecting above-normal rainfall, including most of West Java. Dengue risk was forecast to rise from December. The climate drivers were an ENSO-neutral state, a negative Indian Ocean Dipole and sea surface temperatures 0.42 degrees above average. Disaster data from BNPB, reported up to 27 January 2026, counted 205 events in the year so far, 127 of them floods.

But the human version of this is more telling. A Jakarta resident on the Expat.com forum says flood images make the city look underwater when only some areas are badly hit, and life went on; the advice is to get a flood map and avoid living in flooded areas. The named trouble spots include Kemang, Grogol, Mangga Dua, PIK and the area outside Central Park and Podomoro City, which can flood to 1.5 metres. Another poster kept commuting and noted his stepson's school occasionally closed when the nearby river was about to burst.

Then there is the humid indoor war: laundry that will not dry, mould in the closets, leather that sulks. A poster advises opening windows and curtains after rain to reduce night humidity and setting the AC to dehumidify mode. One rider claims he crosses Jakarta by motorbike in any weather and has never taken more than 50 minutes to get home, blaming SUV drivers who wait for the rain to stop for the worst traffic. That is a Jakarta answer: the rain is not the problem, the reaction to it is.

## 🇲🇾 Malaysia: Heat as a Way of Life

Malaysia does not have a rainy-season spectacle of Indonesian proportions in the sources I managed to retrieve, so I will be plain that this half rests on one forum thread rather than an official page. What that thread offers is a philosophy. One poster, cvco, says trying to avoid the heat is pointless and the body adapts, and that switching off the air conditioning and using ceiling fans, as locals do, helped them adjust. This is the single most useful sentence I have read about equatorial living, and it is also deeply annoying to anyone who loves a thermostat.

The housing advice is the practical corollary. Choose a high-floor, coastal condo for breezes. Avoid west-facing units. Visit a property in the afternoon to judge the heat, because the afternoon is when the building tells the truth. Prevailing winds were described as coming from the east, which is the sort of detail estate agents do not volunteer. A 2023 reply notes air conditioning as a practical option for people with health needs, which is the sensible footnote to the adaptation sermon.

If lowland Malaysia is too much, the thread lists the highlands at roughly 600 to 800 metres: Genting Highlands, Fraser's Hill and the Cameron Highlands at Tanah Rata and Brinchang. Respondents describe them as mostly tourist destinations with limited services, and add that flash floods and slope collapses happen there too. Other options named were Ranau near Mount Kinabalu and the Borneo Highlands near Kuching. In other words: you can escape the heat, but you cannot escape the climate.

I will not invent a KL haze story. I did not find a source, and a haze anecdote without a source is just rumour in a face mask. What I can say is that the Malaysian approach to weather is cultural: adapt, accept, and arrange your life around the afternoon. It is a more peaceful bargain than the Indonesian one, with fewer flood maps and more fans.

## The Verdict

Malaysia wins the quality-of-life argument, and Indonesia wins the character one. Malaysia offers a steadier relationship with the climate: you learn to live with the heat, pick a flat with the right orientation and get on with your day. The risks are manageable and the rhythm is predictable.

Indonesia demands more. You study the rainy season like a stock market, you check flood maps before signing a lease, you schedule around storms and treat dengue as a practical concern. But Indonesians have built a culture of resilience around it, and the Jakarta resident who says life went on is describing something genuinely admirable. People cope because they have no choice, and then they make it look easy.

My sting: Malaysia has the better weather policy, which is to ignore it, and Indonesia has the better weather literacy, which is to read it. I would live in the first and learn from the second.

## Conclusion

I have been rained on in almost every climate zone that has a name, and the rule is universal: the weather is never the story, the adaptation is. Jakarta adapts with maps, meetings scheduled around storms and a certain cheerful fatalism. Malaysia adapts with fans, orientation and a refusal to be rattled.

Whichever you choose, rent high, check the flood map, and buy a dehumidifier before you buy a sofa. The sofa will be fine. The mould will not.
