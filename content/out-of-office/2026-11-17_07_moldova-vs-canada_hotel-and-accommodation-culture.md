# Moldova Puts You Up Like a Cousin and Canada Puts You Up Like a Customer

🇲🇩 Moldova vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Arrive in Chișinău and the first thing the guest house does is feed you. Nobody asked. A plate appears, a tiny glass of something homemade follows, and a woman who owns the building explains in three languages that you are too thin. You came for a bed. You are leaving with a pot of jam and an obligation.

Arrive in a Canadian hotel and a young person behind a screen asks for your name, your card and your postal code, then hands you a key card and a printed sheet about the pool hours. The room is clean, the heating is generous, the mattress is a minor triumph and nobody has asked about your health. It is perfectly civilised, and it is a transaction with excellent carpets.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Moldova**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the hospitality and offer thanks in kind | Refuse the glass and the plate at the first offer |
| Confirm the address and arrival time by message | Assume a small guest house has a staffed desk all night |
| Ask about heating and hot water before a winter stay | Take the water temperature for granted |
| Carry some local currency for small payments | Expect every small place to take a card |

**Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book early for summer, long weekends and big events | Arrive in a tourist town in July without a reservation |
| Check cancellation terms and any resort or parking fees | Trust the headline rate as the final price |
| Dress for the weather even for a short walk from the car | Underestimate a Canadian winter between the lobby and the taxi |
| Tip service staff where the custom expects it | Skip the tip and explain that you are European |

## Moldova

Moldova is not a hotel country in the sense that Switzerland is a hotel country, and that is part of its charm. Travel guides such as the World Travel Guide's overview of where to stay in the country describe a mix: a handful of larger hotels in Chișinău, smaller boutique places, guest houses and family-run pensions in the wine country and the countryside. Booking sites list a long run of guest houses in the capital, many of them in converted flats or old houses.

The business hotels in the capital are what a corporate visitor will meet first. Some are functional Soviet-era survivors with a refurbished lobby and a reputation for thick walls. Others are modern, glossy and eager, with a breakfast buffet designed to overwhelm a man in a suit. Culture Trip's roundup of cultural hotels in the city points to a newer layer of boutique properties that lean on local design, history and wine. In general, the standard is rising, and the price remains modest by Western European standards.

The distinctive thing is the guest house. In much of rural Moldova, accommodation is an extension of family life. You eat with the hosts, you drink the house wine, and you are expected to talk. A family business such as this is not a polished product; it is a person's home with a spare room and a very strong sense of duty. It is wonderful and occasionally exhausting, since privacy is a negotiable concept and refusing a third helping is a social event.

Practicalities matter. Heating and hot water can be uneven in older buildings, English is not guaranteed outside the main hotels, and a late arrival requires a message in advance. Cards are accepted in bigger places, but cash is useful elsewhere. For a long stay, most expats move into an apartment quickly, since rents are low and the local network of landlords and friends is efficient. The hotel is the beginning, not the plan.

## Canada

Canadian accommodation is a different animal altogether: huge, standardised and competent. The big chains dominate the cities and the highway exits, with the Fairmont-style grand railway hotels as a reminder that the country once built a whole tourism industry around the train. Beyond them sit the motels of the road, the lodges of the Rockies and the Maritimes' inns and bed-and-breakfasts, each with its own character.

The value proposition is comfort. Beds are large, showers are powerful, heating works as if it were a moral duty, and a hotel in January is a bubble of warm dry air in a country that has otherwise given up. Service is polite, efficient and slightly scripted. Staff will help with a problem, offer an upgrade if you ask nicely and call you "sir" or "ma'am" with a half-smile. The tone is friendly rather than personal.

The surprises are the fees and the distances. Many hotels charge for parking, particularly in cities, and resort or destination fees can appear on the bill after the booking. Taxes are added on top of the quoted rate. A traveller used to a Moldovan guest house, where the price is the price, will be startled by the arithmetic. Distance is the other trap: a map makes two towns look close and a Canadian road makes them a day apart.

The deeper difference is social. Canadians value privacy, and a hotel respects it, with no one asking about your family or pressing a second dinner on you. For an expat arriving alone, it can feel quietly lonely. The solution is to move on quickly: the hotel is a launchpad, and the real accommodation, a rental flat or a shared house, is where Canadian life starts.

I should add that the difference is not only about hotels. It is about what a country believes it owes a stranger. In Moldova, a guest is a social obligation, and the host's pride is bound up in whether you were fed, warmed and sent off with something in your bag. In Canada, a guest is a customer with rights, and the host's pride is bound up in whether the room was clean, the check-in was quick and the complaint, if any, was resolved by email within a day. Both models are decent. Both produce stories. But one leaves you with a friendship and the other leaves you with a receipt, and the choice between them depends on whether you arrived looking for a place to sleep or a reason to remember the trip.

## The Verdict

Moldova wins on warmth, and Canada wins on comfort. If I am choosing the place I want to be remembered by, I choose a Moldovan guest house, with its terrible stairs, wonderful dinner and a host who will cry at your goodbye. If I am choosing the place where I want to sleep after a nine-hour flight, I choose Canada, because a firm mattress and a working radiator are not trivial things.

Value is a close call. Moldova gives you a night of human contact and a plate of food for a fraction of the Canadian rate, while Canada gives you reliability, hygiene and rules that are enforced. For a business traveller, the answer is Canada, or one of the better hotels in Chișinău. For anyone who wants a story, it is Moldova, without hesitation.

The honest answer is to use both for what they do best. Stay in the guest house when you want to understand a place. Stay in the chain when you need to be productive on Tuesday.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *World Travel Guide, Moldova — the country's accommodation is presented as a mix of hotels in the capital and smaller guest houses and rural stays, which means the character of your trip depends heavily on where you sleep.*

> *Culture Trip, Chisinau hotels — the city's newer boutique properties are described as drawing on local design and history, a sign that the hotel scene is changing faster than the reputation.*

> *Booked.ai, Chisinau accommodation guide — travellers are pointed toward a broad range of stays at moderate prices, which fits the common expat experience that the city is good value for what it offers.*

## Conclusion

A bed is a country's handshake. Moldova's is warm, long and slightly too firm. Canada's is polite, professional and over in three seconds. Both are real, and both tell you what the country thinks a guest is for.

If you are moving from Moldova to Canada, read the fee schedule, book ahead and enjoy the heating. If you are going the other way, bring cash, patience and a stomach prepared for hospitality. Wherever you sleep, be kind to the person who made the bed. They are the real concierge.
