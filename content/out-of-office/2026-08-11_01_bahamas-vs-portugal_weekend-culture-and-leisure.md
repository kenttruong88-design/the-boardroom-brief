# The Bahamas Has Perfected Doing Nothing. Portugal Has Perfected Doing Nothing While Looking Devastatingly Elegant About It

🇧🇸 Bahamas vs 🇵🇹 Portugal — *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have now spent enough consecutive Saturdays in both nations to report, with total confidence, that neither country has the faintest interest in your five-year plan. The Bahamas will hand you a rum punch and a hammock and consider the matter closed. Portugal will hand you a glass of vinho verde, seat you at a lunch that runs four hours, and quietly judge you for checking your phone.

Both are, in their own ways, aggressively pro-leisure cultures that have simply arrived there by different roads — one paved with sand, the other with seventeenth-century cobblestones and a lingering sense of melancholy nobody's in a hurry to explain. Everyone who tells you "island time" and "Mediterranean pace" are the same thing has clearly never tried to get a Bahamian ferry moving on schedule or a Portuguese uncle to leave a lunch table before the digestivo arrives. They are not the same. They are two entirely separate philosophies of ignoring your calendar.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Bahamas**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to a "beach day" invite expecting it to become a beach afternoon-into-evening | Expect a boat, a bar, or a barbecue to start "on time" |
| Learn what Junkanoo actually is before Boxing Day sneaks up on you | Call it a "carnival" in front of anyone who's marched in one |
| Say yes to church on Sunday if invited — it's a genuine social institution, not just religion | Schedule anything important for a Sunday morning |
| Bring a cooler, contribute something, and never show up empty-handed to a cookout | Assume "we'll figure it out" means anyone actually will |

**Portugal**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clear your entire Sunday for family lunch — it is not optional, it is load-bearing | Try to squeeze in errands between courses |
| Learn to enjoy a long, slow praia day with zero itinerary | Rush anyone toward the bill, ever |
| Respect the August exodus — the whole country relocates to the coast | Book meetings in Lisbon in August and expect anyone senior to attend |
| Join a passeio (a proper unhurried walk) without treating it as exercise | Wear gym kit to what is clearly a social stroll |

## The Bahamas: Leisure as a Non-Negotiable Constitutional Right

The Bahamian weekend does not begin at 5pm on Friday. It begins whenever the mood strikes, which is often significantly earlier, and it does not end so much as dissolve gently into Monday. This is a nation of over seven hundred islands where the entire economy is arranged around the assumption that people deserve to enjoy being alive, and it shows in everything from the shortened Friday office hours that many companies quietly observe to the sheer number of boats parked at sandbars on any given Saturday doing absolutely nothing productive whatsoever, magnificently.

Junkanoo is the one place where this leisure culture puts on its most spectacular face — a pre-dawn, drum-driven, cowbell-shaking street parade on Boxing Day and New Year's that outsiders lazily compare to Rio or Trinidad and are swiftly, sometimes furiously, corrected. It is its own thing, built from goatskin drums and homemade costumes and community groups that rehearse for months, and if you show up thinking it's a tourist show rather than the single most important cultural event on the national calendar, the locals standing next to you will let you know.

Church, meanwhile, remains the other pillar of the Bahamian weekend that visitors consistently underrate. Sunday services are packed, multi-hour, and genuinely central to social life — not a chore squeezed in before brunch but the actual anchor around which extended families reorganise. Skip it as a guest of a Bahamian family and you haven't just missed a service, you've missed the weekend's real event.

Then there's the water, which is less a weekend activity than a state of being. Fish fries, sandbar hangs, and the entire ecosystem of "we're heading out on the boat" invitations that arrive with roughly four hours' notice and zero fixed end time. Punctuality, in this context, is a foreign concept that nobody's bothered importing, and rightly so — what exactly would you be rushing toward? The rum punch isn't going anywhere.

## Portugal: Leisure as a Slow, Deliberate, Faintly Melancholic Art Form

Where the Bahamas treats the weekend as a spontaneous unfolding, Portugal treats it as ritual — scheduled, generational, and almost sacred. Sunday lunch with the family is the load-bearing wall of the entire week. It starts at 1pm and finishes somewhere in the vicinity of never, moving through courses at a pace that would induce a panic attack in a New Yorker and that Portuguese grandmothers consider merely "getting started." Interrupting it for anything short of a genuine emergency is a social transgression roughly on par with insulting someone's mother, because you probably just did.

August is when this leisure ethic reveals its true scale: the entire country, or as close to it as physically possible, decamps to the coast. Lisbon empties. Business slows to a theatrical crawl. Anyone still trying to schedule serious meetings in the capital that month is either new here or a masochist, and either way is going to be disappointed by the out-of-office replies.

The Portuguese passeio — an evening or weekend walk, often along a riverfront or through a praça, taken at a deliberately unhurried pace — is where you see the national temperament most clearly. It is not exercise. It is not a means of getting somewhere. It is the thing itself: a slow lap of the neighbourhood, greeting people, existing in public without urgency. Combine this with the beach culture radiating out from Lisbon and Porto on any warm Saturday, and you get a country that has essentially industrialised the art of sitting still attractively.

There's a national mood underpinning all of it — saudade, that famously untranslatable ache of longing — which somehow coexists with genuine, unforced conviviality. The Portuguese aren't gloomy. They've just built a culture that allows melancholy and leisure to share a table without either one ruining the other's afternoon.

## The Verdict

If you want weekends that feel like freedom, go Bahamian: spontaneous, watery, loud with drums twice a year and rum punch the other fifty. If you want weekends that feel like inheritance, go Portuguese: scheduled, generational, four hours long and somehow better for it.

I'll hand this one to Portugal, reluctantly, because there is something in the ritual of the Sunday lunch — the sheer stubborn insistence that this time is sacred and everyone shows up — that the more improvisational Bahamian weekend, glorious as it is, doesn't quite match. The Bahamas gives you better Saturdays. Portugal gives you a better relationship with your own family, whether you asked for one or not.

## Conclusion

Neither country will apologise for slowing you down, and neither should. The Bahamas will teach you that a schedule is a suggestion and a boat is a lifestyle. Portugal will teach you that a lunch table can hold an entire family's week of unspoken business, and that four hours is, actually, not that long. Try to import either work ethic into a Monday back home and you'll be fired within a fortnight — which is precisely the point. These aren't leisure cultures that fit around productivity. They're leisure cultures that have quietly decided productivity can wait its turn. Book the trip, lower your expectations of punctuality, and for heaven's sake don't call either one "exotic" — they've heard that one before, and they're not impressed.
