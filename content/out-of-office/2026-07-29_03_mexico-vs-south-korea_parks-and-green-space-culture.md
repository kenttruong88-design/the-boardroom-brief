# One Country Turns Its Park Into a Living Room. The Other Turns Its Riverbank Into a Delivery Zone.

🇲🇽 Mexico 🇰🇷 South Korea
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every culture reveals itself in how it uses a patch of grass. Mexico treats its parks like an extension of the family home — loud, crowded, fed constantly, and rarely empty before midnight. South Korea treats its parks, particularly the Han River's endless green ribbon, as a logistics-optimized leisure platform where you can have fried chicken and beer delivered to your exact picnic mat coordinates via app. Both are proof that humans will find a way to socialize outdoors. Only one of them has turned it into a supply chain.

I've picnicked, jogged, and people-watched in green spaces on every inhabited continent, and I can say with confidence: Mexico City's Chapultepec and Seoul's Han River parks are two of the best urban green spaces on Earth, and they could not be more different in spirit if they tried.

## Do's & Don'ts: Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go on Sunday — it's the unofficial national park day, packed and joyful | Expect quiet corners on weekends; Chapultepec on a Sunday is a festival |
| Buy snacks from vendors inside the park — it's half the experience | Leave bags or phones unattended, even briefly |
| Bring the whole extended family — multi-generational outings are the norm | Assume every green space is equally maintained; upkeep varies wildly by neighbourhood |
| Enjoy free outdoor concerts and lucha libre-adjacent street performance | Expect strict quiet zones — parks here are for noise, not silence |

## Do's & Don'ts: South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Order chicken and beer (chikinasik) for delivery straight to your picnic spot | Forget to check the exact GPS pin — delivery riders need precision |
| Rent a bike or in-line skates along the Han River paths | Bring your own alcohol into designated no-drinking zones during clean-up drives |
| Reserve a spot early for cherry blossom season — it gets serious | Assume it's quiet — group exercise classes and boomboxes are common on weekends |
| Use the free public exercise equipment scattered throughout parks | Litter — bins are sparse by design to encourage carrying trash out, and locals do |

## Mexico: The Park as Extended Family Room

Chapultepec Park is, depending on which measurement you trust, larger than New York's Central Park, and on a Sunday it functions less like a park and more like the entire city's collective living room. Multiple generations show up together — grandparents, parents, toddlers, the family dog — and stay for hours, fed by an informal economy of vendors selling elote, tacos, aguas frescas, and roasted nuts from carts that have clearly been doing this circuit for decades.

The noise is the point. Mariachi bands rehearse. Someone always has a speaker. Kids play football using jackets as goalposts. This is not a park designed around Scandinavian minimalism or hushed contemplation — it's designed, culturally if not literally, around gathering as many people as possible and feeding them well. Museums, a castle, a zoo, and a lake all sit inside Chapultepec's boundaries, turning what elsewhere would be a single green rectangle into a full day's itinerary.

The caveat, and it's a real one, is vigilance. Petty theft in crowded sections is a known risk, and locals move through the park with a practiced awareness — bags across the body, phones tucked away, valuables minimal — that visitors should absolutely copy rather than romanticize away. Maintenance also varies: the manicured central sections gleam, while more remote corners can feel neglected. It's not a flaw so much as an honest reflection of a city with uneven resources, doing its best to democratize green space anyway.

## South Korea: The Park as Optimized Leisure Infrastructure

The Han River parks — an unbroken stretch of green running through the heart of Seoul — represent something closer to civic engineering than casual landscaping. Bike paths are wide, clearly marked, and genuinely used for commuting as well as leisure. Free public exercise equipment appears at intervals like punctuation. Convenience stores embedded directly into the park sell essentials, and on a warm evening the riverside is dotted with hundreds of blue picnic mats, each one a small self-contained party.

The single most Korean detail of this entire culture: you can order fried chicken, pizza, or an entire barbecue spread through a delivery app, drop a GPS pin on your exact patch of grass, and have a rider deliver it by scooter within the hour. This isn't a novelty story tourists repeat because it's quaint — it is standard weekend behaviour for tens of thousands of Seoulites every single weekend, rain or shine.

Cherry blossom season turns the whole system up several notches, with crowd management, temporary vendor stalls, and lighting installations rolled out with the precision of an event company rather than a parks department. Clean-up culture is strict without being joyless: bins are intentionally sparse to nudge people toward carrying their own rubbish out, and largely, remarkably, they do. It's a park culture that has been optimized the way everything in Korea seems to be optimized — for convenience, for crowd flow, for maximum enjoyment per square metre.

## The Verdict

Mexico wins on soul — Chapultepec is loud, generous, and unmistakably alive in a way that engineered efficiency can't quite replicate. South Korea wins on execution — the Han River parks are a masterclass in making outdoor leisure frictionless, right down to the fried chicken arriving before you've finished laying out the mat. If you want a park that feels like a family reunion, Mexico City has one waiting every Sunday. If you want a park that feels like it was built by people who have genuinely thought hard about human happiness logistics, Seoul's riverbank is unmatched. I'd take a blanket to both. I'd only trust my phone to stay in my pocket in one of them.

## Conclusion

Both cities have figured out something many Western urban planners still haven't: green space is only valuable if people actually use it, loudly and often. Mexico does this through generational tradition and unapologetic noise. Korea does it through app-based convenience and quiet efficiency, chicken included. Neither approach is "correct." But if I had to guess which park culture survives the apocalypse, my money's on the one that can still get delivery.
