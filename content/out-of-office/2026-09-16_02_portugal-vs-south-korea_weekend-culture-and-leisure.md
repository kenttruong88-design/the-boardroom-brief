# One Country Naps Through the Weekend and the Other Conquers a Mountain Before Brunch

🇵🇹 Portugal vs South Korea 🇰🇷
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ask a Portuguese person what they're doing this weekend and you'll get a shrug, a mention of an aunt's house, and possibly three hours of unplanned silence on a beach. Ask a Korean the same question and you'll get a full itinerary complete with a 6 a.m. departure time, matching hiking gear, and a group chat that's been finalising logistics since Tuesday. I have lived by both rhythms. One nearly gave me a hip replacement's worth of stair-climbing at altitude. The other gave me a truly world-class ability to do absolutely nothing for six consecutive hours without a flicker of guilt.

Neither country will accept that the other's approach constitutes an actual weekend. The Portuguese consider the Korean model a kind of leisure-shaped punishment. The Koreans consider the Portuguese model faintly wasteful, an unused Saturday being roughly equivalent to money left on the table. Both are completely correct, and both would be horrified to live the other's version for more than a fortnight.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇵🇹 Portugal**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clear your Sunday afternoon entirely for the extended family lunch | Schedule anything before 11 a.m. on a Saturday — nobody will come |
| Learn to enjoy silence at the table without filling it | Expect shops to be open Sunday afternoon in smaller towns |
| Accept every invitation to "the village" at face value | Rush a coffee — it is a twenty-minute event, minimum |

**🇰🇷 South Korea**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy proper hiking gear — the full North Face uniform, no exceptions | Show up to a group hike in trainers, you will be quietly judged all day |
| Say yes to the after-hike meal, it's non-negotiable | Assume "let's relax this weekend" means staying home |
| Book cafés and brunch spots in advance — queuing culture is fierce | Underestimate how early "early morning" actually means |

## Portugal: The Art of the Sacred Do-Nothing

The Portuguese weekend operates on a principle so foreign to hustle culture it should be trademarked: leisure requires no output. There's a word, saudade, that gets trotted out for everything wistful and melancholic, but the real cultural export is something closer to unbothered — a genuine, structural refusal to let the weekend become another task to optimise. Sunday lunch with the extended family can run four hours, deep into a second bottle of vinho verde, with no agenda beyond being present and occasionally arguing about football.

Shops close. Actually close, not "reduced hours" close — in smaller towns outside Lisbon and Porto, Sunday afternoon can render an entire high street a ghost town, and heaven help the newly arrived expat who needs a hardware store item on a Sunday at 3 p.m. The beach, when the weather allows, is less an activity than a state of being — towels down by 10, a slow lunch at the same kiosk you've gone to for years, home before the evening chill, no itinerary beyond vibes.

What surprises outsiders is how unhurried this all is even among younger, urban, career-driven Portuguese. Lisbon has a startup scene, a genuinely booming one, and yet the cultural gravity of "the weekend is for rest, not conquest" holds even there. Ask a Lisbon tech founder what they did last weekend and there's a real chance the honest answer is "not much, and it was wonderful," delivered without a hint of the anxious self-justification that answer would require almost anywhere else.

## South Korea: The Weekend as a Performance Sport

If Portugal's weekend is a nap, South Korea's is a triathlon with a group chat. Hiking isn't a hobby here, it's closer to a national civic duty — over 70% of South Koreans report hiking regularly, and mountains within an hour of Seoul (Bukhansan chief among them) fill with matching-outfit hiking clubs from dawn, decked out in gear so specialised and colour-coordinated it looks less like exercise and more like a uniform for a religion you didn't know you'd joined.

The social machinery around this is intense and, frankly, exhausting to a newcomer. Group hikes are organised with military precision days in advance, complete with a fixed post-hike meal (usually pajeon and makgeolli, and skipping it is a genuine social faux pas), and the whole affair functions as much as networking and bonding as it does cardiovascular exercise. Corporate hiking trips with your actual coworkers are common enough that "hiking with the boss on Saturday" is a real and slightly dreaded sentence in Korean office life.

Beyond the mountains, Korean leisure runs on an intensity that would floor a Lisboeta — themed café-hopping (each one more architecturally elaborate than the last), marathon karaoke sessions, saunas (jjimjilbang) as genuine all-day family outings, and a brunch culture so competitive that popular Seoul cafés will have queues out the door by 9 a.m. on a Saturday. Doing "nothing" on a Korean weekend carries a faint whiff of failure that took me embarrassingly long to shake.

## The Verdict

If your idea of restoration is genuinely switching off, Portugal will feel like a spa treatment for your nervous system, and you'll wonder why you ever thought rest needed scheduling. If your idea of restoration is achieving something scenic and Instagrammable by 9 a.m., Korea will feel thrillingly purposeful, right up until the fourth consecutive weekend of 5:45 a.m. alarms breaks something in you. I know which one my joints prefer. I know which one produces better photos. These are not the same answer, and that's the whole point.

## Conclusion

Somewhere between these two lies the theoretically perfect weekend, and neither country has any interest in finding it. The Portuguese would rather die than rush a lunch. The Koreans would rather die than waste a Saturday indoors. Spend enough time in either and you'll absorb the local religion completely — I now nap unapologetically for two hours on Sundays and also own, inexplicably, a very good pair of hiking boots I refuse to explain. Choose your weekend culture like you'd choose a spouse: for life, and with full awareness of what you're committing to.
