# Rio Will Sweat You to Death. Lima Will Just Let You Rot in the Fog.

🇧🇷 Brazil 🇵🇪 Peru

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Everyone arrives in Brazil expecting a postcard and leaves four months later with a permanent film of sweat under their collar and a grudging respect for the stamina of a nation that treats forty-two degrees as a minor inconvenience best solved by standing closer to the ocean. Nobody arrives in Peru expecting anything, which is the correct posture, because Lima will greet you with a sky the colour of wet concrete for nine months running and then act baffled when you ask where the sun went.

I have lived through both. I have sat in a São Paulo office where the air conditioning was so aggressive that colleagues wore cardigans in January, and I have stood on a Lima rooftop in July squinting at a white disc behind the clouds that may or may not have been the sun. Both countries will tell you their weather is "no big deal." Both countries are lying, in opposite directions, and both expect you to simply absorb the lie and carry on.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a proper fan before you buy furniture | Assume your rental has functioning AC just because there's a unit on the wall |
| Follow the beach exodus on scorching weekends — everyone who can leave, does | Schedule outdoor client meetings between noon and 3pm in Rio or Salvador |
| Carry a light jacket for over-chilled offices and buses | Complain about the heat to someone who grew up in Manaus — you will lose |
| Check Alerta Rio or COR heat-index alerts during summer, not just the temperature | Leave windows open while the AC runs, unless you enjoy being silently judged |

### Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pack layers for Lima year-round — "warm" and "cold" are both relative lies | Expect central heating in a Lima apartment; it largely doesn't exist |
| Build a 36–48 hour buffer into any Lima-to-Cusco business trip for altitude | Schedule back-to-back meetings the day you land in Cusco or Puno |
| Drink the coca tea when it's offered at altitude — just do it | Drink heavily the night you arrive above 3,000 metres |
| Treat a sunny Lima day in summer as the small miracle it is | Assume the garúa fog means rain is coming — it almost never does |

## Brazil

Brazil's weather problem is really a geography-of-excess problem. This is a country that spans five distinct climate zones, which means a Manauara sweating through year-round Amazonian humidity and a gaúcho in Porto Alegre scraping frost off a windshield in July are both, technically, having a normal Tuesday in Brazil. Rio averages a balmy 26°C annually and climbs past 30°C most summer afternoons, while São Paulo and Brasília sit on a plateau that keeps things to a civilised 19–21°C. Nobody tells you this before you move, so you pack for "Brazil" as a single climate and arrive either wildly overdressed or dangerously underdressed depending on which half of this continental slab you land in.

Then there's the heat itself, which in recent summers has stopped being charming. In March 2024, a weather station in Rio's Guaratiba district logged a heat index of 62.3°C — the highest reading since the city's Alerta Rio system began recording them in 2014 — while the actual air temperature hit 42°C. Scientist Carlos Nobre has pointed out that heatwaves, not the floods or landslides that make better news footage, are now the deadliest extreme-weather event in Brazil. Cariocas responded the only way Cariocas know how: they went to the beach in record numbers, packed Ipanema and Copacabana, and queued for the public pools in São Paulo. This is, genuinely, the national coping strategy. If it's unbearable, go swim in it.

Indoors, Brazil runs an entirely different weather system: perpetual, aggressive, slightly unhinged air conditioning. Brazilians are, by their own admission, addicted to it — one Rio expat blogger counted roughly seventy separate AC units dripping condensation onto a single street outside one Centro office block. The national electricity grid loses a staggering amount of power to theft and ageing infrastructure every year, and yet the collective response to heat is to run every unit in the building at once, often with the front door propped open, because apparently nobody has explained the concept of a closed system to the person holding the thermostat remote. The result is an office culture where you need sunscreen for your commute and a cardigan for your desk, sometimes on the same day, in the same building.

## Peru

Peru's defining weather trick is that it has almost none, visually speaking, for most of the year — and then, in two completely different ways depending on altitude, it tries to kill you anyway. Lima, home to roughly a third of the country's population, is built on a coastal desert cooled by the Humboldt Current, which sounds romantic until you realise what it actually produces: a near-permanent blanket of grey cloud called garúa that smothers the city from about May to November. It does not rain. Genuinely — ask any Limeño and they will tell you, with something between pride and despair, that it essentially never rains in Lima. What falls instead is a fine, sunless mist that leaves a film of soot and damp on everything, which is less "exotic coastal mystique" and more "someone left the humidifier on in a basement with no windows." Expats routinely describe the toll this takes on mood, with comparisons to Seattle coming up often enough that it's practically a cliché of its own — except Seattle gets actual rain to justify the gloom, and Lima just offers the gloom on its own, unearned.

Summer, mercifully, flips the script between December and April: clear skies, 20s°C, and a dash for the coast every weekend that mirrors Brazil's beach exodus almost exactly. But the real plot twist is altitude. Fly ninety minutes from sea-level Lima to Cusco and you've gained 3,400 metres, well past the roughly 2,500-metre threshold where altitude sickness, or soroche, typically sets in. Corporate travellers doing a quick Lima-Cusco turnaround for a site visit or a client meeting routinely get flattened by headaches, breathlessness, and insomnia on arrival, fitness levels be damned — the mountain does not care how many marathons you've run. The accepted local remedy is coca tea, served without ceremony in every highland café, alongside the universal advice to do nothing strenuous for your first two days. It's the one piece of Peruvian weather wisdom that actually works, which feels almost suspicious given how little else about the climate here behaves predictably.

## The Verdict

If you want drama, Brazil wins outright — a country that will hand you a heat index north of 60°C and then act surprised when the beaches fill up like a fire drill. If you want a slow, grinding psychological erosion delivered via sky colour alone, Peru's coastal gloom will quietly disassemble your serotonin over a Lima winter while everyone around you insists this is normal and, worse, fine.

Neither country coped with its weather so much as built an entire personality around ignoring it properly. Brazil's answer to catastrophic heat is a national beach day and an AC unit dripping onto the street outside your office; Peru's answer to four straight months without a sunrise is coca tea, a sweater, and collective denial that any of it is affecting anyone. Both strategies work exactly as well as you'd expect, which is to say: not especially, but with tremendous confidence.

My verdict, for what it's worth, goes to Peru — not because the fog is pleasant, but because at least Lima's misery is consistent. Brazil's heat will blindside you with a 42°C Tuesday after a run of perfectly reasonable weeks; Lima's gloom simply tells you, honestly, from day one, exactly how bad things are going to be. There's something almost refreshing about a country that doesn't bother pretending.

## Conclusion

Here is the part nobody puts in the relocation packet: weather in Brazil and Peru isn't background noise, it's a management issue. It dictates when meetings happen, how offices are built, what your commute does to your blood pressure, and whether your new colleagues think you're being dramatic when you mention the altitude or the heat index. Brazil will physically assault you with temperature and then expect gratitude for the beach. Peru will quietly starve you of sunlight for most of the year and then hand you a cup of coca tea as if that settles the matter entirely.

Neither culture is coping, exactly. They're enduring, with great style and zero acknowledgment that endurance is what's happening. Pack accordingly, pack layers, and stop expecting either country's weather to make sense — it was never going to, and nobody there is losing sleep over your confusion.
