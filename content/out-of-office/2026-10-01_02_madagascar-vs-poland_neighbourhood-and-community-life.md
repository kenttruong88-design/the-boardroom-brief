# One Country Will Know You're Pregnant Before You Do. The Other Won't Learn Your Name for a Decade.

🇲🇬🇵🇱 Madagascar & Poland
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular kind of culture shock that has nothing to do with language and everything to do with how much of your business your neighbours consider their own. In Madagascar, the fokonolona — the traditional community council — means your arrival, departure, marital status, and the suspicious number of parcels you've received this week are all matters of legitimate public interest. In Poland, you can live three years in the same apartment block and still receive only a curt nod in the lift, delivered with the warmth of a parking ticket.

I've lived both versions. One made me feel surveilled. The other made me feel invisible. Neither is wrong, exactly — they're just two entirely different theories of what a neighbourhood is actually for.

## Do's & Don'ts: Madagascar

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Introduce yourself to the fokonolona head when you move in — they will find out anyway, so save everyone the awkwardness | Don't skip community labour days (fokonolona work parties) without a very good excuse — absence is noted and remembered |
| Accept the inevitability that your comings and goings are known | Don't assume privacy works the way it does at home — it simply doesn't exist in the same shape here |
| Greet people by name, even people you've met only once | Don't rush past a greeting — stopping to talk is the baseline, not a bonus |
| Bring a small gift when you're formally welcomed into a neighbourhood | Don't treat community disputes as none of your business — in a fokonolona system, they often are |

## Do's & Don'ts: Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say "dzień dobry" when entering a shared lift or stairwell — it's expected, even if nothing follows | Don't expect your neighbours to introduce themselves first; that's apparently your job, forever |
| Respect the "cisza nocna" (night quiet hours) — loud noise after 10pm is taken seriously, sometimes with a visit | Don't knock on a neighbour's door uninvited unless something is actually on fire |
| Join the spółdzielnia (housing co-op) meetings if you want any say in building matters | Don't assume friendliness equals friendship — Poles separate the two cleanly, and conflating them is a classic foreigner mistake |
| Keep shared stairwells and bins tidy — communal order is taken more seriously than communal chat | Don't take the silence personally. It isn't about you specifically; it's about everyone |

## Madagascar: The Village That Never Quite Became a City

Even in Antananarivo, a city of nearly three million people, the fokonolona system persists in diluted but real form — a neighbourhood-level structure, descended from rural village councils, where community labour, dispute resolution, and basic social order are handled collectively rather than farmed out entirely to the state. Move into a Malagasy neighbourhood and you are not merely renting a room; you are joining an institution with opinions about your conduct.

This produces a specific, intense form of communal life. Your neighbours will know when you're expecting a visitor, when you've had an argument loud enough to carry through the walls, and roughly how much you paid for your taxi. This isn't malicious gossip so much as a functioning early-warning system — in a country where formal institutions can be slow or absent, the community itself fills the gap, which means everyone has a vested interest in knowing everyone else's situation. If your roof needs fixing and you can't afford it, word travels, and help often arrives before you've finished asking for it.

The cost of this is obvious and the benefit is less visible until you actually need it. I found the lack of privacy suffocating for the first six months and genuinely moving by month nine, when half the street turned up, unasked, to help after a burst pipe flooded my kitchen. You don't get that kind of unsolicited mutual aid in a system built around the sanctity of minding your own business. You also don't get to have a bad day in private, which is a real and non-trivial cost that travel writers conveniently leave out when they wax lyrical about "community spirit."

## Poland: Politeness as a Perimeter Fence

Polish neighbourhood life runs on a principle I've come to respect enormously: the distinction between civility and intimacy is sacred, and collapsing the two is considered slightly gauche. You will be greeted. You will be expected to greet back. Shared spaces — stairwells, courtyards, the little patch of grass outside the block — are kept orderly through genuine collective effort, often coordinated by a spółdzielnia or building administrator with the quiet authority of a minor civil servant. And then, having done all of that, your neighbour will go inside and you will likely never learn their surname.

This is not coldness. Polish social life is warm, occasionally fiercely so, but it's rationed carefully and deployed toward people who've earned it — family, old friends, colleagues you've shared a genuine ordeal with. The apartment block is treated as shared infrastructure, like a utility, not as a social club. You maintain it together because it's the decent thing to do, not because doing so is supposed to produce friendship.

What catches expats out, repeatedly, is mistaking the formal politeness for an opening. A nod in the lift is not an invitation to chat. A borrowed cup of sugar does not imply you're now close. The night-quiet rules (cisza nocna) are enforced with a seriousness that surprises people from louder cultures, and that enforcement is itself a form of neighbourliness: respecting everyone's right to be left entirely, blessedly alone.

## The Verdict

Madagascar gives you a safety net woven so tightly it occasionally feels like a straitjacket. Poland gives you a clean, well-run building and absolutely no one checking on you if you go quiet for a fortnight. I know which one I'd want if my roof caved in, and I know which one I'd want if I just wanted to come home, shut the door, and not perform sociability for anyone.

If I'm forced to pick a winner — and I am, this is my job — I'll give it to Madagascar, reluctantly, because a community that actually shows up beats one that merely keeps the stairwell clean. But I'd want Poland's locks on Madagascar's doors. No system is complete, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you a relocation package.

## Conclusion

What both countries have figured out, in opposite directions, is that a neighbourhood is a negotiated agreement about how much of yourself you owe the people living closest to you. Madagascar's answer is: quite a lot, and in return you get a village that will carry your furniture up four flights of stairs without being asked twice. Poland's answer is: very little beyond basic courtesy, and in return you get to exist in peace behind your own front door. Choose based on how you feel about strangers knowing your business — because one of these countries will absolutely know it, and the other will go to admirable lengths to pretend it doesn't care.
