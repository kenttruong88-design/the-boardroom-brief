# The King Gets a Festival, the Ocean Gets a Republic, Neither Apologises for the Scale

🇹🇴 Tonga and 🇰🇮 Kiribati
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Tonga is the last Pacific kingdom still standing, and it throws a festival every year that treats the monarchy less like a ceremonial relic and more like the main character of an ongoing, thoroughly enjoyable national production. Kiribati, scattered across an ocean expanse so vast it spans all four hemispheres simultaneously — a genuinely staggering geographic fact most people can't locate on a map, let alone appreciate — celebrates its independence with a stubbornness that says quite clearly: we may be disappearing under rising seas while the rest of the world debates emissions targets, but we are not skipping the party over it.

Both nations get filed under "remote Pacific paradise" by outlets that have never bothered distinguishing a kingdom with a functioning royal family from a republic of scattered atolls fighting an entirely different, far more existential calendar. Let's actually separate them.

## Do's & Don'ts: Tonga

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend Heilala Festival in July if you're anywhere near Nuku'alofa — it's the cultural event of the year | Treat the festival's beauty pageant element as frivolous; it carries real social weight |
| Dress modestly and respectfully at any event involving the royal family | Wear beachwear anywhere near a royal or church-affiliated public event |
| Expect Sunday to be genuinely, legally quiet — most commerce simply stops | Plan any Sunday activity assuming shops, transport, or entertainment will be open |
| Join the tau'olunga dance performances as a respectful spectator | Applaud mid-performance the way you might at a Western concert; wait for the appropriate pause |

## Do's & Don'ts: Kiribati

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend Independence Day celebrations on July 12th — it's the unmistakable highlight of the year | Assume Kiribati's remoteness means muted celebration; it's the opposite |
| Take part in te buki or communal dance performances if invited | Photograph performances without asking; some routines carry ancestral significance |
| Respect that climate change discussion is a lived reality here, not an abstraction | Bring up climate change casually as a talking point without acknowledging its weight locally |
| Pace yourself for multi-day feasting — botaki gatherings run long and food is central | Decline food at a botaki; it's considered a genuine social misstep |

## Tonga: Heilala and the Weight of the Crown

Tonga's Heilala Festival, held each July around the king's birthday, is the closest thing the South Pacific has to a full-scale national theatre production, and it is not remotely the muted, dignified royal event a Western observer might expect from a monarchy this size. It runs for roughly two weeks and includes parades, traditional dance competitions, brass bands (a genuinely beloved Tongan institution thanks to 19th-century missionary influence that, against all odds, took root and flourished), and the Miss Heilala pageant, which functions as far more than a beauty contest — it's a serious platform for scholarship funding, community representation, and no small amount of local pride and rivalry between island groups.

What outsiders consistently misunderstand is how central the monarchy remains to Tongan identity, in a region where most Pacific nations shed their kingships generations ago. The king's presence at Heilala isn't ceremonial box-ticking; it's a living reaffirmation of a system Tongans have, by and large, chosen to keep, and treating the royal family with anything less than proper deference at these events is a fast way to be quietly but firmly corrected by everyone around you.

Sunday closure laws in Tonga are not a quaint suggestion, they are close to absolute — a legacy of the country's deep Methodist and Free Wesleyan Church influence, and one of the few places left on earth where an entire nation still observes the Sabbath as genuine, near-total commercial silence. Visitors who plan a Sunday beach day assuming they'll find lunch somewhere are in for a hungry surprise. What's remarkable is how little resentment there is about this among locals; the quiet is treated as a communal gift rather than a restriction, which says something about a country that otherwise throws two weeks of uninhibited celebration every July without a hint of contradiction.

## Kiribati: Independence Day on Borrowed Time, Celebrated Anyway

Kiribati's Independence Day, marking its 1979 break from British colonial rule, falls on July 12th and turns Tarawa and the outer islands into a multi-day celebration of dance, feasting, and sport that punches enormously above the weight of a nation with barely 120,000 people spread across an ocean area larger than India. The te buki dance performances, often depicting frigate birds and ocean life through precise, almost hypnotic hand and body movement, are genuinely one of the more mesmerising cultural performances in the Pacific, and treating them as mere photo backdrops rather than serious cultural transmission is a common, avoidable insult.

What makes Kiribati's festival culture distinct — and honestly rather moving, in a way I don't say lightly — is the backdrop against which it all happens. This is a nation whose very existence is threatened by rising sea levels within the century, a fact its own government has openly and repeatedly stated on the international stage while also purchasing land in Fiji as a contingency. And yet the independence celebrations aren't muted by this looming reality; if anything, they're louder for it, a deliberate, communal insistence on national identity and joy that refuses to be pre-emptively mourned by outsiders who'd rather treat Kiribati as a tragic case study than a functioning, celebrating nation.

Botaki, the traditional communal feast, anchors much of Kiribati's public holiday culture, and these gatherings run long, with food — reef fish, breadfruit, coconut in every conceivable form — treated as a central act of hospitality rather than a side detail. Declining food at a botaki is a genuine social misstep, on par with refusing a handshake elsewhere; the gesture is understood as an offer of belonging, and turning it down reads as a rejection of the community itself.

## The Verdict

Tonga wins on sheer institutional grandeur — a genuine kingdom throwing a two-week festival built around a living monarch is a spectacle no other Pacific nation can currently match. Kiribati wins on something harder to quantify but more affecting: a nation celebrating its independence with full, unmuted joy while facing an existential threat most countries would use as an excuse to cancel the party altogether.

If you want pageantry, go to Heilala. If you want to understand what genuine defiance in the face of an uncertain future actually looks like, go to Tarawa on July 12th. Either way, don't bring your climate guilt as a conversation topic uninvited — nobody there asked for your commentary on their survival.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a former Nuku'alofa-based teacher paraphrased: I assumed the Sunday closures were exaggerated for tourists, then spent an entire Sunday afternoon hungry because I hadn't planned properly, and nobody felt sorry for me.*

> *expat.com forum — a Tarawa-based aid worker noted that declining food at a botaki gathering got a visibly hurt reaction from the host, and it took a genuine apology and a second helping to smooth it over.*

> *Internations Pacific forum — a longtime regional traveller paraphrased: the Miss Heilala pageant is treated with the seriousness of a national election in Tonga, don't make the mistake of calling it "just a beauty contest" within earshot of anyone local.*

## Conclusion

Tonga and Kiribati both know something the rest of the world keeps forgetting: a festival isn't diminished by hardship, it's often the clearest response to it. One throws two weeks of royal pageantry with a straight face and total conviction; the other celebrates independence on land that may not exist for its grandchildren, and does so anyway, loudly, with food nobody's allowed to refuse. Neither needs your pity. Both would very much like you to eat something and stop taking photos mid-dance.
