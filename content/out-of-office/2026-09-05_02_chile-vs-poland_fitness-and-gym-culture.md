# One Country Lifts in Silence, the Other Lifts With a View

🇨🇱 Chile × 🇵🇱 Poland
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have done outdoor calisthenics in a Santiago park with the Andes doing their best impression of a postcard directly behind me, and I have trained in a Warsaw gym so grimly serious that the only decoration was a single peeling poster of a bodybuilder who looked personally offended by my presence. Both places got me fitter. Only one of them made me feel like I was being silently judged by a man named Grzegorz who has never once smiled at anyone, including his own reflection.

Fitness culture reveals a country's relationship with itself more honestly than almost anything else — how it treats effort, how it treats bodies, how much noise it thinks self-improvement is allowed to make. Chile treats a workout as a communal event with scenery. Poland treats it as an unspoken contract you signed the moment you walked in the door, and you'd better honour it.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇨🇱 Chile**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the free outdoor "máquinas" in public parks | Assume outdoor gyms are only for the elderly — everyone uses them |
| Show up to a Zumba or aerobics class ready to talk | Expect a quiet, heads-down session — it's social by default |
| Train early to beat the heat in summer months | Wear all black to an outdoor session in January |
| Compliment someone's form if asked — it's welcomed | Give unsolicited advice; that crosses a line fast |

**🇵🇱 Poland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Re-rack your weights immediately, without exception | Leave a barbell loaded "for a second" — this is a cardinal sin |
| Bring your own towel and use it without being asked | Make eye contact and expect conversation mid-set |
| Respect the unofficial "regulars" hierarchy near the squat rack | Hog the bench for a selfie between sets |
| Wear proper training shoes | Show up in the trainers you wore on the metro |

## Chile: Fitness as a Public, Slightly Chaotic Festival

Santiago's answer to the gym membership crisis was, decades ago, to bolt free steel exercise equipment into nearly every public park in the country, and the result is a nation that treats outdoor training as default rather than novelty. On any given evening you'll find teenagers, grandmothers, and a man in full business attire mid-pull-up, all sharing the same rusted-but-functional pull-up bar with a patience that would be unthinkable in a country that actually paid for its gym membership. Nobody owns the equipment. Everyone uses it like they do.

What strikes you fastest is the noise. Chilean group fitness — Zumba classes in plazas, aerobics sessions blasting cumbia from a portable speaker balanced on a park bench — isn't background exercise, it's a full sensory event, and the instructor is as much a local celebrity as a trainer. I've seen entire families turn up not to participate but to watch, cheer, and heckle affectionately, the way you'd watch a football match rather than a fitness class. This is fitness as community theatre, and frankly it's more honest about what most people actually want from exercise: to feel part of something, not to suffer alone.

The private gym scene, where it exists, borrows this same warmth — trainers greet you like family within a week, correction of your form comes wrapped in a joke rather than a command, and rest days are openly discussed as a valid life choice rather than a moral failing. What Chile lacks is rigid infrastructure — machines break and stay broken longer than you'd like, class schedules shift without much warning — but what it has instead is an atmosphere where showing up half-heartedly, sweaty and unbothered, is completely normal. Nobody there is optimising anything. They're just moving, together, loudly, in the sun.

## Poland: The Gym as a Silent Vow of Discipline

Walk into a Polish gym, particularly one of the serious strength-focused ones outside the big glossy chains, and you will feel it immediately: this is not a social space. It is a workshop. Conversation is minimal, eye contact is fleeting, and the unspoken code of conduct is stricter than most employment contracts. Leave a plate on the bar after your set and you will be met not with anger but with something worse — a flicker of quiet disappointment, as though you've let down not just the gym but Poland itself.

This isn't coldness so much as an entirely different philosophy: the gym is a place of individual discipline, not communal celebration, and the respect shown is procedural rather than verbal. You re-rack because that's the deal. You don't hover near someone's set because that's rude. You wipe down the bench because the next person deserves the same clean start you got. It's a kind of etiquette so rigorous it borders on ritual, and once you understand it, it's oddly restful — nobody's going to make you small talk about your programme while you're trying to hit a deadlift PR.

Polish strength sport culture runs deep and serious — powerlifting and strongman have genuine national pride attached, and it shows in the sheer number of unglamorous, function-over-form gyms where the chalk bucket has clearly seen more action than the mirror. Cardio machines are often an afterthought, sometimes literally shoved in a corner as if the gym is embarrassed to own them. What you're there to do is get strong, quietly, and leave. Anything else is noise.

## The Verdict

Chile turns fitness into a public celebration; Poland turns it into a private discipline you observe like a monk. If you want to feel part of a community while barely trying, book a flight to Santiago and join whichever aerobics class has the loudest speaker. If you want to get genuinely, seriously strong and have zero interest in anyone asking how your day's going mid-set, Warsaw will oblige without a single unnecessary word.

My honest take: Chile is more fun, Poland gets better results, and any nation trying to import Polish gym etiquette into a Chilean park would be laughed out of the plaza within minutes. Fittingly, neither country is remotely interested in what the other is doing — which might be the most Chilean and the most Polish response of all.

> *Internations Warsaw — paraphrased: an expat described leaving a barbell loaded for thirty seconds to grab water and returning to find it silently unloaded and racked by someone else, with no words exchanged at all.*

## Conclusion

Both countries will make you fitter. Only one of them will make you feel like you've joined a slightly unhinged block party in the process, and only the other will make you feel like you've taken holy orders in a chalk-dusted monastery. Chile shouts. Poland whispers, sternly. Choose based on whether your ego needs applause or silence — but pack a towel either way, because in Warsaw, showing up without one is its own small scandal.
