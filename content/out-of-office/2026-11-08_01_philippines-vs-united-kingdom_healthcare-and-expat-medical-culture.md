# Manila Will Bill You By Sundown; London Will Just Ask You To Come Back Tuesday

🇵🇭 🇬🇧 *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two ways to be failed by a healthcare system. The Philippines will fail you with blistering, itemised efficiency — a nurse at your bedside within the hour, a bill at your door before the anaesthetic's worn off. Britain will fail you with the serene, unhurried patience of a system that has given up pretending it's in a hurry, and will simply let you marinate in a waiting room until the problem either resolves itself or becomes someone else's.

I have had a kidney stone diagnosed in a Makati hospital faster than I have had a GP in Clapham return a phone call. One of these experiences cost me the equivalent of a long weekend in Palawan. The other cost me nothing, which is also, in its way, the entire point.

## 🇵🇭 Do's and Don'ts: Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comprehensive private insurance before you land — PhilHealth is for citizens and residents, not a safety net for your weekend motorbike habit | Assume a public hospital will move at the speed of the private one down the road; triage here means something closer to "whoever's family is loudest" |
| Befriend a nurse, literally any nurse — the entire Filipino diaspora medical network runs on relationships, and you want to be in one | Walk into St. Luke's or Makati Medical expecting NHS-style free care; they will hand you a bill before they hand you a gown |
| Bring a "watcher" — a family member or hired companion who stays with you overnight, because Philippine hospitals genuinely expect this | Be surprised when every single interaction with a doctor is warm, personal, and slightly evangelical; the bedside manner here is a national treasure |
| Pay the "cash guarantee" deposit on admission without blinking — it's standard, not a scam | Forget your pharmacy also sells, apparently, everything else in the building; ask a guard, not Google |

## 🇬🇧 Do's and Don'ts: United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a GP practice the literal week you arrive, before you're sick, because the registration itself can take longer than most diseases | Expect same-day anything unless you're actively dying, in which case A&E will, to be fair, actually save your life for free |
| Learn the difference between 111, 999, and "just Google it and cry" — triage-by-phone is a genuine British institution | Try to tip anyone. A nurse who saves your arm wants a thank-you card, not a twenty |
| Say thank you to every single person in the chain, from receptionist to consultant — the NHS runs partly on institutional gratitude | Assume private healthcare (BUPA, Vitality) buys you out of the system entirely — it mostly buys you a nicer waiting room for the same six-week wait |
| Learn to love the pharmacist — they'll often solve your problem for free, with less queueing than anyone else in the building | Be shocked that half the staff treating you trained in, or hail from, the Philippines — it is the quiet, uncredited backbone of this entire institution |

## The Philippines: Pay Now, Cry Later, But At Least Someone's Holding Your Hand

Private Philippine healthcare is, by Southeast Asian standards, genuinely excellent — Makati Medical Center and St. Luke's would not embarrass themselves in Singapore. The catch, obviously, is that none of it is free, and the country's own health insurance scheme, PhilHealth, is built for the roughly 110 million people who actually pay into it, not for you, the besotted foreigner who thought "universal coverage" was a geography-blind concept. It isn't. If you are not enrolled as a resident or employee and contributing, you are, healthcare-wise, a tourist with a pulse, and tourists pay rack rate.

What the system does have, in staggering abundance, is warmth. Filipino healthcare culture treats the patient as the centre of a small, orbiting universe of relatives, nurses, and a "watcher" — usually a family member, sometimes a hired professional — who is expected to stay overnight in the room, because the assumption is that nobody heals well alone, and frankly nobody should. This is the same instinct, scaled up, that has sent hundreds of thousands of Filipino nurses to staff half the hospitals of the English-speaking world: an almost cultural compulsion toward bedside devotion that money alone does not explain.

The flip side is a public system groaning under its own popularity — under-resourced regional hospitals, triage that runs on social capital as much as clinical need, and an admissions process that wants to see the colour of your money, in cash, in advance, before anyone so much as looks at the X-ray. I've watched a man negotiate his own deposit at 2am like it was a tuk-tuk fare. It worked. Everything here is, eventually, negotiable, including, apparently, your own appendectomy.

There's also a pharmacy culture here worth understanding before you need it: Mercury Drug and Watsons branches function as de facto first-contact clinics for anything short of an emergency, staffed by pharmacists willing to recommend, diagnose-adjacent, and sell you a course of antibiotics with a confidence that would make a British GP break out in hives over prescribing protocol. It's informal, occasionally alarming to Western sensibilities, and genuinely, overwhelmingly how most Filipinos handle the 80% of minor ailments that never warrant an actual hospital visit. Expats who skip this step and head straight to a private hospital for a cold are, gently, missing the entire point of how the system is meant to work day to day.

## The United Kingdom: Free At The Point Of Use, Expensive At The Point Of Patience

The NHS is one of the last genuinely socialist institutions the British have left, and they will defend it with a ferocity disproportionate to how long they're currently willing to wait for it. And they do wait — for a GP appointment, for a specialist referral, for the privilege of being told, six weeks later, that it's probably nothing. The system is free, which is magnificent, and also slow, which is the price of free, and no amount of outraged op-eds in the broadsheets changes the arithmetic.

What nobody tells incoming expats is how thoroughly the NHS runs on imported labour — and specifically, overwhelmingly, on Filipino nurses. They have been the institutional backbone of British hospitals since the 1960s; Gloucestershire and Ealing hospitals have both marked a full quarter-century of service from Filipino nursing staff, and you will find them, disproportionately and almost comically, running the night shift at every hospital from Aberdeen to Dover. It is the single funniest irony of this comparison: the expat fleeing an underfunded Philippine health system will, in Britain, be nursed back to health by someone who left that exact same system to come here.

Private top-up insurance exists, and plenty of expats buy it, but it mostly just buys a shorter queue for the same NHS-trained consultant — Britain doesn't really have a parallel private medical culture the way Asia does. And A&E, whatever its queues, will never once ask for your card details before stitching you up, which, if you've just negotiated your admission deposit in a Manila car park, feels genuinely radical.

The gratitude culture surrounding the NHS deserves its own paragraph, because it genuinely confuses newcomers from tip-adjacent, service-transactional healthcare cultures. You are expected to say thank you, repeatedly, sincerely, to receptionists, to the phlebotomist, to the consultant who just delivered bad news — not as etiquette theatre but as a real acknowledgment that everyone in that chain is working inside a chronically underfunded, politically contested institution they still, mostly, believe in. Newcomers who treat NHS staff like service workers at a transaction, rather than participants in something closer to a shared civic project, tend to have noticeably worse experiences than those who clock the emotional register and match it.

## The Verdict

If you want speed and don't mind paying, the Philippines wins outright — cash is the fastest triage system ever devised, and the warmth of the bedside care genuinely rivals anything money buys elsewhere in Asia. If you want dignity without a deposit, Britain wins, eventually, assuming you can survive the waiting room long enough to need it, and assuming you've internalised the gratitude ritual well enough not to look like an ingrate on your way out. The honest answer is that neither system was built with you in mind: one assumes you have family to hold your hand and cash to back it up, the other assumes you have decades to live and the patience of a saint. The real winner, quietly, is whichever Filipino nurse ends up treating you in London — because at that point, you've got the best of both systems in one person, and she didn't even charge you a deposit.

## The Parting Shot

Pick your poison: a system that will heal you brilliantly and bill you like it's personal, or one that will heal you for nothing and make you feel, somewhere around week six of waiting, that it might actually be personal too. Neither will apologise. The Philippines will hand you an invoice with a smile; Britain will hand you a leaflet about "managing expectations." Bring your own watcher either way — in Manila because the hospital expects one, in London because, by week four of the waitlist, you'll need the moral support.
