# Ubuntu Has a Neighbourhood Committee. The Highlands Have a Chief. Both Outrank You.

🇿🇼 Zimbabwe · 🇱🇸 Lesotho
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Zimbabwe's neighbourhood life runs on ubuntu — the deeply-rooted philosophy that a person is a person through other people — expressed daily through borrowed sugar, shared childcare, and a genuine communal obligation that survives even brutal economic hardship. Lesotho, the Mountain Kingdom, organizes its rural and village communities around chieftainship, a structure so old and so functional that most modern governance systems would kill for its compliance rate. Both countries will fold you into community life whether or not you were prepared for it.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Zimbabwe**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet everyone individually, even in passing — skipping this reads as rude | Assume economic hardship has weakened community obligation — it hasn't |
| Contribute to a neighbourhood "stokvel" or savings group if invited | Refuse a shared meal outright; share something small even if full |
| Learn basic Shona or Ndebele greetings — the effort is deeply appreciated | Discuss politics loudly with people you've just met |

**Lesotho**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet and, ideally, be introduced to the local chief in rural areas | Build or settle on village land without going through the chieftainship |
| Join a communal work day (letsema) if invited — it's a genuine honour | Dismiss the chief system as merely ceremonial; it holds real authority |
| Accept traditional Basotho blankets or hospitality graciously | Rush highland village visits; distances and pace are both deceptively slow |

## Zimbabwe: Ubuntu Survives Everything, Including the Economy

Zimbabwean neighbourhood life is built on a philosophy that has outlasted hyperinflation, political upheaval, and enough economic hardship to break most societies' sense of mutual obligation. Ubuntu — "I am because we are" — isn't an abstraction here; it's a functioning social insurance system. Neighbours borrow sugar and mealie-meal from each other constantly, not out of desperation exactly, but because the borrowing itself maintains the relationship. Returning the favour eventually is expected, but nobody's counting too precisely.

The stokvel — informal, rotating community savings groups — remains one of the most quietly ingenious pieces of financial infrastructure I've encountered anywhere, Out of Office included. A group of neighbours contributes a fixed amount regularly, and each cycle, one member takes the full pot. It's trust-based, entirely informal, and remarkably resilient even when the formal banking sector has, at various points, been in genuine crisis. Newcomers who get invited into a stokvel should consider it a real vote of confidence, not just a financial arrangement.

Greetings carry more social weight in Zimbabwe than almost anywhere I've lived. Walking past a neighbour without a proper greeting — even a brief one — registers as a small social failure, and Zimbabweans notice, politely, when foreigners skip this. Shared meals work the same way: turning down food outright, even a small portion when you're genuinely full, can read as a rejection of the relationship rather than a comment on your appetite. The correct move is to take a token amount and praise it extravagantly.

What's remarkable, and what took me longest to appreciate, is how little economic hardship has eroded any of this. Communities under real financial strain in Harare's high-density suburbs maintain the same density of mutual obligation as wealthier neighbourhoods — arguably more, since the stakes of that mutual support are higher. Ubuntu isn't performed for outsiders. It's simply how the neighbourhood functions when nothing else reliably does.

## Lesotho: The Chief Still Runs the Village, And That's the Point

Lesotho, entirely surrounded by South Africa and sitting almost entirely above 1,000 metres, has preserved a chieftainship system that governs rural and village community life with an authority that long predates, and in many ways outlasts, the formal state apparatus layered on top of it. Chiefs allocate land, mediate disputes, and organize communal labour, and in the highland villages where most of the country's population still lives, this isn't ceremonial nostalgia — it's the actual functioning system of local governance.

Letsema — communal work days, where a village turns out together to plough a field, build a structure, or bring in a harvest for a family in need — represents Basotho community life at its most literal. Everyone who's able turns up. Nobody keeps score of who contributed more. The labour itself is the social bond, repeated across generations, and being invited to participate as an outsider is a genuine mark of acceptance rather than a courtesy extended to tourists.

The pace of highland village life deserves its own warning label. Distances that look short on a map take hours on foot or horseback across genuinely brutal terrain, and Basotho social visits move at a corresponding tempo — nobody rushes a greeting, nobody rushes tea, and arriving somewhere with an agenda to move quickly through pleasantries will simply not work. The famous Basotho blanket, worn by nearly everyone in the highlands regardless of season, functions as both practical protection against genuinely severe cold and a marker of social identity; being gifted one, which does happen to respected guests, is not a small gesture.

What surprised me most is how functional the dual system is — a modern state and a centuries-old chieftainship operating in parallel, mostly without friction, because Basotho society has never really treated them as competitors. The chief handles what the chief has always handled. The government handles the rest. Nobody seems confused about which is which, except visiting foreigners, who usually take a humbling amount of time to sort it out.

## The Verdict

Zimbabwe's ubuntu is community as active, daily practice — resilient, urban and rural alike, and stubbornly unbroken by decades of hardship that would have shattered a less communal society. Lesotho's chieftainship is community as inherited structure — slower, more hierarchical, but staggeringly durable across centuries most nations' governance systems didn't survive intact. I'd want Zimbabwe's neighbours if something went wrong on a Tuesday. I'd want Lesotho's chief if something went wrong with the land itself. Both understood something the rest of the world keeps having to relearn: community isn't a nice-to-have. It's the actual infrastructure.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations Harare — paraphrased: an expat describing being invited into a neighbourhood stokvel within months of arriving, and calling it the moment they actually felt part of the street.*

> *Quora, on ubuntu — paraphrased: a Zimbabwean explaining that even during the worst years of economic crisis, nobody on their street went a day without someone checking whether they'd eaten.*

## Conclusion

Zimbabwe will teach you that community is a verb, practiced daily, greeting by greeting, borrowed cup of sugar by borrowed cup of sugar. Lesotho will teach you that some structures are simply older and wiser than whatever replaced them elsewhere, and that a chief's authority isn't quaint — it's load-bearing. Show up to either place assuming you can exist quietly on the margins of the community, and both will, gently but firmly, correct you.
