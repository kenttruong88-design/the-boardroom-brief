# Rwanda Closes the Roads So the President Can Jog Past You. Nairobi Just Lets You Run for Free and Calls It a Movement.

🇷🇼 Rwanda vs 🇰🇪 Kenya
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Rwanda is the only country I've lived in where national fitness policy comes with a presidential cameo. Twice a month, Kigali shuts its main roads to cars from 7 to 10am, civil servants are expected to show up unless their supervisor excuses them, and President Kagame himself has been photographed cycling and jogging through it, telling residents that "physical exercise is paramount for better health." Nairobi has no such theater. It just has a free Saturday running club that started with three friends in a parking lot and now draws crowds large enough to fill it before 7:30am — no road closures, no ministerial attendance, no decree required.

One government built a fitness culture from the top down with genuine conviction. The other let one lawyer with a stopwatch build it from the bottom up, for free, out of sheer stubbornness. I know which one I trust more, and it isn't the one with cabinet ministers in the photos.

## Rwanda: Fitness as State Policy, Delivered With a Straight Face

No other country I've covered has turned exercise into literal government infrastructure quite like Rwanda. Car Free Day — main roads closed to vehicles the first and third Sunday of every month, 7 to 10am, twenty-six times a year — began in 2016 and has since been measured, seriously, as public-health and climate policy: a 2024 environmental study credited it with a 15% cut in Kigali's air pollution over five years, projected to reach 20% by 2025. Civil servants are expected to attend barring a supervisor's excuse, and some ministries "strictly enforce" that expectation, which turns a wellness initiative into something with real workplace-compliance teeth if you're anywhere government-adjacent. President Kagame doesn't just endorse this from a podium — he's been photographed actually cycling and jogging alongside his health and sports ministers, telling residents plainly that "there are diseases we just get rid of through exercising." It is, genuinely, hard to find cynicism to apply here; the health ministry's own numbers back up the urgency, with non-communicable disease deaths climbing from 29% of registered deaths in 2011 to 36% by 2014.

What's conspicuously missing is everything that usually surrounds state-endorsed fitness elsewhere: a commercial ecosystem to match it. Gyms charge Rwf 500–5,000 per single visit or Rwf 12,000–45,000 a month, and hotel gyms run Rwf 50,000–200,000 monthly — a sum that can rival or exceed what a civil servant earns in the same period, creating a visible split between a free, mandated public fitness event and a private tier most locals simply can't access. A typical commercial gym draws only 50 to 60 people a day. The actual social-fitness pipeline for expats runs instead through the Kigali Hash House Harriers — a weekly Saturday run-or-walk that ends, by design, in drinks and conversation — and a separate hiking club, both functioning more as expat lifelines than fitness programs. Want a casual pickup football game instead? One expat forum thread shows the real mechanism: ask around, get pointed to a Saturday 8am game at a university field by a stranger who replied to your post, because Rwanda, as one Reddit commenter put it, "isn't fully set up" for foreigners yet in the structured-amenity sense most expats expect.

## Kenya: A Free Running Club Quietly Beating the Entire Premium Market

Nairobi's fitness culture didn't come from a government office — it came from a lawyer named Emily Chepkor, an eleven-time marathoner, who started "We Run Nairobi" in October 2022 with three friends. It now draws hundreds of runners to Kofisi Square in Westlands every Saturday, with the parking lot filling before 7:30am, and a single co-hosted lifestyle run with a Swiss sportswear brand pulled over 500 people to Windsor Golf Club. It remains deliberately free, in direct contrast to rival clubs charging KSh 600 to 1,800 per run including a post-run meal — meaning the biggest, most socially magnetic fitness community in the city is the one that charges nothing at all, a genuinely odd outcome for anyone expecting "premium equals popular."

The commercial tier, by contrast, is a muddle of wildly inconsistent pricing that no single data point captures honestly. Expatistan pegs a Nairobi business-district gym at around KSh 7,363 a month, but actual forum reports from Westlands tell a messier story: one user was "shocked" at a KSh 10,000 quote from Arena gym, while Zealous Gym in Parklands charged just KSh 5,500, and Racing Sports Gym ran roughly $50 a month — meaning your real price depends entirely on which specific gym you wander into, not some citywide average. Running directly alongside this premium confusion is a parallel, invisible budget-gym economy: Potters Gym in Bahati has charged Sh50 a day for 21 years, and in Kayole, a man named Kevin Mwangi built an entire 100-plus-member gym called Rollin Fitness out of improvised metal-rod weights in his mother's cereal shop, because he couldn't originally afford the Sh1,000 gym fee himself — and his lifters have gone on to win trophies at Kenya's Strongest Man competition. Expats essentially never cross into this world, and locals in it rarely cross into Westlands, two fitness economies operating a few kilometers apart as if on separate continents.

The public-health backdrop explains the urgency on both sides of that divide: WHO's 2025 Kenya report attributes 41% of deaths and half of hospital admissions nationally to non-communicable disease, and a separate figure puts hypertension at 24% of adults, with 39% of those undiagnosed. Against that backdrop, Karura Forest and the Arboretum have become genuine free alternatives — yoga, boot camps and meditation groups meet there regularly — and CrossFit, oddly, was reported as basically absent as recently as a decade ago, forcing fitness-minded newcomers into improvised home setups rather than an expected franchise gym.

## The Verdict

Rwanda wins on institutional conviction — Car Free Day is a genuinely well-executed piece of public policy with measurable results, and I respect a government that makes its president show up and sweat for the cause. Kenya wins on organic energy — a free Saturday run founded by one determined lawyer has out-muscled an entire paid fitness industry on community alone, which is the kind of grassroots win no ministry could legislate into existence. If I had to train somewhere long-term, I'd pick Nairobi's chaos over Kigali's decree, purely because nobody there needs a supervisor's permission to skip it.

## Conclusion

Both cities prove the same point from opposite directions: the most meaningful fitness culture rarely comes from where the money is. In Kigali, it comes from a government decree with genuine public-health teeth and measurable air-quality results. In Nairobi, it comes from three friends in a parking lot who decided the paid running clubs weren't good enough and simply made a better, free one. Bring running shoes to both. Bring a healthy skepticism of any gym's first quoted price to only one.
