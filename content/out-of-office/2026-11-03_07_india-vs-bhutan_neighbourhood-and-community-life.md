# Gurgaon Builds a Wall Around Community. Thimphu Just Builds Community.

🇮🇳 India vs 🇧🇹 Bhutan

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here's an uncomfortable fact about "neighbourhood life" as a concept: it can be manufactured, gated, and sold to you as a lifestyle amenity, or it can simply exist, unbothered, because the government built it into the furniture of the village centuries ago. India and Bhutan sit about as close together on a map as two countries can while taking almost opposite positions on which of those approaches actually works.

I've lived behind more gates than I care to admit, and I've also sat in a village assembly I didn't fully understand in a language I didn't speak, and the second experience taught me more about what a neighbourhood is actually for.

## Do's and Don'ts: India 🇮🇳

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join your housing society's WhatsApp group immediately; it's the real decision-making body, not the RWA office | Assume a gated community's security is airtight; one Bangalore resident called it bluntly "just an eyewash" |
| Register domestic staff at the gate properly; it's standard, expected, and protects everyone involved | Expect the bubble to reflect the wider country; one resident noted sidewalk life outside her gates as "the first time she had really seen the rest of India" |
| Learn your society's quiet hours; some genuinely ban repair work between 2-4pm and overnight | Mistake proximity for access; one longtime expat in Gurgaon noted you "can be an outsider forever" without the right introductions |

## Do's and Don'ts: Bhutan 🇧🇹

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend a local tshechu festival if you're anywhere near a dzong during one; it's the real community calendar | Expect a large expat social scene; foreign residency is tightly capped and forum chatter on it is genuinely thin |
| Respect the kabney (ceremonial scarf) requirement when visiting a dzong or meeting an official | Assume rural life is automatically happier; Bhutan's own GNH data shows urban residents scoring higher, not lower |
| Engage with your local tshogpa (elected village representative) if you need anything resolved locally | Wear improvised clothing to anything official; Driglam Namzha dress code has been legally mandatory since 1989 |

India's urban neighbourhood model has, in the space of a generation, split cleanly into two universes that barely acknowledge each other despite sharing a postcode. The Observer Research Foundation projects at least 24 million Indian households will live in gated communities within five years, representing an estimated $500 billion in investment, with 82% of NRI real-estate money flowing specifically into ready-to-move gated housing. These complexes operate as genuine micro-governments: Residents' Welfare Associations elect office-bearers, fine rule-breakers, and in at least one documented case ban repair work between 2pm and 4pm and again overnight, with mobile apps controlling visitor entry down to the minute. India Currents profiled Gurgaon's Laburnum complex, a thirteen-acre gated enclave where at least half the residents are expats or NRIs, effectively an expat ghetto with its own internal dry cleaner, doctor, pharmacy, and restaurants inside the gate — one resident, Natasha Khandera, described the social onboarding bluntly: "Within days, everyone meets you. You just need to show up in the commons area." But the same profile's most telling line came from resident Kitty Singh, who noted that spotting sidewalk-sleepers just outside her complex was "the first time she had really seen the rest of India" — a sentence that does more to describe the gated-community phenomenon than any amount of amenity-list marketing ever could. Outside the gates, "open" neighbourhood life still runs on the older rhythm: the sabzi-wallah and ironing-wallah on fixed daily rounds, informal street commerce, and a level of networked social intimacy that a Bangalore resident, interviewed for Citizen Matters, dismissed with a shrug about the gated alternative: "The only difference is that there is a gate." Security, she added, was "just an eyewash."

Bhutan does not have this split, mostly because it doesn't have the expat population density to produce one. Community life here is formally structured through zomdue — village assemblies — and tshogpa, elected village-level representatives operating under the Local Government Act of 2009, a system that functions closer to direct local democracy than India's resident-association model. The country's social fabric is also, unusually, legislated: Driglam Namzha, the official code of dress and conduct, became legally mandatory during public and business hours in 1989, requiring men to wear the gho and women the kira and wonju, with a ceremonial kabney scarf — its colour signalling rank — required when entering a dzong or meeting an official. The dzongs themselves, fortress-monasteries traditionally built without nails or blueprints, were historically raised through community labour contributed as a form of tax, which is about as literal a definition of "neighbourhood investment" as exists anywhere. Tshechu festivals, held annually at dzongs across the country and built around masked Cham dances, function as the primary community-bonding calendar event, district by district. And Bhutan's own 2022 Gross National Happiness Index complicates the postcard version of all this rather delightfully: the national score rose to 0.781, with 48.1% of citizens reporting as "deeply or extensively happy" — but urban dwellers scored notably higher on the index than rural ones, 0.790 versus 0.726, directly undercutting the easy assumption that Bhutanese village harmony automatically outperforms city life. Meanwhile, because Bhutan tightly restricts foreign tourism and residency through a daily Sustainable Development Fee and capped visas, the actual expat forum footprint here is remarkably thin — there's barely a thread to find online, because barely anyone is let in to write one.

India's gated-community phenomenon also has a quietly uncomfortable ownership story underneath it: reporting on Mumbai specifically found that just nine private landowners and trusts control roughly a fifth of the city's land, which means the "community" being sold inside any given gated development is frequently built on a concentration of ownership most residents never think to ask about. Governance inside the gates can also be oddly granular — one documented Residents' Welfare Association bans repair and construction noise specifically between 2 and 4pm and again overnight, a level of micromanagement that would read as faintly absurd anywhere else but is treated, inside the gate, as simply how civilised people live.

The real divergence: India has built community as a product you can purchase in gated tiers, with the paradoxical result that the wealthiest residents often know their neighbourhood least. Bhutan has built community as a civic obligation baked into dress code, labour tax, and village assembly, with the result that there's very little "neighbourhood" marketplace to speak of at all because nobody's selling access to something that was never gated in the first place.

## The Verdict

If you want a neighbourhood experience you can curate, amenity by amenity, behind a wall with its own WhatsApp group, India's gated-community model will deliver exactly that, for a price, and ask almost nothing of you socially in return. If you want a neighbourhood that legally obligates its own residents to show up — in the right scarf, at the right festival, to the right village assembly — Bhutan wins, assuming you can get a visa at all. I'm giving this to Bhutan, on principle: a system with no "eyewash" security gate, because it never needed one, has already out-argued the entire premise of the question.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *India Currents, Raju Narisetti, paraphrased, on breaking into Indian gated-community social life: "It's about who you know. And you can be an outsider forever."*

> *Citizen Matters, Bangalore resident, paraphrased: the supposed security advantage of a gated complex is "just an eyewash" — the gate itself is the only real difference from an unwalled neighbourhood.*

> *Expat.com / Expat Exchange, Bhutan relocation thread, paraphrased: genuine firsthand community accounts from foreign residents are scarce, largely because so few foreigners are permitted to live there long-term in the first place — the silence itself is the data point.*

## Conclusion

India will sell you a neighbourhood with a gate, a gym, and a resident WhatsApp group, and still somehow leave you unable to name the family three doors down after two years. Bhutan will hand you a scarf, a festival schedule, and a village assembly you barely understand, and call that belonging — correctly, as it turns out. If you want to actually know your neighbours rather than simply pay a service charge near them, go where the dress code is mandatory and the gate doesn't exist.
