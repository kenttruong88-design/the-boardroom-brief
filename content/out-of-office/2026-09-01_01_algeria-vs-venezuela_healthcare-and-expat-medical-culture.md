# Bring Your Own Bandages, Wherever You Land

🇩🇿 Algeria vs 🇻🇪 Venezuela — Healthcare and Expat Medical Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nothing sorts the tourists from the true expats quite like the moment you need a doctor and realise the nearest one who speaks your language is a four-hour flight away. Algeria and Venezuela have spent the last few decades demonstrating, in wildly different ways, that "universal healthcare" is a phrase best read with the same suspicion you'd apply to "unlimited data" or "no hidden fees." One is oil-rich and merely under-resourced. The other is oil-richer and actively collapsing. Guess which one I'd rather break a leg in.

I have had a kidney stone in three different countries and a suspicious rash in five, so believe me when I say I've made a hobby out of watching well-meaning expats discover, usually around week three, that the health insurance PDF they skimmed on the flight over was not aspirational marketing but a legally binding description of exactly how alone they now are.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Algeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get private insurance the day you land, not the week you need it | Assume the public system will move at a pace your employer finds acceptable |
| Learn the French medical vocabulary, not just the pleasantries | Expect every specialist to speak English, even in Algiers |
| Ask colleagues which private clinic they actually trust | Wait for a "good moment" to register with social security |
| Keep cash on hand for consultations | Assume rural coverage matches what you get in the capital |

**Venezuela**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive with a real stock of any medication you can't live without | Assume the pharmacy will have it, even the brand-name version |
| Buy international insurance that covers evacuation, not just treatment | Rely on the public hospital system for anything beyond triage |
| Build a relationship with a private clinic in Caracas before you need one | Wait until a crisis to figure out where the nearest working blood bank is |
| Keep a plan B country on standby for anything serious | Assume "private" means "fully stocked" — ask first |

## Algeria: Bureaucracy With a Pulse

Algeria's healthcare system suffers from the classic affliction of oil economies everywhere: enormous public investment poured into buildings that then wait patiently for the staff and supplies to catch up. The country has roughly one doctor for every 1,200 people, which sounds almost civilised until you learn that ratio evaporates the moment you leave Algiers or Oran for anywhere with fewer boulangeries per capita. CHU Mustapha Pacha remains the reassuring name expats drop when discussing worst-case scenarios, mostly because it is the one hospital everybody has actually heard of.

The public system technically covers foreign employees through social security, which is a bit like being told your gym membership is valid at a facility that occasionally has weights. In practice, every sensible expat I have met in Algiers subscribed to private insurance within the first fortnight, because the alternative is an "up to eight-hour wait" that somehow always becomes eleven. Private clinics, staffed disproportionately by doctors trained abroad — Algeria's chronic local staffing shortage means you are statistically more likely to be treated by someone who also trained in Paris or Montreal than by a purely domestic graduate — fill the gap adequately, provided you can pay the 2,000 to 5,000 dinar consultation fee upfront and don't mind a slightly improvisational approach to appointment scheduling.

What nobody tells you before you arrive is how much of Algerian healthcare culture runs on the currency of personal relationships rather than paperwork. A recommendation from a trusted colleague carries more weight than any insurance card, and the fastest route to competent care is rarely the official one. Learn this early, make friends with your building's concierge, and you will do fine. Assume the system will function the way the brochure promised, and you will spend a memorable afternoon in a corridor practising your French numbers on a nurse who has heard every foreigner's pain scale exaggeration at least twice already this week.

## Venezuela: Bring Your Own Everything

If Algeria's healthcare problem is one of pace, Venezuela's is one of pure physical absence. This is not a system that's merely slow — it's a system where an estimated seven in ten medicines are simply not on the shelf, any shelf, on any given day. Antibiotics, painkillers, insulin: the fundamentals that most of the world assumes will simply be there have become, for ordinary Venezuelans, a matter of luck, foreign relatives, or the black market. I have watched a friend's driver spend three days sourcing a single box of amoxicillin through a WhatsApp chain that stretched to Miami and back.

For expats, the survival strategy is blunt and universally agreed upon: never touch the public system except in a genuine emergency, and even then, treat it as a bridge to somewhere better. Private hospitals in Caracas — Clínica El Ávila and Hospital de Clínicas Caracas are the two names that come up in every conversation — offer a real standard of care, staffed by doctors who trained through a system that, decades ago, was one of Latin America's finest and whose institutional memory hasn't entirely evaporated along with the supply chains. But "real standard of care" assumes the equipment is calibrated, the blood bank has stock, and the specialist you need hasn't already emigrated to Madrid or Bogotá, which, statistically, they may well have.

Every serious expat contract in Venezuela now includes evacuation coverage as a baseline, not a luxury add-on, typically routing patients to Colombia or the US for anything beyond the routine. If your employer's package doesn't include this, that is not an oversight to raise politely in your next one-on-one. That is a reason to reread your contract and possibly your life choices.

## The Verdict

Algeria wins this one, and it isn't close. Algeria's healthcare system has real, well-documented problems — understaffing, rural gaps, a public sector that treats "eventually" as a timestamp — but it is a functioning system straining under legitimate pressure, not a collapsed one running on improvisation and imported amoxicillin. In Algiers you can build a reasonable life around a good private clinic and a trusted GP. In Caracas you are building your entire medical strategy around the assumption that things might get worse before your insurer's evacuation plane can get you out. One country needs patience. The other needs a contingency plan and a friend in Bogotá.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a former NGO worker in Algiers paraphrased: the biggest shock wasn't the wait times, it was realising "urgent" is a relative term that means something completely different depending on who's saying it.*

> *Expat.com forum, Algeria thread — paraphrased: get a private doctor you trust in your first month, because the day you actually need one is the worst possible day to be shopping around.*

> *Expat Focus community, Venezuela thread — paraphrased: an expat described stockpiling six months of a family member's regular medication before relocating, calling it "the single most important packing decision I made."*

## Conclusion

Neither of these countries will hand you a health system that behaves the way the one back home does, and pretending otherwise is how expats end up as cautionary tales at other expats' dinner parties. Algeria asks for patience and a decent private clinic. Venezuela asks for a suitcase full of your own medication, an evacuation policy you've actually read, and a healthy respect for how quickly "we'll figure it out" can become "we should have figured it out three weeks ago." Pack accordingly, insure aggressively, and for heaven's sake stop describing either arrangement as "exotic." It's a hospital system, not a tasting menu.
