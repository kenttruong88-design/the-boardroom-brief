# In Germany, Your Neighbours Judge You Through the Rules. In Fiji, They Just Show Up.

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Germany's neighbourhood life is governed, quite literally, by a document — the Hausordnung, a formal set of building rules covering everything from permitted vacuuming hours to the precise etiquette of communal stairwell cleaning, and community cohesion here is expressed largely through mutual, silent compliance with a shared code nobody discusses out loud but everyone enforces with a raised eyebrow. Fiji operates on the almost total opposite principle: community isn't a set of rules you follow quietly, it's a living, constant, physically present obligation, expressed through kerekere — a customary practice of asking for and sharing resources that makes "my house" and "my stuff" considerably more porous concepts than most Western newcomers arrive expecting.

I have been silently side-eyed in a Berlin stairwell for vacuuming four minutes past the permitted hour. I have also had a Fijian neighbour arrive, entirely without notice, to borrow sugar, stay for two hours, and leave having somehow rearranged my understanding of what "borrowing" means. Both experiences taught me more about community than any guidebook could.

## Do's & Don'ts: Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read your building's Hausordnung carefully — it's genuinely enforced | Run washing machines, vacuum, or make noise during official quiet hours |
| Take your turn on communal duties (Kehrwoche-style stairwell cleaning) seriously | Assume friendliness with neighbours; polite distance is the actual norm |
| Separate your recycling with real precision — neighbours do notice and judge | Expect spontaneous social visits — advance notice is culturally expected |

## Do's & Don'ts: Fiji

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand kerekere — a request-and-share custom — before it catches you off guard | Refuse a genuine request from a neighbour without real cause; it damages standing |
| Participate in village or community obligations if you're living near one | Treat "private property" as an absolute boundary the way you might at home |
| Bring a small gift (sevusevu) when properly visiting or joining a village community | Assume brief, formal greetings are enough — real relationship-building takes time |

## Germany: Community by Unspoken Contract

German neighbourhood life runs on a principle that looks, from the outside, almost paradoxical: deep collective order maintained through minimal direct social contact. The Hausordnung — literally "house rules" — is a genuinely formal document posted in most apartment buildings, specifying quiet hours (typically after 10pm and during the traditional Sunday and midday rest periods), rules around stairwell cleaning rotas, and often surprisingly granular guidance on everything from balcony plant maintenance to bicycle storage. Nobody needs to introduce themselves to enforce it; violations are noted, remembered, and occasionally addressed through a polite but unmistakably pointed note left on a door.

What's genuinely interesting is that this isn't coldness so much as a different theory of what makes a good neighbour. Destatis's social cohesion surveys consistently show Germans valuing reliability and mutual non-interference as core community virtues — a good neighbour is one who respects boundaries, follows shared rules precisely, and doesn't require managing, rather than one who's constantly, warmly present. Spontaneous social visits are genuinely rare and can even read as slightly presumptuous; the cultural expectation is advance notice, even for casual coffee. This produces a real, if quiet, form of community — buildings that run smoothly, disputes that rarely escalate because the rules pre-empt most friction, and a baseline trust that comes precisely from everyone knowing exactly what's expected of them. It's community as infrastructure rather than warmth, and once you understand that distinction, the apparent chilliness makes considerably more sense.

## Fiji: Community as a Constant, Physical Presence

Fijian community life, particularly in and around village settings, operates on an entirely different logic, one where the boundary between "yours" and "your neighbour's" is considerably more fluid than most Western newcomers are prepared for. Central to this is kerekere, a customary practice where requesting to borrow or share resources — food, tools, sometimes money — is a normal, expected part of daily social fabric, and refusing a reasonable request without real cause carries genuine social cost. This isn't freeloading in the Western sense; it's reciprocal, cumulative, and deeply woven into how trust and standing are built and maintained across an entire community over years, not transactions.

Village life, where it applies, extends this further: communal obligations, shared labour for events like weddings or funerals, and a general expectation of participation rather than polite observation from the sidelines. The Fiji Ministry of iTaukei Affairs and Tourism Fiji's own guidance for visitors staying near or in village communities is explicit about this — bringing a sevusevu (a formal gift, traditionally kava root) when properly introduced to a community isn't a quaint tourist ritual, it's the actual, functioning mechanism by which an outsider is granted standing and welcome. What surprises newcomers most isn't the generosity itself, which is well-documented and genuinely warm, but the sheer frequency and casualness of it — neighbours arriving unannounced, extended stays treated as normal rather than presumptuous, and a general assumption that community means active, ongoing, physically present mutual obligation, not a document everyone quietly agrees to follow from a respectful distance.

## The Verdict

Germany offers order, predictability, and a community that functions beautifully precisely because nobody has to like each other to make it work. Fiji offers warmth, obligation, and a community that functions beautifully precisely because everybody's daily life is genuinely, constantly entangled with everyone else's. If I want to be left alone in comfort, Germany wins outright. If I want to actually feel like I belong somewhere, deeply and immediately, Fiji wins without much of a contest — though it will cost you a fair amount of sugar, and eventually, your assumptions about personal space.

## Conclusion

Germany will teach you that community can be an unspoken contract, honoured through precision rather than proximity, and that this quiet reliability is its own genuine form of care. Fiji will teach you that community can also mean total, cheerful entanglement, where your sugar, your time, and occasionally your Saturday afternoon belong a little bit to everyone around you. Both are real belonging. Only one of them will ever leave a note on your door about it.
