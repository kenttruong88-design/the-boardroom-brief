# Spain Will Meet You at the Terrace at Ten, and Mexico Will Ask Whether You Have an Appointment

🇲🇽 Mexico · 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Dating abroad is the cruellest cultural examination available to adults, because the questions are never written down and the examiner is also wearing a nice shirt. You can read all the guides you like on tipping and trains, but no guide will tell you whether the man who just bought you a drink considers it a date or a courtesy. Both Spain and Mexico speak a language that sounds like romance even when it is ordering lunch, which makes the confusion richer and the mistakes more expensive.

The two countries share a language and very little else. Spain meets you outdoors, late, in a group, with a drink and no urgency. Mexico, if the forum record is any guide, tends to treat dating as a more deliberate arrangement with a family, a geography and a risk profile that varies wildly depending on whether you are on a beach or near a border. I have been charmed and baffled in both, usually in the same evening.

## 🇲🇽 Mexico: The Appointment System

Let me be careful about what I know, because dating culture is the one subject where the fastest way to lie is to generalise. What I can report is what expats say on the forum record, and it paints a patchwork. One poster, travellight, says dating in Mexico is much the same as anywhere plus a language barrier, and that locals joked dating "requires an appointment" and quickly work out whether you are available. That joke contains a real observation: in a place where friends, family and neighbours are the network, a foreigner is visible, and visibility cuts both ways.

The retiree-heavy expat belts, Lake Chapala, Puerto Vallarta, San Miguel de Allende, the Yucatán, behave like their own country. A poster in Chapala says the town has more women than men among expats, many divorced or widowed, and that he found a partner within two weeks of becoming single and has stayed with an expat partner for four years. A ten-year resident in another thread says many single Mexican women have children and that large age gaps are accepted, while another says expat women face more competition. This is not a romantic idyll and not a catastrophe; it is a market with its own odd rules.

Safety, as always, is the argument nobody can settle. Posters in the Mexico dating thread disagree sharply, with some warning about border towns and bars and others describing the interior near Chapala as feeling very safe. The correct reading is that Mexico is a country, not a postcode, and you should stop treating it as a single decision. Mexico City has its own scene: MEXPAT holds monthly expat gatherings, and other suggestions include the embassy's club list, English-language churches and your colleagues.

What strikes me is how little the Mexican answers resemble Spain's. In Mexico, the expat scene is often a world unto itself, in the best and worst senses. You can live there for years in a pleasant bubble, date within the bubble, and never be wrong. You can also step outside it and find a country that is warm, frank and wonderfully unhurried about whether your age or history matters. The risk is not that you will be rejected. The risk is that you will be understood.

## 🇪🇸 Spain: Tardeo and the Art of the Ambiguous Drink

Spain is the better-documented battlefield. The expat forum record describes Tinder and Bumble as the dominant dating apps, with Bumble popular among expats because of its more international user base. But the real social engine is offline. "Tardeo", afternoon drinks that drift into evening, is how new faces appear, and the terrace and plaza culture makes spontaneous meeting easier than in most of Europe. Language-exchange groups and hiking clubs are recommended, with Alicante singled out as strong for both.

The etiquette is a small book of its own. Splitting the bill, "pagar a medias", is the norm. Ten to fifteen minutes late counts as on time. Two cheek kisses can end a first date without implying anything serious, which is the sort of detail that prevents a great deal of misery on both sides. Posters also claim a regional contrast: the South is more affectionate, the North more reserved. I would not tattoo that on anything, but it matches my own experience of Seville versus Bilbao.

In Madrid, the thread on making friends lists recurring meetup nights: Tuesdays at an Irish pub off the Puerta del Sol, Wednesdays near the Ópera, Thursdays at a bar on Plaza de Santo Domingo, plus an English-language bookshop café midweek. The crowd runs mostly from the twenties to mid-thirties. I flag that the thread is old, so check that those venues still exist before you stand outside one in the rain. Other routes include English-immersion weeks such as VaughanTown and PuebloInglés, hiking clubs and the long Spanish weekend trips known as puentes.

The warning from the Madrid poster is the one I would underline in red: befriend one Spaniard and you get their network, and do not read a casual drink invitation as a date. The language exchanges, he adds, tend to drift into English-only groups, which defeats the point. The Spanish social scene is open and generous and almost impossible to hurry. If you want to be a participant, stay in the room long enough for the room to rearrange itself around you.

## The Verdict

Spain wins for the newcomer who wants to meet people. The social infrastructure is built for it: the terrace, the afternoon drink, the group outing, the habit of inviting the stranger. You can fail slowly and safely in Spain, which is the best way to fail. The apps work, the clubs work, and the cultural rules, split the bill, be late, don't over-read the kiss, are learnable in a month.

Mexico wins for the person who wants something less rehearsed. The scene is more fragmented, more regional and more dependent on the particular town you choose, but the reward is that the answers are less scripted. A joke about needing an appointment is, finally, a welcome: it means someone has noticed you exist. Pick the right region, bring your Spanish, and be honest about what you want.

My sting: Spain is easier, and Mexico is more interesting, and the cruelty of dating abroad is that you usually only get to enjoy one of those at a time. If you are lonely, go to Spain. If you are curious, go to Mexico. If you are both, go to a bar in Madrid and ask a Spaniard to introduce you to a Mexican.

## Conclusion

I have been on dates in a lot of languages, and the universal truth is this: the culture will always be more complicated than the person. Spain has built a social calendar that makes meeting people possible. Mexico has built a social fabric that makes knowing people possible. The distinction matters more than any app.

So be a little late in Spain, split the bill in Madrid and think twice about the border town in Mexico, and never, in either country, confuse a friendly drink with a proposal. Not everything that smells of romance is a love story. Sometimes it is just tapas.
