# India Invented the Festival of Lights. Singapore Gave It a Lighting Schedule and a Public Holiday

🇮🇳 India vs 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Diwali in India is not an event; it is a climate. For weeks the shops swell, the sweets multiply, the neighbours arrive unannounced with boxes, and the entire country behaves as though a particularly good rumour were going round. Singapore, which treats most things as a project to be managed, has taken the same festival under its wing and produced something I can only describe as an organised glow: dates published in advance, lights on a schedule, and decorations on selected buses and trains.

This is not a contest about who loves the festival more. It is a comparison of two ways of holding a holiday. One is a sprawling, family-driven, slightly chaotic tide. The other is a tidy civic arrangement with a very good lighting contractor. A source note: nothing I could retrieve described Diwali inside India, so my Indian descriptions come from widely known general features, and I will say so where it matters. The Singapore detail comes from a 2026 events guide and two personal essays.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the sweets, all of them, graciously | Refuse a box and expect no one to notice |
| Plan your travel weeks ahead, since the whole country moves | Book a train home on the day itself |
| Learn which days of the five-day festival matter to your hosts | Treat it as a single evening |
| Bring a gift when invited into a home | Arrive empty-handed and apologetic |

**Singapore**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit Little India after dark on Serangoon Road | Go on the final weekend and expect to move |
| Check the published event dates in advance | Assume the lights are only on for the holiday itself |
| Enjoy Deepavali as the public holiday it is | Plan a Monday meeting for the next working day |
| Eat in Little India's old restaurants, not only the new | Treat the district as a theme park |

## India

I cannot give you a fetched citation for India, so treat this as general knowledge. Diwali is the large, flexible family festival of the subcontinent, spread across several days with different rites on each, and arranged around lamps, rangoli, sweets, new clothes and the unspoken rule that no one celebrates alone. The date moves with the lunar calendar. The events are not organised by anyone, which is what makes them work.

The experience is domestic first and civic second. You clean the house, light the lamps, visit relatives and eat things made in kitchens that have been busy for days. Fireworks, shopping and travel crowd the weeks around the day, and the country slows and speeds up at once. A visitor who expects a parade will find instead a dozen thousand small ceremonies, each hosted by someone who is secretly exhausted and wholly delighted.

The sting is the pressure: the travel, the cooking, the visiting, the expectation that you will return to the family home and produce a respectable amount of cheer. Expats who relocate from India often say they miss not the lights but the obligations, the particular noise of a house full of relatives. That is the one thing you cannot import.

If you are posted to India during the festival, say yes to every invitation and say it early. Your job is to be fed, to listen, and to learn the names of the five days. The rest takes care of itself.

A footnote for the planner: the date of Diwali moves every year with the lunar calendar, which means that anyone relocating to or from India should check it before booking flights. Airlines and railways know this well and price accordingly, and a booking made late can cost several times the normal fare. Expats working abroad often burn a precious week of leave on the trip, which is why a country that offers the day as a public holiday is quietly doing something valuable for its migrant workforce. The calendar is not a detail. It is the difference between going home and watching the celebrations on a video call from your kitchen.

## Singapore

Singapore does Deepavali with a precision that would alarm a Diwali purist and delight a logistics manager. The 2026 festival fell on 8 November, and an expat events guide lists the programme: the Little India light-up runs from 3 October to 29 November, with Serangoon Road lit nightly between 6:30 and 9:30pm. A festival village at Campbell Lane operates daily from 7pm to midnight until the day itself, the Indian Heritage Centre runs programmes, and five MRT trains and ten bus services carry festive decorations until 11 November.

Pooja Makhijani, writing as an American-Indian newcomer in 2015, noted that Deepavali is a public holiday in Singapore, which she loved, but that she found it far less visible than her family's celebrations in the United States. She also recorded that nearly nine percent of Singaporeans and permanent residents trace ancestry to the Indian subcontinent, which explains the depth of Little India's roots. She was building new family traditions there, since community gatherings felt hollow without relatives.

A student from India at NTU, writing in 2017, found the district felt like a familiar Indian market during the festival, and the closest he could get to home. He named the Sri Veeramakaliamman Temple, Chettynad Curry Palace and Komala Vilas as anchors. That is the Singaporean achievement: not replacing home, but offering a credible substitute that has street signs and good transport.

The sting is the formality. Singapore turns a festival into a managed event, and the managed event does not have an aunt. It has a schedule. The lights are lovely and the crowds are orderly, and somewhere in all that civic excellence the chaotic warmth gets filtered out.

The Expat Living guide lists the days of the festival by name: Dhanteras, Choti Deepavali, Deepavali, Govardhan Puja and Bhai Dhooj. That is five separate occasions, each with its own meaning, and a newcomer who thinks Deepavali is a single evening has missed most of it. The essay by Pooja Makhijani made a related point, saying the third day is the most auspicious, when Lakshmi is believed to visit. Knowing this changes how you spend the week: you stop waiting for one big event and start paying attention to the small ones. In Singapore, the programming is a useful map. In India, you follow the family.

## The Verdict

India wins on soul, Singapore on service, and I refuse to pretend the second counts for nothing. If you want the festival in its full, noisy, over-fed glory, you go to India and you let a family adopt you. If you want a version that is accessible, beautifully lit and available to anyone with an MRT card, you go to Singapore, where Deepavali is a public holiday and the city tells you where to stand.

For an expatriate far from home, I would call it a tie that breaks differently depending on your family. If you have relatives in India, Singapore is a pleasant echo. If you do not, Singapore is a generous way to meet a festival half way, and India is the long, delicious deep end.

Still, one cannot ignore the achievement of a small country that takes a minority celebration, decorates the public transport and gives everyone the day off. Singapore may lack an aunt in every kitchen, but it has understood that visibility is a form of respect.

A final practical point for anyone choosing between postings: ask which one gives you the festival you actually want. If you want a house full of relatives and sweets, India. If you want a glowing street, a public holiday and a quiet evening with a friend, Singapore. Neither will disappoint if you arrive with the right expectation, and both will disappoint if you carry the wrong one across the border.

## Conclusion

A festival is a mirror. India shows you a people who cannot stop hosting each other. Singapore shows you a state that cannot stop planning for them.

Go to India for the noise, the sweets and the relatives. Go to Singapore for the lights, the timetable and the day off. And if you can manage both in one lifetime, you will have eaten better than most diplomats.
