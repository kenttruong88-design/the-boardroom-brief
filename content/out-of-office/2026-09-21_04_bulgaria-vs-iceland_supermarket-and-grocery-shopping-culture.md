# One Country Sells You a Tomato for Pennies. The Other Sells You Regret.

🇧🇬🇮🇸 By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office

There is a particular kind of financial vertigo that hits you the first time you buy groceries in Reykjavik after flying in from Sofia. In Bulgaria, you walk out of the market with two bulging bags, a bottle of local wine, and change left over from a twenty. In Iceland, you walk out of the supermarket having bought the equivalent, spent four times as much, and developed a new, complicated relationship with the concept of a "reasonable" price for cheese.

Both countries take grocery shopping seriously, just in opposite directions — one through sheer, glorious abundance, the other through an almost punishing scarcity economics that somehow still produces excellent yoghurt.

## Do's & Don'ts: Bulgaria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shop at the local pazar for produce — it's cheaper and better | Assume supermarket produce matches market quality at the same price |
| Bring cash to smaller vendors; card acceptance is patchy outside cities | Expect every stall to take card, especially outside Sofia |
| Try local dairy — Bulgarian yoghurt culture is a point of national pride | Dismiss it as "just yoghurt"; locals will notice, and mind |
| Bargain lightly at markets if buying in bulk | Haggle aggressively over small purchases — it reads as stingy, not savvy |

## Do's & Don'ts: Iceland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shop at Bónus or Krónan for the best prices — the pink piggy logo is your friend | Buy groceries at a convenience store or petrol station out of habit |
| Batch-cook and freeze; imported produce prices punish small, frequent shops | Expect fresh produce prices to resemble anywhere in mainland Europe |
| Buy Icelandic skyr, lamb, and fish — genuinely cheaper and better locally | Assume all dairy and meat carries the same import markup as fruit |
| Budget shopping trips around weekly specials, which shift meaningfully | Assume prices are fixed and stable; they fluctuate more than expected |

## Bulgaria: The Market as Theatre, the Prices as Fiction

Bulgaria remains one of the last places in Europe where you can do a full week's grocery shop and still feel, faintly, like you've gotten away with something. The open-air pazar is the beating heart of it — vendors shouting the day's tomato price like it's breaking news, peaches so ripe they're basically fruit-flavoured apologies, cheese counters where the person behind it will make you taste four varieties before letting you buy the fifth. It's loud, unhurried, and deeply, unapologetically transactional in the best sense: you're there to get good food cheaply, and everyone involved understands the assignment.

Supermarkets exist and are perfectly functional — Kaufland and Lidl have thoroughly colonised the Bulgarian retail landscape — but they're where you go for the boring stuff: cleaning supplies, packaged goods, the things a market stall doesn't sell. For produce, dairy, and anything you actually want to taste, the market wins, and locals will tell you so with the mild disdain reserved for anyone who'd choose shrink-wrapped peppers over the real thing.

The catch, and there's always a catch, is that this abundance runs almost entirely on cash and almost entirely in Bulgarian, particularly outside Sofia and Plovdiv. Card readers at smaller stalls are rare-to-nonexistent, and vendors have limited patience for elaborate pointing-and-gesturing negotiations during the Saturday morning rush. Come prepared, come with lev in your pocket, and come ready to learn the numbers one through twenty in Bulgarian faster than you ever intended to.

## Iceland: Groceries as an Extreme Sport

Icelandic grocery shopping is an exercise in humility for anyone arriving from literally almost anywhere else in Europe. The country imports the overwhelming majority of its fresh produce across genuinely daunting logistical distances, and the price tags reflect it with a bluntness that borders on aggressive: a punnet of strawberries that would cost pocket change in Bulgaria can run several times that in Reykjavik, and a casual "I'll just grab a few things" trip has a way of turning into a receipt that requires a moment of quiet reflection in the car park.

The two dominant discount chains, Bónus (pink piggy logo, genuinely beloved as a national institution) and Krónan, exist specifically to soften this blow, and locals build entire shopping strategies around them — batch cooking, freezing in bulk, timing purchases to weekly deals that can meaningfully move the needle on a family's budget. This isn't optional frugality; it's the default operating mode for anyone who isn't independently wealthy or allergic to eating.

What redeems Icelandic grocery shopping, and it genuinely does redeem it, is that the things Iceland actually produces are extraordinary and comparatively affordable: skyr in more flavours than seems reasonable, lamb that tastes like it spent its life somewhere spectacular (because it did), fish so fresh it feels faintly insulting to anything you've eaten elsewhere labelled "fresh." The trick to surviving Icelandic groceries isn't fighting the import economics — it's building your diet around what the island does brilliantly and treating everything imported as an occasional, carefully budgeted indulgence rather than a weekly habit.

## The Verdict

Bulgaria wins on pure, uncomplicated joy — the market, the prices, the sense that food shopping can be a pleasure rather than a small financial crisis repeated weekly. But Iceland wins something else: a strange, hard-won respect for what a place can produce brilliantly under genuine constraint, and a grocery culture that's forced locals to become smarter, more deliberate shoppers as a result. If I'm counting my money, Sofia's market wins outright. If I'm counting my blessings over a bowl of skyr that costs what it costs because the sheep that made it had a nicer view than I'll ever have, I begrudgingly understand the premium. Begrudgingly. I still checked the receipt twice.

## Conclusion

Grocery shopping tells you more about a country's relationship with geography and abundance than almost anything else you'll do as a visitor. Bulgaria has land, sun, and a market culture that turns produce shopping into a genuine pleasure. Iceland has volcanic rock, brutal shipping logistics, and has responded by getting ruthlessly good at what it can actually grow and catch itself. Shop accordingly in both: haggle gently in Sofia, budget ruthlessly in Reykjavik, and in neither country make the mistake of assuming the supermarket down the road works the same way it did back home. It never does. That's rather the point of leaving.
