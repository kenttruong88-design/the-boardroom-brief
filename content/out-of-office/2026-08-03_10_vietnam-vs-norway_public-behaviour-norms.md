# One Country's Public Space Is a Negotiation. The Other's Is a Silent Agreement Not to Be Noticed.

🇻🇳 Vietnam · 🇳🇴 Norway
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Vietnam's public life runs on constant, cheerful negotiation — crossing the street means walking calmly into a river of motorbikes and trusting, correctly, that they will flow around you; queues are theoretical; personal space is a foreign concept nobody's bothered translating; and the ambient noise level of any given street at any given hour sits somewhere between "market day" and "minor celebration." Norway's public life runs on the opposite principle entirely, one so culturally specific it has its own name — Jantelov, the unwritten law that you should never think you're special, never draw attention, never sit next to a stranger on a bus if an entire empty row exists elsewhere, which in Norway, it always does.

One country wants you fully present in the collective chaos. The other wants you to disappear into the collective quiet.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Cross busy streets at a slow, steady, predictable pace — traffic flows around consistent movement, not hesitation | Stop suddenly or run while crossing — sudden unpredictable movement is what actually causes accidents |
| Accept that queues are more of a general suggestion than a strict system, especially at markets and bus stops | Take mild jostling or close physical proximity personally — dense personal space norms aren't rudeness, just density |
| Embrace the ambient noise — street life, karaoke, traffic, and vendors calling out are the actual soundtrack of daily life | Expect quiet residential norms after dark in most neighbourhoods — street activity often continues well into the evening |

### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Respect personal space defaults — leave real distance in queues, on transport, and in general conversation | Sit directly next to a stranger on a mostly empty bus or train — it will be read as odd, even mildly intrusive |
| Keep your voice down in public spaces — loud conversation, especially on transport, draws visible discomfort | Boast about achievements or possessions — Jantelov discourages standing out, even in casual conversation |
| Queue precisely and patiently — Norwegians take orderly queuing seriously, including numbered ticket systems in small shops | Assume reserved public behaviour means unfriendliness — it's social norm, not personal coldness, and warms up in private settings |

## Vietnam: Public Space as Constant, Trusting Negotiation

Vietnamese public life, especially in cities like Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, operates on a level of density and improvisation that overwhelms most first-time visitors before it eventually, usually within a week or two, starts to make a strange kind of sense. The defining experience is crossing the street: with millions of motorbikes and often no meaningfully enforced traffic signal compliance, pedestrians are expected to simply walk, steadily and predictably, directly into oncoming traffic, trusting that the flow of bikes will part and reform around them like water around a stone. It sounds terrifying. It works, consistently, precisely because everyone understands and honours the same unwritten rule: move steadily, don't stop, don't run, don't panic.

This same principle — dense, fluid, negotiated coexistence rather than rigid rule-following — extends across most of Vietnamese public behaviour. Queues at markets, bus stops, and smaller shops function more as a general cluster with an understood rough order than a strict single-file line; jostling and close physical proximity are simply features of high-density urban life, not breaches of etiquette. Visitors from more space-generous cultures often misread this as rudeness, when it's really just a completely different, equally functional set of unspoken agreements about shared space.

Noise operates on its own logic too. Vietnamese street life is loud, constantly — vendors calling out prices, traffic, karaoke bars (a genuinely beloved national pastime) spilling music into the street well into the evening, motorbike horns used liberally and without any of the aggression they'd imply elsewhere. This isn't a breakdown of public order; it's the actual texture of daily life, a soundscape residents have grown so accustomed to that true silence, when it occasionally happens, can feel more unsettling than the noise ever did.

What ties all of this together is an underlying current of trust — trust that the traffic will flow correctly, trust that the queue will roughly sort itself out, trust that proximity doesn't require apology. It's chaotic on the surface and remarkably functional underneath, a public behaviour system built on flexibility rather than rules.

## Norway: Public Space as a Silent Agreement Not to Intrude

Norwegian public behaviour is shaped overwhelmingly by a cultural concept most Norwegians won't explicitly cite but nearly all instinctively practice: Jantelov, the "Law of Jante" — a set of unwritten social norms, originating from a 1933 satirical novel but genuinely reflective of deep-rooted Scandinavian cultural values, that essentially discourage standing out, boasting, or drawing unnecessary attention to oneself in any public or social context.

This produces some of the most consistently cited "culture shock" moments for newcomers to Norway: an almost sacred respect for personal space in public that goes well beyond simple politeness. Norwegians will genuinely choose to stand rather than sit next to a stranger on a bus with available alternative seating; an empty row exists specifically to remain empty as long as possible. It's not coldness — Norwegians are, in private and among friends, warm and generous — it's a public-space norm that treats unnecessary proximity to strangers as a minor imposition on both parties, one easily avoided simply by leaving distance.

Volume follows the same logic. Loud conversation in public — on trains, in cafes, in queues — draws visible, if silent, discomfort; Norwegians default to a notably quiet public register, saving louder, more expressive social energy for private settings among people they actually know. Queuing, similarly, is taken seriously and precisely — many smaller shops and services still use numbered ticket systems specifically to formalise what other cultures leave to informal ordering, removing any ambiguity or need for social negotiation at all.

The throughline across all of this is a cultural discomfort with unnecessary social friction or attention, whether that's the friction of forced physical proximity, the imposition of unrequested conversation, or the social attention drawn by boasting or visibly standing out. It can read, to visitors from more expressive or dense cultures, as standoffish. It's better understood as an entire public behaviour system built around a specific value: minimising any unnecessary impact on strangers, at almost any cost to spontaneous social interaction.

## The Verdict

Vietnam's public behaviour asks you to trust the chaos and move with it — dense, loud, negotiated, and functioning entirely on collective confidence rather than formal rules. Norway's asks you to minimise your footprint entirely — quiet, spaced, and functioning on a shared, almost instinctive agreement to never impose. Both systems produce genuinely low rates of public conflict, remarkably, despite looking like polar opposites on paper.

I find Vietnam's version more alive and Norway's version more restful, and I refuse to pick a winner, because they're not actually solving the same problem. Vietnam is managing density. Norway is managing space it has in genuine abundance. Different problems, different — equally valid — solutions.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Vietnam — a new arrival asking if it's normal to feel a jolt of panic every time they cross the street, and the top reply: "the panic goes away in about two weeks, trust the flow, don't stop."*

> *Reddit r/Norway — an expat asking why a Norwegian colleague seemed uncomfortable being complimented on a promotion, with the top comment explaining "Jantelov, downplaying is the polite response here, not modesty theatre, genuine instinct."*

> *Internations Hanoi — a long thread on adjusting to constant ambient noise, with one poster admitting "I now can't sleep in silence anymore, it's the opposite problem I expected to have."*

## Conclusion

Vietnam and Norway sit at true opposite poles of public behaviour, and both, remarkably, function beautifully on their own terms. Vietnam's crowded, noisy, trust-based negotiation of shared space produces a public life that feels alive at every hour. Norway's quiet, spaced, attention-averse restraint produces a public life that feels almost meditative by comparison. Neither culture would function for a week inside the other's norms — a Norwegian would find Hanoi's traffic genuinely alarming, a Hanoian would find Oslo's empty bus rows faintly absurd. Go to Vietnam to relearn how to trust a crowd. Go to Norway to relearn how to enjoy not being noticed.
