# One Country Will Never Let You See Them Angry, the Other Will Never Let You See Them at All

🇰🇭 Cambodia 🇳🇴 Norway
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Cambodian public life is governed by an unshakeable commitment to keeping the peace on the surface, whatever's happening underneath. Raise your voice, show open frustration, or cause someone public embarrassment, and you haven't won the argument, you've lost the room entirely. Face, for everyone involved, matters more than being right.

Norwegians, meanwhile, have built an entire public behaviour code around a simpler premise: just don't. Don't make eye contact on the bus. Don't sit next to a stranger if an empty row exists. Don't, under any circumstances, start a conversation with someone waiting for a tram. It's not hostility, it's a profound, near-religious respect for other people's personal bubble, defended at all costs, including basic acknowledgment that you exist.

## Do's & Don'ts: Cambodia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stay calm and smile even in frustrating situations; it defuses rather than escalates | Raise your voice in public disputes — it causes both parties to lose face |
| Address elders and authority figures with visible respect and softened language | Point your feet at people or Buddha images; it's genuinely offensive |
| Accept that "yes" sometimes means "I heard you," not necessarily agreement | Publicly correct or embarrass someone, even gently; it's deeply damaging socially |
| Remove shoes when entering homes and many businesses without being asked | Touch someone's head, even a child's; it's considered spiritually inappropriate |

## Do's & Don'ts: Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Respect the unwritten rule of personal space on public transport — spread out, don't cluster | Sit directly next to a stranger on an empty bus; it will register as odd, even alarming |
| Queue properly and silently; the system is trusted and enforced socially | Make small talk with strangers in lifts, queues, or transit; it's rarely welcomed |
| Let silence sit comfortably in conversation; it's not awkward, it's normal | Boast or stand out deliberately; "janteloven" discourages open self-promotion |
| Be punctual, precisely, for everything, social or professional | Interrupt or talk over someone; turn-taking in conversation is taken seriously |

## Cambodia: Harmony as the Highest Public Virtue

Public behaviour in Cambodia is shaped overwhelmingly by the concept of "face," a social currency that governs nearly every interaction outside the home. Losing face, or causing someone else to lose it, is treated as a genuine social injury, and Cambodians organise an enormous amount of daily behaviour around avoiding exactly that, for themselves and for everyone around them.

This produces a public culture that can look, to outsiders raised on more confrontational norms, almost impossibly calm. Disputes that would trigger raised voices elsewhere get handled through soft language, indirect suggestion, and a great deal of smiling that isn't insincerity, it's active social management. A Cambodian who seems to agree with you may simply be avoiding the loss of face that open disagreement would cause both of you; reading actual consent requires attention to context, tone, and what's left unsaid rather than the words themselves.

Buddhist influence runs through nearly all of this. The head is considered the most spiritually significant part of the body and shouldn't be touched, even affectionately, while feet are the lowest and shouldn't be pointed at people, religious images, or anything deserving respect. These aren't quaint customs, they're actively, consistently observed, and violating them, even accidentally, registers as a real breach rather than a forgivable foreign misstep.

What's easy to miss beneath the surface calm is how much genuine warmth exists once you're inside the circle of trust. Cambodian hospitality, once extended, is generous and heartfelt; the reserve in public isn't coldness, it's the maintenance of a shared social peace that visitors are expected to respect and, ideally, learn to perform themselves, however imperfectly, smile included.

## Norway: Silence as a Form of Respect

Norwegian public behaviour is often summarised, slightly reductively but not inaccurately, by the concept of "janteloven," the informal Nordic social code that discourages standing out, boasting, or drawing attention to oneself above the collective. In practice, this manifests as a public culture built almost entirely around not imposing on others: minimal eye contact, minimal small talk, and an almost sacred respect for personal space that foreigners frequently misread as unfriendliness.

The bus is the classic proving ground. An empty Norwegian bus, boarded by two unrelated strangers, will typically result in each taking their own row, spread as far apart as the vehicle allows, not out of rudeness but out of genuine consideration: why would you sit next to someone when space exists not to? Sitting directly beside a stranger with empty seats available can actually register as socially strange, even mildly concerning.

Silence itself is treated completely differently than in more conversation-hungry cultures. A comfortable silence between Norwegians, in conversation, on transit, at a dinner table, isn't a gap that needs filling, it's simply part of the exchange, and the compulsion some visitors feel to fill every pause with chatter can come across as slightly overwhelming, even intrusive.

None of this reflects coldness once you understand the underlying logic: not imposing on someone is, in Norwegian social terms, a form of active respect, not withdrawal. Norwegians are famously warm and generous with people they actually know, the reserve is purely a public-space phenomenon, a way of extending everyone, stranger and friend alike, maximum autonomy and minimum intrusion, which, once you stop expecting a smile from the person next to you on the tram, is actually rather restful.

## The Verdict

Cambodia's public culture is built on managing collective harmony actively, moment to moment, through warmth, deference, and careful face-saving. Norway's is built on managing collective comfort through distance, silence, and the fierce protection of personal space. Cambodia will teach you to read a room with extraordinary subtlety. Norway will teach you to stop needing the room to acknowledge you at all. Both are, in their own ways, deeply considerate cultures; they've simply arrived at opposite conclusions about what consideration actually requires.

## Conclusion

Cambodia and Norway sit at genuinely opposite ends of the public behaviour spectrum, and both will initially confuse visitors for exactly the same underlying reason: unfamiliarity with what respect actually looks like when it isn't performed the way you expect. Cambodia performs respect through warmth, softness, and relentless face-saving. Norway performs it through space, silence, and a fierce commitment to not being a bother. Learn to read either system properly and you'll walk away with a genuinely useful skill: understanding that public behaviour is never really about rules, it's about what a culture has quietly decided counts as consideration.
