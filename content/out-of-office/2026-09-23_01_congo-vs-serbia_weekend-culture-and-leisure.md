# The River Wins Either Way, But Only One Country Puts a DJ On It

🇨🇬 Congo · 🇷🇸 Serbia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two nations, two rivers, two entirely different theories of how a weekend should sound. Brazzaville sits across the Congo River from Kinshasa and treats Saturday like a slow-building fête that starts with church and ends somewhere near dawn with someone's cousin running the sound system off a car battery. Belgrade, meanwhile, has turned the Sava and Danube confluence into a floating nightclub archipelago and calls it "splavovi" as if that word alone excuses the noise complaints.

I have spent weekends in both cities wondering whether the concept of "rest" ever made it into either country's dictionary, and I have concluded: it did not, and thank god for that.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Congo**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress up properly for Sunday church — it's a social event, not an afterthought | Assume the day ends when the service does; it's the opening act |
| Join a neighbourhood football match if invited — it's the real social currency | Expect punctuality; "weekend time" stretches like taffy |
| Try the riverside grilled fish stalls on Saturday afternoon | Turn down an invitation to a family gathering — it's rarely optional |

**Serbia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book a splav (river barge club) table in advance for Friday | Expect anything to start before 11pm and mean it |
| Sit in a kafana for three hours over one coffee — that's the point | Rush anyone through a toast; it's basically a small ceremony |
| Take the obligatory Sunday countryside drive if a local invites you | Confuse a kafana with a bar; the etiquette is entirely different |

## Congo: Church, Then Chaos, In That Order

Brazzaville does not do quiet weekends, and it does not pretend to. The rhythm starts early Sunday morning, when entire families turn out for church in outfits that would embarrass most wedding guests elsewhere — tailored suits, elaborate headwraps, shoes shined to a genuine hazard. This is not incidental to the culture; it is the culture. The Congolese have never separated devotion from display, and a Sunday service functions as much as a social registry as a religious one. You see who's dating whom, who's back from Paris, who's doing well enough to buy a new car.

What foreigners consistently underestimate is what happens after. The sapeurs — Brazzaville and Kinshasa's famously dandyish subculture of men who dress in outrageous, meticulously coordinated designer outfits purely for the pleasure of being seen — treat weekend afternoons as a runway. It is genuinely one of the most delightful things I have witnessed anywhere: grown men in three-piece suits the colour of tropical fruit, strutting down unpaved streets with the gravity of a Milan show, entirely unbothered by the potholes.

Saturday itself belongs to informal economy and informal sport in equal measure. Riverside stalls sell grilled fish and cassava leaf stew from mid-morning, and by early afternoon every patch of open ground has become a football pitch, played in flip-flops with a startling level of skill. Nightlife arrives late and loud — Brazzaville's nganda bars operate on a schedule that assumes nobody has anywhere to be the next morning, which, to be fair, on a Sunday, is usually correct.

What Congo does not offer is convenience. Public transport is unreliable enough that "weekend plans" often mean walking distances that would make a Londoner file a complaint. But the payoff is a kind of leisure that's deeply, insistently communal — nobody weekends alone in Brazzaville if they can possibly help it, and the idea of a solo Saturday would strike most locals as faintly tragic.

## Serbia: The Kafana Never Blinks

Belgrade's weekend architecture is built on two structures that seem, at first, contradictory: the kafana, a slow, smoke-stained, deeply ritualised tavern culture that dates back centuries, and the splav, a modern floating nightclub scene that makes Ibiza look restrained. Serbians move between the two without apparent cognitive dissonance, which tells you something about the national tolerance for contrast.

The kafana is not a bar. Do not treat it like one. You sit, you order slowly, someone eventually produces a bottle of rakija you did not technically order, and the afternoon disappears into a haze of toasts, live accordion or tamburitza music, and conversations that loop back on themselves for hours. Rushing a kafana session is considered, at minimum, rude, and at worst, a small act of cultural vandalism. I once tried to leave after ninety minutes and was informed, not unkindly, that I had "barely arrived."

Then the sun sets and Belgrade transforms. The splavovi — barges moored along the Sava — turn into the kind of nightlife that starts at midnight and treats 4am as early. Weekend crowds are enormous, genuinely international, and startlingly undeterred by the fact that most of these boats look structurally questionable. Nobody in Belgrade seems concerned. I found this either admirable or alarming, depending on the night.

Sunday, by contrast, is when Serbia performs its plot twist: everyone disappears to the countryside. A drive to a village house, a slow lunch of roast meat and ajvar that takes three hours to finish, an afternoon nap under a walnut tree — this is the counterbalance to Friday and Saturday's chaos, and Serbians guard it fiercely. Do not schedule anything with a Belgrade local on a Sunday afternoon. You will lose.

## The Verdict

Congo wins on sheer communal warmth — nobody weekends alone, everybody is fed, and the sapeurs alone are worth the plane ticket. But Serbia wins on structural genius: a culture that has engineered three completely different registers of leisure — ritual, riot, and retreat — into one 48-hour cycle, and expects you to keep up with all three without complaint. If forced to choose, I'm picking Belgrade, purely because I respect a nation that can go from a barge rave to a walnut tree nap in under twelve hours and never once look tired doing it.

## Conclusion

Neither city will let you rest, not really, but both will convince you that resting was never really the point. Congo hands you community whether you asked for it or not; Serbia hands you ritual and expects you to honour it properly, toast by toast, barge by barge. What they share is a flat refusal to treat the weekend as recovery time from the week — it's the main event, the week is the interruption. Pack accordingly, and for God's sake, don't book a Monday morning flight out of either place. You won't make it, and somewhere deep down, you won't actually mind.
