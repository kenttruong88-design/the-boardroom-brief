# Singapore Prices Its Lettuce by the Gram. Indonesia Sells You Durian in Three Layers of Plastic

🇮🇩 Indonesia vs 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

An hour's ferry separates these two countries, and about four decades of retail philosophy. Singapore sells groceries as an optimisation problem: tiered supermarkets, labelled hierarchies, delivery thresholds, and a social ladder that runs from wet market to the gleaming chiller aisles of the premium chains. Indonesia sells groceries as a negotiation between three simultaneous economies, and you are expected to know which one you are in.

The expat who moves from one to the other gets whiplash. Singapore will tell you, to the cent, that you overpaid for your butter. Indonesia will let you overpay for your butter in cheerful ignorance, then deliver the rest of your shopping within two hours to a door you did not know it could find. Neither is wrong. Both are exhausting in their own register.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Indonesia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a reusable bag, plastic is charged or banned in places | Expect a free carrier at Jakarta or Bali stores |
| Use delivery apps for the heavy shop | Haul bottled water home on a motorbike |
| Carry cash for small shops and traditional markets | Assume everywhere takes a card |
| Learn which chain stocks your imported staples | Pay hypermarket prices for expat-store items |

**Singapore**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Compare unit prices before you commit | Trust the shelf price without reading the per-100g tag |
| Shop wet markets for fresh produce and fish | Buy all your veg at the premium tier |
| Plan your weekly shop around delivery thresholds | Order a single carton of milk online |
| Try the cheaper chains, they are not scary | Equate price with quality |

## Indonesia

Indonesian grocery shopping happens on three levels at once. At the bottom sit the traditional markets and the small warung, which run on cash, haggling and the unspoken knowledge that today's price depends on who you are. In the middle sit the convenience chains Indomaret and Alfamart, which seem to occupy every corner of the archipelago and have made the idea of walking more than three hundred metres for a snack medically unnecessary. At the top sit the expat-friendly names, among them Hero, Ranch Market, Food Hall and Farmers Market, plus hypermarkets such as Transmart Carrefour and Lotte Mart for people who like their shopping with a car park.

The practical rules are charming. Jakarta and Bali have restricted single-use plastic bags, so you either pay or bring your own. Many stores run a bag-deposit counter where you hand over your possessions in exchange for a numbered tag, as though shopping were a day at the pool. And delivery is superb: apps such as GoMart and GrabMart typically turn up within an hour or two, which makes the Indonesian supermarket a kind of showroom for people who never carry anything.

Where it bites is imports. One long-term expat writer described a friend who bought a tray of durian from a Jakarta supermarket, watched staff wrap it three times, and then had to eat it outdoors because her husband could not tolerate the smell. That is Indonesia in one anecdote: the supermarket will bend over backwards to accommodate the fruit, and the household will still lose. Imported fruit, spices and cheese are expensive and unpredictable, which is why Indonesians abroad hunt for sambal in suitcases.

Price anxiety is mostly a middle-class game in Indonesia, because the same shopper can drift between a warung, a convenience store and a hypermarket in a single morning without changing mood. The upshot is that the household budget is spread across several kinds of shop, each with its own cash habits and its own mental ledger. Cards and e-wallets are accepted in modern stores, but smaller shops and traditional markets still prefer cash, so the sensible expat carries both and learns which pocket pays for which kind of mistake. It is less efficient than Singapore and, if you enjoy shopping, considerably more fun.

## Singapore

Singapore's expat association guide ranks the supermarkets like a school year. Marketplace and Cold Storage sit at the top as the priciest, FairPrice Finest and Xtra are the middle class, and Giant and ordinary FairPrice follow. The cheapest, reported to be Sheng Siong, comes with a footnote from the author that she never actually shops there, which tells you everything about Singapore's relationship with price and status.

The guide's real revelation is that premium stores can quote produce per 100 grams rather than per kilo, so a lettuce that looks like a snip becomes ten dollars at the till. This is not fraud. It is typography. Singapore will never lie to you about the price; it will merely present it in the unit that makes you feel smartest for not reading it.

The practical sensible expat strategy, repeated across an anonymous Talesmag Q&A that runs from 2008 to 2022, is to ignore the premium chains. One poster keeps costs down with Sheng Siong, Giant and local grocers and orders online through RedMart. Another credits Hyper FairPrice, Giant, Mustafa and the wet markets for making food affordable. An older reply noted that American brands cost around thirty percent more than at home, which is the moral equivalent of a tax on nostalgia.

The ANZA author rotates between Cold Storage and FairPrice Finest depending on her list, and on whether durian is on sale. She also notes that her local Finest stocks only iceberg lettuce, so a salad requires a second stop. That is Singapore too: first-world retail with a thin spot exactly where you were counting on it.

The Singapore system also rewards homework. The older delivery guide I read lists free click-and-collect at modest minimum spends, delivery thresholds that vary by chain, and a free-delivery bar of about a hundred dollars at the cheapest chain. Those figures date from 2018 and are surely stale, but the lesson survives: in Singapore, the way you shop is as much a price decision as what you buy. Plan the basket, hit the threshold, and use the wet market for what the chains cannot do. The system is generous to people who plan and quietly punitive to people who do not.

## The Verdict

Singapore is better organised. Indonesia is more fun. If you want certainty, take the island. If you want to learn how a society actually eats, take the archipelago and keep a spare phone for the delivery rider.

I will give the point to Singapore for one reason: a Singaporean shopper can predict the price of dinner, and a Jakarta one cannot. That predictability is worth real money to a family on a corporate package and even more to anybody newly arrived. The tiered supermarkets, the transparent pricing, the dependable fresh produce and the wet markets for everything the chains cannot do make Singapore the easier home.

But I would not trade Indonesia's corner convenience stores, its two-hour delivery or the sheer abundance of the market stall for all the tidy hierarchy in the world. Singapore has the better system. Indonesia has the better stories. Fairness requires admitting that stories do not go in a fridge.

## Conclusion

The grocery aisle is where an expat's fantasies meet the budget. In Singapore you will be told the truth about your spending, in the smallest possible print. In Indonesia you will be quoted three prices, depending on where and how you ask.

Learn the tiers. Learn the apps. Carry your own bag in Jakarta, carry a calculator in Singapore, and do not buy lettuce anywhere that quotes it per hundred grams without a sit-down first.

Whichever country you choose, the lesson is the same: the cheapest shop is the one you do not make on impulse, and the best meal is whichever one arrives with a good story attached.
