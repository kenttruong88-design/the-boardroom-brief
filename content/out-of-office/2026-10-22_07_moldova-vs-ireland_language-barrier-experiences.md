# The Language You Don't Speak Will Never Embarrass You As Much As The One You Do

🇲🇩 Moldova vs 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Everyone prepares for Moldova like it's a Cyrillic ambush and arrives to find half the country speaking a Latin-alphabet Romanian that looks suspiciously like Italian's poorer, more put-upon cousin, and the other half speaking Russian with the casual confidence of people who have been politely ignoring a border dispute for thirty years. You will guess wrong about which one to use at least four times a day, and nobody will tell you that's the actual local sport.

Ireland, meanwhile, gets filed under "doesn't count" by every lazy traveller who assumes a shared language means zero friction, which is the single most expensive assumption you can make before a Dubliner asks if you're "sound" and you panic because you genuinely do not know if that's a compliment, a threat, or a question about your hearing. English-speaking countries are not a barrier-free holiday from language barriers. They are language barriers wearing a very convincing disguise.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Moldova:**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Open with a neutral "Bună" and let the reply tell you which language to switch to | Assume Russian is universally welcome, or universally rejected — both are wrong in different rooms |
| Learn that "Moldovan" is politically retired; the constitution now says Romanian | Call the language "Moldovan" to anyone under 40 in Chișinău and expect a lecture |
| Carry both a Romanian and a Russian phrase list if you're heading to Gagauzia or near Transnistria | Expect English to bail you out in rural villages — it mostly won't |
| Treat language choice as geography, not loyalty, and move on | Turn a waiter's language preference into a referendum on Moldovan identity — it's rude and it's exhausting for them |

**Ireland:**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept that "grand" can mean fine, terrible, or "I have given up on this conversation" | Assume fluent English means you'll understand the first sentence spoken to you in Cork or Kerry |
| Ask people to repeat slang once, cheerfully — Irish people enjoy explaining "the craic" | Nod along to avoid looking lost; you will agree to something you didn't understand |
| Notice that Irish (Gaeilge) is the first official language on every sign, even if you rarely hear it spoken | Assume "Do you have Irish?" is small talk — it's a real, loaded cultural question |
| Watch for Hiberno-English grammar like "I'm after eating" (I just ate) | Correct someone's English — it isn't broken, it's a dialect with four hundred years of history behind it |

## Moldova

Moldova's language situation isn't a barrier so much as a constant, low-grade multiple-choice exam you didn't study for. The constitutional headline, as of the 2023 parliamentary vote covered by ConstitutionNet, is that the country's official language is now explicitly named Romanian, not the Soviet-era invention "Moldovan" — a distinction that sounds pedantic until you watch how much it still matters to the person you just said it to. Say the wrong word to the wrong generation and you'll get a short, pointed history lesson you did not request.

Underneath that headline, Russian hasn't gone anywhere. It remains the default in large stretches of Chișinău's older population, in business and services generally, and is simply the operating language of Gagauzia, the autonomous south where Russian functions as the practical lingua franca between Gagauz and Moldovan speakers, and of breakaway Transnistria, which doesn't recognise Chișinău's language policy at all. Global Voices' interview with novelist Iulian Ciocan nails the nuance tourists flatten into nothing: plenty of Moldovans speak Russian fluently and comfortably while actively rejecting the "Russian world" narrative Moscow would like that fact to imply. Language and loyalty are not the same axis, and assuming they are is the fastest way to say something clumsy at a dinner table.

For the visitor, this means there is no single safe script. You cannot default to Russian the way you might in, say, Central Asia, and you cannot default to Romanian the way you might assume from a map. You learn, fast, to read a room — a market stall, a shared taxi, a pharmacy counter — and pick based on cues: age, neighbourhood, the first word someone uses back at you. English barely registers outside the capital's younger crowd and a handful of hospitality staff, so a phrase list in both official-adjacent languages isn't optional charm, it's infrastructure. The upside, and there is one, is that Moldovans are almost universally forgiving of a foreigner's clumsy guess, because everyone there has spent their whole life guessing too.

## Ireland

Ireland's trick is that it doesn't feel like a language barrier until you're already three sentences deep and quietly drowning. On paper, per the Irish constitution, Irish (Gaeilge) is the first official language and English the second, which is a lovely piece of civic trivia that will not help you at all, because CSO census data has shown for decades that well under a tenth of the population speaks Irish daily outside the education system, concentrated mostly in small Gaeltacht pockets. English is what you'll actually hear everywhere — the catch is that it's Hiberno-English, a dialect with its own grammar, its own idiom, and a regional accent spread so wide that Dublin, Cork, Kerry, and Donegal can sound like four different countries having a mild disagreement.

Study in Ireland's own material for international students puts it plainly: Hiberno-English is "a challenge" precisely because learners arrive expecting standard British or American English and instead get syntax borrowed directly from Irish — "I'm after eating my dinner" doesn't mean you're hungry, it means you've already eaten, and good luck deducing that from a textbook. Add a vocabulary layer of "craic," "grand," "yer man," "deadly," and "yoke" (a word for literally any object you can't immediately name), and you have a dialect that technically ticks the "English-speaking country" box while functionally requiring its own onboarding period.

The cruelest part is the social cost of admitting confusion. Nobody mocks you outright for bad Romanian in Moldova — it's expected. But confess you didn't understand a sentence in Ireland, where everyone insists you're both speaking English, and you risk the conversational equivalent of being told the joke slower, which is somehow worse than not getting it at all.

## The Verdict

Moldova wins on honesty. It tells you upfront, through Cyrillic and Latin script sitting on the same street sign, that you are entering a genuine multilingual negotiation, and it never pretends otherwise. You arrive braced, you guess wrong sometimes, and the forgiveness is built into the system because everyone there is guessing too.

Ireland wins on sheer audacity. It markets itself globally as the easy, friendly, English-speaking entry point to Europe, and then quietly makes you decode a dialect with its own grammar, its own slang dictionary, and four distinct regional accents, while insisting the whole time that nothing is different. That's not a language barrier. That's a language barrier wearing camouflage, and camouflage is always more dangerous than an open fence.

If you want a barrier you can see coming, go to Moldova. If you want one that ambushes you mid-sentence while you're nodding along politely at a pub, go to Ireland. Only one of these countries will let you leave with your dignity fully intact, and it is not the one with the shamrocks.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Moldova — paraphrased: newcomers describe defaulting to Russian out of habit, then getting a cold shoulder from younger Romanian-speaking locals who read the choice as politically loaded rather than merely practical.*

> *expat.com Moldova forum — paraphrased: long-term expats warn that English gets you through a hotel lobby in Chișinău and nowhere else, and that learning even basic Romanian changes how fast landlords and officials actually help you.*

## Conclusion

Here is the thing nobody puts on the tourist board copy: the language barrier you can see is always easier than the one disguised as a shared vocabulary. Moldova hands you an honest, visible puzzle — two scripts, two languages, a clear signal to read the room before you open your mouth. Ireland hands you a trap dressed as a shortcut, English that isn't quite English, grammar borrowed from a language most of the country doesn't speak, and a social contract that punishes you for admitting confusion. Both will humble you. Only one will let you see it coming. Pack a phrase list for Chișinău and pack your patience for Cork, because you'll need completely different survival kits for countries that, on paper, couldn't look more different and, in practice, will embarrass you in exactly the same way: by assuming you understood when you very much did not.
