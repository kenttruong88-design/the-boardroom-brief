# One Country Will Stand So Close You Can Smell Its Lunch. The Other Will Form A Perfect Queue And Silently Judge Anyone Who Breaks It.

🇪🇬 Egypt vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nothing exposes a culture's unwritten rules faster than watching what happens the moment more than three strangers need to share a small space. Some countries have turned queuing into something close to a national virtue, policed by nothing but mutual disapproval. Others have never really bought into the queue as a concept at all, preferring a more fluid, contact-heavy approach to who gets served next.

Egypt and Australia sit at genuinely opposite poles here, and having been gently, repeatedly reshuffled in a Cairo crowd and just as gently, repeatedly tutted at in a Melbourne tram queue, I feel qualified to report on both.

## Do's & Don'ts: Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect closer physical proximity in queues, markets, and transport than you're likely used to | Interpret close proximity or being "cut in front of" as personal rudeness; it rarely is |
| Accept that raised voices in markets or traffic are normal volume, not a sign of conflict | Mistake a loud, animated conversation between friends for an argument requiring intervention |
| Lean into small talk with shopkeepers and taxi drivers; distance is not the social default here | Expect strict, silent, single-file queue discipline at a bus stop or ticket counter |
| Adjust your expectations around unsolicited advice or opinions from near-strangers | Be shocked when a shared taxi fills every seat before anyone considers it "full" |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Queue properly, in order, and wait your turn without crowding the person ahead of you | Cut a queue, even "just to ask a quick question"; it will be noticed and silently resented |
| Keep voices down in public transport, waiting rooms, and most indoor public spaces | Have a loud phone conversation on a quiet train carriage; expect dirty looks, not comments |
| Respect roughly an arm's length of personal space in casual conversation | Stand closer than that to a stranger, even briefly, without a very good reason |
| Say "sorry" reflexively, even when someone else bumps into you. It's just a verbal tic here | Expect that reflexive "sorry" to mean an actual apology is owed or required |

## Egypt: Proximity Is Just How a Crowded, Warm Country Talks

Egypt's approach to personal space and public behaviour makes considerably more sense once you understand it as a function of density, warmth, and an entirely different cultural default around boundaries. Cairo is one of the most densely packed urban environments on the planet, and that density has shaped a public culture where physical closeness in queues, on microbuses, and in markets is simply the operating normal rather than an imposition. A microbus will be considered "full" at a passenger count that would make a Melbourne commuter write a strongly worded letter.

Volume follows the same logic. Conversations that sound, to a newly arrived outsider, like the opening stages of a serious argument are frequently just two friends discussing dinner plans with appropriate Cairo-standard enthusiasm. The social default leans toward engagement rather than avoidance — strangers will offer opinions on your purchase, your plans, your general life choices, with a warmth that can read as intrusive before it reads as generous, which, more often than not, it genuinely is.

There is a more serious edge to this conversation that deserves honesty rather than a cheerful travel-blog gloss: commentary on crowding and personal space in Egypt has, in more serious public discourse, extended into discussions of harassment, particularly affecting women in crowded public spaces, a problem Egyptian civil society groups have organised seriously to address in recent years, including monitoring efforts in dense areas and public campaigns shifting blame away from victims. That is not the same conversation as a crowded bus queue, and conflating "warm, close, chatty" with "anything goes" does real disservice to a culture that is, in most everyday contexts, simply operating on a different, perfectly coherent set of social defaults.

## Australia: The Queue Is Sacred, the Volume Knob Stays Low

Australia runs on the opposite set of instincts entirely. Queuing is treated with something approaching moral seriousness — line up, wait your turn, and do not even consider asking "just a quick question" at the front, because it will be noticed, silently resented, and possibly commented on later to a friend, though never to your face. The arm's-length personal space rule applies with remarkable consistency across contexts, from a bus stop to a work function, and closing that gap without clear social permission reads as an actual transgression rather than a cultural quirk.

Public volume control follows the same logic. Loud phone conversations on trains, raised voices in waiting rooms, and generally any noise above a measured indoor speaking register will earn you looks rather than words — Australians are famously disinclined to confront directly, preferring passive disapproval delivered with Olympic-level precision. The reflexive "sorry," uttered constantly, even when someone else does the bumping, functions less as an actual apology and more as a social lubricant, a verbal tic that smooths over the mildest of frictions without requiring either party to actually process what happened.

What's genuinely interesting, once you've lived inside it a while, is how much emotional labour the system quietly outsources to self-restraint rather than open conflict. Nobody shouts at a queue-jumper. Somebody simply remembers it, mentions it to a friend later with impressive specificity, and moves on having registered a tiny, permanent demerit against that person's character. It is, in its own understated way, just as socially intense as anywhere louder. It just happens entirely below the surface.

Traffic noise offers a particularly vivid version of this same contrast. In Egypt, honking functions as an entire secondary language — a quick tap to say "I'm here," a longer blast to say "move," a rhythmic pattern among friends that functions as a greeting, all woven into traffic noise that would read, to an unfamiliar visitor, as constant low-grade road rage, when it is in fact closer to constant low-grade conversation. In Australia, by contrast, honking carries real social weight precisely because it's used so rarely — a single horn blast is treated as a notable event, closer to an actual rebuke than routine communication, and using it liberally the way Cairo traffic does would mark you out as either dangerously aggressive or wildly unfamiliar with local norms. Both systems are internally consistent and perfectly legible to locals. It's only the newcomer, caught between two entirely different calibrations of what a car horn is supposed to mean, who ends up either flinching at nothing or missing something genuinely important.

## The Verdict

Egypt's public behaviour runs warm, close, loud, and socially engaged by default; Australia's runs reserved, spaced-out, quiet, and conflict-averse to a degree that borders on performance art. Neither is objectively ruder than the other — they're simply optimised for different things: Egypt for warmth and connection in genuine density, Australia for personal space and order in a country with plenty of both to spare.

I will say that adjusting from Cairo to Melbourne feels like someone turned the entire country's volume and proximity dial down by roughly seventy percent overnight, and adjusting back the other direction feels like being hugged by a city that didn't ask first. Both took getting used to. Neither was wrong.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat Exchange — a long-term Cairo resident's advice to new arrivals: stop interpreting proximity and unsolicited opinions as aggression, and start treating them as the local version of small talk, because that's genuinely what most of it is.*

> *Carnegie Mellon University Study Abroad Blog — a student's honest account of initial discomfort with Cairo crowding, followed by a genuine shift once local friends explained that the "pushing" at a ticket window isn't rudeness, it's just how a queue works when nobody trusts a line to hold its order.*

> *NPR, on cross-cultural personal space research — a recurring finding worth remembering before judging either country: comfortable interpersonal distance varies by culture far more than most people assume, and "too close" or "too cold" are almost always measured against your own default, not a universal standard.*

## Conclusion

Public behaviour norms are never really about politeness in the abstract; they're a culture's accumulated answer to its own density, climate, and history of trust between strangers. Egypt has built a public culture of warmth and proximity out of genuine urban density and strong social instincts toward engagement, alongside real, serious conversations about where that proximity crosses into harassment that deserve more attention, not less. Australia has built a public culture of space and quiet restraint out of relative abundance and a deep cultural aversion to direct confrontation. Both work, on their own terms, for the societies that built them. Neither translates cleanly to the other, and pretending otherwise is how you end up standing far too close to a stranger in Melbourne, or far too far from one in Cairo, wondering why everyone seems faintly unimpressed with you.
