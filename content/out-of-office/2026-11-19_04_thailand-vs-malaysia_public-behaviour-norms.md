# Thailand Wants You Calm and Barefoot, Malaysia Wants You Right-Handed and Reasonable

🇹🇭 Thailand · 🇲🇾 Malaysia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have relocated fourteen times and the single most reliable predictor of a happy arrival is whether you have learned to behave before you have learned to speak. Southeast Asia is where this lesson comes with a smile that conceals a very clear list of things you must not do. Thailand and Malaysia sit side by side on the map and share a talent for gentle indirectness, and both will forgive an enormous amount, right up until you point your foot at the wrong object.

The rules are different, though, and the differences are worth the effort. Thailand runs on a philosophy of the cool heart, jai yen, in which the loudest person in the room has already lost. Malaysia runs on three cultures at once, Malay, Chinese and Indian, which means the etiquette changes depending on whose house you are standing in and what festival is about to begin. One country asks you to be serene. The other asks you to be adaptable. Neither is a trick, and neither will forgive you for forgetting.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇹🇭 Thailand**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Cover shoulders and knees and remove shoes at temples | Walk into a wat dressed for the beach |
| Use the wai, and if in doubt offer a smile and a nod | Touch anyone's head, children included |
| Tuck your feet away when seated | Point your feet at people or Buddha images |
| Stay calm; keep your voice level in disputes | Criticise the royal family or ignore the royal anthem |

**🇲🇾 Malaysia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use your right hand to eat or pass things | Use your left hand to eat or give a gift |
| Point with the thumb | Point with the index finger |
| Remove shoes before entering homes and some businesses | Barge into a Muslim home at Hari Raya in outdoor shoes |
| Dress modestly at religious sites and formal events | Eat, drink or smoke in public during Ramadan fasting hours |

## 🇹🇭 Thailand: The Cult of the Cool Heart

Thailand's public manners are an advanced system disguised as hospitality. Begin with the body: the head is sacred, the feet are not. Do not touch anyone's head, children's included, and do not point your feet at a person or at a Buddha image. When sitting, tuck them out of the way. These are not quaint suggestions, they are the sort of rules that make a room go quiet if broken. At a temple, shoulders and knees are covered and shoes come off, and the same shoe rule applies in most homes and often in shops and guesthouses. A row of shoes at the door is the signal. Look for it.

Then there is the wai, the pressed-palms greeting that doubles as thanks and goodbye. The height of your hands and the depth of your bow depend on the other person's status, which is a lot to calculate while you are carrying luggage. If you are unsure, a smile and a nod is perfectly acceptable. Dining has its own choreography: spoon in the right hand, fork in the left to push food onto it, chopsticks mostly for noodles, and let the eldest start.

The deeper rule is emotional. Public anger is a social failure in Thailand, and the expat guides keep returning to it: stay calm, avoid raising your voice, keep hugging and kissing private, though hand-holding is generally fine. A Bangkok expat describes living through a coup and the Yellow and Red Shirt protests with little personal disruption, saying they worried more for Thailand than themselves, while another says they have learned to go with the flow, "mai pen rai", and no longer get easily upset. That is not resignation. It is a trained response to a society that prizes smoothness.

The sharpest line is the royal family. Criticising it is rude at best and can bring severe penalties at worst, and you should stand for the royal anthem before films and public events. I will not pretend to hand you a legal citation, because the sources I checked do not give one. I will simply tell you to treat the subject as closed. You are a guest in a house with one locked room.

## 🇲🇾 Malaysia: Three Cultures, One Right Hand

Malaysia is the harder country to read because it is not one etiquette but three layered on top of each other. Malay, Chinese and Indian traditions shape daily life, with Hari Raya, Chinese New Year and Deepavali setting the national calendar, and good manners shift with the room. The anchor rules are physical and therefore forgettable only once. Use the right hand to eat or pass things, because the left is reserved for hygiene. Point with the thumb rather than the index finger. Remove your shoes before entering homes and some businesses. Some Muslims prefer not to shake hands across the sexes, so let the other person lead.

Religion shapes public behaviour in a way that matters. Avoid eating, drinking or smoking in public during Ramadan fasting hours. At religious sites and formal events, women should wear long skirts or trousers with shoulders covered, and men long trousers and no sleeveless tops. Public displays of affection should be minimal, though hand-holding is generally fine. One expat on the faux-pas page says it more bluntly: speaking loudly or criticising another culture or religion is rude, dress conservatively, bring a small gift when invited to someone's home, and never use the left hand to eat or hand over a present.

Time works differently too. "Jam kelapa", coconut time, is the relaxed Malaysian approach to schedules, and it rewards patience and punishes the person who arrives on the dot with a spreadsheet. Neighbourly kindness is real: a Seremban 2 expat describes neighbours helping when you are sick or sad, then promptly complains that the dawn prayer chant wakes the street early. The same page grumbles that many drivers "do not even look at the lines in the road". It is a brisk reminder that polite does not mean tidy.

The point I would press is that Malaysia's etiquette is more portable than Thailand's. Once you have the right-hand, shoes-off, modest-dress triad, you can enter most rooms. Thailand demands a more intuitive mastery of social temperature, which is wonderful and exhausting in roughly equal measure.

## The Verdict

Malaysia wins for the newcomer, and Thailand wins for the connoisseur. I will take the former, because a country that writes its major rules on the body, right hand, shoes, thumb, is a country that teaches you quickly and corrects you gently. Malaysia's difficulty is complexity, not hostility. You are always asking whose house you are in.

Thailand, bless it, asks for something harder: emotional discipline. The Thai ideal of the cool heart is genuinely lovely, and genuinely demanding if your native register is sardonic. I have tried it. I lasted a week before sighing audibly in a queue and watching a stranger flinch. The Thai system rewards the person who has already absorbed its values. The Malaysian system rewards the person willing to learn them.

So: choose Malaysia if you want the rules written in checklists. Choose Thailand if you want to be quietly reshaped into a better person. My sting is that only one of those is optional, and I have yet to meet the expat who left Bangkok without either a calmer temperament or a very good story about why they didn't.

## Conclusion

Public behaviour is the one exam where nobody tells you the questions. In Thailand, the paper is about composure: head, feet, voice, royals. In Malaysia, it is about context: hand, shoes, dress, calendar. Both are generous graders and both keep score.

Learn the physical rules first, because bodies are easier to correct than temperaments. Then spend a year learning the rest. And if you see a row of shoes at a door, in either country, take yours off before anyone has to ask.
