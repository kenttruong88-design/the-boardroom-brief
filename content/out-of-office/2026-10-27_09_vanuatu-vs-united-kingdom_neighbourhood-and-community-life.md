# Nobody In Vanuatu Will Let You Starve Alone, And Nobody In Britain Will Let You Borrow A Hose

🇻🇺 Vanuatu vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have lived in fourteen countries, which means I have been "the new neighbour" fourteen times, and I can tell you with total confidence that the quality of that experience varies more wildly than almost anything else you will encounter abroad. Forget the food, forget the weather, forget whether the plug sockets are sensible — the thing that actually determines whether you'll be happy somewhere is whether the people next door will notice if you die.

In Vanuatu, someone will notice within the hour, bring you soup you didn't ask for, and expect you at the nakamal that evening regardless. In the United Kingdom, someone will notice roughly three weeks later, when the recycling bin hasn't moved and the postman mentions it to literally everyone except you. Both approaches have their charms. One of them is community. The other is a Richard Curtis film pretending to be community while actually being several hundred strangers politely avoiding eye contact over a shared fence.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Vanuatu**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up at the nakamal at dusk even if you don't drink kava, just to sit and be seen | Assume "custom land" means you can buy a nice plot and ignore the chief who actually controls it |
| Bring something — kava, food, labour — when you're invited anywhere, even casually | Expect privacy to be the default; your business is the village's business within a day |
| Learn who your extended "family" is in the community sense, not just blood | Rush a greeting; a hurried hello reads as rude, not efficient |
| Contribute to a wedding, funeral, or church roof fund when asked — it is not optional | Refuse a kastom obligation because you're "just visiting"; residency implies belonging |

**United Kingdom**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Nod and say "alright" to neighbours; this is the entire conversation and it is sufficient | Knock on someone's door without a genuinely urgent reason — it will be remembered forever |
| Join the street WhatsApp group; it is where actual community happens now | Mistake politeness for friendliness — they are different currencies entirely |
| Keep your front garden tidy; it is read as a moral statement about your character | Play music, mow the lawn, or exist loudly before 9am on a Sunday |
| Offer to help during an actual crisis — floods, fires, funerals — Britain mobilises instantly for these | Expect that goodwill to extend to ordinary Tuesdays |

## Vanuatu: Where Community Isn't A Choice, It's The Operating System

Vanuatu doesn't really have "neighbourhoods" in the Western sense of adjoining plots owned by unrelated people who nod at each other over bins. It has villages, and villages run on kastom — the body of customary law, obligation, and social choreography that predates the colonial carve-up and has quietly outlasted it. Roughly ninety percent of land in Vanuatu remains under customary ownership rather than private title, which the Vanuatu National Statistics Office documents in dry bureaucratic language, but which in practice means land is never just yours. It belongs to a lineage, a chief, a web of obligation that stretches sideways through cousins and backwards through ancestors. You don't buy into a street here. You marry into a community, and the community notices immediately if you try to skip the marrying part.

The nakamal — the communal meeting ground, usually also where kava is drunk at sunset — is the actual town square, parliament, and gossip mill rolled into one, and attendance is less an invitation than a quiet expectation. Miss too many evenings and people ask after you, which sounds sweet until you realise it's also a form of surveillance with excellent manners. Extended family isn't a nice idea in Vanuatu; it's an economic and emotional safety net with teeth. Contribute to the wrong funeral, skip the wrong wedding levy, and you haven't been rude — you've defaulted on a social loan everyone else is still paying into.

What foreigners romanticise as "island hospitality" is actually a far tougher system than anything in the glossy tourism-board copy about kastom as colourful heritage. It is mutual obligation, enforced socially rather than legally, and it is why nobody in a Vanuatu village starves quietly while their neighbours mind their own business. There is no such thing as minding your own business here. Your business is everyone's business, your harvest is partly everyone's harvest, and in exchange you are never, structurally, alone. It is suffocating by Western standards and also the most honest version of community I've encountered in sixty countries of eating other people's food.

## United Kingdom: Where Privacy Is The Highest Form Of Respect

The British have engineered an entire civilisation around the principle that the kindest thing you can do for another human being is leave them completely alone. The government's own Community Life Survey tracks exactly how well this is going, and the headline finding, year after year, is that most people can name their neighbours but wouldn't dream of actually asking them for anything — borrowing a cup of sugar is now a historical reenactment, not a real behaviour. The phrase "keep yourself to yourself" isn't an insult in Britain. It's a character reference.

This produces a strange, specific texture to British neighbourliness: enormous latent capacity for solidarity that only activates during genuine emergencies. Floods, fires, a death — the street mobilises with casseroles and rotas within hours, properly, generously, no fuss made about it. But on an ordinary Wednesday, that same street will let you move boxes in for a week without a single knock, because knocking implies you expect something, and expecting something is the real crime. Net curtains exist for a reason: to watch everything while appearing to watch nothing. It's surveillance with better manners than Vanuatu's, somehow, despite having less actual community underneath it.

The one place genuine neighbourhood community still visibly happens is, bizarrely, group chats — WhatsApp groups for the street, the stair, the close, humming with lost-cat alerts and bin-day reminders and increasingly furious debates about parking. It's community conducted at a safe digital remove, which tells you everything about modern Britain in one sentence. The data backs the vibe: surveys on community spirit keep finding that roughly three-quarters of people wouldn't call their neighbours friends. Pleasant, reserved acquaintances, yes. Friends, no. The semantic precision there is very British and, frankly, exhausting to live inside once you've experienced the alternative.

## The Verdict

Vanuatu wins, and it isn't close. Britain has built a beautiful, polite simulation of community — WhatsApp groups, net curtains, casserole rotas for emergencies only — but it is fundamentally an opt-in system, and most people quietly opt out of everything except the group chat. Vanuatu's version isn't opt-in at all. You are simply held, whether you like it or not, by a web of kastom obligation that feeds you, polices you, and never once asks if you'd rather be left alone. That's the whole difference: Britain respects your right to be lonely; Vanuatu doesn't believe loneliness should be an available option. One culture protects your privacy. The other protects you, full stop, privacy be damned. I know which one I'd want if my pipes burst at 2am in a country where nobody has my number. Britain will find out via the group chat eventually. Vanuatu would already be inside, fixing it, and telling three other villages about it by breakfast.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com Vanuatu forum, "Retiring in Vanuatu" thread — a long-term resident describes how quickly village obligations arrive once you settle, noting newcomers are expected to contribute to weddings, funerals, and church fundraising almost as soon as they're recognised as part of the community, custom land ownership or not.*

> *Expat Exchange, UK forum thread on the hardest parts of relocating — a contributor describes years of living on the same street without ever being invited inside a neighbour's home, calling British friendliness "a performance that never quite becomes a relationship."*

> *ExpatArrivals' United Kingdom culture guide — newcomers are warned that unsolicited visits, loud enthusiasm, or assuming familiarity too quickly will be read as intrusive, and that genuine closeness with British neighbours, if it comes at all, takes years rather than weeks.*

## Conclusion

Here's the sting, because you came for it: Britain has spent centuries perfecting the art of being surrounded by people while remaining utterly alone, and calling it good manners. Vanuatu never bothered engineering that particular tragedy, because kastom simply doesn't allow for it — you are accounted for, fed, and occasionally smothered, but never forgotten. I'm not romanticising poverty or pretending obligation doesn't chafe; it does, constantly, and Vanuatu's system has its own cruelties. But at least it's honest about what community actually costs, which is everything, sometimes, from everyone, always. Britain charges you nothing and gives you a group chat. Decide for yourself which is the better deal. I already have.
