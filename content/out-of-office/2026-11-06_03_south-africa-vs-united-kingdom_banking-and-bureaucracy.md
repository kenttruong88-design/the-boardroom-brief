🇿🇦 South Africa vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular kind of despair reserved for the moment you realise that opening a bank account requires proof of address, and proof of address requires a bank account, and nobody in either country has thought to fix this, possibly on purpose. South Africa and the United Kingdom have built two entirely different bureaucratic nightmares around the identical structural flaw, and the only real difference is which one is more likely to get you mugged while you wait.

InterNations' own data settles the first half of the argument before we even start: 68% of expats in South Africa find local bureaucracy difficult, against a 42% global average, while the UK's single best-performing category, in a survey where it ranked 41st of 46 countries overall, was administrative efficiency. Read that twice. The United Kingdom cannot keep its expats happy, fed, warm, or paid on time, but it will process your paperwork with real, if cold, precision. South Africa will not even pretend.

## Do's & Don'ts: South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get an employment contract and an employer letter sorted before you touch a bank branch — it's the real workaround for the proof-of-address catch-22 | Bring a P.O. Box as your proof of address; FICA rules explicitly reject it for foreign nationals |
| Expect your proof-of-residence document to be rejected if it's more than three months old, no exceptions, no appeals | Go to Home Affairs alone or unprepared for a long, exposed wait — bring water, patience, and ideally company |
| Budget months, not weeks, for a Temporary Resident Permit renewal | Assume a rejected permanent residence application comes with an appeal process — during Covid-era backlogs, it didn't |
| Ask around for which specific bank branch has a reputation for actually helping foreigners — FNB and Standard Bank get better forum reviews than most | Treat an informal "queue holder" as a scam; paying someone to hold your place at Home Affairs is a real, normalised workaround, not a con |

## Do's & Don'ts: United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get on the electoral roll and request a National Insurance number letter immediately — it can substitute as address proof when nothing else will | Expect a bank statement to count as address proof on day one; you need the account to get the statement, which you need to get the account |
| Try a digital challenger bank for faster onboarding before you fight the high-street banks | Assume a British partner's address alone will get you through the door; several applicants have been refused even with a partner's address on the lease |
| Accept that you will be credit-checked, and that you will fail it purely for having no UK credit history yet, through no fault of your own | Bring a DocuSigned tenancy agreement expecting it to be accepted; one bank specifically demanded an original, physically signed copy |
| Treat the admin itself as the one thing Britain will actually get right, eventually | Mistake bureaucratic efficiency for warmth; the paperwork moving fast does not mean the people behind the counter are glad to see you |

## South Africa: a system that is slow, dangerous, and still somehow improvisable

The Financial Intelligence Centre Act — FICA — governs who can open a South African bank account and how, and it is unforgiving on one specific point: proof of residence must not be older than three months, and a P.O. Box simply does not count, for citizens or foreign nationals alike. For a brand-new arrival with no lease history and no utility bill in their name yet, this is the first catch-22 of many, and forum veterans on Expat.com's Johannesburg thread agree on the actual workaround: a South African employment contract plus an employer letter will get you through most banks' doors even without a conventional lease. FNB comes out of these threads looking almost charming by comparison — one poster describes being personally introduced to the branch manager — while Absa's own published FICA guidance reads like a door specifically designed to be narrow.

Home Affairs, the government department responsible for IDs, passports, and visas, is where the real horror stories live, and Daily Maverick's reader-submitted "Hell Affairs" series reads less like bureaucratic inconvenience and more like a hazard report. Luis Dias, 77, was mugged outside a Home Affairs office with no police presence in sight while applying for his granddaughter's passport. Moira Dewil's son waited more than fifteen months for a reissued passport, during which his driver's licence, college enrolment, and visa status were all jeopardised — the application was ultimately rejected and had to be restarted from scratch. Robyn DeWet's family made six separate visits for a daughter's ID and five failed passport attempts, watched a newborn's mother and a wheelchair-bound elderly woman wait with no seating and no toilets provided, and eventually paid an informal "contact" to hold their place in the queue, a practice that is apparently normal enough to need no further explanation. Meanwhile a Filipino expat in Nelspruit describes a seven-month Temporary Resident Permit renewal odyssey — bounced between a local office, a visa-processing contractor, and a head-office call centre that kept telling him to "call back in two or three weeks" — before eventually threatening legal action simply to get the department to move. The permit did, eventually, arrive. Eventually is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

## United Kingdom: a system that is fast, cold, and structurally identical anyway

Here is the uncomfortable truth the InterNations ranking doesn't quite capture: Britain's bureaucracy is fast relative to South Africa's, but it runs on the exact same structural flaw. A UK bank wants a bank statement as proof of address. You cannot get a bank statement without an account. You cannot get an account without proof of address. Suits Me's own explainer of this problem calls it precisely what it is — a catch-22 — and layers a second obstacle on top that South Africa's FICA system doesn't impose in quite the same way: a UK credit check, which brand-new arrivals fail automatically, not because they're untrustworthy, but because they simply don't exist yet in the UK's financial memory.

Expat.com's Scotland forum thread reads like a genuinely absurd sequence of near-misses. A South African passport holder was refused an account despite listing his partner's address, because the tenancy was in his name only and she wasn't a UK resident or citizen. An American retiree had Bank of Scotland demand an originally, physically signed tenancy agreement — a DocuSigned copy wasn't acceptable — then had Santander approve an account in principle and then simply refuse to activate it without a UK visa or biometric residence permit, before being turned down outright by TSB and RBS in succession. One poster finally succeeded only after registering on the electoral roll and using a National Insurance number letter as substitute address proof — a genuinely useful, specific workaround buried thirty replies deep in a forum thread, which tells you everything about how undocumented this process officially is.

And yet, by its own expats' account, this is the system working at its best. InterNations' 2025 data has the UK falling to 41st of 46 countries overall, driven largely by cost of living and the housing crisis — but administrative efficiency was still, somehow, its strongest single category. Britain, in other words, is extremely good at bureaucracy and extremely bad at almost everything bureaucracy is supposed to make possible.

## The Verdict

South Africa's bureaucracy will cost you months, possibly your physical safety, and almost certainly a payment to an informal queue-holder whose job title does not officially exist. The United Kingdom's bureaucracy will cost you a UK credit history you cannot possibly have yet, a DocuSigned document a bank will reject on a technicality, and the quiet realisation that "efficient" and "functional for actual new arrivals" are not the same word. Neither system deserves your loyalty. But if forced to choose which paperwork nightmare I'd rather be trapped in, I'll take the one where the banks are at least occasionally, individually, humanly kind — South Africa's forum record has a branch manager personally walking a customer through the process. Britain's record has banks approving an account and then refusing, out of pure procedural spite, to switch it on.

## Conclusion

Both countries have built elaborate, self-defeating proof-of-address rituals around the same basic absurdity, and both expect you to simply absorb the cost of fixing it yourself — in time, in South Africa's case, or in a very specific type of procedural humiliation, in Britain's. The lesson for any corporate relocatee is identical either way: start the paperwork before you need it, assume the first answer is wrong, and keep the name of a good employer-letter template and a National Insurance number handy. Bureaucracy, in the end, doesn't care which hemisphere it's filed in.
