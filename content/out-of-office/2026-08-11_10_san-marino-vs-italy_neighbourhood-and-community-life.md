# San Marino Is So Small Everyone's Basically Neighbours By Default. Italy Turned Neighbourhood Rivalry Into An Art Form

🇸🇲 San Marino vs 🇮🇹 Italy — *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

San Marino, perched on a mountainside and entirely surrounded by Italy, is the world's fifth-smallest country and one of the oldest surviving republics on earth, with a population smaller than a mid-sized Italian secondary school. Community life here isn't a cultural feature so much as an unavoidable physical fact — everyone genuinely does know everyone, because there simply aren't enough people for anonymity to function as an option. Italy, the vastly larger nation wrapped entirely around it, has instead perfected something called campanilismo — literally, attachment to one's own bell tower — a fierce, often comedic sense of hyperlocal identity where the town two kilometres away is regarded with the kind of suspicion usually reserved for a rival nation.

Watching these two coexist, one nested entirely inside the other, is a genuinely instructive study in how differently "community" can function depending on scale. San Marino has community because it has no choice. Italy has community because, historically fragmented into city-states and rival municipalities for centuries, it decided fierce local identity was worth preserving even after unification arrived and tried to convince everyone they were now, technically, one country.

## Do's & Don'ts

**San Marino**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to be recognised quickly as a resident, even in the capital's more touristed old town | Assume big-city anonymity is available anywhere in the country — it simply isn't |
| Engage with the nine castelli (municipalities) — each has its own distinct micro-identity | Treat San Marino as culturally identical to the Italian region surrounding it | 
| Participate in local civic and religious festivals — turnout is high, participation is expected | Underestimate how seriously locals take the country's centuries-old republican independence |
| Learn names quickly — in a country this size, being "the person who doesn't know anyone" stands out fast | Expect Italian-style regional rivalry; San Marino's identity is unified around its own sovereignty instead |

**Italy**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn your neighbourhood's or town's specific identity and quirks — it matters more than the national one | Assume "Italian culture" is uniform; campanilismo runs extremely deep, town to town |
| Frequent the same local bar or café consistently — it's the actual social anchor of neighbourhood life | Switch loyalties between competing local bars carelessly; it's noticed, and occasionally minded |
| Respect the intensity of local football and patron saint rivalries between neighbouring towns | Dismiss inter-town rivalry as trivial; it's centuries-deep and genuinely, passionately felt |
| Join the passeggiata (the evening neighbourhood walk) as the real social event it is | Rush through it as mere transit; it's meant to be slow, social, and seen |

## San Marino: A Community By Necessity, Not Design

With a population hovering around 33,000, San Marino functions less like a nation and more like a very well-organised, extremely proud small town that happens to have its own flag, currency arrangement, and centuries of independent republican governance to back up the swagger. The capital, also called San Marino, blends into a UNESCO-listed old town where locals and tourists share impossibly narrow medieval streets, and where the sheer density of shared physical space means community isn't cultivated so much as unavoidable — you will run into the same people, repeatedly, whether you intend to or not.

The country's nine castelli, or municipalities, each carry their own distinct local identity and civic pride, a structure that echoes Italy's famous hyperlocal loyalties but operates at a dramatically smaller, gentler scale — more friendly distinction than genuine rivalry, since everyone ultimately shares the same tiny, fiercely guarded national sovereignty that predates Italy's own unification by well over a millennium. San Marino has been an independent republic since, by its own telling, the year 301, and that historical continuity gives its community identity a specific flavour: less about local rivalry, more about a shared, almost defiant pride in having remained sovereign and distinct for this long, entirely surrounded by a much larger neighbour that could have absorbed it at any point and simply never did.

Civic and religious festivals see genuinely high participation given the population size — when a meaningful fraction of the entire country turns out for an event, it doesn't take much to make it feel like a true community-wide occasion rather than a niche local gathering. Newcomers settling in San Marino report the same core experience repeatedly: anonymity simply isn't available, integration happens fast whether you're ready or not, and learning names and faces quickly isn't optional politeness, it's basic functional necessity in a country this size.

## Italy: Campanilismo, or Why the Next Town Over Is Basically a Rival Nation

Italy's community life operates on a scale and logic that can baffle outsiders expecting one unified national culture. Campanilismo — the intense, often gently comic attachment to one's own town, parish, or bell tower over any neighbouring one — is a genuine, historically rooted social force, not just a charming stereotype. It traces back to Italy's long history as a patchwork of independent city-states and communes, unified as a single nation only in 1861, a relatively recent development that never fully erased the far older, far deeper loyalty to one's specific comune.

This plays out constantly in everyday neighbourhood life. The local bar — not a chain, but the specific, usually family-run establishment where the same regulars order the same coffee from the same barista every morning — functions as genuine social infrastructure, a place where local news, gossip, and civic grievances circulate more effectively than any official channel. Switching allegiance to a different bar without good reason can actually register as a small social event, noticed by regulars in ways that would seem bizarre anywhere community loyalty runs thinner.

Inter-town rivalries, particularly around football and religious patron saint festivals, carry a passionate intensity that consistently surprises visitors expecting good-natured fun rather than genuine, centuries-old competitive weight. Palio-style events and local derby matches aren't just entertainment; they're the periodic, ritualised expression of identities that predate the modern Italian state by centuries and that unification never quite managed to fully dissolve. Layered underneath all of this is the passeggiata, the evening walk through the neighbourhood or town centre, which functions as neighbourhood life's actual daily assembly — not exercise, not errand-running, but the deliberate, unhurried act of being seen and seeing everyone else, every single evening, without fail.

## The Verdict

San Marino offers community as an inescapable structural fact — small, warm, occasionally suffocating in its total lack of anonymity. Italy offers something more layered and more combustible: genuine national unity existing in permanent, good-humoured tension with fierce, centuries-old hyperlocal loyalty that shows absolutely no signs of fading.

I'll give this one to Italy, for sheer richness of texture — campanilismo is one of the most genuinely fascinating community structures in Europe, precisely because it refuses to resolve into simple national identity no matter how many times the history books insist Italy became one country in 1861. San Marino's community feels like family. Italy's feels like an ongoing, passionately contested family reunion that's been running for a thousand years and shows no sign of ending.

## Conclusion

Scale changes everything about how community forms, and San Marino and Italy demonstrate that beautifully from opposite ends of the same peninsula. San Marino gives you community because there's genuinely nowhere else for social life to go in a country this size. Italy gives you community as a passionately defended, historically stubborn act of local identity that refuses to simplify itself into one tidy national culture no matter how many maps insist otherwise. Learn your neighbours' names either way. In San Marino, you'll have no choice. In Italy, you'll want to — mostly so you can hold your own when the campanilismo debate about whose piazza is better inevitably starts.
