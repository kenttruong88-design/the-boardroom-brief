# One Country Will Bill You Later. The Other Will Ask You to Bring Your Own Gauze.

🇮🇶 Iraq · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Healthcare is the single fastest way to learn what a country actually thinks of you, and I've had the specific pleasure of finding out in both a glossy Melbourne bulk-billing clinic and a private hospital in Erbil where the receptionist asked, with total sincerity, whether I'd brought cash for the deposit before anyone had even looked at the, frankly, concerning rash in question.

Australia built a genuinely excellent public system and then wrapped it in enough reciprocal-agreement paperwork to make sure you spend your first month figuring out if you're even covered. Iraq skipped the paperwork entirely and went straight to "pay now, ask questions never," which, perversely, might be the more honest of the two systems.

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check if your home country has a reciprocal Medicare agreement before you land | Assume Medicare covers you automatically just because you're now a resident |
| Get private health insurance fast if you're on most work visas — it's often compulsory | Skip the Medicare Levy Surcharge math; it's a real cost if you go without cover |
| Register with a local GP early — bulk-billing clinics fill up fast | Expect specialist appointments quickly; public wait lists can run for months |
| Carry your Medicare card everywhere once you have one — it is genuinely magic | Confuse "free" healthcare with "fast" healthcare; they are not the same promise |

## Do's & Don'ts: Iraq

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comprehensive medical evacuation insurance before you arrive, not after | Rely on public hospitals for anything beyond genuine emergencies |
| Build a relationship with one trusted private clinic in your city early | Assume a pharmacy visit is a substitute for seeing an actual doctor |
| Keep cash on hand for deposits — many private clinics expect payment upfront | Assume English will get you through a serious medical conversation outside Erbil |
| Ask colleagues which hospital they'd personally choose, not just which is nearest | Travel for elective or non-urgent procedures without a confirmed, vetted provider |

## Australia: Excellent, Free, and Occasionally Not Fast Enough to Matter

Medicare is, genuinely, one of the better public healthcare arguments on the planet, and if you land here as a citizen, permanent resident, or a national of one of the handful of countries with a reciprocal agreement — the UK, Ireland, New Zealand, and a short list of others — you get access to a system that will bulk-bill a GP visit, subsidise your prescriptions, and treat a public hospital emergency without ever mentioning money, which after enough time abroad starts to feel almost suspicious.

The catch, and it's a real one, is that most skilled-worker and temporary visas do not come with Medicare attached, full stop, which means a meaningful chunk of the expat population here is navigating private health insurance as a condition of their visa rather than a lifestyle choice. The insurance market is competitive but genuinely confusing, stacked with excesses, waiting periods, and a Private Health Insurance Rebate system calibrated by income that nobody explains to you clearly until you've already guessed wrong once on your tax return. Get it wrong and the Medicare Levy Surcharge quietly taxes you for the privilege of not having private cover, which is the most Australian possible punishment: a fine, politely worded, for insufficient planning.

Where Australia genuinely humbles newcomers is wait times for anything non-urgent. The emergency system is fast and excellent — nobody waits dangerously long with a broken arm — but a referral to a dermatologist or an orthopaedic specialist through the public system can take months, sometimes the better part of a year in regional areas, which comes as a genuine shock to anyone arriving from a system where money simply bought speed. Private health cover shortens that wait considerably, which is the actual, unspoken function private insurance performs here: not better care, necessarily, just faster access to the same excellent care everyone's entitled to eventually.

## Iraq: A Two-Tier System Where the Honest Tier Costs Money

Iraq's public healthcare system carries real, visible scars from decades of war, sanctions, and underinvestment, and most expats — correctly — never go near it except in a genuine emergency where there's simply no alternative. The country does, however, have a growing private hospital sector, concentrated heavily in Baghdad and especially Erbil in the Kurdistan region, that ranges from genuinely solid, internationally-trained-staffed clinics to operations you'd want a second local opinion on before committing to anything invasive.

The defining feature of private healthcare here, for an expat, is that money moves first and treatment follows, not the other way around. Deposits are commonly requested before a procedure, sometimes before so much as a consultation for anything beyond the most routine visit, and the expectation of cash-in-hand rather than invoice-later is one of the most disorienting adjustments for anyone used to a bill-you-afterwards system. It isn't predatory so much as structurally necessary — insurance networks and billing infrastructure that Western systems take for granted simply aren't consistently built out here, so clinics protect themselves the only reliable way they can.

Comprehensive medical evacuation insurance isn't a luxury add-on here; for most contractors and corporate postings it's treated, correctly, as an absolute baseline requirement, because anything genuinely serious — a major surgery, a complex diagnosis, a cardiac event — tends to mean evacuation to Jordan, Turkey, or further afield rather than treatment in-country. The expat community's actual coping mechanism isn't a better understanding of local hospitals; it's a shared, carefully curated word-of-mouth list of which specific clinic, which specific doctor, is trusted for which specific problem, passed between contractors like a vital piece of equipment nobody travels here without.

## The Verdict

Australia wins decisively on fairness and outcomes — a system that treats you well regardless of your wallet is objectively the better civilisation to live inside. Iraq wins, oddly, on transparency: you know exactly where you stand, financially, before anyone touches you, and there's no surprise invoice six weeks later disguised as a Levy Surcharge.

Give me Medicare's slow fairness over Erbil's fast honesty any day — but I'll say this for Iraq's private clinics: at least they tell you the price before the needle comes out, which is more than I can say for half the billing systems in countries that claim to be developed.

## Conclusion

Australia and Iraq represent two honest, opposite answers to the same question: who pays, and when. Australia says the state pays, mostly, eventually, fairly, if you're patient enough to wait your turn in the queue. Iraq says you pay, now, upfront, and the queue simply doesn't exist for anyone who can't. Neither system is pretending to be something it's not, which, after enough time spent abroad discovering which countries quietly lie about their healthcare, counts for more than people realise. Sort your cover before you land in either, and whatever you do, don't assume "developed" and "fast" are the same word. They very rarely are.
