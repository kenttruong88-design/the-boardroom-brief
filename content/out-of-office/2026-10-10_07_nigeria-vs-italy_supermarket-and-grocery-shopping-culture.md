# Haggling Is A Language You Don't Speak Yet. Loyalty To A Tiny Shop Is A Language You'll Never Fully Learn Either.

🇳🇬 Nigeria 🇮🇹 Italy
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country tells you something true about its values in how it sells you vegetables. Nigeria tells you, loudly, that a fixed price is a starting offer and that commerce is a conversation, not a transaction. Italy tells you, with equal conviction, that the correct number of shops to visit for one dinner is not one but four, each fiercely, almost religiously specific about what it sells and nothing else.

I arrived in both places assuming grocery shopping was the most boring, universal task a relocation could offer. I was wrong both times, in opposite directions. One taught me to negotiate for tomatoes like my dignity depended on it. The other taught me that my dignity depended, instead, on knowing which tiny shop sold the good prosciutto and never, under any circumstances, buying it at the supermarket instead.

## Do's & Don'ts: Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat the first quoted price at an open market as a conversational opening bid | Accept the first price without at least a token counter-offer — it insults the ritual |
| Build rapport with specific vendors over repeat visits for better ongoing prices | Haggle aggressively at a fixed-price supermarket chain — different rules apply there |
| Bring smaller denominations of cash; change can be genuinely hard to source | Assume all markets operate the same way — Lagos's Balogun differs hugely from a suburban one |
| Shop early for the best produce selection before the day's best stock sells out | Rush the exchange — a bit of banter is expected and often improves your price |

## Do's & Don'ts: Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn which small shop specialises in what — macelleria, panificio, alimentari all differ | Expect one supermarket trip to cover everything a proper meal requires |
| Greet the shopkeeper by name once you're a regular — it genuinely changes your service | Squeeze or handle produce without asking; many shopkeepers select it for you |
| Shop daily or every few days for freshness rather than one large weekly haul | Show up right at riposo (the midday closure) expecting a shop to be open |
| Ask for a recommendation — shopkeepers expect and enjoy being asked what's good today | Assume a supermarket chain's quality matches what the specialty shops offer |

## Nigeria: Commerce As Conversation, Price As Opening Position

Grocery shopping in Nigeria, particularly at the open markets that still handle a substantial share of everyday food commerce according to National Bureau of Statistics retail data, operates on a principle that takes most Western arrivals weeks to genuinely internalise: the first price you hear is not the price. It is an invitation to negotiate, and declining the invitation — simply paying what's asked without a counter — is read less as politeness and more as a slightly odd refusal to participate in a shared social ritual everyone else is enjoying.

Lagos's markets, Balogun chief among them, run on a scale and intensity that can overwhelm a first-timer completely, but the underlying logic holds at a neighbourhood market too: price is relational, not fixed, and the relationship you build with a specific vendor over repeat visits genuinely shapes what you pay going forward. This isn't a tourist tax being extracted from the unwary — locals haggle with each other exactly as hard, and vendors clearly enjoy a customer who engages properly rather than one who either overpays meekly or haggles without any warmth at all.

The supermarket sector, growing steadily in Lagos and Abuja per the Lagos State Ministry of Commerce's own retail-sector tracking, operates on entirely different, fixed-price rules, and conflating the two systems is one of the more common early mistakes — trying to haggle at a supermarket checkout gets you nowhere and mildly confuses the cashier, while paying full asking price at an open-air stall without a single counter-offer leaves real money on the table and, more importantly, misses what the exchange was actually for. It was never just about the tomatoes.

## Italy: Four Tiny Shops Are Not An Inconvenience, They're The System

Italy's grocery culture runs on an entirely different inefficiency, one that looks, from the outside, like a failure of retail consolidation and reveals itself, from the inside, as a deliberately maintained web of specialised relationships. A proper Italian household shop involves the macelleria for meat, the panificio for bread, the alimentari for everything in between, and — depending on the neighbourhood — a dedicated fishmonger, cheese shop, and produce stall, each one fiercely proud of its specific, narrow expertise and mildly suspicious of anyone trying to get all of it in one stop.

Istat's household spending data shows Italians still directing a meaningfully higher share of food spending toward these small specialty retailers than most of Western Europe, and Coldiretti's own market reporting frames this not as inefficiency but as a genuine point of agricultural and culinary pride — a system that privileges knowing your butcher's name over the supermarket's lower prices and longer hours. The supermarket chains exist, are perfectly competent, and are used constantly for the boring staples. They are simply never going to be where a serious Italian cook sources Sunday's main course.

What took real adjustment was the relational weight of each small shop. Shopkeepers remember regulars, recommend what's good that specific day, and in several cases, select produce on your behalf rather than let you handle it yourself — a norm that reads as controlling to an outsider and reveals itself, with time, as genuine care about quality leaving the shop in good condition. Riposo, the midday closure most small shops still observe, adds its own rhythm: showing up at 1pm expecting service is a newcomer's mistake made exactly once, usually loudly, in front of a shuttered grate that will not be moved by your schedule.

## The Verdict

Nigeria's market culture rewards engagement, banter, and a willingness to negotiate your own worth into every transaction. Italy's shop culture rewards patience, loyalty, and a willingness to let your weekly errands take four stops instead of one. Both, in their own way, refuse to let grocery shopping become the mindless task it's become almost everywhere else, and both will make you a noticeably better cook as a side effect of forcing real engagement with where food actually comes from. I'm handing this one to Italy, narrowly, because there's something genuinely restorative about a shopkeeper who remembers your name and picks your peaches for you, even if it does mean four stops before lunch.

## Conclusion

Both countries are quietly making the same argument, dressed in opposite clothing: that buying food should involve another human being, not just a transaction. Nigeria insists on that humanity through negotiation — a bit of friction, a bit of theatre, a fair price arrived at together. Italy insists on it through loyalty — a slower, more specialised relationship built shop by shop, name by name. Arrive expecting either to be a quick, efficient errand, and you'll miss the actual point entirely. The inefficiency isn't a bug in either system. It's the whole reason the tomatoes taste better than the ones back home.
