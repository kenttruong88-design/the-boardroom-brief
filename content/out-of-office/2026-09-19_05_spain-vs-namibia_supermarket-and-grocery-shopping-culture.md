# One Country Built Its Food Culture Around a Market That Argues With You. The Other Built Its Food Prices Around a Border It Can't Escape

🇪🇸 Spain vs 🇳🇦 Namibia — Supermarket and Grocery Shopping Culture
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Grocery shopping is the single most honest window into how a country actually lives, because unlike restaurants or landmarks, nobody performs for the camera at the vegetable stall. Spain and Namibia could not offer a more different lesson in this regard: one is a shopping culture built on abundance, ritual, and the fierce personal relationships between shopper and stallholder; the other is a shopping culture built almost entirely around logistics, distance, and the quiet tyranny of import costs. Both will teach you something about the country you didn't get from the guidebook.

I have had an actual argument with a Madrid fishmonger about whether I needed the whole hake or just a fillet (I did not win; I bought the whole hake). I have also stood in a Windhoek supermarket doing mental arithmetic on whether imported Australian avocados were worth the price of a small used car. These are both valid ways to buy dinner. They are not, in any sense, the same experience.

## Do's & Don'ts: Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shop the mercado for produce, fish, and meat, then the supermarket for packaged goods | Expect the mercado to be cheaper by default — you're paying for quality and relationship, not always a bargain |
| Let the stallholder recommend what's good that day — they will, whether you ask or not | Rush the interaction; a "quick" mercado visit doesn't exist once a vendor decides you need advice |
| Shop daily or every other day like most locals, for freshness | Bulk-buy fresh produce for the week — Spanish kitchens aren't built around it, and neither is the produce |

## Do's & Don'ts: Namibia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget for imported goods costing significantly more than in South Africa or Europe | Expect fresh produce variety matching what you're used to — Windhoek's selection is real but limited |
| Buy South African brands as your reliable, better-value default in most categories | Assume rural towns have the same supermarket access as Windhoek — stock up before travelling |
| Appreciate the genuinely good local meat and biltong — this is one category Namibia does better and cheaper than almost anywhere | Underestimate how much of the shelf is imported; check labels if "local" matters to you |

## Spain: The Market as Social Contract, Not Just Errand

Spanish grocery culture runs on two parallel systems that locals move between without a second thought: the mercado municipal, a covered market of independent stallholders selling fish, meat, cheese, and produce, and the supermarket chain (Mercadona being the near-universal favourite, discussed with a devotion that borders on brand loyalty as personality trait) for everything packaged, processed, or simply less fun to negotiate over. The mercado is not a tourist nostalgia act — it is genuinely how most Spaniards, across generations, still source their fresh food, particularly fish and meat, where quality and freshness matter enough to justify the extra ritual.

What makes the mercado remarkable isn't the produce, it's the relationship. Spanish stallholders take an almost proprietary interest in what you're cooking, will talk you out of a bad choice, and remember regular customers' preferences with a specificity that would feel invasive anywhere else and feels, here, like being cared for. This isn't transactional efficiency — it's closer to a long-running conversation that happens to also involve buying dinner, and expats who try to treat it as a quick in-and-out errand will be gently, firmly slowed down by a vendor who has opinions about your plans.

The supermarket side of Spanish life is comparatively unglamorous but genuinely well-run — Mercadona in particular has a near-cult following for consistency, private-label quality, and an efficient layout that locals navigate with the practiced ease of a daily ritual, because for most Spanish households, it is one. Spain's daily-or-every-other-day shopping rhythm, rather than the weekly mega-shop common elsewhere, reflects both smaller urban kitchens and a cultural preference for freshness that treats a week-old vegetable with genuine suspicion.

## Namibia: Abundance Undone by Distance, and a Surprising Meat Exception

Namibia's grocery reality is shaped by one inescapable fact: it is one of the most sparsely populated countries on Earth, with most consumer goods travelling enormous distances — either from South Africa, which supplies the bulk of branded groceries, or from further afield entirely for anything more exotic. Windhoek's supermarkets — Pick n Pay, Shoprite, Spar, all South African chains operating essentially as they do at home — are well-stocked and modern, but the price tag on anything imported carries the visible cost of that journey, and expats accustomed to European or American grocery pricing often experience a genuine, recurring sticker shock on categories like fresh berries, specialty cheese, or anything requiring refrigerated air freight.

The exception, gloriously, is meat. Namibia's beef, lamb, and particularly its biltong and dried game meats are excellent and, by international standards, remarkably affordable — a rare category where being a large, sparsely populated country with serious livestock farming actually works in the consumer's favour rather than against it. Expats who arrive bracing for expensive groceries across the board are often pleasantly stunned by the butcher counter, even as they wince at the avocado prices two aisles over.

Fresh produce variety is the other honest tradeoff — Windhoek's supermarkets do stock fruit and vegetables, but the range, particularly outside of what grows well locally, is narrower than what a European or North American shopper takes for granted, and rural towns beyond the capital offer considerably less still, making a supply run before any trip outside Windhoek a genuine logistical consideration rather than an afterthought.

## The Verdict

Spain wins this one comfortably, and not narrowly — a country that has built genuine daily ritual, relationship, and pleasure into the act of buying dinner is simply operating on a different level than a country whose grocery experience is dominated by import logistics, however well-run the supermarkets themselves are. Namibia isn't doing anything wrong; it's doing the best any sparsely populated, import-dependent economy could reasonably do, and the meat counter deserves genuine applause. But nobody has ever described a Windhoek Pick n Pay run as the highlight of their week, and plenty of people say exactly that about a Spanish mercado.

## Conclusion

Spain has turned grocery shopping into a daily social ritual so ingrained that skipping it feels like skipping a meal. Namibia has turned it into an exercise in logistics management, redeemed almost entirely by a butcher counter that punches so far above its weight it deserves its own tourism campaign. If you want your groceries to feel like an event, move to Spain. If you want them to feel like a spreadsheet with excellent biltong, Namibia will oblige — just don't expect the avocados to forgive you for it.
