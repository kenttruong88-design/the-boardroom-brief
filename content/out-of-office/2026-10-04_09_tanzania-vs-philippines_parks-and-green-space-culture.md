# One Country's Green Space Has Lions In It, The Other's Has a Jogging Track and a View

🇹🇿 Tanzania 🇵🇭 Philippines

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Tanzania's relationship with green space operates on an entirely different scale to most of the world — when your national parks include Serengeti and Ngorongoro, the humble city park inevitably feels like an afterthought, and urban green space in Dar es Salaam is genuinely modest by comparison to the wild magnificence just a flight away. The Philippines takes the opposite approach, cramming genuine, well-used green space into dense, humid cities like Manila, where a public park isn't an afterthought but a hard-won, actively defended pocket of breathing room in a city that doesn't have much space to spare.

I've stood in Serengeti National Park feeling genuinely small next to a landscape that didn't care about my presence at all, and I've jogged around Manila's Rizal Park at dawn, dodging street vendors setting up for the day, feeling like I'd found the one sliver of calm in a city that otherwise never quite exhales. Different scales of green entirely.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Tanzania**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book national park visits (Serengeti, Ngorongoro, Tarangire) well ahead, especially in dry season | Expect casual, spontaneous access — most major parks require permits and planning |
| Hire a licensed guide for any national park visit — it's required and genuinely improves the experience | Attempt a self-guided safari without proper vehicle and local knowledge |
| Treat Dar es Salaam's smaller public spaces (like Coco Beach) as legitimate local leisure spots | Expect urban Tanzanian parks to match the scale or polish of its national parks |
| Respect park fees as funding real conservation — they're substantial for good reason | Haggle over national park entrance fees; they're fixed and non-negotiable |

**Philippines**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit Manila's Rizal Park or Intramuros green spaces early morning to avoid heat and crowds | Expect quiet solitude at midday — these are genuinely busy, well-used public spaces |
| Explore provincial green spaces (Baguio's parks, Palawan's protected areas) for a quieter experience | Assume all Philippine green space is as developed or as busy as central Manila's |
| Bring sun protection and water — tropical humidity makes outdoor time more demanding than it looks | Underestimate the heat; even a short park walk can be more draining than expected |
| Support local eco-tourism fees in protected marine and forest areas | Treat protected natural areas as free-for-all; many now charge conservation fees for good reason |

## Tanzania: Green Space on an Entirely Different Scale

Tanzania's national parks operate on a scale that reframes the entire concept of "green space" — Serengeti alone covers nearly 15,000 square kilometres, and Ngorongoro Crater is a self-contained ecosystem dense enough with wildlife that the word "park" feels almost insultingly small for what's actually there. These aren't leisure spaces in any conventional sense; they're heavily regulated, fee-funded conservation areas requiring licensed guides, proper vehicles, and genuine planning, and the entire experience is built around respecting that this land fundamentally belongs to the animals, with human visitors as careful, paying guests.

This creates a genuinely interesting split in how Tanzanians themselves relate to green space day-to-day. Urban residents in Dar es Salaam don't have casual access to Serengeti on a weekday — it's a destination, not a backyard — so daily green space needs are met instead through smaller, humbler options: Coco Beach's informal waterfront gathering spot, pockets of shaded public space around the city, and the simple relief of the Indian Ocean breeze substituting for a park bench. The contrast between the almost mythic scale of Tanzania's protected wilderness and the modest, practical green space available in its largest city is stark, and it's a genuine recalibration for visitors who arrive expecting national-park grandeur to somehow extend into everyday urban life.

What's easy to miss is how seriously conservation funding is taken here. Park fees are substantial, non-negotiable, and genuinely fund anti-poaching efforts, habitat management, and community programs tied to the parks — paying full price isn't a tourist tax to grumble about, it's a direct contribution to keeping Serengeti's ecosystem intact for the long term, and Tanzanians are rightly protective of that funding model against any pressure to cut costs.

## The Philippines: Green Space as Hard-Won Urban Oxygen

The Philippines, and Manila in particular, treats green space as something actively fought for and defended within one of Southeast Asia's densest urban environments. Rizal Park, sprawling at the heart of Manila, functions as the city's genuine lungs — used constantly, from dawn joggers and tai chi groups to evening families spreading out picnic blankets, a well-loved, heavily trafficked public space that locals treat with real affection precisely because dense, humid Manila doesn't offer much comparable breathing room elsewhere.

Beyond the capital, the picture broadens considerably — Baguio's cooler mountain parks offer genuine respite from tropical heat, and the Philippines' extraordinary marine and forest protected areas, from Palawan's lagoons to Bohol's forests, represent a different register of green space entirely, closer in spirit to Tanzania's grand conservation areas than to a city park. These protected natural spaces increasingly charge conservation fees too, a relatively recent but necessary shift as tourism pressure on fragile ecosystems like Palawan's has grown, mirroring Tanzania's own funding logic even if the landscapes themselves couldn't look more different.

What catches visitors off guard in Manila specifically is just how used its green spaces are, constantly, by actual residents rather than tourists. Rizal Park at dawn is already full of joggers, vendors setting up, and elderly groups doing coordinated exercises — there's no quiet, empty version of this park to stumble into, because Manila's density means every patch of green gets claimed and used, all day, by a city that genuinely needs it. The tropical heat adds its own demand: a casual stroll that looks easy on a map can be genuinely draining by midday, and locals plan outdoor time around early mornings and late afternoons with a discipline visitors often learn the hard way.

## The Verdict

Tanzania wins, unambiguously, if the measure is sheer natural grandeur — nothing in the Philippines competes with Serengeti's scale, and nothing should have to. The Philippines wins if the measure is everyday urban green space actually working for the people who live there — Rizal Park earns its keep daily in a way Dar es Salaam's modest options simply can't match pound for pound. Different countries, different needs: one built a wilderness worth protecting at enormous scale, the other built a park worth using every single morning. Both deserve the entrance fee.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/tanzania — paraphrased: a visitor recalls being surprised that Dar es Salaam itself has relatively little formal park space, and that locals instead treat certain beach stretches as the city's real communal outdoor gathering spots.*

> *expat.com Manila forum — paraphrased: a relocated resident says the single best adjustment was learning to treat Rizal Park as a 6am destination rather than a midday one, after one too many overheated afternoon walks.*

> *Quora — paraphrased: a response to a question about Dar es Salaam parks notes that most meaningful green space access in Tanzania requires a genuine trip and a park fee, rather than the casual, free neighbourhood park experience assumed by the question itself.*

## Conclusion

Tanzania and the Philippines have built green space cultures shaped by entirely different pressures — one by the sheer, protected scale of its wilderness, the other by the dense, daily necessity of urban breathing room. Visit Tanzania and you'll pay for access to something genuinely vast and wild. Visit Manila and you'll join a crowded, well-loved park at dawn alongside half the neighbourhood. Neither is lesser for the comparison; they're simply answering different questions about what green space is actually for.
