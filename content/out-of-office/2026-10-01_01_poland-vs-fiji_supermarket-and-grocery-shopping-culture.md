# One Country Alphabetises Its Pickles. The Other Just Hands You a Coconut.

🇵🇱🇫🇯 Poland & Fiji
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have shopped for dinner in forty-odd countries, and exactly one of them made me feel personally prosecuted by a cashier for buying the wrong brand of kielbasa. That was Poland, where the supermarket isn't really a shop so much as a small, humming bureaucracy that happens to sell cheese. Fiji, by contrast, runs on the principle that if the fish is fresh and the man selling it is your cousin's rugby teammate, paperwork is for people who don't trust each other yet.

New arrivals in Warsaw spend their first fortnight relearning how to buy bread without triggering a disapproving tut from the queue. New arrivals in Suva spend their first fortnight realising the "corner shop" is a verandah with a fridge on it and a ledger of who owes whom two dollars. Both systems function beautifully. Neither will explain itself to you in English, and frankly, neither owes you that courtesy.

## Do's & Don'ts: Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your own bag — bags cost extra and the cashier will not pretend to be surprised you forgot | Don't dawdle bagging your groceries at the till; the queue behind you is already drafting a complaint |
| Weigh your own fruit and veg and print the barcode sticker before you reach the register | Don't assume every "supermarket" is a Biedronka — a Żabka is a different, smaller, pricier animal |
| Learn the word "pietruszka" before you urgently need parsley | Don't expect card payment under a few złoty at a kiosk — some still want coins |
| Buy pickles loose from the barrel at the market for the good stuff | Don't ask staff for substitutions or special orders — this is not a concierge desk |

## Do's & Don'ts: Fiji

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet the vendor properly before you start shopping — "bula" comes before the shopping list, not after | Don't call the produce "exotic" to the person who grew it. It's just lunch, to them |
| Haggle gently at the municipal market; it's a conversation, not a war | Don't expect fixed opening hours at a village shop — "open" is more of a mood |
| Buy fish straight off the boat at dawn if you can manage it | Don't rush the transaction — small talk is part of the price, and skipping it is rude |
| Carry small notes and coins everywhere you go | Don't assume the shop in your village stocks what the one in town does |

## Poland: The Spreadsheet With a Soul

Poland does grocery shopping the way it does most things: with a system, a hierarchy, and an unspoken contempt for anyone who hasn't memorised it. The supermarket landscape is a strict pecking order — Biedronka and Lidl for the weekly haul, Żabka on every corner for the 11pm beer run, and the Saturday market (targ) for anyone who actually wants their tomatoes to taste like tomatoes. Get this hierarchy wrong — showing up to Żabka expecting Biedronka prices, say — and you'll be treated the way Poles treat anyone who doesn't understand that efficiency is a moral position, not a preference.

The checkout experience deserves its own chapter in a book about low-grade psychological warfare. You scan your own loyalty card, you bag your own shopping at a speed that would embarrass an Olympic sprinter, and you do all of this while the cashier watches with the weary patience of a woman who has seen ten thousand tourists fumble with a five-złoty coin. There is no small talk. There is barely eye contact. Efficiency here is not rudeness; it's respect, expressed through silence and the absence of wasted motion.

Then there's the market, which operates on an entirely different emotional register — warm, specific, occasionally combative in the way that haggling over pickled cucumbers can turn surprisingly personal. The woman who sells you cheese on Tuesday will remember you on Thursday and will also remember that you asked a stupid question about smoked twaróg. Polish grocery culture rewards consistency and punishes vagueness. Know what you want. Say it plainly. Pay the correct amount. Leave. It's less a shopping trip than a small weekly audit, and you will pass or fail depending on how well you've done your homework.

## Fiji: Time Moves Differently in the Produce Aisle

Fiji's grocery culture operates on an entirely different clock, and pretending otherwise will only make you miserable. The municipal markets in Suva and Nadi are loud, colourful, unhurried institutions where buying a pawpaw involves roughly four minutes of conversation about your family, the weather, and whether you've tried the dalo yet. This is not inefficiency. This is the actual point. A transaction here is a relationship, however brief, and skipping the pleasantries to "save time" marks you as someone who has badly misunderstood what you're doing here.

The village shop — often just a front room with a fridge, some tinned fish, and a hand-written tab system — runs on trust in a way that would give a Polish shopkeeper a minor stroke. Credit is extended on a nod. Change is sometimes approximate. Hours are a suggestion rather than a commitment, and "we're closed" can mean anything from "we are definitely closed" to "I'm having my lunch, come back in twenty." Supermarkets exist in the bigger towns, stocked with imported goods at prices that make you flinch, but they're treated as a backup plan rather than the main event.

What nobody tells you is how much of Fijian shopping culture is about redistribution rather than accumulation. Buy too many mangoes and you're expected to share them, not hoard them in a fridge like a suburban squirrel. The entire system assumes a community that looks out for itself, which is lovely right up until you, the newly arrived foreigner, try to apply Western efficiency logic to it and get gently, firmly corrected by someone's grandmother.

## The Verdict

If you want your groceries handled with Teutonic precision and zero unnecessary emotional labour, Poland wins, and wins decisively — it's the only place I've lived where I genuinely enjoyed the admin of buying lettuce. If you want to remember that shopping used to be a human activity rather than a logistics exercise, Fiji will recalibrate your soul, slowly, over several humid months, whether you asked for it or not.

But here's my bias, stated outright: Fiji wins this one. Not because it's "relaxed" — I loathe that word, it's what lazy travel writers say about any country with a beach — but because its system scales trust in a way Poland's never bothers to attempt. Poland optimises for strangers who will never see each other again. Fiji optimises for a village that will see each other at church on Sunday regardless of how the mango transaction went. One of these is a more honest description of how humans actually live.

## Conclusion

Neither of these systems is backward, and neither is enlightened — they're just honest about what they value. Poland values your time and nobody else's feelings; Fiji values the relationship and treats your schedule as a minor, slightly amusing inconvenience. Move between the two, as I have, and you'll spend the first month doing everything wrong in both directions — rushing the Fijian fishmonger, lingering too long at the Polish till — before you finally learn to read the room. That, ultimately, is the whole job of living abroad: not finding the "right" way to buy a cucumber, but working out, country by country, which version of rude you're about to accidentally be.
