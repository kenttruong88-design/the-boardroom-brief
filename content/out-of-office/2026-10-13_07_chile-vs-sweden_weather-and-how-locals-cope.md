# Chile Has Four Climates in One Country and Complains About All of Them. Sweden Has One Climate and Has Simply Decided Not to Complain About It.

🇨🇱 Chile vs 🇸🇪 Sweden

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Chile is a country so absurdly long that it contains desert, Mediterranean warmth, temperate rainforest, and glacial fjords within the same national border, and yet Chileans will still find something to complain about regarding whichever microclimate they happen to live in — Santiago's smog-trapping heat, the south's relentless rain, the desert's brutal temperature swings between day and night. Sweden, meanwhile, has one climate — long, dark, genuinely difficult winters — and has instead built an entire cultural infrastructure around not complaining about it, as though complaining would be both pointless and faintly undignified.

This is the real divide: Chile's relationship with weather is emotional and vocal, shaped by a geography so extreme it practically demands commentary. Sweden's relationship with weather is stoic and procedural, shaped by decades of collectively deciding that darkness is simply a logistics problem to be solved with candles, routine, and an alarming amount of vitamin D.

## Chile: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn which of Chile's many microclimates you're actually moving to before packing — Santiago, Patagonia, and the Atacama are different planets | Don't assume one wardrobe covers the country; a jacket perfect for Punta Arenas is useless in the Atacama |
| Prepare for Santiago's smog and thermal inversion in winter — the mountains that make the city beautiful also trap the pollution | Don't underestimate the Atacama Desert's temperature swing; scorching days can be followed by genuinely freezing nights |
| Embrace the asado (barbecue) culture that kicks into gear the moment decent weather arrives | Don't expect central heating as standard in older buildings; many rely on space heaters, and winters indoors can be colder than expected |
| Pack layers for the south, where rain is a near-constant feature rather than an occasional event | Don't plan outdoor plans in Patagonia without a backup; weather there can change dramatically within hours |

## Sweden: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in proper winter gear immediately — layering, good boots, and reflective strips for the dark months aren't optional extras | Don't assume you'll acclimatise quickly to the short winter daylight hours; most newcomers need at least one full season |
| Embrace "fika" and candlelight as genuine coping mechanisms, not just aesthetic choices | Don't skip vitamin D supplements through winter; the lack of sunlight affects mood more than most expect going in |
| Get outside even in the cold — Swedes treat winter walks and "friluftsliv" as essential, not optional | Don't retreat entirely indoors for months; locals will gently judge the habit as unhealthy |
| Appreciate the long, bright summer days as the payoff for the winter you just survived | Don't expect air conditioning as standard; summers are shorter and milder than the winters, so most buildings aren't built for heat |

### Chile: A Country Too Long to Agree on a Climate

Chile stretches over 4,300 kilometres from the driest desert on Earth to the edge of Antarctica, and this geography produces not one national relationship with weather but several, loosely stitched together by a shared passport. Santiago sits in a valley ringed by mountains that make for a genuinely stunning skyline and a genuinely miserable winter smog problem, as thermal inversion traps pollution over the city for weeks at a time — a tradeoff residents have learned to narrate with a kind of weary, specific complaint that non-Santiaguinos find almost comically detailed.

Head north and the Atacama Desert offers an entirely different weather personality: scorching, intense daytime sun followed by a temperature drop so severe after dark that visitors frequently underpack for the night and regret it within hours. Head south, toward Patagonia and the Lakes District, and the story flips again — persistent rain, dramatic and fast-changing conditions, and a landscape so wet and green it barely resembles the desert a thousand kilometres north, despite sharing a flag.

What unites Chileans across all these climates isn't shared weather so much as a shared willingness to discuss it at length, usually with some complaint attached, and a near-universal devotion to the asado the instant decent weather allows one — grilling meat outdoors is less a hobby here than a seasonal reflex, triggered the moment temperatures cooperate anywhere in the country. Indoor heating, somewhat ironically for a country with such weather extremes, remains inconsistent outside modern apartments; older housing stock often relies on space heaters, meaning a surprising number of Chileans spend winter nights colder indoors than the outside temperature alone would suggest.

### Sweden: A Country That Decided Darkness Was Manageable

Sweden's weather story is simpler on paper — long, cold, dark winters; short, bright, cherished summers — but the cultural apparatus built around managing that one basic fact is genuinely sophisticated. Stockholm in December sees daylight for only a handful of hours, and the adjustment this requires isn't trivial; most newcomers underestimate how much a lack of light affects mood and energy until they're several weeks into their first real winter, at which point vitamin D supplements stop sounding like a quirky local habit and start sounding like essential equipment.

What Swedes have built instead of complaint is ritual. Fika — the daily coffee-and-pastry pause, taken seriously as a social institution rather than a snack break — becomes something closer to a survival mechanism in the darker months, a scheduled point of warmth and light in an otherwise grey day. Candles appear everywhere, not as decoration but as a genuine mood-management tool, and the aesthetic of cosy Scandinavian interiors that gets exported worldwide as a lifestyle trend is, at its root, a practical response to needing to make artificial light feel inviting for roughly six months a year.

Crucially, Swedes do not treat winter as a reason to disappear indoors. "Friluftsliv" — a cultural commitment to outdoor life regardless of weather — means walks, outdoor exercise, and general fresh-air exposure continue through the cold rather than pausing for it, and newcomers who retreat entirely indoors for the winter are gently, repeatedly encouraged to get outside anyway, because the local consensus is that avoiding the cold makes the psychological toll of the dark months considerably worse, not better. The payoff, when it finally arrives, is a summer of nearly endless daylight that the entire country seems to treat as sacred, scheduling vacations and outdoor life around it with an intensity that only makes sense once you've survived the months that preceded it.

## The Verdict

Chile's weather is more dramatic and more varied, and Chileans respond by talking about it constantly, with real feeling, as though narrating a long-running family dispute. Sweden's weather is less varied but arguably more punishing in its own way, and Swedes respond by building an entire set of rituals specifically designed to make darkness bearable without ever really discussing how hard it is.

I find something more honest about the Chilean approach — if the weather is genuinely difficult, why pretend otherwise? But I have enormous respect for Sweden's quiet competence at actually solving the problem rather than just venting about it. Chile wins on candour. Sweden wins on coping mechanisms. If I had to survive a truly dark winter, I know which country has actually done the homework.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat Arrivals, interview with a French expat living in Chile — paraphrased: the interview describes Santiago's winter smog as a genuine, recurring adjustment, with locals discussing air quality almost as a matter of daily small talk rather than an occasional concern.*

> *expat.com forums, Sweden — paraphrased: several newcomers describe underestimating how disorienting the short winter daylight hours would feel, with multiple posters recommending a light therapy lamp as something they wished they'd bought before their first winter, not during it.*

> *Internations Stockholm community notes — paraphrased: members frequently mention that fika stopped feeling like an optional social nicety within their first winter and started feeling like a genuinely necessary routine for getting through the darker months.*

## Conclusion

Both countries, in their own way, have made peace with weather that would break a less adapted population. Chile does it through sheer geographic familiarity — when your country contains a desert, a rainforest, and a glacier, you develop an unusually broad vocabulary for discomfort, and a corresponding willingness to air it. Sweden does it through collective discipline, treating darkness as a solvable engineering problem rather than a mood to be endured in silence.

I'd personally rather vent like a Chilean than quietly ritualise like a Swede, but I'll admit the Swedish system produces fewer bad moods per capita in February. Pick your climate philosophy accordingly — and pack a jacket either way, because neither country is going to apologise for its weather on your behalf.
