# One Country Won't Let You Practise Its Language. The Other Assumes You'll Learn Charades Instead.

🇳🇱🇧🇩 By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office

The Dutch language barrier is unique in world history: it doesn't exist, and that's the entire problem. Try to order a coffee in your painstakingly rehearsed Dutch and the barista will clock your accent in half a syllable, smile, and reply in flawless English before you've finished the sentence. In Bangladesh, the barrier is real, cheerfully acknowledged, and gloriously overcome through a shared national talent for expressive hand gestures, patient repetition, and an apparently bottomless well of goodwill toward anyone attempting three words of Bengali.

I have been humiliated by kindness in both countries. Only one of them let me actually learn anything.

## Do's & Don'ts: Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Insist, repeatedly, on continuing in Dutch if you want to actually learn | Give up the moment someone switches to English — they will, immediately |
| Practise with older generations in smaller towns; English fluency drops | Assume Amsterdam's English fluency reflects the whole country |
| Take a formal inburgering course if settling long-term | Rely on osmosis; the Dutch will not let osmosis happen naturally |
| Apologise for your Dutch and ask them to bear with you | Be offended when they switch — it's efficiency, not judgment |

## Do's & Don'ts: Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Bengali greetings — the effort is met with real warmth | Expect English to work reliably outside Dhaka's business districts |
| Use a translation app freely; it's completely normal and expected | Feel embarrassed pulling out your phone mid-conversation |
| Lean on gestures and patience in markets and rickshaws | Rush the interaction; miming takes time and works better slowly |
| Hire a local guide or fixer for anything administrative | Attempt complex bureaucratic Bengali-language forms solo, ever |

## The Netherlands: Fluent in Everything, Patient in Nothing

The single most demoralising experience available to a language learner in Europe is trying to practise Dutch in the Netherlands. You've drilled your verb conjugations. You've memorised the numbers. You walk into a bakery, deliver your carefully constructed sentence about a loaf of bread, and the person behind the counter — without malice, without even really thinking about it — responds in English so immediate and so fluent it's genuinely startling. The Dutch aren't trying to be unhelpful. Quite the opposite: they're being ruthlessly efficient, correctly calculating that the fastest path to you getting your bread is bypassing your Dutch entirely.

This is a direct consequence of one of the highest English proficiency rates on the planet, and it turns language learning into an uphill battle against the country's own competence. Ask any expat who's lived in the Netherlands for years and still can't hold a proper Dutch conversation, and they'll tell you the same story: it's not for lack of trying, it's that the Dutch simply won't let the awkward, halting practice conversation happen. The moment you stumble, they rescue you, and the rescue happens in English, every single time, until you eventually stop bothering to try.

The workaround is almost comically deliberate: seek out smaller towns, older generations, and formal inburgering integration courses where Dutch is the explicit, non-negotiable medium. Some expats resort to point-blank insisting, "please, continue in Dutch," repeatedly, until the message lands. It works, eventually, but it requires a level of social friction most people aren't naturally inclined to manufacture. The Netherlands isn't a hard place to live without the local language. It's a nearly impossible place to learn it, precisely because everyone's too good at yours.

## Bangladesh: A Barrier You Can Actually See, and Cross

Bangladesh offers the opposite problem, and in its own way, the more honest one. English proficiency outside Dhaka's business and university circles drops off fast, and Bengali dominates daily life almost everywhere — markets, rickshaws, government offices, the extended small talk that accompanies literally every transaction. The barrier is real, visible, and unavoidable, which paradoxically makes it easier to navigate than the Netherlands' polite, invisible one: you know exactly where you stand, and so does everyone you're talking to.

What follows from that shared acknowledgment is a kind of collaborative problem-solving that's genuinely touching once you stop being embarrassed by it. Rickshaw negotiations happen through a combination of pointing, finger-counted numbers, and expressive facial contortions that somehow convey "too expensive" more clearly than words ever could. Market vendors will patiently repeat a price three times, slower each time, as though volume and pace alone might bridge the gap — and often, remarkably, they do. Translation apps have become such a normalised crutch that pulling out your phone mid-conversation draws no attention whatsoever; everyone's doing some version of the same thing.

The genuine difficulty surfaces in anything official — banking forms, visa paperwork, medical consultations — where miming your way through simply isn't an option and a translator or local fixer becomes not a convenience but a necessity. This is where Bangladesh demands real humility: you cannot bluff your way through a government office the way you might bluff your way through buying vegetables. But the day-to-day barrier, for all its visibility, comes wrapped in a warmth and patience that makes the struggle feel less like failure and more like a shared, slightly absurd project both parties are equally invested in completing.

## The Verdict

If I want to actually acquire a language, Bangladesh wins by default — the barrier is honest, the stakes of miscommunication are low in daily settings, and the patience on offer gives you room to fail loudly and often without consequence. The Netherlands, maddeningly, offers no such room: its own excellence forecloses the very struggle you need to improve. But if I just want to live comfortably without ever learning a word, the Netherlands wins in a walk — you'll never once be stuck, never once genuinely lost, just perpetually, gently out-competed in your own language-learning attempt by a nation of extremely polite overachievers. Pick your frustration. I'll take the rickshaw negotiation over the bakery rescue any day.

## Conclusion

A language barrier says less about a place's hospitality than you'd think, and more about its confidence. The Netherlands is so linguistically self-assured it accidentally shuts foreigners out of the very practice they need. Bangladesh, less globally fluent but infinitely more patient with the attempt, turns every miscommunication into a small act of collaboration. If you're serious about learning either language, go in with a plan and, ideally, a stubborn streak. If you're not, go in with a translation app and low expectations of your own progress. Both countries will get you fed, housed, and vaguely understood. Only one will actually teach you something.
