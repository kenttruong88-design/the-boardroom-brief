# Getting to Work Is a Negotiation With God in One of These Cities and a Cardio Session in the Other

🇲🇾 Malaysia 🇵🇹 Portugal
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Kuala Lumpur commutes on a transport network that looks, on a map, thoroughly modern — monorail, LRT, MRT, a whole alphabet of acronyms — and yet somehow still requires a working knowledge of which private ride-hailing app is cheapest at any given hour, because the actual public system connects everything except, mysteriously, the places you need to go. Lisbon commutes largely by leg strength, because someone built a beautiful hillside city several centuries before anyone thought to ask whether trams could handle a 17% gradient reliably, and the answer, it turns out, is "occasionally."

I've stood on a KL monorail platform during evening rush hour feeling optimistic about Southeast Asian infrastructure and immediately been disabused of that optimism. I've also climbed the same Lisbon hill four times in one day because the funicular was "temporarily out of service" in a way that felt permanent. Neither city has this fully solved. Only one of them is charming about the failure.

## Do's & Don'ts: Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a Touch 'n Go card on day one — it works across rail, toll, and parking, and saves real time | Assume the LRT and MRT lines connect conveniently; some interchanges require a genuine walk outdoors in tropical heat |
| Use Grab for the "last mile" without shame — locals do it constantly and it's cheap | Expect buses to run to a schedule you can rely on; treat printed timetables as suggestions |
| Travel outside 7:30–9:30am and 5:30–7:30pm if you have any flexibility at all | Stand near the LRT doors during rush hour unless you're prepared to be swept out at the wrong stop |
| Carry cash or a working e-wallet for older bus routes that haven't caught up digitally | Underestimate KL's heat and humidity on the walk between transit and destination — plan accordingly |

## Do's & Don'ts: Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a Viva Viagem or Navegante card immediately — it covers metro, tram, bus, and ferry in Lisbon | Wear anything but proper shoes; the cobblestones and hills are not negotiable, ever |
| Ride Tram 28 once for the experience, then never again during commuting hours — it's a tourist magnet | Expect punctuality from buses on steep or narrow routes; delays are structural, not occasional |
| Use the funiculars and elevadores guilt-free — they exist specifically because the hills are absurd | Assume Porto and Lisbon transit systems work the same way; each city's logic is its own |
| Download an app that tracks real-time delays — official schedules are optimistic at best | Try to bike commute in Lisbon unless you have serious leg strength and low blood pressure |

## Malaysia: A Network That's Almost There

Kuala Lumpur's rail system, on paper, is genuinely impressive for the region — multiple lines, driverless trains, a monorail that loops through the city centre like something out of a slightly more ambitious future. The problem isn't the infrastructure itself. It's the seams between the pieces. Interchange stations that should be a two-minute walk turn into ten-minute treks through shopping malls, skybridges, and humid outdoor stretches that leave you re-showering before you've even started your workday. The system was built in phases, by different operators, on different timelines, and it shows in ways that no amount of signage fixes.

What actually works, brutally efficiently, is the informal layer built on top of the formal one. Grab has become such an embedded part of the KL commute that "just Grab it" functions as a verb, a plan, and a coping mechanism all at once. It's cheap by international standards, plentiful, and solves the last-mile problem the rail network can't. This is, depending on your mood, either an admirable adaptation or a quiet admission that public transit alone isn't cutting it. I lean toward the latter, but I use Grab constantly, so take that criticism with the hypocrisy it deserves.

Buses are the system's weak flank — routes exist, but reliability is patchy enough that most Kuala Lumpur commuters with any alternative simply don't bother. The heat compounds everything; waiting fifteen unplanned minutes at a bus stop in KL humidity is a genuinely different experience from waiting fifteen minutes anywhere temperate, and the city's transport planning doesn't always seem to have fully reckoned with its own climate.

## Portugal: Beautiful, Breathless, and Frequently Broken

Lisbon's commute is a workout disguised as a lifestyle. The city was built on seven hills with the confident indifference of people who did not anticipate mass transit, and every solution invented since — trams, funiculars, elevadores, an entire metro system carved improbably beneath it all — is essentially a patch on a fundamentally vertical problem. It's romantic in postcards. It's a genuine quad workout in practice, twice daily, for anyone who lives more than a flat block from a metro stop.

Tram 28 gets photographed by every tourist alive and avoided by every Lisboeta with anywhere to actually be, because it's been colonised by visitors clutching phones, moving at the speed of sightseeing rather than commuting. The real workhorses are the metro's four lines, genuinely decent when they're running, and the funiculars, which solve the hill problem elegantly right up until one of them is "under maintenance," a phrase Lisbon deploys with a frequency that borders on structural policy rather than occasional bad luck.

Porto has its own charming dysfunction — a tram-and-metro hybrid that mostly works, undercut by streets so narrow that buses occasionally have to negotiate directly with parked cars for right of way, an actual daily occurrence rather than a colourful exaggeration. What I'll say for Portugal, though: even the delays have a kind of unbothered grace to them. Nobody's furious. Everybody just waits, has a coffee, tries again.

## The Verdict

Malaysia wins on raw functionality — when the KL rail network works, and it mostly does, it moves an enormous number of people efficiently across real distances, and the Grab layer patches the rest with startling reliability. Portugal wins on charm, and only on charm, which is a genuinely different currency and one that doesn't get you to a 9am meeting.

If you need to actually be somewhere on time, take Kuala Lumpur, congestion and all. If you'd rather arrive fifteen minutes late having had a perfectly nice time and a small hill-induced cardiac event, Lisbon's your city. I'll admit a soft spot for Portugal's failures — they're at least aesthetically pleasing. But functionality wins the actual argument here.

## Conclusion

Two cities, two very different relationships with the word "commute." Kuala Lumpur has built something that mostly works and quietly leans on private ride-hailing to cover its gaps. Lisbon has built something that photographs beautifully and occasionally leaves you stranded on a hill with a dead funicular and a growing sense of betrayal. Efficient or picturesque — pick one, because on current evidence, no one's yet managed both.
