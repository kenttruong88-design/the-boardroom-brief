# One Country's Grocery Run Closes for a Nap. The Other's Involves Tea, Conversation, and Possibly a New Friend

🇴🇲 Oman vs 🇹🇷 Turkey

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular kind of newcomer panic that sets in when you realise the supermarket you were relying on has simply closed for several hours in the middle of the day, for reasons that have nothing to do with staffing and everything to do with heat, prayer, or the fundamental Omani conviction that nothing worth doing happens between one and four in the afternoon. Turkey, meanwhile, will happily sell you groceries around the clock at the supermarket, but will judge you, gently and with real affection, for skipping the actual theatre of the weekly bazaar in favour of fluorescent-lit convenience.

Both countries know exactly what they're doing. Neither is going to change it for you.

## Do's & Don'ts: Oman

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check souq hours before you go — many close midday and shorten drastically on Fridays | Assume Friday is a normal shopping day; Oman's weekend is Thursday–Friday, and it shows |
| Visit the Friday livestock and produce markets early — activity peaks from 6am | Expect Western-style fixed browsing hours from small independent shops |
| Use large supermarkets for reliable, longer hours if souq timing doesn't suit you | Show up expecting bustling souq energy during the afternoon lull — you'll find shutters |
| Bring cash for smaller stalls; card acceptance thins out fast outside major chains | Rush a transaction — unhurried courtesy is the norm, not a delay to work around |

## Do's & Don'ts: Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit the weekly neighbourhood pazar for the best, freshest, cheapest produce | Try to haggle at a supermarket — prices there are genuinely fixed, no exceptions |
| Accept tea if offered at a bazaar stall — it's hospitality, not a sales trap | Assume every vendor expects or wants haggling; many ordinary Turks never bargain at all |
| Chat before buying — a little conversation is part of the actual transaction | Rush the exchange; a hurried shopper is a poorly served shopper here |
| Explore discount chain markets for genuinely good deals on imported specialty items | Expect alcohol at every small grocery chain — several discount stores don't stock it |

## Oman: Groceries on Islamic Time, and Nobody's Apologising for It

Shopping in Oman operates on a rhythm dictated by heat and faith rather than retail convenience, and the sooner a visitor accepts this, the sooner grocery runs stop feeling like a series of small defeats. Traditional souqs — Muttrah in Muscat, Nizwa's famous market, dozens of smaller wilayat markets across the country — typically run mornings from around eight or nine until early afternoon, close for several hours during the hottest part of the day, then reopen in the early evening until well after dark. This isn't inefficiency; it's a completely rational response to a climate that makes midday outdoor commerce actively unpleasant for everyone involved, vendors included.

Friday complicates things further for anyone still calibrated to a Western Monday-to-Friday mental model. Oman's weekend runs Thursday and Friday, and Friday mornings in particular see souqs either closed entirely or running drastically shortened hours — Nizwa Souq, for instance, compresses to a brief window before midday prayers dominate the schedule. And yet Friday is paradoxically also when things get busiest, as families flood markets to stock up for the week ahead once the shortened window opens, and the famous livestock markets kick into gear as early as six in the morning, a genuinely spectacular scene that has nothing to do with grocery shopping and everything to do with centuries of trading tradition still very much alive.

For newcomers who find this rhythm too unpredictable to build a weekly routine around, large supermarket chains offer the obvious workaround — longer, more consistent hours, familiar fixed pricing, air conditioning that makes the whole exercise considerably less character-building. But treating the souq purely as a scheduling inconvenience misses what it actually offers: fresher produce, better prices on staples if you know roughly what things should cost, and a genuine window into how the country actually organises its days around something other than a retail calendar imported from somewhere else entirely.

## Turkey: The Bazaar Is a Relationship, the Supermarket Is a Transaction

Turkish shopping culture runs two entirely parallel systems that rarely overlap in either pricing or etiquette, and understanding which one you're in at any given moment saves considerable embarrassment. Supermarkets and department stores operate on fixed prices, full stop — attempting to haggle there marks you instantly, unmistakably, as someone who hasn't done their homework. The weekly neighbourhood pazar, by contrast, is where the actual food culture lives: seasonal, regional produce, genuinely better quality than most supermarket equivalents, sold by vendors who've often worked the same patch for decades and know their regulars by name and preference.

Here's the twist that catches most first-timers off guard: haggling, while culturally present and historically rooted in bazaar tradition, is not actually the reflexive norm many visitors assume it to be. Plenty of ordinary Turks shopping their own local pazar never attempt to negotiate at all, treating posted or quoted prices as reasonably fair to begin with. Where bargaining genuinely happens — carpet shops, tourist-facing stalls in Istanbul's Grand Bazaar, certain boutique purchases — it functions less as aggressive price warfare and more as a social ritual, one that typically opens with tea, a bit of conversation about where you're from and what brings you here, and only then, unhurried, moves toward an actual number.

The etiquette that trips up visitors most consistently isn't the pricing at all — it's the pace. A Turkish market transaction, particularly at the pazar, is not meant to be efficient in the Western retail sense. Skipping the small talk, refusing the offered tea, rushing straight to payment: none of this is catastrophic, but all of it reads as slightly cold, a missed opportunity for the actual social texture that makes Turkish market culture what it is. And for genuinely good deals on imported specialty items — foreign cheeses, unusual pantry staples — the smaller discount supermarket chains, not the bazaars, are where savvy expats actually shop, even if a few skip alcohol entirely and require a separate stop.

## The Verdict

Oman asks you to reorganise your entire concept of when shopping happens, around heat and faith rather than convenience, and rewards the adjustment with a genuinely fascinating window into a rhythm most Western visitors have never had to consider seriously. Turkey asks something gentler but arguably more socially demanding: slow down, accept the tea, have the conversation, understand that haggling is a relationship ritual rather than a transaction hack. If I'm grading on sheer logistical challenge, Oman wins by a wide margin. If I'm grading on which grocery run I'd rather actually be doing on a Tuesday afternoon, it's Istanbul's pazar, tea in hand, every single time.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com Oman forum — paraphrased: a newcomer was warned that planning a Friday grocery run around Western weekday assumptions leads to repeated disappointment, since Thursday–Friday is the actual local weekend and shopping hours shift accordingly.*

> *Canvas Club Oman blog — paraphrased: a longtime resident advised visiting the livestock and Friday markets at first light, noting that the energy and freshest selection both disappear noticeably by mid-morning.*

> *Daily Sabah Expat Corner — paraphrased: a columnist cautioned newcomers against assuming every pazar vendor wants to haggle, pointing out that many Turks have never bargained in their lives and find aggressive negotiation attempts more awkward than charming.*

## Conclusion

Neither of these shopping cultures is going to bend toward the twenty-four-hour, frictionless convenience most visitors arrive expecting, and that's precisely the point. Oman's shuttered midday souqs and Thursday–Friday weekend aren't inefficiencies waiting to be optimised away — they're a country organising commerce around its own climate and faith rather than an imported retail calendar. Turkey's tea-first, unhurried bazaar pace isn't a sales tactic — it's the actual social fabric the transaction is wrapped in. Show up expecting either to run on your schedule and you'll spend your whole stay mildly frustrated. Show up willing to reorganise your week around theirs, and you'll leave with better produce, better stories, and considerably less patience for the sterile supermarket aisles waiting for you back home.
