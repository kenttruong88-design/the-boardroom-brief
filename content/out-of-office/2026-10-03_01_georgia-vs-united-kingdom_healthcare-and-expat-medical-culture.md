# The NHS Will See You Eventually. Tbilisi Will See You This Afternoon.

🇬🇪 Georgia 🇬🇧 United Kingdom
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two healthcare systems, two entirely different relationships with the concept of time. One nation has built a cradle-to-grave service so beloved that criticising it is treated as a minor treason, and so overwhelmed that "beloved" and "accessible within a reasonable window" have quietly become unrelated concepts. The other, a small Caucasian republic most people can't find on a map, runs a wildly deregulated, cash-friendly private system where you can see a cardiologist on a Tuesday and pay less for the full consultation than you'd pay for a round of pub drinks in Clapham.

I have used both. I have sat in a Tbilisi clinic lobby that looked like a boutique hotel, and I have sat in an NHS walk-in centre in Leeds that looked like the set of a documentary about civilisational collapse. Neither experience was what I expected, and both taught me something uncomfortable about what "good healthcare" actually means when you strip away the national mythology.

## Do's & Don'ts: Georgia 🇬🇪

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry cash or card for private clinics — most expat-grade care runs private and pays out of pocket, with insurance reimbursement after | Assume the public system will accommodate you — it exists, but it's built for citizens with Georgian-language paperwork and patience |
| Shop pharmacies like a connoisseur — prices and stock genuinely vary block to block, and pharmacists diagnose freely | Expect an appointment system — in most private clinics you simply turn up, or call same-day, and get seen |
| Get a reputable expat-insurance policy before you need one — New Hospitals and Medicover network clinics are excellent if you're covered | Wait for a "proper" referral chain before seeing a specialist — Georgians go straight to the specialist, no GP gatekeeper required |

## Do's & Don'ts: United Kingdom 🇬🇧

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a GP practice within days of arriving, even if you feel invincible — it's the gateway to everything else | Expect to be seen same-day for anything short of an emergency — "urgent" and "this week" are not synonyms here |
| Use 111 online or NHS 111 by phone before defaulting to A&E — triage genuinely routes you faster | Assume private healthcare doesn't exist — it does, it's just culturally treated like a guilty secret the middle class doesn't discuss at dinner |
| Pay the Immigration Health Surcharge upfront as part of your visa and treat the NHS as "free at the point of use," not "fast" | Confuse "free" with "unlimited" — dental, optical and a great deal of mental health support require real money regardless |

## Georgia: The Deregulated Miracle Nobody Talks About

Georgia's healthcare system is what happens when a post-Soviet state privatises almost everything and lets the market sort out the rest, for better and for occasionally alarming worse. There is a nominal public system, the Universal Healthcare Programme, which covers citizens for a surprisingly generous basket of services — but foreigners, expats, and digital nomads overwhelmingly operate in the private sector, where the entire culture of medicine is unrecognisable to anyone raised on the NHS model.

Here is the thing that stuns every Western transplant: you do not need a referral to see a specialist. None. You wake up with a weird heart flutter, you call a cardiology clinic, and you are sitting across from an actual cardiologist within 48 hours, often sooner, for a fee that would make a Manhattan urgent-care bill blush — typically 80 to 150 lari, somewhere around £25 to £45. Blood panels, ultrasounds, MRIs: all available on a walk-in or next-day basis at private diagnostic centres scattered through Tbilisi, Batumi and Kutaisi, priced like a nice dinner rather than a financial emergency.

Pharmacies compound the strangeness. Georgian pharmacists dispense a startling range of medications over the counter that would require a prescription almost anywhere in Western Europe — antibiotics among them, which is a genuine public health concern the country is only belatedly beginning to address, but which in the short term means a sore throat doesn't require booking a doctor at all. You describe your symptoms to the pharmacist, who is functionally a general practitioner with a till.

The catch, and it is a real one, is quality variance. The best private clinics — New Hospitals, Medicover, Evex's flagship facilities — are genuinely excellent, staffed by Western-trained doctors and kitted out like Dubai spas. Venture outside that tier, particularly into regional or public facilities, and standards drop fast, English disappears, and equipment ages visibly. The system rewards people who do their homework and punishes those who assume "doctor" means the same baseline everywhere. Dentistry, meanwhile, is such good value that "dental tourism to Tbilisi" is now a genuine, searchable phrase, and I have personally watched a Welsh friend fly in for a full set of veneers and fly out having spent less than a UK consultation fee alone.

## United Kingdom: Religion With a Waiting List

The NHS occupies a psychologically bizarre position in British life: it is simultaneously a source of near-religious national pride and the single most reliable topic to make a Brit sigh theatrically into their tea. Both reactions are earned. The principle — comprehensive care, free at the point of use, funded through general taxation — is one of the more genuinely admirable things a wealthy nation has ever organised itself to do. The execution, after more than a decade of underfunding and a workforce crisis nobody in Westminster seems able to fix, is a lottery of waiting times that would be considered a national emergency literally anywhere else.

Registering with a GP is the entire system's front door, and it is not optional in practice — without a GP, you cannot be referred onward for anything short of an ambulance-worthy emergency. Getting that first GP appointment, however, now routinely takes one to three weeks for anything non-urgent, longer in underserved areas, and the booking ritual itself — ringing at 8am sharp, redialling forty times, being told all slots are gone by 8:04 — has become a dark national in-joke. Specialist referrals compound the wait: an elective orthopaedic consultation can run four to twelve months depending on your postcode, and that variance by geography, the so-called postcode lottery, is one of the system's most quietly damning features.

What the NHS does extraordinarily well is catastrophe. Heart attack, stroke, a car accident, cancer diagnosed and treated — the system mobilises with a speed and competence that private American healthcare, for all its gleaming lobbies, frequently cannot match, and certainly not without a six-figure bill attached. Where it fails is the mundane middle: the dodgy knee, the recurring migraine, the skin thing you've been meaning to get looked at for eight months. For that tier, a growing number of residents — not just wealthy ones, ordinary middle-class families — now carry private health insurance or pay out of pocket for a same-week private GP appointment, treating the NHS as the emergency net and private care as the actual front line, a cultural shift the system's most devoted defenders are reluctant to admit is already well underway.

## The Verdict

If I'm choosing on sheer speed and value for routine care, Georgia wins embarrassingly easily — a next-day cardiologist for £30 is not a healthcare system, it's a magic trick, and nobody who's waited four months for an NHS referral gets to pretend otherwise. But Georgia's excellence is a private-tier excellence; fall outside the good clinics or into a genuine catastrophe, and you want Britain's free, deep, disaster-grade safety net underneath you, not a lari-denominated bill. The honest answer is that the smart expat wants Georgian speed for the Tuesday niggle and NHS depth for the Wednesday emergency — and the fact that no single country currently offers both is, frankly, an indictment of everyone's healthcare policy, not just one government's.

## Conclusion

Neither system is designed with you, specifically, in mind — Georgia's is designed for a market of transactions, Britain's for a population it has long since outgrown the budget to serve properly. The genuine skill in expat life is knowing which country to be sick in for which problem, and having the insurance, cash reserve, or sheer luck to get there. Georgia will make you believe healthcare can be fast, cheap and dignified all at once. Britain will remind you, usually around month four of a referral wait, that it can also be free, excellent, and agonisingly slow, all at the same time, and that you're meant to be grateful regardless.
