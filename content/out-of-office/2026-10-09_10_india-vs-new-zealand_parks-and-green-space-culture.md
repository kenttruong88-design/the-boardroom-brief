# One Country Treats a Park Like Precious, Contested Real Estate. The Other Just Has So Much Nature It Barely Notices.

🇮🇳 India 🇳🇿 New Zealand
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Green space reveals a country's relationship with scarcity faster than almost anything else, and India and New Zealand sit at genuinely opposite extremes of that scarcity, in ways that reshape everything from your morning routine to your weekend plans.

In India's major cities, a good park is a precious, fiercely used civic resource — packed by six in the morning with walkers, yoga groups, badminton players, and laughter-yoga clubs (yes, that's a real and serious thing) all competing for a limited patch of green before the heat and the day's business take over. In New Zealand, green space is so abundant, so constitutionally built into the national identity, that it's barely treated as a resource at all — it's simply the given backdrop to ordinary life, a third of the country under conservation protection, national parks a casual weekend drive away rather than a planned pilgrimage. One nation treasures its parks because they're scarce. The other barely registers its abundance because it's never had to.

## Do's & Don'ts: India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get to a good urban park early — by 6am in most major cities, it's already genuinely busy with walkers and exercise groups | Expect consistent park quality and maintenance across a city; it varies dramatically by neighbourhood and municipal investment |
| Join a morning walking group or outdoor yoga circle — it's one of the fastest, friendliest ways to build a local social circle | Dismiss laughter yoga or group calisthenics in the park as eccentric; these are genuinely mainstream, well-attended daily rituals |
| Treat a well-maintained park like Lodhi Garden or Cubbon Park as a real civic achievement worth respecting and supporting | Assume green space is evenly distributed; access skews heavily toward wealthier neighbourhoods in most Indian cities |

## Do's & Don'ts: New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clean your hiking boots before and after a track walk — biosecurity rules against spreading pests and disease are taken extremely seriously | Feed wildlife, pick native plants, or go off-track in a national park; conservation etiquette here is close to sacred, not a polite suggestion |
| Take weekend access to genuinely wild nature for granted — it's a normal part of life here, not a special occasion | Underestimate a "walk" — many popular tracks are genuine multi-hour hikes requiring real preparation, not a casual park stroll |
| Visit a Great Walk if you can get a booking — they're internationally renowned and often require reserving well in advance | Assume all green space is equally wild; urban parks like Hagley Park or the Auckland Domain are manicured civic spaces, distinct from the backcountry |

## India: Scarce, Precious, and Used to the Last Blade of Grass

Urban green space in India is scarce enough, relative to population density, that the parks which do exist get used with an intensity that surprises most newcomers. Reporting from citizenmatters.in and similar city-focused outlets has tracked just how unevenly tree cover and park access are distributed across Indian metros — wealthier neighbourhoods tend to have dramatically better green infrastructure than poorer ones, a gap that mirrors broader inequality rather than correcting it. But where a genuinely good park does exist — Delhi's Lodhi Garden, Bangalore's Cubbon Park — it becomes an extraordinary, fiercely loved piece of civic life, packed from dawn with a cross-section of the city that cuts across class in a way few other public spaces manage.

That dawn crowd is worth describing properly, because it's one of the more charming things about Indian urban life: walking groups moving in loose, chatting clusters, informal badminton games, yoga circles, and yes, laughter yoga clubs — groups that gather specifically to practise forced, rhythmic laughter as a genuine wellness exercise, taken completely seriously by participants and genuinely effective by most accounts. This is a culture that has figured out how to extract maximum communal and physical value out of a genuinely limited green resource, compressing an entire morning's worth of exercise, socialising, and ritual into the window before the heat and the working day take over. The urgency isn't really about the park closing — most stay open — it's about getting there before the window of pleasant, walkable morning air closes, which in a hot, polluted megacity is a real and shrinking resource in its own right.

## New Zealand: So Much Nature It's Barely a Talking Point

New Zealand's relationship with green space is almost the structural opposite: abundance so complete it's stopped being treated as a resource at all, simply absorbed into the texture of ordinary life. The Department of Conservation manages an extraordinary share of the country's total land area as protected conservation estate — national parks, forest parks, marine reserves — accessible, in most cases, via a weekend drive rather than a planned expedition, and New Zealanders treat this access with a casualness that can genuinely astonish visitors from more land-scarce countries. A "quick walk" suggested by a local colleague might turn out to be a genuine multi-hour hike through genuinely remote terrain, undertaken with the same offhand energy someone else might bring to suggesting a trip to the corner shop.

What takes longer to absorb, and matters more than the hiking itself, is how seriously the conservation ethic runs underneath all that casual access. Biosecurity rules — cleaning boots between tracks to avoid spreading pathogens like kauri dieback, staying strictly on marked trails, never feeding or disturbing native wildlife — aren't polite suggestions here, they're close to a civic religion, enforced by social pressure as much as by law, because New Zealand's isolated ecosystem and unique native species are genuinely fragile in ways locals understand instinctively and newcomers need to actively learn. Urban parks, meanwhile — Christchurch's Hagley Park, Auckland's Domain — occupy a different, more manicured category entirely, civic green space rather than wilderness, and distinguishing the two registers, when to treat a space as backcountry versus ordinary park, is one of the first real cultural literacies a newcomer needs to build.

## The Verdict

New Zealand, and honestly it isn't close, though I want to be fair to India's parks on their own terms — the dawn energy in a good Indian city park, the laughter yoga, the sheer democratic mixing of a crowded morning walk, has a social vibrancy New Zealand's quieter, more solitary nature access simply doesn't replicate. But abundance changes what's possible in a way scarcity never can: New Zealand offers access to genuine, vast, barely-touched wilderness as an unremarkable weekend option, while India's best parks, wonderful as they are, remain fundamentally limited, contested civic resources serving a population that will always outstrip the available green space. I'll miss the dawn badminton games. I won't miss competing for a park bench.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/india — a newcomer to Bangalore asked why Cubbon Park was "basically a festival" at 6:30am, and the top reply explained that the walking-and-yoga crowd had simply colonised the only pleasant hour of the day before the heat and traffic took over.*

> *Reddit r/newzealand — a thread on biosecurity boot-cleaning stations included a visitor admitting they'd skipped one once, out of laziness, and been quietly but firmly corrected by a stranger on the trail who clearly considered it a matter of genuine civic importance.*

> *Internations Auckland — a longtime resident described realising, a year in, that they'd stopped being impressed by weekend access to literal rainforest, calling it "the most New Zealand thing that's ever happened to me — taking a national park for granted."*

## Conclusion

What India and New Zealand really demonstrate, side by side, is that a relationship with nature is shaped entirely by how much of it you've got. Scarcity in India has produced an intensely social, almost competitive daily ritual around a precious shared resource. Abundance in New Zealand has produced something closer to quiet reverence mixed with total casualness, a population that genuinely doesn't have to fight for its green space and has built an entire conservation ethic around protecting that privilege instead. Neither country is wrong about what a park is for. They've just never had to agree on how much of it there should be.
