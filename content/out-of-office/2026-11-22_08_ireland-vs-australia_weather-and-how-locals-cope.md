# Ireland Has Weather as a Personality; Australia Has Weather as a Threat

🇮🇪 Ireland vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ireland has no climate. It has a mood. The sky is grey, the rain is the kind that hangs in the air without falling, and the temperature has the commitment of a man deciding between two pubs. An Irish person can describe four seasons in one afternoon and will assume you cannot. Weather is the first thing they discuss, the last thing they complain about and the only subject on which strangers will agree.

Australia has a different relationship with the sky. It is bright, enormous and slightly murderous. The sun can burn you in fifteen minutes, the summers are for survival and the winters, which nobody warned you about, are cold in a way that feels like a betrayal. The great Australian secret is that the country is spectacular outdoors and a bit rubbish indoors. I have lived in both. One country makes you wet and cheerful. The other makes you hot, and then cold, and wonders why you are upset.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇮🇪 Ireland ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry a waterproof jacket every day | Trust a sunny morning |
| Talk about the weather with anyone, anywhere | Treat it as small talk and ignore the answer |
| Dress in layers, in any season | Leave the house without a plan B |
| Embrace the rain on a walk | Cancel plans for a drizzle |

| 🇦🇺 Australia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a hat and sunscreen outdoors | Assume cloud means safety |
| Check a house's heating and insulation before you rent | Believe it never gets cold |
| Drink water and plan your day around the heat | Run at noon in January |
| Buy a decent heater and a good jumper | Treat winter as a joke |

## 🇮🇪 Ireland

Irish weather is changeable rather than extreme. It rarely gets very hot or very cold, but it gets wet, windy and dull on a scale that shapes the whole national character. Locals cope by not coping. They have decided that the weather is a fact, like gravity, and that complaining about it is a kind of hospitality. A greeting is often a weather report, and a good conversation can start with a comment about the rain and finish three hours later with the meaning of life.

The practical side is simple. You wear layers, a good waterproof and sensible shoes. You plan your day around the possibility of a shower, and you keep an umbrella in the car, in the office and in the porch. Houses are built to keep the weather out, with insulation, double glazing and central heating, because the cold and the damp are constant companions. You do not freeze indoors, and the idea that you might would be received with horror. In Ireland, the weather happens outside, where it belongs.

What you get, in exchange, is a certain softness. The countryside is green, the light is gentle and nobody gets sunburnt at a funeral. The downside is the greyness, which can sap the spirits of those who are not born to it. Winter days are short, the rain is persistent and the occasional blue sky is celebrated like a minor miracle. An Irish expat in Australia is, often, someone who wanted the sun and has since discovered the price.

For a newcomer, the best Irish advice is to carry a jacket and stop fighting the rain. People will not apologise for it, and they will not cancel because of it. A wet Saturday is just a Saturday, and the pub is warm. It is a philosophy of acceptance, and I find it quite restful.

## 🇦🇺 Australia

Australian weather is the opposite of understatement. In a January 2024 column, Irish Times writer Laura Kennedy, an Irish emigrant in Canberra, described sitting in a hair salon where the temperature outside read 34 degrees on her smartwatch, and explained to the hairdresser that she gets sleepy when her head is warm. It is a small, funny moment, and it tells you everything: heat in Australia is a fact of life, and the newcomer is permanently behind.

The shock is winter. A July 2025 column by the same writer notes that Canberra temperatures hit below minus seven one night that week, and that Australian homes are built, in her phrase, neither for summer nor winter. SBS reported in June 2024 that Sydney and Melbourne had their chilliest morning of the year, with lows of 6.5C and 1.4C respectively, and that central Queensland had its iciest night on record at 5.6C. Those numbers would barely trouble a Dublin morning. But Dublin has a heating system.

The reason is housing. SBS quotes RMIT senior lecturer Nicola Willand saying most homes in Australia are poorly insulated, with inefficient heaters that can be expensive to run, and that homes built before the national house energy rating scheme began in 2004 are particularly affected. At the University of Melbourne, Dr Chris Jensen wrote that Australian houses lack thick insulation, tight air seals and high-performance glazing, and that research for the Australian Greenhouse Office as far back as 2005 showed Australia's then five-star minimum standards were about two stars below equivalent standards in the UK, US and Canada. He also notes that a heater's warmth will only last for as long as it is on. That is the sentence every Irish expat tattoos on their heart in July.

So Australians cope in their own way: with tough skin, a good jumper and an attitude. Kennedy's 2024 column carried the headline that Australians are tougher than we are, and one gets the sense that is partly because they have to be. The country is built for the outdoors, and indoor comfort has long been an afterthought. If you are moving from Ireland, check the heating, ask about the insulation, and do not believe anyone who tells you it never gets cold.

## The Verdict

Ireland wins for indoor comfort, and Australia wins for outdoor life. That is the headline, and it matters more than most expats admit. An Irish winter is wet and grey, but you are warm and dry when you come inside. An Australian winter can be sunny and clear, but you may be colder in your living room than you were in the street.

If you can find a well-insulated flat, Australia is a better place for most of the year, and the beaches and the light will lift your mood for the long haul. If you cannot, you will spend your first winter in three layers, wondering whether the heater is a decoration. My honest recommendation is to treat the home as the first priority. Find a place built after 2004, with a heating system you trust and windows that close, and you will love the climate. Otherwise, you will love it from a safe distance.

## Conclusion

Ireland will give you rain and a warm kitchen. Australia will give you sun and a cold lounge, and a profound respect for the humble jumper. Pick your house before you pick your suburb, buy a decent heater and a better hat, and accept that you will complain about the weather in both countries. That, at least, is a skill you already have.
