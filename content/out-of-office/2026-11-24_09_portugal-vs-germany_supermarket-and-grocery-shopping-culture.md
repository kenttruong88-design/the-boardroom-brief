# The Till Is Not a Race, Unless You Are in Germany

🇵🇹 Portugal vs 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have bought groceries in sixty countries and been shouted at, silently, in precisely one. Nobody in Portugal has ever made me feel like a criminal for being slow with a bag of oranges. In Germany, I once felt the collective disapproval of a queue land on the back of my neck like a damp towel, and all I was doing was finding my debit card.

A supermarket is the fastest way to understand a country, faster than any museum and considerably more honest. It tells you what people eat, how they treat strangers, and whether they believe a Sunday is for living or for rest. Portugal and Germany both sell tinned fish and both have a baker on the corner, and that is where the similarities politely end. One of them wants you to enjoy your shopping. The other wants you gone.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇹 Portugal ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your own heavy-duty bag, or pay for one at the till | Expect a freebie carrier to appear out of politeness |
| Go to the fish counter and ask what came in this morning | Assume the packaged tray is the best fish in the shop |
| Use the loyalty card, because everyone does | Skip it and watch the receipt wince at you |
| Visit the municipal market for produce | Treat the market as a tourist set and haggle like a pirate |

| 🇩🇪 Germany ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Move to the packing shelf and bag after paying | Pack slowly at the conveyor while a queue forms |
| Return your bottles to the Pfand machine for the deposit | Throw away the cash you were quietly charged |
| Carry a one-euro coin for the trolley | Expect to unlock a trolley with good intentions |
| Shop on Saturday for the Sunday ahead | Arrive on a Sunday and expect anything but a shuttered door |

## 🇵🇹 Portugal

The Portuguese supermarket is a place where people actually spend time. Continente is the biggest chain, and most of its stores come with a bakery, a butcher, a fish counter and a deli, so a weekly shop feels less like a chore and more like a small tour of the day's food. Pingo Doce is everywhere in towns and residential streets and is famous for its bakery and its takeaway. Lidl and Aldi are there for the thrifty, Mercadona for own-brand loyalists, and Auchan for the hypermarket crowd who like a fresh fish counter the size of a lorry. The Portugal News guide to these chains reads like a menu, which tells you plenty.

The hours are civilised. Most supermarkets open around eight or nine and close around nine at night, with some bigger ones running until eleven or midnight, and, crucially, they generally do not close on Sundays. That alone makes Portugal a more functional country than half of northern Europe. Smaller towns close earlier and some rural shops disappear for lunch, which I consider a feature rather than a bug, because a country that closes for lunch is a country that has its priorities in order.

The loyalty card is not optional in spirit. Continente, Pingo Doce, Lidl and Auchan all have one, free, and the whole population is carrying all of them. Hand over your phone at the till and you are given the faint approval of the cashier. Bags are the one place I will give the Portuguese a small telling-off: locals bring their own sturdy ones, and if you forget yours you pay. Carry one. It is not a punishment, it is a rehearsal for adulthood.

Then there are the municipal markets, which in bigger towns run daily and in rural ones once or twice a week. Lisbon's Campo de Ourique and Porto's Bolhão are the famous ones, and yes, they are lovely, and no, you do not need to treat them like a pilgrimage. The point is that a Portuguese person can buy tomatoes from a human who knows which field they came from and then buy washing-up liquid from a hypermarket in the same afternoon without any existential crisis. Imported British or American products exist, but they are limited and expensive, so go native or go without. Your craving for salt-and-vinegar crisps is a personal failing, not a supply-chain problem, and the nearest decent substitute is a tin of something in olive oil. Eat it on the pavement, like a local, and feel your standards improve.

## 🇩🇪 Germany

German supermarkets are brilliant at what they do, and what they do is process you. The best-known quirk is the checkout, designed around the idea that the till is a conveyor belt and you are an inconvenience on it. At the discounters such as Aldi and Lidl, you are expected to scan, pay, then retreat to a long shelf behind the cashier to pack. A British forum thread from years back is full of someone baffled by this, and the answers were exactly what you would expect: it is the rule everywhere, discounters are the worst, and bagging at the till will irritate whoever is next in the queue. The Local's survival guide suggests the same, with the extra tip of buying yourself packing time by putting a kiwi and a potato in the same bag. I did not make that up, and I am delighted by it.

Then there is Pfand, the bottle deposit of up to twenty-five cents, which sounds trivial until you realise you are carrying a bag full of someone else's money. You feed the empties into a machine, receive a ticket, and spend that ticket at the till. It is the most functional piece of environmental policy I have ever used, and it makes the average German supermarket a place where waste is quietly ashamed of itself.

Trolleys are chained together and need a one-euro coin, returned when you bring the trolley back. Cash is still taken seriously, and a cashier might say yes to a card that turns out to mean only German debit cards, so ask properly. Self-checkouts exist, for instance at Rewe, but plenty of shoppers still queue at staffed lanes, because Germans trust a human cashier the way they trust a train timetable.

And then there is Sunday. Most supermarkets are shut. Not closed in the lazy Mediterranean sense, but closed with the finality of a legal principle. Shops can also close the day before a public holiday, so a bank-holiday weekend becomes a logistical operation requiring forward planning. Train station shops are the exception. If you run out of milk on Sunday, you are a tourist or a fool, and possibly both.

## The Verdict

Portugal wins, and it is not even close. I admire German efficiency in the way I admire a very good dentist: the results are impressive, I would not stay for dinner. The Pfand system is genuinely clever and I will defend it to my grave, and the discounters are remarkably cheap and consistent. But a shopping trip is a human activity, and Germany designed its checkout as if humans were the problem.

Portugal understands that food shopping is half logistics and half pleasure. You can buy decent bread, argue with a fish, use a loyalty card that actually does something, and do it all on a Sunday without being judged by a queue. Germany gives you speed and rules. Portugal gives you open doors and a bit of time. If I am hungry and it is a Sunday, I know which flag I am standing under.

## Conclusion

Here is the real difference. In Germany the shop serves the system, and the system is very good, until you are the one slow customer standing between it and its quarterly targets. In Portugal the shop serves the afternoon. You are allowed to pause, to ask, to hold up the queue by two seconds because the fish looked interesting.

I do not say Germany is wrong. I say Germany is efficient, and efficiency is a lovely quality in a dentist and a grim one in a place where you buy your dinner. Pack your own bags in both countries, carry coins in one of them, and remember which country will let you buy bread on Sunday. The rest is just tinned fish.
