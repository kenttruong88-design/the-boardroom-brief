# Bangkok Commutes on Hope and a Motorbike; China Commutes on a Schedule and a QR Code

🇹🇭 Thailand vs 🇨🇳 China

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Getting to work in Bangkok is an act of faith. You leave the house at an hour that feels morally wrong, negotiate a motorbike taxi through a gap that physics disapproves of, ascend a staircase that is in the middle of the pavement for reasons nobody has ever explained, and then board an elevated train that is air-conditioned to the temperature of a meat locker. You arrive, sweaty and cold, with the faint sense that you have won something.

Getting to work in a big Chinese city is an exercise in being processed. You scan a code at a turnstile, you follow a floor arrow to the precise spot on the platform where the door will open, and you enter a train so well organised that the only chaos is the interior of the carriage itself. One country commutes by improvisation and the other commutes by infrastructure, and the result is that expats in both complain about the other country's version while secretly admiring it. I have done both for long enough to know that the truth is more complicated, and more humid.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇹🇭 Thailand ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry a Rabbit Card or stored-value pass and top it up in advance | Queue at the ticket machine in your suit at 8am |
| Insist on the meter in a taxi, it is required by law | Accept a fixed price from the first driver who waves |
| Give up your seat to monks, the elderly and the pregnant | Plant yourself in the priority seat and look at your phone |
| Wear a layer, the train is freezing | Wear a shirt you cannot afford to sweat through outdoors |

| 🇨🇳 China ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Have a phone wallet or transit app ready before you reach the gate | Count on a foreign card to open the barrier |
| Let passengers off before you push on | Stand in front of the doors and block the flow |
| Learn which end of the platform your exit is nearest to | Walk the length of the station during rush hour |
| Keep your bag in front of you in a crush | Assume personal space is a recognised concept |

## 🇹🇭 Thailand

Bangkok is a city that has spent decades trying to solve traffic and has produced an extraordinary amount of evidence that it cannot. The BTS Skytrain and the MRT are the escape hatches. The Bangkok Post's profile of BTS commuters makes the case in human terms: a physiotherapist who used to wake at five in the morning and spend close to two hours in traffic now makes a ten-minute train ride; an accountant combines a motorbike taxi at 20 baht with a 40-baht train fare each way, spending around 120 baht a day, and describes it as a penny-pinching but worthwhile bargain. The paper says more than 700,000 people use the BTS each day.

The system is neat, if limited. Expatica, updated in November 2026, lists BTS single fares of 17 to 47 baht, a one-day pass at 150 baht valid on the Green Line only, and a Rabbit Card with a 120-baht issuing fee that may not work across all MRT lines. That last point is the most Bangkok sentence ever written: the transport system has more than one payment system, and they do not always talk to each other. Expat Arrivals notes that the MRT has four operating lines running from 6am to midnight, that buses are a lottery, and that taxi meters are required by law to be switched on, which is a law enforced with the enthusiasm of a mild suggestion.

And then there is the human texture. Riders complain that the priority seats are occupied by those who have no business in them, and that rush hour carriages become so densely packed that a school administrator once needed two stations to wiggle to the exit. It is a commute with soul, a sense of shared enterprise, and the permanent smell of street food on the wind. You do not so much travel in Bangkok as you are collected, deposited and humbled. It is wonderful, and I would not want to do it in a suit.

The cost of the commute is another part of the story. In Bangkok, the cheap tier of transport, the motorbike taxi, the songthaew, the river boat, is where the real city lives, and it is gloriously cheap. A short hop on the back of a bike can cost less than a coffee, and it will teach you more about traffic physics than any textbook. But the cost of the system is borne by those who can least afford it, and the gap between the glass-fronted Skytrain and the street below is a fair summary of the city's inequality. You ride the train above it all, and look down on the people who built it.

## 🇨🇳 China

China's big-city metros are the sort of thing that makes visitors from older cities go quiet. Shanghai and Beijing run some of the largest and busiest metro networks on earth, new lines open at a pace that would make a London planner weep, and the system is thoroughly integrated: one phone, one code, one tap, and you are through. Stations are clean, signage is bilingual, and the platform has floor markings that tell you exactly where to stand, and, more importantly, where to look foolish if you do not.

What the guidebooks rarely say is how much of the experience is crowd choreography. At peak hours, stations install barriers to hold back the flow, staff with megaphones direct people into lines, and the carriage fills to the point where the concept of personal space becomes a historical curiosity. Commuting is not so much a ride as a managed compression. It is astonishingly efficient, and it is also, in a very particular way, a contact sport.

For an expat, the real obstacle is the digital layer. The best experience requires a local phone wallet linked to a Chinese bank account, which is not instant to arrange for a newcomer, and until then you are fumbling with ticket machines and apps in a language you are still learning. Once you are in, though, the whole thing is smooth. Taxis arrive via app within minutes. Bikes are unlocked by code. There is almost no need to speak to anyone, which, after a week in Bangkok, can feel like a luxury. I will note that I am drawing here on years of living with these systems rather than on any one source, and the details change fast, so check the current local rules.

In China, the cost of commuting is mostly hidden by the sheer scale of subsidy and the extraordinary spread of cheap fares. A metro ride can cost a few yuan, and the network penetrates districts that older systems abandoned decades ago. That makes the metro a great leveller: office workers, delivery riders and grandmothers with shopping trolleys all share the same carriage. The result is a commuting culture that is less about class and more about stamina. I recall a woman asleep standing up in a packed carriage, perfectly balanced, and I have never been so impressed by anything in a transport system.

## The Verdict

China wins on efficiency, and Thailand wins on human beings, and it is the latter that I miss. A Chinese commute will get you to work on time, in a bearable condition, with your dignity broadly intact. A Bangkok commute will deliver you to work late, a bit damp, and with a story. If your priority is predictability, move to China, learn the app and never look back. If your priority is having a good anecdote for the office, Bangkok is the cheaper option.

But let me be honest: I would take the Chinese metro every working day of my life, and I would take the Bangkok motorbike taxi every night I wanted to feel alive. The Bangkok system is improving, and the Rabbit Card is a step in the right direction. The Chinese system is excellent, and perfectly cold. A commute is a daily reminder of what a city thinks of its people. Bangkok thinks you are resourceful. China thinks you are a unit of throughput. Both, in their way, are right.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Bangkok Post, a physiotherapist who rides the BTS — her ten-minute train journey replaced a commute that could take close to two hours in traffic, though the seats reserved for the elderly and pregnant are often taken by others.*

> *Bangkok Post, an accountant — combines a 20-baht motorbike taxi with a 40-baht train fare each way, about 120 baht a day, calls the motorbike unsafe but quick, and says she has to penny-pinch but still thinks it worth it.*

> *Bangkok Post, a school administrator — rush-hour trains are so packed that once she needed two stations to wiggle her way to the exit, and she is asking for more carriages.*

## Conclusion

If you move to Bangkok, buy a Rabbit Card, make peace with the humidity and learn the exact word for "meter". If you move to China, set up your phone wallet on day one, memorise your station exit and accept that you will be touched by strangers on a daily basis. In either country you will be late once, lost twice and, at some point, deeply moved by a stranger who shows you which way to go. That is the real purpose of public transport. It is, whatever the engineers tell you, a social experiment with wheels.
