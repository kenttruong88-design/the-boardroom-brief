# One Country Makes Healthcare Free and the Wait Nearly Fatal, the Other Makes You Pay and Skip the Line Entirely

🇨🇦 Canada vs Nigeria 🇳🇬
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I once waited eleven hours in a Toronto emergency room with what turned out to be a straightforward kidney stone, watched by a triage nurse who assured me, not unkindly, that I "wasn't dying enough" to be seen faster. In Lagos, I called a private hospital about a nasty ear infection at 9 p.m. and had a doctor examining me within ninety minutes, cash in hand, no appointment necessary, no questions about my citizenship status beyond my ability to pay. Both systems worked, eventually. Neither worked the way I expected.

Canada has built a healthcare system on the principle that care shouldn't bankrupt you, funded by taxes and delivered, when it's delivered, with genuine clinical quality — the catch being the wait, which can stretch from mildly annoying to genuinely dangerous depending on what you need and where you live. Nigeria runs largely on a private, pay-as-you-go model where money buys speed and access with startling efficiency, provided you have it, and provided you understand that the public alternative is a very different, much harder experience entirely. Both systems will surprise you, and neither will apologise for the surprise.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇨🇦 Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for provincial health coverage the moment you're eligible | Expect a same-day specialist appointment for anything non-urgent |
| Use walk-in clinics for minor issues rather than the ER | Assume "free" means fast — budget serious patience for anything routine |
| Get private supplemental insurance for dental and prescriptions | Skip travel insurance during your waiting period before coverage kicks in |

**🇳🇬 Nigeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comprehensive private health insurance with evacuation coverage before you land | Rely on public hospitals for anything beyond the most basic care |
| Ask other expats which private hospitals they personally trust | Assume all private clinics maintain the same quality standard — vet carefully |
| Keep cash or a card ready — many private facilities expect payment upfront | Delay treatment hoping symptoms resolve — private care is fast, so use it |

## Canada: Free at the Point of Use, Expensive in Every Other Currency

Canadian healthcare's core promise is genuinely radical by global standards: walk into an emergency room, get treated, walk out without a bill, funded instead through taxation and delivered provincially rather than federally, which means the actual experience varies noticeably depending on whether you're in Ontario, Quebec, or a rural province stretched thin on specialists. The clinical quality, once you're actually seen, is consistently strong — Canadian-trained doctors and nurses are excellent, the equipment is modern, the follow-up care is thorough.

The wait, however, is the trade-off nobody undersells to newcomers, because it's structurally baked into a system without a price mechanism to ration demand. Non-urgent specialist referrals can take months. Family doctors, in many cities, aren't accepting new patients at all, forcing residents into a rotating cast of walk-in clinics for anything that isn't a true emergency. Emergency rooms triage by severity, not by arrival time, which is clinically correct and means that anyone with a painful-but-not-fatal condition can genuinely expect to wait the better part of a day watching sicker people, quite reasonably, jump the queue ahead of them.

Newly landed expats, particularly, hit an additional wrinkle: most provinces impose a waiting period, often three months, before public coverage kicks in for new residents, leaving a gap that catches people out if they haven't arranged private travel insurance to bridge it. Once you're through that gap and inside the system, though, the peace of mind is real — a serious illness here won't ruin you financially, even if it will test your patience in the waiting room chair.

## Nigeria: Pay to Play, and the Play Moves Fast

Nigeria's healthcare landscape is sharply, unapologetically bifurcated. Public hospitals exist, are technically affordable, and are also frequently under-resourced, understaffed, and stretched well past capacity, a reality most Nigerians and virtually all expats navigate around rather than through. The private sector, by contrast, particularly in Lagos and Abuja, has built a genuinely fast, responsive, service-oriented alternative for anyone who can pay — same-day appointments, short waits, attentive doctors, and a transactional clarity (you pay, you're seen) that Canadian patients would find almost shocking in its simplicity.

This isn't a niche expat workaround, it's the default operating mode for anyone with means, Nigerian or foreign alike, and the private hospitals that serve the expat and upper-income local community have built real reputations for quality that circulate almost entirely through word of mouth — asking fellow expats which specific hospital and even which specific doctor they trust is standard practice, because quality varies significantly between facilities in a market without the kind of uniform national accreditation newcomers might assume exists.

The genuinely essential piece of the puzzle, and the one every serious expat resource in Nigeria repeats until it becomes background noise, is comprehensive insurance with medical evacuation coverage built in — for anything beyond routine care, particularly complex surgery or serious trauma, the honest local advice is often to stabilise in Lagos and fly to London, Dubai, or Johannesburg for definitive treatment, and having evacuation coverage arranged before you need it rather than during a crisis is treated as absolutely non-negotiable by anyone who's actually lived the alternative.

## The Verdict

Canada offers dignity without a bill and asks for your patience as payment instead. Nigeria offers speed and service to anyone who can pay, and a much harder road for anyone who can't. As someone who's needed care in both, I'll admit the Nigerian private system's responsiveness spoiled me in ways that made the Canadian wait feel almost personally insulting afterward — but I've also seen what happens to people without means in each system, and Canada's floor, however slow, is a genuinely more humane one. Speed is nice. A safety net that doesn't require a credit card is nicer.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/askTO — a patient recounts an eight-hour emergency room wait for a suspected broken finger, eventually treated correctly but only after genuinely questioning whether it was worth coming in at all.*

> *Internations Lagos — an expat describes calling three different private hospitals before finding one whose English-speaking specialist and equipment quality matched what other trusted expats had actually experienced, rather than just what a hospital's own website claimed.*

> *expat.com Nigeria forum — a longtime resident stresses that evacuation insurance isn't a luxury add-on but the single most important line item in any relocation budget, recounting a colleague's costly scramble to arrange one mid-emergency.*

## Conclusion

Canada will teach you the particular, grinding patience of a system that treats you fairly but not quickly. Nigeria will teach you the sharper, more anxious vigilance of a system that treats you well only if you've done your homework and can pay for it. I've walked away from both with useful, if uncomfortable, lessons about what "good healthcare" actually means once you strip away the assumptions you arrived with. Get the insurance. Ask the other expats. Bring a book for the waiting room, wherever you land.
