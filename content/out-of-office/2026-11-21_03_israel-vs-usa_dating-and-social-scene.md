# In Israel You Get Engaged by Date Ten; in America You Are Still Defining the Relationship

🇮🇱 Israel vs 🇺🇸 United States

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

American dating is a long, anxious negotiation conducted mostly in text messages and almost entirely about hypothetical futures. You match, you banter, you meet for a drink that will be assessed against forty variables, you wait three days to reply because three days is the rule, and eventually you have a conversation about whether you are exclusive, which is a conversation that exists solely because nobody wants to be the first one to say anything real.

Israel looks at that and shrugs. Israelis say what they mean, usually before the starters arrive, and generally assume that you will say it back. A matchmaker interviewed by Business Insider put it with admirable bluntness: in Israel it would not be unheard of to meet someone, go on ten dates, get engaged and be married three months later. I have lived in cities where people took longer to choose a sofa. The difference is cultural, bureaucratic, military and entirely unsentimental, and it is a delight to watch if you are not personally on the receiving end.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇮🇱 Israel ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say what you want, plainly and early | Hint, wait and hope they pick up the subtext |
| Accept a Shabbat dinner invitation, it is a compliment | Treat an invitation to meet the family as alarming |
| Learn enough Hebrew to make people laugh | Rely on English and a charming smile alone |
| Expect strangers to approach you in cafés | Take a direct approach as rudeness |

| 🇺🇸 United States ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat the first date as an audition for a second | Announce your feelings before the third date |
| Expect the app to do the introducing | Approach a stranger at the supermarket and expect it to go well |
| Have the "what are we" talk eventually | Mention marriage before six months have passed |
| Split or negotiate the bill gracefully | Assume anyone knows who pays |

## 🇮🇱 Israel

Israeli dating is fast, warm and relentlessly direct. Jacob Maslow, writing on the Times of Israel blogs after moving there, admitted that Israeli directness first read to him as plain rude, and then noticed that a woman who is interested will simply give you her number and a man will ask a stranger out for coffee. Sophie Mendel, who left New York for Tel Aviv, describes being approached at coffee shops, on the beach, on the street and in the supermarket, and being invited to Shabbat dinner by people who barely knew her. Where Americans date through screens, Israelis date through proximity, and proximity in a small country is hard to avoid.

The structure behind this is demographic. Israelis serve in the army, meet their friends early, are often back in their parents' homes for Friday dinner, and view marriage as a thing that actually happens. The Business Insider matchmaker notes that American twenty-somethings put independence first while young Israelis tend to be more family-focused and motivated to marry. Expat.com's guide lists the usual apps, from JSwipe to OkCupid, but the culture lives off them: introductions through friends, family and the bar down the road.

For a foreigner it is not all pleasure. Maslow found that on dating apps the man is expected to message first, and replies might arrive only after ten or more messages, so he called it a numbers game. Mendel, a year in, was still single, and she put it down to language gaps and difficulty reading intentions. Friendship, she wrote, was far easier than romance. Directness, it turns out, is easier to enjoy in your own language. If you do not speak Hebrew you will be admired, fed and politely misunderstood.

The social scene around dating matters as much as the dates. In Israel, the social circle is dense, overlapping and surprisingly unavoidable: the friend of a friend turns out to be your colleague's cousin, and a bad evening can follow you into the office. That sounds claustrophobic and sometimes is, but it also gives dating a safety net that apps cannot provide. People vouch for each other. Introductions come with a reference. For a foreigner, being absorbed into a friendship group is the fastest route to meeting someone, and the least likely to end with a stranger asking for your life story in a bar.

## 🇺🇸 United States

American dating is a vast, delicately engineered ecosystem designed to keep you from ever committing to anything by accident. The first date happens in a bar, the second date is a more expensive bar, and somewhere around the fourth, you have the conversation. The census puts the average age at first marriage at roughly 29 for women and 30 for men, and you can see it in the way people behave. Nobody is in a hurry. Everyone is protecting their independence, their career, their therapist's schedule and their three-year plan.

There is a certain genius to the system, and I do not wish to be unfair. It gives people room. It lets you take your time, correct your mistakes and know yourself before you promise yourself to someone else. A first date in America can be low-stakes, a coffee and a polite goodbye, and nobody will be asking for your parents' address. It is a country that has built a great many tools for finding someone, apps for every kind of person and a polite vocabulary for every stage of ambiguity.

The cost is the vagueness. Americans have invented an entire lexicon, situationships, ghosting, soft launching, because the culture would rather invent a word than state a feeling. It is a nation of the brilliantly articulate who cannot, under any circumstances, say "I would like to see you again". If you come from a more direct place you will find it baffling, and exhausting. If you come from a more reserved one, you will find it a relief.

What America does well, and I say this with some reluctance, is give you permission to be bad at it. The culture of dating is so saturated with advice, apps, podcasts and weekend-brunch post-mortems that you can fail publicly and still be forgiven by Tuesday. Nobody expects you to know what you want. Nobody will propose to you on the fourth date. There is a long, gentle runway of possibility, and plenty of people who will happily spend it with you. It is a safe place to be uncertain, and for some people, particularly those arriving from stricter societies, that is the entire point.

## The Verdict

I give it to Israel, with a slight asterisk. There is something enormously refreshing about a culture where the question "what do you want" has an answer by dessert. I would rather be rejected in ten minutes than be strung along for ten weeks, and an Israeli will oblige you on the first count every time. America, by contrast, will give you a delightful spring of possibilities followed by a long summer of mixed signals.

But the asterisk is language. Dating in Israel without Hebrew is a bit like arriving at a party where everyone has already finished the first three jokes. Both Maslow and Mendel say as much, one noting that Hebrew helped him more than anything, the other that friendships came far easier than romance. So the honest verdict is that Israel is better for people who are fluent, and America is better for people who need time and a translator. Choose your humiliation accordingly.

## Conclusion

If you want love that moves at the speed of a Tel Aviv taxi, Israel will oblige you. If you want love that arrives politely, on the third date, preceded by a text asking if you are free, America is waiting, phone in hand. Both countries will break your heart, but one of them will do it quickly and look you in the eye. The other will do it slowly, by omission, and tell you it is not about you. I know which one I would choose. I also know which one I keep ending up with.
