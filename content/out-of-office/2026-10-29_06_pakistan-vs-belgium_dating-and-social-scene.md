# One Country Will Have You Married By Spring. The Other Won't Even Confirm Saturday.

🇵🇰 Pakistan & 🇧🇪 Belgium
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here is a sentence I never thought I'd write with a straight face: Pakistan's dating culture is, in several measurable ways, more efficient than Belgium's. In Lahore, a stranger's auntie can identify your income bracket, sect, and cooking skills within one WhatsApp forward and have you engaged by Eid. In Brussels, a man will cancel a date an hour beforehand because your neighbourhood is "too far," having previously agreed to meet you there on purpose. One system runs on intention. The other runs on an app that's mostly used because it's cheaper than a beer.

I have been single in both countries, the only qualification anyone should need to write about romance, and each place has engineered its own specific flavour of heartbreak. Pakistan's is communal and occasionally resolved by a Facebook group with better vetting standards than most banks. Belgium's is private, reserved, and resolved by nothing at all, because resolution would require someone to text back.

## Do's & Don'ts: Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a vetted Facebook matchmaking group (Two Rings and its peers have blocked over 15,000 bad actors for a reason) if you're serious about meeting someone here | Expect Tinder, Grindr, or Facebook Dating to work — all have been blocked by the state since 2020 |
| Socialise through diplomatic-club dinners, compound parties, and expat associations, which is where actual adult social life happens | Assume "nightlife" means what it means in Berlin — bars exist mainly inside a handful of permitted hotels, not on the high street |
| Dress modestly in public — shoulders and legs covered — and save anything else for private, invite-only spaces | Hold hands or kiss in public and assume nobody noticed; someone always noticed |
| Let people know early if your intentions are marriage, friendship, or something you haven't worked out yet — ambiguity reads as dishonesty here, not mystery | Casually out a local friend's relationship, sexuality, or dating status to anyone — Pakistan's gay scene is real and lively but deliberately invisible, and it stays that way for safety, not shame |

## Do's & Don'ts: Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up on time, or ideally five minutes early — lateness is read as a character flaw, not a personality trait | Expect spontaneity; a Belgian's social calendar is planned with the rigour of a NATO briefing |
| Split the bill without making a production of it; 50/50 is the default, not a feminist statement | Bring up Flanders versus Wallonia, religion, or politics on a first date unless you enjoy silence |
| Say exactly what you mean — Belgians value blunt honesty over polite vagueness, and will tell you directly if they're not interested | Mistake reserve for rudeness, or silence for lack of interest; it usually just means they're thinking, slowly, forever |
| Expect a slow burn — meeting the family and moving in together typically happens on a one-to-three-year timeline | Perform a grand romantic gesture; it will not land as charming, it will land as alarming |

## Pakistan

Pakistan's social scene operates on two parallel tracks that rarely touch, and understanding which one you're on will save you months of confusion. Track one is the expat and diplomatic circuit: a "nearly non-stop rotation of parties and dinner parties," as one Islamabad post report on TalesMag puts it, run through the French, British, Canadian, and Australian clubs, plus whichever embassy is hosting that month. If you're posted here for work, this track is generous, international, and surprisingly easy to join — provided you break out of whichever national "bubble" you arrived in, because the real fun happens between bubbles, not inside them.

Track two is everyone else's actual dating life, and it's been reshaped by state censorship in a way that's almost impressive in its unintended consequences. In 2020, Pakistan's telecom regulator blocked Tinder, Grindr, Tagged, Skout, and SayHi for failing to filter "immoral" content, after Tinder alone had clocked over 440,000 downloads in the preceding year. The apps vanished. The desire to meet someone without your mother's direct involvement did not. So an entire generation migrated to Facebook groups — Two Rings, Soul Wonders, and university-specific pages like the LUMS and IBA "Rishta Edition" groups — which now function as crowdsourced, moderator-vetted matchmaking services with memberships in the hundreds of thousands. Two Rings alone has helped broker over 335 marriages and banned more than 15,000 members for bad behaviour, which is a better safety record than most apps manage with venture capital behind them.

What makes this genuinely interesting, rather than merely restrictive, is that it's not a retreat into tradition so much as a renegotiation of it. Co-founder Amna Kashmiri describes the groups as a "hybrid version" of matchmaking — arranged marriage's family oversight, minus the part where you don't get a say. Users post their own profiles instead of waiting for relatives to do it. One woman told RestOfWorld she joined after being stalked by a man who'd tracked her down through her Tinder profile and mutual friends; the Facebook groups, with actual moderation, felt safer. Others find the process reductive — a 27-year-old MBA graduate described feeling "seen as a commodity," assessed on height, sect, and income like a listing. Both things are true. Pakistan didn't abolish swiping; it just made swiping answer to a committee.

For the visiting professional, the upshot is this: your social life here is built through people, not apps, and it's warmer and more structured than almost anywhere else on this list — right up until someone asks, with complete sincerity, when you're getting married.

## Belgium

Belgium's dating culture is what happens when a deeply private, deeply honest people get handed a tool designed for the opposite of both. The apps — Tinder, Hinge, Bumble, plus homegrown oddities like Fruitz, which uses fruit emoji to signal whether you want something casual or serious — are, per Expatica's own expat dating guide, where most of the actual matching happens now, mainly because going out costs money and swiping doesn't. A Brussels Times columnist covering her own dating life for the paper admitted most of her search happens on-screen too, partly because her designated in-person alternative — a networking night called Plux near the EU quarter — is, in her words, a place you're "more likely to end up with a business card than a date."

Here's the part that will wreck your expectations if you arrive assuming "reserved" means "shy": Belgians are blunt. Painfully, refreshingly blunt. They will tell you, directly, if they're not interested, which is more consideration than you'll get from half the Western dating markets, and they despise mixed signals with a passion usually reserved for bad fries. The catch is that this honesty coexists with an almost pathological aversion to spontaneity. Plans are made, confirmed, and then made again. A date can end — as the Brussels Times columnist recounted — with a Flemish man explaining men and women "could never just be friends," immediately followed by the admission that he had no female friends but had "been with lots of men," at which point the date, sensibly, ended.

The data backs the drift toward solitude: 36.1% of Belgians live alone per Statbel's 2024 figures, rising to 47.1% in Brussels, and a 2022 study found young people staying single longer, partly from "dating fatigue" — too much choice, too little payoff. PDA is moderate and actively frowned upon in Flanders past hand-holding. Relationships, once started, crawl: cohabitation typically arrives one to three years in, and meeting the family is a genuine milestone, not a Tuesday. It's a country that will be honest about not wanting to see you again, then take eighteen months deciding it wants to see you forever.

## The Verdict

If you want a relationship with momentum, Pakistan wins, loudly and without apology. The social infrastructure — family networks, vetted matchmaking groups, an expat party circuit that runs on genuine hospitality — is built for people who know what they want and aren't precious about how fast they get it. The trade-off is privacy: your love life becomes, to some degree, a community project, and a fairly surveilled one if you're part of the LGBT scene, which is real, warm, and necessarily underground.

If you want a relationship built on brutal, unflinching honesty, Belgium wins, and then immediately undercuts the win by taking three years to introduce you to anyone's mother. Belgians won't lie to you. They also won't rush, won't gesture, and won't necessarily text back before Thursday, because Thursday was always the plan.

Neither country is going to hand you a whirlwind romance that looks like a phone advert. Pakistan will hand you a spreadsheet with your name already on it. Belgium will hand you silence, scheduled eleven days in advance. Choose your chaos.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com Lahore forum — a UK-born member who relocated says he's missing a social life entirely, because locally "people don't have time" for the casual, drop-in socialising he was used to back home.*

> *TalesMag Islamabad post report — a diplomatic spouse notes the city has a genuinely "vibrant" gay scene within expat and elite Pakistani circles, but stresses that outside those private settings, "everything is under wraps in public."*

> *Brussels Times dating column — the paper's own singles correspondent describes being sent a single snowflake emoji as an opening message on a dating app, and, months later, still has no idea what it meant.*

## Conclusion

Pack for both of these countries assuming the brochure is lying to you, because it is. Pakistan's social scene isn't closed off — it's just gated, and the gate has an auntie standing in front of it with a clipboard and genuinely good intentions. Belgium's isn't cold — it's just slow, in the specific way that a country becomes when honesty is mandatory and enthusiasm is optional. Neither will give you the easy, cinematic expat romance you were quietly hoping for on the flight over.

What you'll get instead is better material. Pakistan teaches you that community can do matchmaking's job better than any algorithm. Belgium teaches you that someone can be completely truthful with you and still never confirm Saturday. I've been ghosted by both systems, in entirely different registers, and only one had the decency to involve my future mother-in-law. You can guess which.
