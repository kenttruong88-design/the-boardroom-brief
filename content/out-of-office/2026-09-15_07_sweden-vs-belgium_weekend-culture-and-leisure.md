# Sweden Has Turned Rest Into a Religion. Belgium Has Turned It Into a Pub Crawl

🇸🇪 Sweden 🇧🇪 Belgium
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a certain kind of person who hears "Swedish weekend" and pictures a lakeside cabin, a sauna, and someone in linen quietly foraging chanterelles for four hours in companionable silence. That person is correct, and also slightly smug about it, because the Swedes have engineered leisure the way they engineer flat-pack furniture: efficiently, with clear instructions, and with an unsettling amount of pine involved.

Then there is Belgium, a country that looked at the concept of "weekend relaxation" and decided the correct response was a café terrace, a Trappist beer with a number on it suggesting radioactivity, and a leisurely three-hour argument about whether the fries are better in Bruges or Ghent. The Belgians do not optimise their leisure. They marinate in it.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book your allemansrätten forest walk and treat "friluftsliv" as a genuine verb, not a lifestyle Instagram caption | Don't schedule anything loud, urgent, or remotely commercial for a Sunday — the country basically closes its personality |
| Do bring your own thermos to a fika; sharing is fine, hoarding the good cinnamon buns is expected | Don't try to make small talk in a sauna — the silence is the point, and you are the problem |
| Do learn to love a "kräftskiva" crayfish party, paper hats and all, come August | Don't show up to someone's summer house uninvited, even if you're "basically neighbours" |
| Do treat a countryside cottage (stuga) as the actual weekend, not a detour from it | Don't assume city nightlife on a Saturday — most of Stockholm has already left for the archipelago |

### 🇧🇪 Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Do commit to a full café-terrace afternoon; ordering one coffee and leaving after twenty minutes is basically a hate crime | Don't rush a beer with a monastic pedigree — some of these are 9% and unimpressed by your schedule |
| Do cycle somewhere, anywhere, ideally through mud, ideally while eating a waffle | Don't confuse a "gezellig" Flemish Sunday with a Walloon one — regional pride runs deeper than the language border |
| Do go to a local market on Saturday morning and buy more cheese than one household needs | Don't expect shops open on Sunday outside the cities — plan your errands like it's 1994 |
| Do attend a village fair (kermis/kermesse) at least once, fried dough and all | Don't skip the football — even a third-division match doubles as a social calendar event |

## Sweden: The Republic of Deliberate Stillness

Sweden's weekend runs on a national operating system called lagom — not too much, not too little, just enough — and it shows in everything from portion sizes to leisure ambition. Saturdays begin, unofficially, with "Lördagsgodis," Saturday sweets, a ritualised pick-and-mix hour that exists specifically so children (and, let's be honest, every adult I've met in Malmö) don't eat sugar the other six days. Charmingly authoritarian: freedom, but scheduled.

From spring onward, the entire country decants itself into the archipelago, the forest, or a red-painted cottage with white trim that appears contractually mandated by the Ministry of Aesthetic Consistency. Swedes do not "go to the countryside" as a novelty; they consider it a biological requirement, like sleep. Foraging for mushrooms, swimming in water cold enough to reorganise your internal organs, grilling in near-silence — this is not deprivation, it's the point. Allemansrätten, the "right to roam," lets anyone walk, forage, or camp on almost any land, either the most generous public policy in Europe or proof Swedes trust each other more than is strictly reasonable.

Come winter, the outdoor virtue doesn't stop — it just adds cross-country skis. Through all seasons, fika remains sacred: a coffee-and-pastry pause so embedded that Swedish employers schedule it, as if joy were a line item. There's a quiet superiority to it, an unspoken sense that everyone else is doing leisure wrong by doing it loudly. The Swedish weekend doesn't ask you to have fun. It asks you to be well — a much higher bar, and one most nations would rather not clear if it costs eight hours of forest silence.

## Belgium: The Kingdom of Convivial Excess

Belgium's weekend has no interest in wellness. It has interest in gezelligheid — cosiness, the specific pleasure of being wedged into a warm café with too many people and not enough chairs — pursued with the seriousness Sweden reserves for its lakes. The Belgian Saturday begins at a market, ideally selling more cheese and charcuterie than any household rationally needs, then drifts, without urgency, toward a café terrace where the afternoon simply happens to you.

Beer is a regional identity system here. Order the wrong glass shape in the wrong province and a stranger will correct you, kindly but firmly. Trappist ales, brewed by monks who took vows of silence but apparently not quality control, are consumed at a pace that would alarm a Swedish liver, over conversations that resolve nothing — exactly the intention.

Then there's the unresolved rivalry between Flanders and Wallonia, surfacing even in leisure: whose fries are superior, whose kermis is livelier, whose cycling routes are more scenic. The Tour of Flanders isn't a bike race, it's a liturgical event. And nothing interrupts a Sunday football match, even amateur-level, where whole villages turn out as though the outcome affects GDP.

Belgian leisure is loud, caloric, argumentative, and defiantly social. It does not optimise. It indulges — with an authority no wellness retreat could fake.

## The Verdict

Want to return from your weekend having achieved something — inner peace, a personal best in stillness, a new respect for pine trees? Go to Sweden. Want to return three kilograms heavier, mildly hungover, and four friends richer than Friday? Go to Belgium. I refuse to pick a "winner" outright: one is meditation, the other a house party that never quite ends.

But if pressed — and Suki is always eventually pressed — Belgium wins on pure human warmth. Sweden's weekend is enviable and, frankly, a little smug in its own perfection; you finish it well-rested and vaguely lectured at by nature. Belgium's weekend is chaotic, caloric, and occasionally incomprehensible across a forty-kilometre language border, but nobody ever left a Belgian Sunday afternoon feeling unloved. Rest is important. Being fed cheese by six strangers who disagree about fries is better.

## Conclusion

Neither country is doing leisure "wrong," the most annoying possible conclusion for anyone hoping for a clean scandal. Sweden decided a weekend well spent leaves you calmer, fitter, mildly evangelical about cold-water swimming. Belgium decided a weekend well spent leaves you fuller, funnier, on first-name terms with the café regulars. Both are forms of genius. Both would find the other exhausting for opposite reasons — the Swedes appalled by the noise, the Belgians baffled by the silence.

My advice, as someone who has attempted both: do a Belgian Saturday and a Swedish Sunday. Gorge yourself on frites and opinions on day one, then disappear into a forest and say nothing to anyone on day two. It's the only two-day itinerary in Europe that functions as both confession and penance.
