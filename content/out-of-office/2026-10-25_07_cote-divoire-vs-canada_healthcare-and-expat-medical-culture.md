🇨🇮 **Côte d'Ivoire** vs 🇨🇦 **Canada**
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Côte d'Ivoire and Canada represent two completely different answers to the same question: what happens when you get sick far from home? Côte d'Ivoire's answer is a two-tier system where public hospitals are affordable but inconsistent, and private clinics in Abidjan are genuinely excellent — if you can pay for them, which most expats can. Canada's answer is universal, free-at-the-point-of-use healthcare that is the envy of its southern neighbour right up until you actually try to get a family doctor, at which point you discover a waiting list with the patience of a glacier.

One system makes you pay for speed. The other makes you wait for free. I've needed a doctor urgently in both countries, and in both cases ended up mildly furious — just about completely different things.

## Do's & Don'ts: Côte d'Ivoire

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comprehensive private health insurance before you arrive, not after | Rely on public hospitals for anything beyond basic, non-urgent care |
| Research and pick a trusted private clinic in Abidjan in advance | Assume all private clinics are equally reliable — quality varies sharply |
| Carry cash or a card for upfront payment — many clinics expect payment before treatment | Expect insurance to be billed directly the way it might be back home |
| Ask other expats for specific doctor recommendations rather than guessing | Wait to find a doctor until you're actually sick — do it in your first month |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for your provincial health card the day you arrive — some provinces have waiting periods | Assume "universal healthcare" means immediate access to a family doctor |
| Use walk-in clinics or telehealth lines for non-emergencies while you wait for a GP | Go to the emergency room for anything that isn't actually an emergency — wait times are brutal |
| Get private supplemental insurance for dental, vision, and prescriptions, which aren't fully covered | Expect specialist referrals to move quickly — some waits run into months |
| Register with a local walk-in clinic as a regular, even without a assigned family doctor | Panic if you can't find a family doctor immediately — it's a widely shared, structural problem, not a personal failure |

## Côte d'Ivoire: Pay for Speed, or Wait for the Public System

Healthcare in Côte d'Ivoire runs on a visible, well-understood divide. Public hospitals exist, are genuinely affordable, and handle large volumes of basic care — but resourcing is inconsistent, equipment can be outdated, and wait times for anything beyond urgent triage can stretch long. Private clinics, concentrated heavily in Abidjan, are a different story entirely: well-equipped, staffed by doctors trained domestically and often abroad, and capable of handling everything from routine care to fairly serious procedures with a standard that genuinely surprises newcomers who arrived expecting the opposite. The catch, obviously, is cost — private care here runs on direct payment or insurance billing arranged in advance, and going in without either is a genuinely bad position to be caught in.

What expats consistently get wrong is assuming the public-private split maps neatly onto "bad" and "good." It's more accurate to say the public system handles volume and the private system handles reliability, and most resident expats simply opt out of the first tier entirely, treating comprehensive private insurance as a non-negotiable cost of living there rather than an optional extra. The social layer matters too: word of mouth among the expat and diplomatic community is how most people find a trustworthy doctor, since online reviews are thin and formal rating systems barely exist. Get that insurance and that referral sorted before you need either — doing it reactively, mid-emergency, is the single most common regret.

Pharmacies occupy an interesting middle ground in this system, often functioning as a genuine first stop for minor ailments, staffed by pharmacists willing to give real medical guidance and dispense treatment without requiring a full clinic visit — a habit that surprises newcomers used to pharmacies as purely transactional. For anything more serious, medical evacuation insurance is something most long-term expats and diplomatic staff carry as standard, not as paranoia, since the most advanced procedures — certain surgeries, specialist cancer care — are generally still handled by flying patients to better-resourced facilities abroad, typically in France or South Africa, rather than attempting them locally.

## Canada: Free, Except for the Part Where You Wait

Canada's universal healthcare system is real, genuinely comprehensive for anything serious, and funded in a way that means nobody goes bankrupt over a hospital stay — a fact Canadians will mention, gently but pointedly, at any opportunity when comparing notes with American friends. What the system does not deliver, and what newcomers are rarely warned about, is speed for routine, non-urgent care. Finding a family doctor accepting new patients can take months in many cities, and some newcomers go a year or more relying entirely on walk-in clinics and telehealth lines, never quite landing a dedicated GP.

The emergency room becomes, almost by default, the backstop for anything that feels remotely urgent but isn't life-threatening — and the wait times there are notoriously long precisely because the system is absorbing demand that a properly staffed network of family doctors would otherwise handle. Specialist referrals compound the problem: a referral to a dermatologist or an orthopaedist can mean a wait measured in months, not weeks, a fact that genuinely shocks newcomers from countries with faster private options, even ones who fully support the principle of universal coverage. What softens this in practice is that the system is free at every step along the way — the frustration is time, not money, which for most people is still, on balance, a trade worth making. The real skill newcomers need to learn fast is triage literacy: knowing which ailment genuinely warrants the ER, which can wait for a walk-in clinic, and which just needs you to add your name to another waiting list and get on with your week.

Telehealth has become a genuine release valve for the family-doctor shortage, with several provinces now offering free, around-the-clock virtual consultation lines staffed by actual physicians and nurse practitioners — not a perfect substitute for continuity of care, but a legitimately useful stopgap for anyone stuck on a months-long waitlist. Newcomers from countries with faster private options sometimes supplement the public system with a paid virtual-care subscription specifically to shortcut this bottleneck, a workaround increasingly normalised even among Canadians themselves, who are just as frustrated by the family-doctor shortage as anyone arriving fresh to it.

## The Verdict

Côte d'Ivoire's system is honest about its trade-off: pay, and you get fast, high-quality private care; don't, and you get the public system's limitations. Canada's system is honest about a different trade-off: nobody pays directly, and in exchange, nearly everyone waits — for a GP, for a specialist, sometimes for a basic appointment. Neither country is lying to you about what you're getting.

If money isn't the constraint, I'd rather pay and be seen the same week than wait eight months for a dermatologist referral while my skin situation becomes a whole personality. Côte d'Ivoire's private tier wins on sheer responsiveness — but only for those who can actually afford to play that game, which is the quiet asterisk on the entire comparison.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Abidjan forum — several posters warned newcomers to confirm upfront, every single time, whether a clinic bills insurance directly or expects payment first and reimbursement later.*

> *Internations Toronto — a member described calling over a dozen family practices across their city before finding even one accepting new patients, nearly a year after arriving.*

> *Reddit r/ivorycoast — one long-term expat said the real local knowledge wasn't which clinic was "best," but which doctor actually answered their phone after hours.*

## Conclusion

Both countries have made a genuine, defensible choice about how to allocate a scarce resource — money in Côte d'Ivoire's case, time in Canada's. Neither system is secretly broken; they're both just visibly rationing something, and it's only disorienting because most newcomers arrive assuming healthcare works the way it did back home. It never does. Figure out which currency your new country charges in, time or money, and budget accordingly before you're sick enough to need either in a hurry.
