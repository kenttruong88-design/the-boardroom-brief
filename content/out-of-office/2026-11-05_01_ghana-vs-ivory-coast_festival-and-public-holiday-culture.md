# Two Countries Share a Border and Absolutely Nothing Else About How They Mark Time

🇬🇭🇨🇮 Ghana and Ivory Coast, reviewed by someone who has missed more flights to attend other people's holidays than she cares to admit

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Share a border, share a climate, share roughly the same latitude and the same colonial-era habit of exporting cocoa to people who will never thank them properly for it — and yet Ghana and Ivory Coast have arrived at two almost comically opposite philosophies of what a public holiday is *for*. Ghana treats the calendar like a courtroom: every date is a verdict on who gets remembered as a founder of the nation, and the verdicts keep getting re-litigated. Ivory Coast treats one specific week in Grand-Bassam as a licence to say absolutely anything to absolutely anyone, consequence-free, which is either the most honest piece of civic infrastructure on the continent or a terrifying glimpse of what your coworkers actually think of you.

I have now spent enough holiday seasons in both countries to form Opinions, which is the only currency I deal in.

## Do's & Don'ts: Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the difference between Founders' Day (Aug 4) and Kwame Nkrumah Memorial Day (Sept 21) before you say something stupid at a dinner party | Casually refer to "the independence holiday" as if there's only one — there are, confusingly, two separate days honouring overlapping nation-founding claims |
| Expect at least one weekend a month to be quietly colonised by a funeral | Assume a "no reply" to a calendar invite means the person isn't coming — Ghanaian scheduling operates on its own clock entirely |
| Ask who the Chief Imam is before planning around Eid — the date isn't fixed in advance | Show up to Dipo season treating girls' puberty rites as a photo op rather than a serious rite of passage |

## Do's & Don'ts: Ivory Coast

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Brace yourself for Abissa week in Grand-Bassam, when total honesty is not just tolerated but ritually required | Expect to eat the new yam before the village chief and elders have ritually eaten it first — this is not a suggestion |

## Ghana: A Country That Litigates Its Own Founding Myth in Public

Ghana's 2025 statutory calendar runs eleven official holidays deep, and buried in that list is one of the more quietly unhinged pieces of civic theatre I've encountered anywhere: two separate national holidays — Founders' Day on August 4th and Kwame Nkrumah Memorial Day on September 21st — honouring what was, until 2017, a single occasion. The government split the old "Founder's Day," which celebrated Nkrumah alone on his actual birthday, into a broader "Founders' Day" recognising a wider cast of independence leaders, and left Nkrumah's own memorial where his birthday always was. The opposition CPP called it rewriting history. Minority MPs walked out of parliament. I would pay real money to have been in that room. Most countries argue about their founding myths in textbooks and occasionally in statues getting toppled at 2am; Ghana just gave itself two bank holidays to keep the argument going annually, forever, with pay.

Then there's Homowo, the Ga people's harvest festival, whose name translates roughly to "hooting at hunger" — a direct, unsentimental rebuke of a historic famine, marked by ritually sprinkling kpokpoi, a fermented corn-and-palm-oil dish, as an offering before anyone's allowed to actually tuck in. There's a sub-festival exclusively for twins. There's Damba, nominally marking the Prophet Muhammad's birth, which Ghana's own culture ministry describes as being "mainly" about glorifying chieftaincy — an Islamic commemoration quietly repurposed into royal pageantry, which is either syncretism at its most elegant or the single boldest bait-and-switch in the religious calendar.

What nobody warns you about is the funerals. Ghana's own statistical service found that more than 80% of domestic overnight trips are made to attend funerals or visit relatives — funerals specifically accounted for nearly 40% of those. This is not a culture that treats death as a private grief to be managed quietly; it's a measurable economic sector, complete with custom-printed cloth, week-long event sequences, and — I say this with real admiration — better production values than most weddings I've attended in countries that are supposedly richer. And good luck getting anyone to confirm a meeting time during one. Expats on forum threads complain, with the weary consistency of people who've given up fighting it, that "absolutely no one respects meeting time, deadlines etc." They're right. They're also missing the point. The calendar isn't broken. You're just reading it with the wrong instrument.

## Ivory Coast: One Week a Year, Everyone Tells the Truth

If Ghana's holidays are about who gets remembered, Ivory Coast's flagship festival is about who gets *heard*, whether they like it or not. Abissa, celebrated by the Nzema and Ndenye peoples of Grand-Bassam across the last week of October, suspends ordinary social rules entirely. Farming stops. Weddings pause. And for one extraordinary week, anyone can walk up to anyone else — their boss, their mother-in-law, a stranger — and demand total honesty: old grievances aired, confessions of love declared, scores settled, with complete social immunity. Cross-dressing by men and women alike is simply part of the proceedings, unremarkable and old. On the final morning, at 5am, the sacred drum Edogbole is ritually consulted to determine whether the community has earned the right to enter the new year. One participant, quoted in the New York Times, put it better than I could: Africans "have always had our ways of managing tensions." Compared, not unreasonably, to Rio's carnival or New Orleans' Mardi Gras, Abissa has the distinct advantage of actual psychological function rather than just excellent costuming.

The Yam Festival, celebrated by Akan communities including the Baoulé, Agni and Abron at the close of the rainy season, runs on a precedence taboo I find almost moving in its strictness: the chief, elders and traditional priests must eat the first yams of the new harvest, quietly, after libations — before anyone else in the community is permitted to eat from it at all. Only then does the parade start. It's hierarchy expressed through appetite, and it works.

And then there's National Peace Day, November 15th, instituted in 1996 — a holiday that exists for no reason other than that the country needed, officially, collectively, to stop fighting each other for a while. Ghana memorialises its founders. Ivory Coast memorialises the fact that it survived itself. That's a meaningfully different relationship with your own history, and it shows in how both countries show up to a Tuesday.

## The Verdict

Ghana wins on sheer administrative audacity — nobody else has managed to turn a contested national myth into a recurring annual event with its own bank holiday, twice. But Ivory Coast wins the only category that actually matters to me, which is: does the festival *do* something. Abissa isn't decorative. It's a pressure valve with a drumbeat, built by people who understood that a society without a sanctioned outlet for honesty eventually finds an unsanctioned one, and the unsanctioned kind tends to involve machetes rather than metaphor. Ghana gives you a very good party and an argument about history. Ivory Coast gives you one week a year where your landlord might finally tell you what she really thinks of your rent cheques. I know which one I'd rather be stuck at a dinner table for.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com, Ghana forum — paraphrased: "Absolutely NO ONE respects meeting time, deadlines etc." — and that, apparently, includes the holidays themselves, which simply arrive when the Chief Imam says they do.*

> *ExpatExchange, Ghana Q&A — paraphrased: a Accra-based poster says locals will "only tell you what you want to hear, not the real situation" — which makes Ivory Coast's one-week honesty amnesty look less like a quirky festival and more like a public service.*

> *Expat.com, Ivory Coast forum — paraphrased: a newer arrival describes Abidjan's unmetered taxis as requiring a haggle before you even get in, which is its own small, year-round, unofficial Abissa — just with worse drumming.*

## Conclusion

Neither country is doing this for your benefit, and that's precisely the point. Ghana's holiday calendar is a living argument about legitimacy that the rest of us just get to watch, slightly confused, with a day off. Ivory Coast's Abissa is not a tourist pageant dressed up as tradition — it is tradition that happens to be extraordinary to watch, which is a completely different thing and one that far too many newcomers confuse. Go to both if you can. Bring a notebook, not a tripod, and for heaven's sake don't describe either one as "exotic" within earshot of a Ghanaian funeral director or a woman in Grand-Bassam wearing a man's agbada with more conviction than you've shown about anything in your entire life.
