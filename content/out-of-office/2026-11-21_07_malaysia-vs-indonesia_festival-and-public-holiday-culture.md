# Malaysia Goes Home for Raya; Indonesia Moves Half the Country to Do It

🇲🇾 Malaysia vs 🇮🇩 Indonesia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

At the end of Ramadan, two neighbouring countries do the same thing and then behave as though they invented it. Malaysia calls it balik kampung, going back to the village, and treats it as a sacred pilgrimage conducted at a speed of eleven kilometres an hour on the North-South Expressway. Indonesia calls it mudik, borrows a word that originally meant to travel upstream, and mobilises a population the size of a large European continent to do it.

Both countries observe Hari Raya Aidilfitri, or Lebaran, with the same ingredients: ketupat, rendang, apologies, new clothes, money in envelopes and the quiet horror of being asked when you are getting married. But the scale, the mood and the logistics diverge in ways that make an expat's head spin. Neither country can be said to have invented sentimentality, though they are both extremely good at it. And both will, with total sincerity, ask you whether you are eating enough.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇲🇾 Malaysia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept every invitation to an open house, and bring something small | Refuse food, it is the closest thing to an insult |
| Wear modest, bright festive clothes if invited | Turn up to a Raya open house in beachwear |
| Book travel for balik kampung months ahead | Try to drive out of Kuala Lumpur on the last working day |
| Say "maaf zahir dan batin" and mean it | Treat it as a throwaway phrase |

| 🇮🇩 Indonesia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask forgiveness from older relatives and friends at Lebaran | Expect to avoid the hand-kissing ritual |
| Budget for gifts, a holiday bonus and money envelopes | Plan on normal prices for transport in the fortnight before |
| Book train or flight tickets the moment sales open | Wait until the week before and hope |
| Stay put in a city that has emptied out, if you can | Try to get a plumber in the middle of mudik |

## 🇲🇾 Malaysia

Hari Raya in Malaysia is a national mood. The country effectively closes for a week or so, and the highways turn into slow-moving parking lots filled with cars stacked with luggage, children, boxes of cookies and a certain sense of destiny. The SCMP has noted that up to 1.7 million people a day may use the North-South Expressway during the festive exodus, which means that the journey home becomes a seasonal event in itself, complete with its own cuisine, traffic updates and roadside prayers.

Then comes the open house. Malaysia's great cultural trick is that festivals are shared. Raya, Chinese New Year, Deepavali and Christmas are all national occasions, and invitations cross the divide with casual generosity. An expat will find themselves at a Malay open house eating lemang, ketupat and rendang with colleagues who might, in a different week, be hosting a Chinese New Year reunion. It is warm, sticky, abundant and entirely inclusive. It is also exhausting, because the food does not stop and the hospitality requires you to have at least three plates.

For Malaysians abroad, the festival can be bittersweet. A Newswav opinion piece describes a Malaysian in London joining her family in Kuala Lumpur by video call on Raya morning, and holding back tears as they ate ketupat and rendang without her; her mother asked whether she was eating well. The piece is an illustrative anecdote rather than a verified account, but I have seen the real version in a dozen cities. There is a Malaysian community abroad that builds its own balik kampung style event with lemang and rendang, as a mission in Hungary reportedly did. Home, as it turns out, can be rebuilt in a function room, but it takes a great deal of coconut.

It is worth noting what the festival does to the working lives of expats. In both countries, the weeks before Raya or Lebaran are dominated by bonuses, leave requests, and the polite but unmistakable disappearance of colleagues. Payroll teams hurry, couriers vanish, and the office takes on the atmosphere of a ship in the last hour before landfall. Managers who ignore this do so at their peril. Smart expats plan their own holiday around it, because the city is quiet, the roads are empty and the beaches, if you are lucky, are yours.

## 🇮🇩 Indonesia

Mudik is on another scale. Wikipedia reports that Indonesia's national police recorded a record 193 million people travelling in the 2024 season, up from around 30 million in 2013, and the SCMP says that some 19 million people leave Jakarta and Surabaya alone, in what is the region's biggest annual migration outside Lunar New Year. The word itself, Wikipedia notes, comes from the Indonesian for travelling upstream, and its modern meaning dates from the 1970s, when the migration to Jakarta began under Suharto's New Order. In 2016, motorcycle mudik peaked at 5.6 million bikes, despite a government scheme to carry them by train, truck or ship.

The human side is as large as the numbers. The SCMP spoke to a 26-year-old roadside meatball-soup seller earning a little over twenty dollars a day who waited at Pasar Senen station from before sunrise for a train to Bojonegoro, and who said that people would sleep on the floor or on the street to get a ticket. He has sent money home every month, but he felt he ought to go at least once in person. That is mudik: not nostalgia, but obligation made joyful, a promise to be seen by your elders at least once a year.

An IIUM Today essay by a Jakarta-born student in Malaysia captures the personal texture: a missed flight, a week at home, relatives greeting her at the airport with questions about her studies and whether she was eating, gawan bags of snacks pressed into her hands, and sungkeman, the ritual of asking forgiveness from elders, followed by her mother reminding her to look after herself for the hundredth time. For a foreigner in Jakarta, the practical effect is that the city empties. Roads clear, restaurants close, staff vanish, and you realise you have been living in a metropolis of guests.

The other great pleasure of the season is the food, and Indonesia deserves its own paragraph. Lebaran tables bring out opor ayam, ketupat, sambal goreng ati and an enormous range of regional cakes, and the dish you eat depends entirely on whose grandmother you are visiting. Each household has a different recipe, and each defends it. The visitor's job is to eat everything, praise everything and ask for the recipe, and never, under any circumstances, to say that the other house did it better. I have been caught out at least once and I am still not forgiven.

## The Verdict

Indonesia wins on scale and ceremony, and Malaysia wins on comfort and company. If you want to understand what the festival means to someone, go to Indonesia and watch a train station at dawn. If you want to eat an extraordinary amount of rendang with people of every faith in the country, go to Malaysia, and bring an appetite and a loose waistband.

For an expat who is not Muslim, Malaysia offers the more accessible experience, because open houses are an open invitation. For an expat who wants to be moved, Indonesia has the more profound one. I would add a warning for both: the week around Lebaran is a terrible time to expect service, speed or sanity. You will be told repeatedly that everyone has gone home, and by the end of the week, you will want to. I do not have a better argument for the pilgrimage than that.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *South China Morning Post, a meatball-soup seller in Jakarta — waited at the station from before sunrise for a ten-hour train home, saying people will sleep on the floor or the street to get a ticket, and that having sent money home all year he ought to go in person at least once.*

> *Newswav opinion piece, a Malaysian in London — joined Raya morning by video call, held back tears as her family ate ketupat and rendang, and was asked by her mother whether she was eating well.*

## Conclusion

Whichever of the two you choose, you will be asked whether you have eaten, whether you are married, and whether you have forgiven everyone you should. Say yes to the first, deflect the second and think about the third. In Malaysia you will go home by car and in Indonesia by train, bike or ferry, but you will go. The festival is not really about the food or the clothes. It is about the unspoken contract that wherever you end up in the world, someone back home is still counting the days. Bring a gift. Eat the rendang. Say sorry properly.
