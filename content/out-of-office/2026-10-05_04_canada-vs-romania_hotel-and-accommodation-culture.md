# One Country Will Charge You a "Resort Fee" in a City With No Resort; the Other Will Feed You Until You Apologise

🇨🇦 Canada 🇷🇴 Romania
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Canadian hotels have perfected a particular kind of quiet financial violence: the resort fee, charged in cities with no discernible resort, for amenities you did not use, disclosed in font sizes designed by people who have clearly thought very hard about minimum legal compliance. Romanian *pensiuni* — family-run guesthouses — have perfected something closer to hospitality as an act of personal honour, where the host will feed you a three-course breakfast you did not order and take mild offence if you don't finish the second helping of *mici*. I have paid forty-two dollars in "destination fees" for a hotel room in downtown Calgary with no destination-adjacent features whatsoever. I have also been personally walked to a bus stop by a Transylvanian guesthouse owner who insisted on carrying my bag the entire way. These are not comparable experiences, and yet both happened to me within the same accommodation category.

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the fine print for "resort fees," "destination fees," and parking charges | Assume the advertised nightly rate is the final rate — it almost never is |
| Tip housekeeping a few dollars per night; it's expected, not optional | Expect a hotel bar to stay open past a modest hour outside major cities |
| Book well ahead during peak summer season in national-park-adjacent towns | Expect check-in before 3pm or late checkout without a direct, polite ask |
| Use provincial tourism sites to check actual star ratings, not just marketing | Assume "boutique" means cheaper — it is almost always a euphemism for pricier |

## Do's & Don'ts: Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the extra food your pensiune host offers — refusing can genuinely offend | Expect 24-hour reception at smaller guesthouses outside major cities |
| Pay in cash where possible; smaller properties often prefer it, sometimes insist | Assume all listed "hotels" meet Western star-rating expectations — verify reviews |
| Ask your host for local recommendations; they take personal pride in giving them | Overstay a checkout time without asking — some hosts have same-day turnovers planned |
| Bring small RON notes for any extras; card machines are inconsistent rurally | Expect central heating reliability in older rural guesthouses during deep winter |

## Canada: Hospitality With an Itemised Invoice

Canadian hotel culture operates on a baseline of professional, competent, thoroughly pleasant service that somehow still manages to extract every possible dollar from you via a fee structure that would make an airline blush. The "resort fee" phenomenon — mandatory charges for pools you didn't swim in, gyms you didn't visit, and Wi-Fi that used to be free before someone in corporate decided ambient oxygen was also technically a premium amenity — has metastasised well beyond actual resorts into perfectly ordinary city hotels in Toronto, Vancouver, and Calgary. You will be quoted one price. You will pay a meaningfully higher one. Everyone involved will remain entirely polite throughout, which somehow makes it worse.

Where Canadian accommodation genuinely shines is in its baseline consistency. A mid-range hotel in Winnipeg will perform almost identically to a mid-range hotel in Halifax — clean, competent, forgettable in the specific way that reliable infrastructure is always forgettable. Housekeeping shows up. Check-in works. Nobody tries to upsell you on a "wellness experience" involving a single eucalyptus-scented towel, mostly. The tipping culture extends fully into hospitality — housekeeping expects a few dollars per night, and skipping this, while not catastrophic, marks you clearly as someone who learned about Canadian customs from a brochure rather than an actual Canadian.

National-park-adjacent accommodation (Banff, Jasper, the whole Rocky Mountain circuit) operates on a different, more punishing economy entirely — book months ahead or pay triple, with "triple" sometimes meaning genuinely eye-watering sums for what amounts to a converted motel room near a very photogenic mountain. The mountains are, admittedly, worth quite a lot. The industry built around them knows this and charges accordingly, without a flicker of shame.

## Romania: Hospitality as Personal Honour

Walk into a Romanian *pensiune* — the family-run guesthouses that dominate accommodation outside Bucharest, particularly through Transylvania and the Carpathians — and you are entering not a transaction but a relationship, whether you wanted one or not. The host will ask about your journey. They will feed you more than you can reasonably eat, present the extra food as entirely ordinary, and take visible, personal pride in your enjoyment of a breakfast spread that could comfortably serve a small wedding party. Declining seconds is technically permitted. It is not, socially, advisable.

This hospitality operates on genuine warmth rather than corporate script, which means it is wonderfully inconsistent — the best Romanian guesthouses offer an intimacy and generosity no five-star Canadian chain could manufacture if it tried for a decade, while the weaker ones can suffer from unreliable heating, patchy Wi-Fi, and reception hours that assume you'll simply text ahead like a normal, considerate human being rather than expecting someone on call at 2am. Payment culture leans cash-forward, particularly in rural areas, and card machines have an unreliable relationship with actual functioning internet the further you get from a city centre.

Bucharest's hotel scene has modernised considerably and now offers genuinely international-standard properties, but the soul of Romanian accommodation remains firmly in its countryside guesthouses — places where the owner personally drives you to the train station, insists you take some preserved plums for the journey, and makes you feel, however briefly, like an actual guest in someone's actual life rather than a room number on a nightly revenue spreadsheet.

## The Verdict

Canada wins on consistency and professional reliability — you will always, roughly, know what you're getting, fee structure notwithstanding. Romania wins, decisively, on warmth, generosity, and the sheer human texture of being hosted rather than merely accommodated. If I'm choosing where to stay for a week of genuine comfort, Canada's clean efficiency has its charms. But if I want to remember the place I stayed in five years from now — to actually feel something about a guesthouse — I'm taking the Transylvanian pensiune, the extra plum preserves, and the mild guilt of a third helping of mici, every single time.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/canada — paraphrased: a traveller was quoted $189 a night, charged $230, and told the $41 difference was a "destination amenity fee" for a hotel with no amenities they could identify.*

> *Reddit r/Romania — paraphrased: a tourist asked why their pensiune host seemed personally hurt when they skipped breakfast, and was told that in rural Romania, declining food offered by a host is close to an insult.*

> *expat.com Bucharest — paraphrased: a relocated teacher warned that some rural guesthouses genuinely shut down heating overnight in winter, so always ask directly before booking in the colder months.*

## Conclusion

Canada and Romania sit at opposite ends of the same spectrum: one has industrialised hospitality into a smooth, fee-laden, professionally pleasant machine; the other has kept it stubbornly, beautifully personal, fuelled by plum brandy and genuine pride rather than quarterly earnings targets. Both will get you a bed and a shower. Only one of them will make you feel, however briefly, like you've been adopted. Pack an appetite for Romania. Pack a calculator for Canada.
