# Honiara's Bus System Is Chaos You Can Trust — Auckland's Is Order You Can't

🇸🇧 Solomon Islands 🇳🇿 New Zealand
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country lies about its commute, but only some countries have the decency to lie badly. New Zealand will hand you a glossy transport app, a tap-and-go card, and a straight face while telling you the motorway network is "world-class" — right before you spend fifty-five minutes travelling eleven kilometres on Auckland's Northwestern at rush hour. The Solomon Islands, by contrast, has never once pretended. There is no app. There is a minibus with a name painted on the windscreen, a driver who stops wherever gravity and commerce intersect, and a shared understanding that the timetable is, at best, a rumour. One of these systems makes you feel engineered. The other makes you feel seen.

I have commuted on four continents in shoes that were never built for the task, and I can tell you the difference between a country that has transport infrastructure and a country that has transport culture. New Zealand has the former in spades and the latter in short, resentful bursts — it built roads for a population that insists on driving alone to buy a single flat white. The Solomon Islands has no illusions about infrastructure, so it built a culture instead: a flexible, slightly feral network of trucks and minibuses that runs on word of mouth, hand signals, and the universal currency of exact change. Civilisation, it turns out, is optional. Reliability is negotiable. Getting where you're going is not.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Solomon Islands**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the specific wrist-flick wave that flags down a passing truck | Expect it to stop only at an official, marked bus stop |
| Carry small coins for the fare, paid hand to hand | Hand over a large note and expect anyone to have change |
| Ask a local which truck is actually going your way today | Trust a painted route number — it may be years out of date |
| Greet the driver and fellow passengers getting on | Mistake the quiet for rudeness; it's just efficiency |

**New Zealand**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get an AT HOP (or regional equivalent) card on arrival | Try to pay cash on an Auckland bus — drivers will refuse you |
| Check a real-time transit app before you leave | Trust the "estimated arrival" time printed on it, ever |
| Learn to merge onto the motorway with total confidence | Expect anyone to let you in out of kindness |
| Walk or cycle where the city is genuinely compact, like Wellington | Assume Auckland's layout rewards anything but driving |

## Solomon Islands: The Bus Has No Name, Only a Destiny

In Honiara, "public transport" means a procession of privately owned minibuses and trucks — locally just called buses, though officially they're PMVs, public motor vehicles — careening along the one real artery of the city, the stretch connecting Point Cruz to the eastern suburbs, each one packed to a density that would make a Tokyo subway blush. There is technically a route system. Almost nobody consults it. You stand roughly where a bus might pass, extend an arm with the particular gesture that signals "I would like to board," and a vehicle slows just enough for you to climb aboard without it ever fully stopping. Fares are agreed by informal consensus rather than posted anywhere, hover around a few Solomon dollars, and are passed hand to hand up the rows like a collection plate.

The government has, to its credit, noticed that this is somewhat chaotic. A parliamentary select committee has investigated Honiara's short bus routes directly, and the city has since been overhauling its network with help from JICA — new driver standards, proposed route rationalisation, actual designated stops. This is progress, but only the way fitting a seatbelt into a speedboat is progress. The point is that the current, largely unregulated system works precisely because everyone involved has agreed to make it work. Drivers know their regulars. Regulars know a truck leaves not when a clock says so, but when enough people have climbed in to make the trip worthwhile.

Outside Honiara it gets more honest still: many islands have no connecting road network at all, so "commuting" means a dinghy, a dugout canoe, or — if you've got the patience for a schedule that treats "daily" as aspirational — a short domestic flight. Walking remains the default mode for most Solomon Islanders going about their day, which sounds quaint until you're doing it in equatorial humidity with a sack of cassava on your shoulder. The less a system pretends to be organised, the more honest it is about its limits — and the Solomon Islands wears its limits like a scar, not a secret.

## New Zealand: A Motorway for Every Excuse Not to Take the Bus

New Zealand has spent decades building some of the most expensive, meticulously engineered roads in the Pacific, and then filled them with people who refuse to share a vehicle with anyone outside their own household. Auckland, the country's largest city, has repeatedly been ranked among the most congested cities in Australasia — worse, by some measures, than far denser Hong Kong. The Northwestern Motorway at five o'clock is not a road so much as a very slow, very expensive car park with excellent lane markings. Ask any Aucklander about their commute and you will get an unprompted monologue about the Harbour Bridge, delivered with the weary fatalism of someone describing a chronic illness.

The infrastructure itself is genuinely good: tap-and-go cards work seamlessly across buses, trains and ferries, and the long-awaited City Rail Link, once it finally opens after a decade of road cones and delays, should actually move the needle. The problem is cultural, not civil-engineering. New Zealanders, true to their rural, pioneer self-image, treat the car as a birthright rather than a choice, even in a dense city with a perfectly functional bus network sitting right there. Surveys find Aucklanders simultaneously the most resigned to their congestion and the least likely to change how they commute — admirable stoicism, or a national failure of imagination; I'm not diplomatic enough today to pick the kind one.

Outside the big cities it flips entirely: Wellington's commute is a genuinely pleasant, walkable, cable-car-adjacent affair, and most provincial New Zealand simply doesn't have "traffic" as a concept — you drive because there is, quite literally, nothing else, and the nearest bus stop is forty minutes and several paddocks away. It is a country that built two transport cultures side by side and let the louder one, stuck on the Northwestern cursing at brake lights, define the national mood.

## The Verdict

Here is the uncomfortable truth neither New Zealand's transport ministers nor Honiara's bus drivers particularly want to hear: the Solomon Islands' system, chaotic as it looks on paper, delivers a more honest commuting experience than New Zealand's. Not a faster one — nobody is claiming a flagged-down minibus beats a motorway on raw kilometres per hour. But New Zealand sells itself as a logistics success story and then buries its own citizens in congestion it refuses to meaningfully price or redesign, while quietly producing some of the most car-dependent sprawl in the developed world. That is not infrastructure. That is a brochure with a traffic jam folded inside it.

The Solomon Islands never wrote the brochure. It built a transport system out of improvisation, trust, and small coins, and that system does exactly what it says on the tin: it gets you from Point Cruz to wherever, eventually, with someone else's elbow in your ribs. There is a dignity in a system that never lies to you about what it is.

Winner: Solomon Islands, on honesty. New Zealand wins on pavement. Pavement, it turns out, is not the same thing as a functioning culture of getting around.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com forum (New Zealand) — paraphrased: newcomers warn that Auckland's public transport looks tidy on an app but bus routes get rerouted or cut with little notice, so new arrivals are told to always keep a backup plan and never rely on a single line to get anywhere on time.*

> *Expat.com forum (Solomon Islands) — paraphrased: long-term residents caution that there's no real taxi-hailing system in Honiara — you either know a driver personally or flag down a passing truck, and turning up anywhere at an agreed time is treated as a polite fiction, not an expectation.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Honiara — paraphrased: a posted account from a resident diplomat notes that a four-wheel-drive isn't optional if you want to get anywhere outside the main road during the wet season, since even short trips can mean fording stretches where the road has simply stopped behaving like one.*

## Conclusion

I have relocated fourteen times, which means I have spent an embarrassing fraction of my adult life standing at bus stops in countries that promised me a timetable and delivered a philosophy lecture instead. New Zealand and the Solomon Islands sit at opposite ends of the same Pacific spectrum, and neither one is "exotic" for it — they are simply two very different answers to the same unglamorous question of how to move bodies across a city without losing your mind. New Zealand answered with tarmac, apps, and a quiet national resentment simmering under every windscreen. The Solomon Islands answered with trucks, trust, and the radical idea that a system can be entirely informal and still function better than its reputation suggests. If you want predictability, book New Zealand and budget for traffic-induced despair. If you want to understand what transport looks like when nobody is pretending, go to Honiara, flag down a truck, and hang on.
