# One Country Waves Politely From the Balcony. The Other Just Walks In With Coffee.

🇧🇪 Belgium · 🇧🇦 Bosnia and Herzegovina
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Belgium has spent centuries perfecting the art of the friendly, permanent distance — neighbours who wave, nod, water each other's plants while you're away, and never once ask what you actually do for a living. Bosnia and Herzegovina has the mahala, a neighbourhood structure so socially dense that privacy becomes something you negotiate rather than assume. Both are, in their own ways, communities that function. They simply disagree, fundamentally, on what "knowing your neighbour" is supposed to mean.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Belgium**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Introduce yourself formally when you move in — a knock, a brief chat | Expect spontaneous drop-ins; visits are usually arranged in advance |
| Join the local buurtcomité (neighbourhood committee) if one exists | Play music or run noisy renovations outside strictly permitted hours |
| Bring something small to a street or block party if invited | Assume friendliness equals closeness — warmth here stays fairly measured |

**Bosnia and Herzegovina**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept coffee when a neighbour offers it — declining reads as a rebuff | Be surprised when neighbours know your business before you've told them |
| Return the invitation eventually — reciprocity matters deeply | Treat a mahala as just a street; it's closer to an extended household |
| Greet everyone on the street individually — anonymity isn't really an option | Rush a coffee visit; budgeting under an hour will offend the host |

## Belgium: Politeness As Load-Bearing Infrastructure

Belgian neighbourhood life runs on an unspoken contract: everyone will be reliably, consistently kind, and almost nobody will ask you a genuinely personal question for the first several years. This isn't coldness — Belgians will absolutely help you carry furniture up four flights of stairs, water your plants for a fortnight, and alert you the instant your bin is out on the wrong collection day — but it operates with a formality that surprises people who arrive expecting Mediterranean-style warmth.

The buurtcomité, or neighbourhood committee, is where actual community life concentrates — organizing the street barbecue, the seasonal cleanup, occasionally a genuinely fierce dispute over a proposed bike lane. These committees are where you earn real neighbourhood standing, and showing up consistently matters more than any single grand gesture. Belgians reward reliability over charm, which is either refreshing or maddening depending on your own cultural defaults.

What trips up newcomers most is the noise regulation, enforced with a rigor that borders on the theological. Sunday quiet hours, weekday evening cutoffs for renovation work, precise rules about when a lawnmower may legally operate — Belgians take collective quiet extremely seriously, and a neighbour will absolutely knock to remind you, politely, that it is 9:58pm and your music needs to stop in two minutes. There's an entire genre of local folklore around passive-aggressive notes left on shared stairwells, and having lived through several, I can confirm the genre is not exaggerated.

Where Belgium quietly excels is longevity. Once you're actually known on a street — genuinely known, ten years in, invited to the annual barbecue without being asked twice — the relationships have a startling permanence. Belgian neighbourliness isn't fast. It compounds.

## Bosnia and Herzegovina: The Mahala Doesn't Believe in Strangers

The mahala — the traditional Ottoman-era neighbourhood unit still very much alive in Sarajevo, Mostar, and smaller Bosnian towns — operates on a completely different premise: that a neighbourhood is basically an extended family that happens to live in separate houses. Coffee, or kafa, is the connective tissue. It is offered constantly, expected to be accepted, and treated as a genuine social obligation rather than a casual gesture. Turning down a neighbour's coffee invitation without an extremely good reason is a real, if minor, social offence.

What foreigners find disorienting, at first, is the sheer information density of mahala life. Your neighbours will know, often before you've mentioned it yourself, that you had a job interview, that your cousin visited, that you were out late on Thursday. This isn't malicious gossip so much as a structural feature — in a mahala, everyone's business is, to a real degree, communal business, and the flip side of that intrusion is a genuine, unhesitating support network. Illness, a death in the family, a financial setback — the mahala shows up, with food, with help, with presence, immediately and without being asked.

Reciprocity governs everything. If a neighbour brings you baklava, you are now, functionally, on the clock to reciprocate — not necessarily with baklava, but with something, eventually. Skipping this isn't catastrophic, but it registers, quietly, as a small debt left unpaid. Sarajevo in particular retains this structure remarkably intact despite decades of upheaval, war, and reconstruction — if anything, residents describe the mahala bonds as having strengthened through the siege years, when literal survival depended on exactly this kind of dense mutual obligation.

## The Verdict

Belgium offers a neighbourhood life that respects boundaries almost to a fault but rewards patience with real, durable community. Bosnia and Herzegovina offers something warmer and faster but genuinely more invasive — you will be known, whether you consented to it or not, and the trade for that loss of privacy is a support network most Western cities have engineered out of existence entirely. I know which one is more comfortable. I also know which one I'd actually want showing up at my door if something went badly wrong, and it isn't the country that leaves a polite note about the lawnmower.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations Brussels — paraphrased: a longtime resident admitting it took nearly four years before a Belgian neighbour invited them inside their home, and that it felt like a genuine milestone when it finally happened.*

> *expat.com Sarajevo forum — paraphrased: a newcomer describing being fed, informally adopted, and thoroughly interrogated by an elderly neighbour within their first week in a mahala.*

> *Quora, on mahala culture — paraphrased: a Sarajevan explaining that during the siege, mahala neighbours quite literally kept each other alive, sharing what little food existed street by street.*

## Conclusion

Belgium will teach you patience and the quiet dignity of a well-timed wave across a garden fence. Bosnia and Herzegovina will teach you that community, done properly, has no real concept of "too much." Both are working models of neighbourhood life, built for entirely different temperaments. Pick the one that matches your tolerance for unannounced coffee — and if you move to Sarajevo, budget the hour. You're not getting out of that visit early.
