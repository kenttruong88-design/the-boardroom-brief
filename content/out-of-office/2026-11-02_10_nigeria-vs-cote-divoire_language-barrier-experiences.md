# One Country Has 500 Languages And Uses English. The Other Has Three And Won't Budge.

🇳🇬🇨🇮 Nigeria & Côte d'Ivoire
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nigeria is, linguistically, one of the most complicated countries on the planet — more than five hundred languages, three dominant ones in Hausa, Yoruba, and Igbo, and somehow, through the specific accident of British colonial administration, English sits on top of all of it as the genuine working language of business, government, and most daily professional life in Lagos and Abuja. Côte d'Ivoire, by contrast, inherited French from its own colonial administration, and unlike Nigeria's pragmatic English-as-lingua-franca solution, French in Abidjan is not merely administrative convenience, it is the actual, enforced, no-exceptions medium of professional life, and arriving without it is a genuinely different order of obstacle.

I have bluffed my way through entire business meetings in Lagos on English alone and nobody blinked. I have attempted the same bluff in Abidjan and watched a perfectly polite French-speaking colleague simply, gently, continue the entire conversation in French anyway, because why would they do otherwise in their own capital.

## Do's & Don'ts: Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn a handful of Pidgin English phrases — it's the real social glue across ethnic and linguistic lines, more than standard English | Assume formal British-style English covers every social situation; Pidgin carries warmth and humour standard English can't |
| Pick up basic greetings in Yoruba, Hausa, or Igbo depending on your region — the effort is noticed and genuinely appreciated | Expect one of the three major languages to cover the whole country; regional loyalty to local languages runs deep |
| Rely on English confidently for business and official matters — it's the genuine working language at that level, not a workaround | Assume English fluency means cultural fluency; the language barrier you won't see coming is contextual, not literal |
| Ask colleagues to explain local idioms and Pidgin expressions — the richness of everyday speech rewards genuine curiosity | Laugh along without understanding — Nigerians will absolutely notice and will absolutely call it out |

## Do's & Don'ts: Côte d'Ivoire

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest seriously in French before arriving — functional business French is close to a hard requirement in Abidjan's professional world | Expect English to get you through professional life the way it does in Lagos; it genuinely doesn't, even in international business settings |
| Learn that Nouchi, the local urban slang blend, carries real social currency among younger Abidjanais | Assume formal French alone unlocks full social fluency; Nouchi signals you're actually plugged into the city, not just visiting it |
| Use French-language apps and local tutors rather than relying on translation tools in real-time conversation | Lean on a translation app mid-meeting; it reads as unprepared in a business culture that values language fluency as professionalism |
| Accept that most official and corporate documentation will be exclusively in French, no bilingual version provided | Expect English-language contracts or official paperwork as a default option; French is the only track, full stop |

## Nigeria: English As The Accidental National Compromise

Nigeria's linguistic landscape, with over five hundred distinct languages according to the National Bureau of Statistics' own survey data, could easily have become an impossible daily obstacle to national business and governance. Instead, British colonial administration left English sitting on top of the whole structure as a genuinely neutral, widely spoken working language — not because anyone's first language, but because it belongs to no single ethnic group, which, in a country this linguistically fractured, functions as an actual advantage rather than a colonial hangover people resent day to day.

What actually trips up newcomers isn't English comprehension, it's Nigerian Pidgin, the real connective social tissue that carries humour, solidarity, and context that formal English simply can't touch. The British Council's own regional proficiency reporting consistently ranks Nigeria among the strongest English-proficiency markets in West Africa, and that strength genuinely does translate into professional ease for English-speaking expats — you will be understood everywhere that matters. You just won't always understand the half of the conversation happening in Pidgin around you, and that's where the real cultural barrier quietly lives.

## Côte d'Ivoire: French Without Compromise

Abidjan runs almost entirely on French at every level that matters professionally, and the Organisation Internationale de la Francophonie's own business language reporting makes clear this isn't a soft preference, it's the structural default. Unlike Nigeria's English-as-neutral-compromise solution, Côte d'Ivoire's French came from a colonial administration that pursued a far more thorough linguistic assimilation policy, and the result today is a business culture where fluency in French isn't a nice-to-have for an expat, it's close to a precondition for being taken seriously in a meeting room.

Underneath the formal French sits Nouchi, Abidjan's own vibrant urban slang blend drawing on French, English, and various local languages, which functions for young Abidjanais roughly the way Pidgin functions in Lagos — the real social language, carrying identity and humour that textbook French doesn't capture. The INS census data confirms French literacy is near-universal in urban areas, which removes any excuse for an Anglophone professional hoping English might quietly suffice; it will not, and the Ivorian business world has absolutely no obligation to accommodate that hope.

## The Verdict

Nigeria wins this one for pure practical accessibility — English speakers land, function, and thrive professionally with minimal friction, Pidgin's social subtleties aside. Côte d'Ivoire demands actual investment before you arrive, no shortcuts available, and the business culture will not soften that requirement out of politeness. If your French is weak, Abidjan will find out immediately, and so will everyone in the room with you.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Nigeria — paraphrased: a thread where several Lagos professionals agreed that understanding Pidgin fluently took longer than most expats expect, well after English comprehension stopped being an issue at all.*

> *Quora — paraphrased: a response to a question about working in Abidjan without French bluntly stated that "it is not possible beyond a tourist stay," and that even bilingual multinational offices default to French internally.*

> *Internations Abidjan — paraphrased: a relocated auditor admitted that after two years, their biggest professional handicap remained not formal French grammar, but missing the Nouchi slang younger colleagues used constantly in informal office chat.*

## Conclusion

Nigeria built an accidental linguistic compromise that happens to work beautifully for English-speaking outsiders, even if the real cultural fluency still lives one layer down in Pidgin. Côte d'Ivoire built no such compromise, and Abidjan has absolutely no intention of apologising for that. If you're choosing where to land without fluency prepared, the choice makes itself. If you're choosing where to actually grow, learn French properly, and I promise Abidjan will respect you considerably more for the effort than it ever will for the attempt to skip it.
