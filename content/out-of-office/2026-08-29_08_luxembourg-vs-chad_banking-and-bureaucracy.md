# One Country Has Too Much Paperwork. The Other Barely Has a System to Paper

🇱🇺 Luxembourg vs 🇹🇩 Chad — Banking and Bureaucracy

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have been rejected by a Luxembourg bank for showing up without a "promesse d'embauche" I did not know existed, and I have opened, or attempted to open, a bank account in N'Djamena where the actual requirements seemed to be assembled on the spot by someone doing their genuine best with very little institutional scaffolding behind them. Both experiences took roughly the same number of visits. Only one of them had air conditioning.

Luxembourg's bureaucracy is the product of decades spent perfecting itself into one of the most rigid financial compliance regimes on the planet, because when your entire economy runs on being a trusted European financial hub, you cannot afford to let paperwork be casual about anything. Chad's banking sector, concentrated in a handful of urban centres and mostly run by regional players like Ecobank and Société Générale, doesn't have that luxury of decades of refinement — it operates lean, with lending criteria rigid mostly because the underlying systems supporting risk assessment simply aren't as developed. One country's bureaucracy is a fortress. The other's is closer to a work in progress, honestly labelled as such.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇱🇺 Luxembourg**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Assemble your full KYC dossier — ID, address proof, employment contract — before any appointment | Show up expecting to open an account with just a passport and good intentions |
| Get your déclaration d'arrivée from the commune early; you'll need it as address proof | Assume EU and non-EU nationals face the same requirements — they don't |
| Expect one to two weeks for a salaried EU resident with full paperwork | Expect a live card same-day; even the fast track takes over a week |
| Have your promesse d'embauche ready if you haven't started work yet | Treat KYC compliance as negotiable — it isn't, anywhere in this sector |

**🇹🇩 Chad**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Establish legal residency first — it's the real prerequisite for everything else | Expect a fixed, published checklist; requirements vary by bank and by circumstance |
| Keep a foreign account running alongside any local one for larger transactions | Rely solely on a local account for major business or personal banking needs |
| Get a travel permit from the Ministry of the Interior before heading outside N'Djamena | Assume banking infrastructure exists at the same level outside the capital |
| Budget realistic time and patience for any administrative process | Expect medium-term loans; lending criteria here are rigid and collateral-heavy |

## Luxembourg: Compliance as a National Identity

Luxembourg didn't stumble into being a financial hub, it built the entire country around it, and the bureaucracy reflects that with a precision that borders on the theatrical. Opening a bank account here means arriving with a genuinely complete dossier — national ID for EU citizens, or a passport plus residence permit for everyone else, proof of address via a utility bill or the déclaration d'arrivée issued by your local commune, and an employment contract or recent salary slip, or, if you haven't started your job yet, a signed promesse d'embauche from your employer. Miss one document and you don't get a partial pass, you get sent home to reassemble the file and rebook, because Know Your Customer compliance here isn't a box-ticking suggestion, it's the entire reason foreign capital trusts this country in the first place.

The timeline rewards preparation and punishes improvisation: a salaried EU resident with a complete dossier can be looking at a live card in one to two weeks, while non-EU nationals with an already-issued residence permit are typically looking at two to three weeks. What nobody tells you before you arrive is how much of this friction is actually reassuring once you understand it — this is a country whose entire brand depends on rigid compliance, and the same system that makes your first bank appointment feel like a small trial is the reason Luxembourg's financial sector is trusted with money from the rest of the planet. You just have to show up with the paperwork already assembled, because nobody here is going to meet you halfway on incomplete documentation, ever.

## Chad: Building the Plane While Flying It

Chad's banking sector operates at a completely different scale and maturity, concentrated mainly in N'Djamena and other urban centres, dominated by regional subsidiaries of banks like Ecobank, Société Générale, and UBA rather than a deep domestic banking tradition. Opening an account as a foreigner requires legal residency first — that's the actual gate you need to pass through — after which the specific documentation required tends to follow classic lines (passport, residence card, proof of address, employer letter) without the country having published a single rigid, universal checklist the way Luxembourg has. In practice, this means requirements can shift slightly depending on which bank and which branch you're dealing with, which is less a design flaw than an honest reflection of a system that's still developing its own internal consistency.

Credit is available but expensive and cautious — short-term loans commonly run twelve to twenty-five percent, and medium-term financing is genuinely difficult to secure because lending criteria remain rigid and collateral-heavy in a way that reflects real limits on the depth of the domestic financial system. Sensible expats and larger local businesses alike often keep accounts with foreign or regional banks running in parallel to whatever local account they hold, using the local account for day-to-day expenses and routing anything substantial through more established institutions elsewhere. Beyond banking itself, day-to-day bureaucracy carries its own quiet friction — travel outside N'Djamena, for instance, often requires a permit from the Ministry of the Interior, a reminder that Chad's administrative systems are still built around caution and control rather than convenience. None of this makes Chad's system worse than Luxembourg's, exactly — it makes it younger, and considerably more honest about being younger.

## The Verdict

Luxembourg will make you suffer through a rigid, document-heavy process precisely because that rigidity is the entire point — it's the price of admission to one of the most trusted financial systems on earth. Chad will make you suffer through an inconsistent, developing process because the system itself is still finding its footing, and the friction you feel is the friction of infrastructure still being built rather than deliberately enforced. I'd rather deal with Luxembourg's exhausting precision than Chad's improvisation any day of the week, if only because at least in Luxembourg, once you've cleared the gate, you actually know where you stand.

## Conclusion

Neither of these countries will let you bank casually, and both will teach you, in very different registers, that bureaucracy is really just a country's confidence made visible on paper. Luxembourg's confidence is total, exacting, and unforgiving of shortcuts. Chad's is still under construction, honestly so, and asks for patience rather than perfection. Bring every document you own to Luxembourg. Bring patience, a foreign account, and low expectations to Chad. Neither will apologise for the wait.
