# China Makes You Queue for Everything, Poland Makes You Choose a Side

🇨🇳 China vs 🇵🇱 Poland — Healthcare and Expat Medical Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

China's public hospitals move an almost incomprehensible volume of patients through a system that is simultaneously chaotic, crowded, and — against all visual evidence — often remarkably fast once you understand the choreography. Poland runs a genuinely solid public healthcare system (NFZ) alongside a thriving private sector, and the entire expat medical experience there boils down to one decision made early and revisited often: how much are you willing to pay to skip the wait.

I have queued for a numbered ticket at a Shanghai hospital at 6am alongside a hundred grandmothers who all arrived earlier than me and somehow still let me go first, and I have waited four months for a routine specialist appointment on Poland's public system before caving and paying for a private one that happened the following Tuesday. Both systems worked, eventually. Neither warned me what I was in for.

## Do's & Don'ts: China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use a hospital app (like those from major tertiary hospitals) to book tickets in advance | Show up without cash or a Chinese payment app linked — many systems still expect it |
| Go early — arriving at 6am for a 8am opening is completely normal and expected | Assume a "top-tier" (三甲) hospital is automatically the right choice for a minor issue |
| Bring a Mandarin-speaking friend or use a translation app for anything beyond basic care | Expect Western-style scheduled appointment slots at most public hospitals |
| Get international health insurance covering private or VIP hospital wings for serious issues | Skip travel/health insurance thinking public care will be simple to navigate as a foreigner |

## Do's & Don'ts: Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with the NFZ if you're a resident — it's genuinely free at the point of care | Expect a quick specialist appointment through the public system without a long wait |
| Get supplementary private insurance (like Luxmed or Medicover) if you can afford it | Assume private care means poor public care — Poland's public doctors are well trained |
| Learn the difference between a POZ (primary care) referral and direct specialist access | Ignore a referral requirement — public specialist access usually needs one |
| Keep both public and private options on your radar depending on urgency | Wait passively for a public appointment if something is genuinely urgent — go private |

## China: Volume, Speed, and a System That Somehow Works

Chinese public hospitals operate at a scale that simply doesn't exist in most Western healthcare systems — a single major tertiary hospital in Beijing or Shanghai can see tens of thousands of outpatients a day, and the resulting queues, crowds, and ticket-number systems look, to a first-time visitor, like total disorder. It isn't. China's hospital tiering system (三甲, 三乙, and so on) creates a clear hierarchy of facility quality, and most hospitals now run app-based ticketing (挂号) that lets you book a specific doctor's slot in advance, entirely bypassing the in-person scrum that used to define the experience — the app is the actual system; the physical queue is what happens to people who didn't use it.

Once you're actually being seen, Chinese healthcare moves with startling efficiency: tests are often run and results returned the same day, specialists see high volumes and make decisions quickly, and the overall throughput of a Chinese public hospital would make many Western emergency departments weep with envy. What foreigners often find jarring is the lack of privacy and unhurried bedside manner that Western medicine has trained them to expect — consultations are brief, direct, and transactional, not because the care is careless but because the sheer patient volume makes lingering conversation a luxury the system cannot structurally support.

For anything beyond routine care, international and VIP wings attached to major hospitals offer a genuinely different experience — English-speaking staff, appointment-based scheduling, and prices that reflect it, functioning almost as a parallel healthcare system for expats and wealthier locals willing to pay for the queue-free version of the same excellent doctors.

## Poland: A Perfectly Good System You'll Probably Pay to Avoid

Poland's public healthcare system, funded through the NFZ, is free at the point of use for residents and covers a genuinely comprehensive range of care, with well-trained doctors who, in many specialties, are every bit the equal of their Western European counterparts. The catch, and it's a significant one, is time: public specialist appointments routinely involve waits measured in months rather than weeks, a structural bottleneck that has become such a defining feature of Polish healthcare that private insurance has grown from a nice-to-have into something close to a practical necessity for anyone who can afford roughly the price of a gym membership per month.

Private providers like Luxmed and Medicover have built entire business models around this gap, offering appointment slots within days rather than months, English-speaking doctors in major cities, and a level of scheduling convenience that mirrors Western private healthcare almost exactly. Most middle-class Poles and virtually all expats who can afford it end up running a hybrid system: NFZ registration kept active as a safety net for genuinely serious or expensive treatment, private insurance used for the actual day-to-day business of seeing a doctor without losing a season of your life to the waiting list.

The structural logic that trips up newcomers is the referral system — public specialist access typically requires a referral from a POZ (primary care) doctor first, adding another queue before the queue you actually came for, whereas private clinics let you book a specialist directly, no gatekeeper required, which is precisely the convenience you're paying the monthly fee for in the first place.

## The Verdict

China's system is a marvel of volume and speed once you learn to work its app-based choreography; Poland's system is a genuinely solid public foundation undermined by wait times that push almost everyone with means into a parallel private track. If I had to pick which country's healthcare I'd rather need urgently on a random Tuesday, China wins on raw throughput — you will be seen, tested, and diagnosed faster than almost anywhere else on earth, provided you can navigate the app and tolerate a briefer bedside manner. Poland wins on trust and long-term relationship with a single doctor, provided you're willing to pay for the privilege of not waiting four months to see one.

## Conclusion

China's healthcare system proves that scale and technology can substitute for the intimacy Western patients expect, and does so with genuine, if brusque, efficiency. Poland's proves that a solid public foundation can coexist with a private sector that quietly becomes the default for anyone with the means to use it. Neither country is broken. Both simply expect you to learn their particular choreography before you actually need it — which, inconveniently, is exactly when you have the least patience to learn anything new at all.
