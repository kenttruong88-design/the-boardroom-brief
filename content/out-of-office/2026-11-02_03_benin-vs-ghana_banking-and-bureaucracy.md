# One Country Makes You Beg A Bank For A Favour. The Other Makes You Beg Several.

🇧🇯🇬🇭 Benin & Ghana
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a very specific kind of despair reserved for the moment you realise the document you need to open a bank account requires a document you can only get from a government office that requires the bank account you don't have yet. Benin and Ghana have both built bureaucracies that specialise in this particular circular cruelty, and having been strangled by both, I feel qualified to rank them, loudly, with no patience whatsoever for anyone who calls either of these systems "charmingly inefficient." They are not charming. They are structurally designed to make you grateful for the smallest competence, and that gratitude is the whole trick.

Benin runs on the CFA franc and the regional BCEAO banking framework shared with seven other West African states, which sounds like it should mean standardisation and ease. It does not. Ghana runs its own currency, the cedi, with its own central bank and its own mobile-money-first financial inclusion miracle that genuinely is one of the better stories in African fintech — right up until you need something a mobile wallet can't do, at which point you discover the formal banking sector underneath hasn't caught up to its own marketing.

## Do's & Don'ts: Benin

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring certified, stamped copies of every document — originals alone are routinely rejected | Assume one branch's requirements match another branch of the same bank; they don't, consistently |
| Use a local fixer or well-connected colleague for your first account opening — it genuinely speeds things up | Attempt major bureaucratic errands alone in your first month; local social capital is the actual system |
| Expect in-person visits for anything beyond the simplest transaction — digital banking exists but doesn't cover everything | Expect email or phone follow-up to work; most resolution happens by showing up again in person |
| Keep a folder of certified document copies permanently on hand — you will be asked again | Throw away "unnecessary" paperwork; nothing is ever truly finished with Beninese bureaucracy |

## Do's & Don'ts: Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comfortable with mobile money (MoMo) immediately — it's often faster and more reliable than formal banking for daily transactions | Assume mobile money replaces a bank account entirely; larger transfers, loans, and business accounts still need a bank |
| Bring your Ghana Card (national ID) to every financial errand — it's now central to KYC checks | Try to open a business account without a Tax Identification Number sorted first; it's a hard blocker |
| Budget real time for business registration — weeks, not days, even with the online portal | Expect the online registration system to be glitch-free; have a backup in-person plan |
| Ask which specific branch of a bank has a reputation for competence — it varies wildly even within the same chain | Assume all Ghanaian banks move at the same pace; some genuinely outperform others by a wide margin |

## Benin: Bureaucracy As Inherited Colonial Furniture

Benin's administrative culture carries the unmistakable fingerprints of French bureaucratic tradition — the love of the stamped and certified copy, the insistence on the correctly notarised original, the suspicion of anything that looks too easy — grafted onto a West African regional banking system shared across the CFA zone. The BCEAO's own regional reporting on banking access shows Benin still trailing several of its franc-zone neighbours on basic account penetration, and anyone who's tried to open an account in Cotonou will tell you the paperwork alone explains a meaningful chunk of that gap.

The ritual goes roughly like this: you need a residence document to open an account, a bank account to register certain residence paperwork, and a local guarantor or fixer to cut through the loop, because the system was never actually designed to be navigated solo by someone without existing local relationships. Once you're in, it holds — the regional banking system is stable, the currency peg to the euro removes a layer of volatility risk entirely, and the staff, once you've cleared the gate, are frequently more helpful than the process that got you there deserves credit for. It's a system that punishes the newcomer and rewards the embedded.

## Ghana: Two Financial Systems Wearing One Trench Coat

Ghana gets rightly celebrated for its mobile money revolution — the Bank of Ghana's own financial inclusion data shows mobile wallets have done more for everyday financial access in a decade than formal banking managed in half a century, and for day-to-day life, paying a vendor, splitting a bill, sending money to a cousin upcountry, it genuinely works beautifully. The problem is that this success has let the formal banking sector get away with staying exactly as slow and inconsistent as it's always been, because it no longer has to compete for your daily transactions.

So the moment you need something mobile money can't do — a business account, an international wire that doesn't vanish into a three-week limbo, a loan application — you hit the old system in full, and the old system has not had the same incentive to modernise. The Ghana Revenue Authority's registration requirements are reasonably well documented on paper, but the actual processing time depends enormously on which office, which officer, and frankly which day you show up. Ghanaian colleagues will tell you, bluntly, which bank branches are worth your time and which to avoid entirely, and this local knowledge is worth more than any official guide.

## The Verdict

Ghana wins on day-to-day friction, no argument — MoMo alone makes ordinary life easier than Benin manages for the same tasks. But Benin's system, once you're embedded in it, has a strange stability to it that Ghana's two-speed banking sector doesn't quite match: once you've cleared the gate, you're actually in, rather than perpetually discovering new gates. I'd rather suffer once, properly, than suffer intermittently forever.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations Cotonou — paraphrased: a relocated engineer counted eleven separate branch visits across six weeks before a business account was finally approved, and called it "a personality test disguised as paperwork."*

> *Reddit r/Ghana — paraphrased: a thread where multiple expats agreed that international wire transfers into Ghanaian accounts "disappear into a void" for anywhere from four days to three weeks with no tracking.*

> *Quora — paraphrased: a response to a business-registration question noted that the online portal's uptime was "a coin flip," and that showing up in person was still the only reliable method, portal or not.*

## Conclusion

Both systems are run by people doing their actual best inside structures that weren't built with your convenience in mind — Benin's inherited from a colonial administrative obsession with paper, Ghana's straining under its own fintech success story outrunning the institutions underneath it. If you're moving to either, build in the time, build in the patience, and build in a local friend who already knows exactly which stamp goes where. You will need all three. Repeatedly.
