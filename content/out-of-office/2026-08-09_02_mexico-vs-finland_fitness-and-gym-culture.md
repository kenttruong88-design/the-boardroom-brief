# Mexico Will Sell You A Gym Membership You Can Never Cancel. Finland Will Just Send You Outside To Suffer, For Free, In The Dark.

🇲🇽 🇫🇮 *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Fitness culture, more than almost any other category of daily life, reveals what a country actually believes about willpower. Mexico believes willpower needs to be sold to you, contractually, with a sales rep who will not let you leave the building without a signature. Finland believes willpower is simply what happens to a person who has been outside in November enough times that the gym stops feeling optional. Both nations produce genuinely fit populations. Neither nation produces them the way you'd expect.

I've had gym inductions in both countries, and I can tell you the Mexican one lasted ninety minutes and ended with me quietly signing a twelve-month contract I didn't fully understand, and the Finnish one lasted four minutes because the trainer assumed, correctly, that I already knew how a squat rack worked and had better things to do than be supervised.

## Do's & Don'ts: Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read every line of a gym contract before signing — cancellation clauses are notoriously punishing and rarely explained upfront | Assume a "day pass" price quoted verbally matches what shows up on the card machine |
| Use the free public outdoor gyms (gimnasios al aire libre) found in parks across most cities — they're genuinely excellent and free | Show up to a serious gym in flip-flops; closed shoes are enforced more strictly than in most countries |
| Expect a full sales pitch and a personal trainer upsell within your first five minutes in the building | Skip the AC in summer gyms; some budget locations run hot, and locals plan workout times around it |

## Do's & Don'ts: Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comfortable running, cycling, or cross-country skiing in genuine darkness and cold — it's the national default, not an eccentricity | Expect small talk at the gym; headphones in, eyes down, is the universal signal and it's respected absolutely |
| Use the sauna after your workout — it's assumed, not optional, and most gyms have one built in | Wear too much; Finnish sauna and locker room nudity norms are matter-of-fact and modesty around it reads as odd |
| Take advantage of extensive, well-lit, well-maintained public exercise trails — city investment in outdoor fitness infrastructure is enormous | Judge fitness by gym attendance alone; huge amounts of Finnish exercise happens entirely outdoors, unrecorded, unphotographed |

## Mexico: Fitness As Sales Funnel, And Also As Free Park Culture

Mexican gym culture splits cleanly into two universes that barely acknowledge each other. At the top: glossy chains like Sports World and Smart Fit, air-conditioned, mirrored, staffed by trainers whose real job is to sell you a "personalizado" package you did not ask for and will be gently guilt-tripped about at every visit. The contracts are legendary among expats for their opacity — annual commitments, automatic renewals, cancellation processes that require an in-person visit during oddly specific hours. Reddit threads on the subject read like hostage negotiations.

But run parallel to that, and largely ignored by anyone who's just moved from a country without the tradition, is one of the best-kept fitness secrets in Latin America: gimnasios al aire libre, the free public outdoor gyms scattered through parks in Mexico City, Guadalajara, and beyond. Steel pull-up bars, elliptical stations, leg presses, all bolted into concrete, all free, all used from 6 a.m. onward by a cross-section of the city that has nothing to do with the gym-membership economy at all — abuelas doing leg lifts next to guys doing pull-up sets before their shift starts. It's fitness stripped of the sales pitch entirely, funded by the city, used by everyone, and it says more about actual Mexican fitness culture than any Smart Fit branch ever will.

The social layer matters too. Mexican gym-goers tend to work out in pairs or small groups, chatting between sets, treating the gym as a mildly social space rather than a monastic one. Silence is not the default. Neither is minimalism — bright branding, loud reggaeton, and mirrors everywhere are the house style.

## Finland: Fitness As Ambient Cultural Fact

Finns don't really talk about being fit, because being fit isn't treated as an achievement — it's treated as basic maintenance, like brushing your teeth, and boasting about it would be strange. This is a country where cross-country skiing to the shop in winter is unremarkable, where "exercise" and "commute" are frequently the same seven kilometres on a bike, and where running in complete darkness at minus fifteen degrees is not a personality trait, just a Tuesday.

Gyms exist — Elixia, SATS, and a strong municipal-gym network keep prices reasonable and quality high — but they're treated as one tool among many, not the centre of fitness identity. The sauna, appended to nearly every serious gym as a matter of course, is the actual social ritual: sweaty, silent, communal, entirely undramatic about nudity in a way that stuns visitors from more modest cultures. Nobody talks in it much either. You sit, you sweat, you leave, refreshed and unbothered.

What's most striking to outsiders is the sheer infrastructure invested in outdoor movement — lit running trails through actual forest, cross-country ski tracks groomed through city parks in winter, public sports fields maintained to a standard most countries reserve for professional venues. Finland has essentially built an entire nation-sized gym out of its own geography and made using it free.

## The Verdict

Mexico will hook you into a contract you'll regret and then, almost as an apology, hand you a free world-class outdoor gym you didn't know to look for. Finland will never sell you anything, but it will expect you to run through a blizzard and call it Tuesday. If you want energy, community, and a sales rep who remembers your name, Mexico. If you want silence, competence, and a sauna that asks no questions, Finland. I've done both. My knees prefer Finland. My social life prefers Mexico. My wallet prefers neither, because gyms are a scam everywhere, philosophically speaking, and I say that as someone who has paid for six of them across four continents.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/mexicocity — a user described spending forty minutes trying to cancel a gym membership in person, only to be told the cancellation window had technically closed the week prior and next year's dues were already processing.*

> *Reddit r/Finland — an expat admitted they thought their Finnish colleagues were joking about running outside in sub-zero darkness until they realised the entire office did it before work, unremarked upon, every single day.*

> *expat.com Helsinki — a new arrival asked whether sauna nudity was really as casual as described and was told, bluntly, that overthinking it was the only thing that would actually make it awkward.*

## Conclusion

What both countries share, oddly, is total conviction. Mexico is convinced fitness needs a sales pitch and a park bench, sometimes both. Finland is convinced fitness needs no explanation at all, just a coat and the will to survive your own commute. Neither is wrong. Neither is entirely comfortable either, if you're new to it — you'll either be signing something you don't understand or standing naked in a sauna next to your new colleague within the first month, guaranteed. Pack accordingly. Read contracts. Buy a headlamp.
