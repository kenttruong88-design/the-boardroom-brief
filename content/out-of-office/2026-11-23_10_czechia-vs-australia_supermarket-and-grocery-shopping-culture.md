# Australia Has Two Supermarkets and a Rewards Programme; Czechia Has a Dozen and a Holiday

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A supermarket is the most honest museum a country has. It tells you what people eat, what they can afford, who they trust and what the government has decided they are not allowed to do on a Sunday. Walk into one at nine in the morning and you can read the national character from the shelf layout, which in Australia says "abundance, mild chaos and an unreasonable number of cleaning products", and in Czechia says "order, cabbage and a very clear opinion about the opening hours".

I have shopped in both with the serene confidence of a woman who has done grocery runs in sixty countries and the nagging conviction that she has still got the wrong sort of bag. The surprises are the small ones: a loyalty app that wants your phone number, a shop that closes without warning, a cheese counter you did not see coming. Expats will tell you the supermarket is where culture shock lives. I would go further and say it is where it pays rent.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇨🇿 Czechia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the national holiday list before you plan a shop | Assume a big supermarket will be open on Easter Monday |
| Use loyalty apps such as the Tesco Clubcard or Kaufland coupons | Pay full price when the app is free |
| Try Albert, Lidl, Kaufland and Penny and compare | Assume all chains are the same price |
| Look for medicine at a pharmacy, not the supermarket | Expect paracetamol next to the bread |

| 🇦🇺 Australia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Compare weekly specials at Woolworths and Coles | Assume one chain is always cheaper |
| Try ALDI for the basics | Dismiss it as a discount-only store |
| Visit Asian grocers for rice, noodles, spices and frozen goods | Pay supermarket prices for everything |
| Use a price-comparison app | Shop without checking the specials |

## 🇨🇿 Czechia

Czech grocery shopping is a matter of many chains and one firm rule. The Expats.cz reader poll, from 2023, asked 599 readers where they shop most often and got Albert on 36 percent, Lidl on 19, Kaufland and Tesco on 9 each, Billa on 6, Delmart and Penny Market on 3 each and the rest scattered. Asked which was cheapest, about 140 respondents ranked Lidl first, with Penny Market, Kaufland, Albert, Billa and Tesco following. About half shop online sometimes, with Rohlik the most popular delivery service. It is a poll of expat readers, not the national population, so I would take it as a picture of the foreign shopper's world rather than the Czech household's.

The named voices add colour. A reader called Margarita praises Albert for its selection of healthy and high-quality food. Laura likes Kaufland's in-app coupons. Christian rates Makro for foreign products. Giovanni prefers Globus for its Scan&Go. Casey says Marks & Spencer's range shrank after Brexit. Several readers complained about staff who do not speak English, and a few reported xenophobia at certain chains, which is worth knowing and difficult to measure.

Magdalena Marsden, a Czech who lives in Prague and writes the View from Prague guide, offers the native shopper's cynicism. She says all of the supermarkets are pretty much the same, with Penny usually the cheapest, then Kaufland, Billa, Tesco and Albert. Delmart is the only premium chain, around 30 to 50 percent dearer than regular supermarkets, and minimarkets cost at least 25 to 30 percent more. Pharmacy items are not sold in supermarkets, and Teta, Rossmann and DM sell cosmetics but not medicine.

And then the rule. Marsden explains that supermarkets over 200 square metres must close on seven national holidays, including New Year's Day, Easter Monday, 8 May, 28 September, 28 October and 25 and 26 December, with hospitals, airports and train stations exempt. If you have not planned ahead, you will find the shutters down and the cupboards bare, and you will learn what a Czech grandmother already knows: buy the potatoes on the 23rd.

It is, I would argue, a charming piece of public policy. The shop closes, the family stays at home and the economy survives the strain. For an expat from a country where everything is open all the time, it is a lesson in the value of scarcity.

## 🇦🇺 Australia

Australia's grocery landscape is a different animal: a duopoly of giants, a disruptor and a fringe of independents. The Insider Guides comparison, published in July, says Woolworths and Coles are the two dominant chains, both with rewards programmes, and advises shoppers to compare weekly specials at both. ALDI, according to a Choice survey it cites, came out around ten dollars cheaper on a full basket. IGA is independently owned and often found in rural towns. Asian grocers offer competitive prices on rice, noodles, spices and frozen items, and fresh markets are often good for seasonal produce but not always cheapest. An app called Frugl compares prices across the main chains.

The author does not offer a Czech comparison, and I could not reach a second Australian guide, so I will say plainly that this is a thinner picture than I would like. What it describes, though, is familiar to anyone who has lived in the country: the market is concentrated, and the consumer's main defence is to shop around. The duopoly is a recurring national grievance, the specials culture is a minor religion and the fact that you need two loyalty cards to feel you are not being robbed says something about the market structure.

The Australian supermarket is also a place of comfortable abundance. The aisles are wide, the produce is generally excellent and the range of everyday goods is broad. The expat arriving from Europe tends to be struck by the size of the shelves, the cost of fresh food and the way the checkout assistant asks how your day is going and appears to wait for an answer.

The other point worth making is about convenience. An Australian store is likely to be open late, including on weekends, and the idea of a nationwide enforced day of rest for supermarkets would not survive the first newspaper column. The upside is flexibility. The downside is that nobody ever gets a quiet Easter Monday.

## The Verdict

Czechia wins for charm and value, and Australia wins for abundance and convenience. If you have a budget and a calendar, the Czech approach is hard to beat: a handful of reasonable chains, decent prices, a clear set of rules and a healthy suspicion of anything labelled premium. The holiday closures are an irritation for ten minutes a year and a virtue for the rest of it.

Australia gives you more of everything, including a duopoly and the feeling of being slightly overcharged while smiling. I would shop in Prague for a week and in Sydney for a year, and I would never, in either country, forget to check the opening hours. The best advice is the same: find the cheap chain, find the specials, find the good market and stock up on the day before the holiday.

## Conclusion

Czechia will close the shop and expect you to have planned. Australia will keep it open and expect you to have a loyalty card. In both places the cheapest basket belongs to the person who reads the specials, checks the calendar and refuses to be impressed by the word premium. Buy the potatoes early. Bring your own bag. And if the shutters are down on a public holiday, remember that someone, somewhere, has decided that you should go and talk to your family instead.
