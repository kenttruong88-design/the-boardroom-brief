# One Country's Neighbours Know Your Business Before You Do, the Other's Share a Generator and a Prayer

🇦🇲 Armenia 🇳🇬 Nigeria

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There's a certain kind of community intensity you only find in places where the neighbourhood, not the nuclear family, not the state, is the actual functioning unit of daily life. Yerevan and Lagos both run on this principle, just through completely different mechanisms. In Yerevan, it's the Soviet-era courtyard, a shared concrete square hemmed in by apartment blocks, where three generations of the same families have watched each other's children grow up, argue, marry, and occasionally move to Glendale. In Lagos, it's the residential "estate," a fortified compound of houses or flats where the shared generator, the shared borehole, and the shared security guard turn strangers into something closer to extended family within about six months.

I've sat in both, a Yerevan courtyard with an elderly neighbour insisting I try her mulberry vodka before 10am, and a Lagos estate WhatsApp group arguing furiously about whose turn it was to top up diesel for the generator, and I can report that both represent genuinely functional community infrastructure that most Western cities have completely lost and don't seem to know they're missing.

## Do's & Don'ts: Armenia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say hello to every neighbour in the courtyard, every single time, without exception | Refuse food or drink offered by an elderly neighbour, it will genuinely wound them |
| Expect and accept unsolicited advice about your life choices from people you barely know | Assume courtyard gossip stays contained, it reaches every household within a day |
| Contribute to shared courtyard upkeep, even informally, it buys serious social capital | Move furniture or renovate loudly without warning the building, word travels instantly anyway |

## Do's & Don'ts: Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the estate WhatsApp group immediately, it's the actual nervous system of the community | Refuse to contribute to shared generator diesel costs, this will not be forgotten |
| Greet the security guards by name and treat them as key community figures, because they are | Assume "estate" living means anonymity like a Western apartment complex, it absolutely doesn't |
| Attend at least one estate meeting, decisions about shared costs and rules happen there | Skip contributing to a neighbour's celebration or condolence collection, it's expected, not optional |

## Armenia: The Courtyard Remembers Everything

The Soviet-designed apartment blocks that dominate Yerevan produced, almost by accident, one of the most tightly wound community structures I've encountered anywhere. The courtyard, that shared concrete space between buildings, functions as a genuine social organ. Elderly women sit out on benches monitoring all comings and goings with an intelligence-agency level of attentiveness. Children play unsupervised because six different apartments' worth of adults are informally keeping half an eye on them. And everyone, genuinely everyone, knows whose son just got engaged, whose daughter is struggling at university, and precisely which family hasn't paid their share of the shared stairwell lightbulb fund.

This isn't nosiness in the mean-spirited sense, though it can absolutely feel that way at first, particularly if you're the newcomer whose relationship status suddenly becomes courtyard-wide public knowledge within a fortnight. It's closer to a genuinely functioning mutual aid network that happens to run on gossip as its primary currency. Someone's grandmother falls ill, and within hours there's a rotation of neighbours bringing soup. Someone's car won't start, and a retired mechanic three floors up is out there in his slippers fixing it before you've finished asking.

The hospitality expectations run deep and are, frankly, non-negotiable. Refusing an offered coffee, a plate of fruit, a shot of homemade vodka from a neighbour is read as a genuine social slight, not a polite decline. You learn quickly that Armenian community life operates on a currency of reciprocal generosity that has survived Soviet collectivism, post-Soviet hardship, and now, somewhat improbably, a wave of tech-industry remote workers who mostly have no idea what they've walked into.

## Nigeria: The Estate as Infrastructure, Not Just Address

Lagos solves a problem Yerevan never had to: the state's basic services are often unreliable enough that the neighbourhood itself has to become the infrastructure. Power cuts are routine, so estates pool money for a shared generator and diesel, with contributions tracked, sometimes obsessively, on a WhatsApp group that functions as the estate's actual central nervous system, more responsive than any government office. Water can be inconsistent, so boreholes get shared. Security is a serious, non-abstract concern, so estates hire guards collectively, and those guards become genuinely important, well-known figures in daily life, greeted by name, trusted with spare keys, informed of who's travelling and when.

What struck me most, coming from cultures where a gated community often signals anonymity and mutual indifference, is how much the opposite is true in Lagos. The estate model produces intense familiarity precisely because survival-adjacent logistics, power, water, safety, force constant coordination. You cannot free-ride on the generator fund without everyone noticing and, eventually, someone politely but firmly bringing it up at the next estate meeting.

There's also a warmth that runs alongside the logistics. Naming ceremonies, weddings, funerals, these aren't private family affairs, they're estate events, with contribution envelopes circulating and attendance quietly expected. A Lagos estate absorbs you fast, sometimes faster than you're emotionally prepared for, and within months you'll find yourself invested, literally and figuratively, in the wellbeing of people you'd never have chosen as neighbours anywhere else, and wouldn't now trade for anyone.

## The Verdict

Armenia wins on sheer emotional intimacy, a community structure so tightly wound it makes privacy essentially theoretical, but delivers warmth and mutual care as an automatic byproduct. Nigeria wins on practical resilience, building genuine functioning infrastructure out of collective necessity, and getting a fierce, loyal community as the bonus feature. Both make Western suburban anonymity look, frankly, a little sad by comparison. If you want your grandmother's illness known and soup delivered within the hour, move to a Yerevan courtyard. If you want your lights to actually stay on and a WhatsApp group that will fight for you, join a Lagos estate. Either way, forget privacy. It was never on the table.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — "What is Armenian courtyard culture actually like?" paraphrased: a resident said their entire dating history became courtyard-wide knowledge before they'd even told their own mother, and there was nothing to be done about it.*

> *expat.com Yerevan forum, paraphrased: a new arrival described an elderly neighbour showing up unannounced with homemade preserves within their first week, and then checking in weekly ever since, uninvited but somehow always welcome.*

> *InterNations Lagos, paraphrased: an expat admitted they didn't understand the estate generator fund system for months, quietly underpaid, and were eventually, kindly but very directly, corrected by three separate neighbours in one week.*

## Conclusion

Community in Yerevan and Lagos isn't a lifestyle choice or a wellness trend, it's load-bearing, structurally necessary, and completely inescapable once you're in it. That's precisely what makes both places so disarming to newcomers used to nodding at neighbours once a year over a shared bin collection. You will be known. You will be fed, or billed, or both. And you will, whether you asked for it or not, belong to something. Try finding that in a London new-build with a concierge and a keycard.
