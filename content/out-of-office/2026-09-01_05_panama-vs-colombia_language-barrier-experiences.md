# Same Language, Two Completely Different Traps

🇵🇦 Panama vs 🇨🇴 Colombia — Language Barrier Experiences

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Here's a trap that catches out an embarrassing number of otherwise sensible expats: assuming that because Panama and Colombia both speak Spanish, the language experience will be roughly interchangeable. It is not. Panama's decades under American administration of the Canal Zone left behind a genuine bilingual streak in its business class that will lull you into complacency. Colombia will let you coast on that same complacency for exactly as long as it takes to leave a wealthy Bogotá neighbourhood, at which point your rehearsed restaurant Spanish will meet a paisa accent moving at double speed and quietly fall apart.

I've ordered a coffee in flawless textbook Spanish in Panama City and been answered in English before I'd finished the sentence, purely because the barista clocked my accent and decided to save us both the trouble. I've asked the exact same question in Medellín and been met with a good-natured, utterly incomprehensible torrent of slang that required a second, slower, visibly amused repetition. Both countries humbled me. Only one warned me first.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Panama**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn real Spanish anyway — don't lean on the bilingual bubble | Assume Panama City's English fluency extends to the interior provinces |
| Expect a lot of American-inflected business vocabulary | Be surprised when Canal Zone history comes up in casual conversation |
| Use English as a bridge in formal meetings if genuinely stuck | Assume everyone appreciates being answered in English — some find it presumptuous |
| Learn Panamanian slang separately — it's not generic Spanish | Confuse fluency in the capital with fluency nationwide |

**Colombia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn regional accent differences before you need them urgently | Assume Bogotá Spanish prepares you for Medellín, Cali, or the Caribbean coast |
| Slow requests down explicitly — "más despacio, por favor" is your best friend | Expect widespread English fluency outside upscale Bogotá neighbourhoods |
| Embrace "parce" and other regional slang once you're ready | Try to fake fluency — Colombians clock it fast and switch to help you anyway |
| Take a proper accent-immersion trip beyond your home city | Assume your textbook Spanish maps cleanly onto street Spanish |

## Panama: The Bilingual Mirage

Panama's peculiar linguistic history is a direct legacy of nearly a century of American administrative presence around the Canal Zone, and it left behind something genuinely unusual in Latin America: a business and professional class in Panama City that moves between English and Spanish with an ease that borders on unnerving for anyone who spent months preparing their Duolingo streak for a "real challenge." Meetings in banking, logistics, and the Canal-adjacent industries frequently happen in English by default, not as an accommodation but as the actual working language.

This creates a genuine trap. New arrivals settle into Panama City, get answered in English at the bank, the supermarket, the taxi rank, and quietly conclude their Spanish is either unnecessary or, worse, that they've somehow already achieved fluency by osmosis. Then they take a work trip to Chiriquí province or the Darién region, where the bilingual bubble evaporates entirely, and discover with some alarm that their actual working vocabulary tops out at "hola" and a strong opinion about Canal tolls.

The subtler trap is social rather than practical. Some Panamanians genuinely appreciate a foreigner making the effort in Spanish, even badly, over being smoothly redirected into English — it reads as respect rather than convenience. Lean too hard on the bilingual safety net, and you risk being perceived less as an integrated resident and more as someone who never quite bothered to show up. Panamanian Spanish also carries its own distinct slang and rhythm, borrowed vocabulary and all, that a generic Spanish course simply won't prepare you for — learning "conmigo" doesn't help much when your neighbour's inviting you somewhere "ahorita," a word that in Panama can mean anywhere from right now to sometime this week, and figuring out which requires context you don't yet have.

## Colombia: One Country, Several Different Languages

Colombia doesn't offer a bilingual safety net at all, and that turns out to be the more honest starting point. What it offers instead is staggering regional variation dressed up as a single national language. Bogotá Spanish is famously clear and deliberately paced, prized across Latin America as some of the "purest" Spanish on the continent, which lures newly arrived expats into a dangerous confidence. Then they take a weekend trip to Medellín, where paisa Spanish arrives faster, warmer, and thick with regional slang like "parce" and "qué más," and the confidence collapses within about four sentences.

Head to the Caribbean coast — Cartagena, Barranquilla — and the accent shifts again, dropping consonants and speeding up further still, closer in rhythm to the Spanish of the Caribbean islands than to anything you'd have practised in a Bogotá classroom. Head south to Cali and you'll hit yet another cadence entirely. Colombia isn't one language-learning challenge. It's four or five, stacked, and nobody warns you at the airport.

What actually works, according to every long-settled expat I've compared notes with, isn't trying to pre-learn every regional variant. It's radical honesty about your limits: "más despacio, por favor" gets deployed constantly and without embarrassment, and Colombians respond to it not with condescension but with genuine, warm patience — this is a country that takes real pride in its language and generally delights in watching a foreigner attempt it properly, slang and all, rather than retreating to English. English fluency outside a thin layer of wealthy Bogotá professionals is genuinely limited, which sounds like a disadvantage until you realise it's also the thing that forces you to actually learn the language instead of quietly avoiding it for two years.

## The Verdict

Colombia, and it's not particularly close. Panama's bilingual comfort is real, but comfort is exactly the trap — it lets you stall indefinitely while feeling like you're progressing. Colombia offers no such mercy, which sounds brutal and is actually a gift: within six honest months of "más despacio, por favor," you will speak better Spanish in Bogotá than you would after two comfortable years coasting through Panama City's English-speaking banks and boardrooms. Difficulty, infuriatingly, is sometimes just efficiency wearing a disguise.

> *Expat.com forum, Colombia thread — paraphrased: a Bogotá-based teacher warned newcomers that "your first trip to Medellín will feel like your Spanish broke overnight — it didn't, the accent just did."*

## Conclusion

Both countries speak Spanish. Neither will let you get away with assuming that settles the matter. Panama tempts you into complacency with genuine, functional English in exactly the places you'll first encounter the country, then quietly withdraws it the moment you leave the capital. Colombia offers no comfortable off-ramp at all, forces the issue immediately, and rewards the effort with warmth most newcomers don't expect. If you actually want to learn the language rather than simply survive around it, go where nobody's going to rescue you in English. It works. It's also, frankly, character-building — a phrase I use here with the maximum sarcasm it deserves.
