# Czechia Treats You Cheaply and Switzerland Treats You Expensively

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There are two ways to run a health system. You can make it cheap and accept that it will arrive with paperwork, queues and a clerk who has never heard of your situation. Or you can make it excellent and send the bill to the patient every single month, whether or not they get ill. Czechia chose the first. Switzerland, being Switzerland, chose the second and made it mandatory.

I have been ill in both, which is the only way to understand either. In Prague I was seen by a doctor who spoke better English than my bank and handled a prescription in under ten minutes. In Zurich I was seen in a clinic that smelled of lemons, treated with something approaching tenderness and billed within the fortnight. The experiences were not equal. They were not meant to be.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇨🇿 Czechia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a GP as soon as you arrive | Wait until you are ill to look for a doctor |
| Check which insurers your doctor contracts with | Assume every clinic accepts every plan |
| Bring your insurance card and a patient attitude | Expect English at every desk |
| Ask about co-payments for visits and prescriptions | Be surprised by small charges at the counter |

| 🇨🇭 Switzerland ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take out the compulsory basic insurance within the deadline | Treat health insurance as optional |
| Compare insurers and deductible levels each autumn | Stay with the first plan you were given |
| Book appointments in advance | Walk into a surgery and expect to be seen |
| Keep every bill and reimbursement form | Throw away anything with a franc sign on it |

## 🇨🇿 Czechia

According to Expats.cz in June 2026, Czech healthcare is fundamentally excellent and affordable, but foreigners often hit bureaucratic walls. That sentence is the whole country. The care itself, by most accounts, is good. The route to it is not always signposted. The same article stresses that registering with a GP is essential, and cites about 1,000 formal complaints a year against medical professionals, which tells you the system takes patients seriously even when it can be maddening.

The structure is public health insurance, run through several insurers. Expat Exchange names VZP as the largest, state-owned and the default for most, with ČPZP and OZP among the others. Expats qualify with residency or a visa. A forum poster known as mosu, in a thread now several years old, explains that all Czech residents must have health insurance, that doctors contract with specific insurers and that employers must insure workers who hold a labour permit. The fees he lists were small even for 2008: 30 koruna for a doctor visit or prescription, 60 koruna per day in hospital, 90 for the emergency room and a cap of 5,000 koruna a year. Those figures are old and should be checked, but the principle stands. The patient pays a token.

The reports from people who have actually used it are warm. An American poster named boldadventurer says he had four major surgeries at Na Homolce, a Prague hospital, and calls the care excellent and inexpensive. Another, Stevemilgrom, says the system shines a spotlight on how bad the American one is and that quick access to a doctor is normal. That is high praise from the country that invented the hospital bill.

The warning is simple. English is not guaranteed, forms are often in Czech, and a clerk who has been told the rules by a computer will follow them with mechanical calm. Bring a friend if you can.

A few practical points save grief. Employers must insure workers who hold a labour permit, according to the forum poster mosu, and the choice of insurer matters because doctors contract with specific ones. Expat Exchange notes that VZP is the largest insurer and state-owned. If you arrive on a visa without employer cover, you will have to work out your own, and the system gives you a surprising amount of rope. A phone call to the insurer in advance is worth more than a month of guessing.

## 🇨🇭 Switzerland

Switzerland has a different idea of healthcare. Everyone who lives there has to take out basic health insurance from a private insurer, within a few months of arriving, and the law regulates what that basic plan covers. You choose the insurer. You choose the deductible, which is the amount you pay yourself before the insurer starts to pay, and the higher it is, the lower your monthly premium. You then pay a share of costs above that, up to a yearly cap. Premiums vary by canton and rise, as a rule, every year. I have not verified current figures for this piece, so check the official comparison before you sign anything.

What you get is a system that is quick, clean and deeply competent. Doctors are well trained, wait times for most things are short and the equipment is the kind that looks expensive because it is. The system is organised around choice, which means a lot of homework in the autumn, when insurers publish next year's premiums. A foreigner arriving in Switzerland and not understanding that the plan is their responsibility is a foreigner heading for an unpleasant letter.

The culture is formal. Appointments are booked, punctuality is expected and the bill arrives by post, with a polite explanation, a reference number and a deadline. Nobody is rude. Everybody is exact. A doctor will take your concerns seriously and your insurer will take your premium even more seriously.

That is the trade-off. Swiss healthcare is superb, and you will pay for it before, during and after. The experience is smooth and the receipt is not.

There is a cultural point underneath the money. In Switzerland, a patient is expected to be an informed customer: to compare, to ask about costs and to treat the system as a service with a price list. In Czechia a patient is expected to be a patient, in the old sense, someone who turns up, waits and trusts. Neither is wrong. They reflect two different bargains between citizen and state, and the expat's challenge is learning which bargain they have just signed.

## The Verdict

Czechia wins for the ordinary person, and Switzerland for the person who does not mind the bill. If you want a system that fixes your knee quickly and charges you the price of a pizza, Prague is the better bet. If you want clean corridors, quick appointments and a sense that someone has thought about everything, Zurich is the dream, as long as you have a salary to match.

I do not think the comparison is close in value. The Czech approach delivers good medicine to everyone at a price almost anyone can pay, and the only real cost is patience. The Swiss approach delivers excellent medicine to everyone who can afford the premiums, and everyone is legally required to, which turns healthcare into a compulsory subscription.

So I pick Czechia, with one warning: it will test your tolerance for a counter clerk. Take a Czech friend to your first appointment and a good book to your second. By the third you will have learned the system, and it will probably have cured you of something.

## Conclusion

Healthcare is where you find out what a country thinks of you. Czechia thinks you are a patient and will treat you like one, with a form and a slightly stern expression. Switzerland thinks you are a customer and treats you like one, with excellent service and an invoice.

Whichever you land in, do the paperwork before you get ill. Register with a doctor, find your insurer, read the small print and keep every receipt. The system will take care of you if you take care of the system, and in both countries that is a very short sentence with a very long list attached.
