# Croatia Has Perfected the Art of Doing Nothing Beautifully. Peru Has Perfected the Art of Doing Everything at Once.

🇭🇷 Croatia + 🇵🇪 Peru
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ask a Croatian what they did last weekend and you'll get one word: "*more*" — the sea. Ask a Peruvian and you'll get an itinerary that would exhaust a UN diplomat: family lunch at eleven, a wedding at two, a football match on the radio the entire time, and somehow still home by dark for more family. I have spent weekends in both countries and come away with two entirely different definitions of rest, neither of which resembles anything a British person would recognise as a "quiet one."

Croatians treat the weekend as a sacred, near-religious withdrawal from productivity. The coast empties of ambition the second Friday hits five o'clock. Peruvians, meanwhile, treat the weekend as a second, louder version of the week — just with more ceviche and considerably more relatives. Both are right. Both will insist the other is doing it wrong. I am simply here to referee, with the smugness of someone who has genuinely wasted weekends on four continents.

## Do's & Don'ts: Croatia 🇭🇷

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to linger for three hours over one coffee — it's not slowness, it's a lifestyle | Rush anyone toward "the point" of a conversation; there isn't one, that's the point |
| Bring your own towel to the beach and claim your rock like it's sovereign territory | Expect a sandy beach — bring sturdy sandals or accept bruised feet |
| Join a *korzo* (evening promenade) — it's the real social network | Schedule anything important on a Sunday; the country is functionally closed |
| Say yes to the invitation to someone's *konoba* (family tavern) — it's an honour | Refuse the third course; it's rude, and also you're wrong, it's delicious |

## Do's & Don'ts: Peru 🇵🇪

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept every family lunch invitation — declining is a diplomatic incident | Assume Sunday lunch will end before 4pm; it is a marathon, not a meal |
| Learn to love a market crawl — Saturday mornings are for haggling, not sleeping in | Wear your nicest shoes to a market; you will regret it within ten minutes |
| Follow a local football club — even mild interest earns you instant friends | Criticise the referee out loud unless you're prepared to defend that opinion for an hour |
| Try *chicha morada* before judging Peruvian weekend drink culture | Assume the "quick" gathering will be quick. Nothing here is quick. It is a compliment to your company |

## Croatia: The Republic of Horizontal Ambition

Croatia's weekend culture runs on a simple, devastating logic: the sea exists, therefore you must be near it, doing as little as physically possible while near it. This is not laziness — Croatians will tell you, at length, that this is *discipline*. The discipline of refusing to let capitalism follow you to the coast.

From May to September, Friday afternoon triggers a national exodus. Zagreb empties toward Split, Split empties toward the islands, and by Saturday morning half the country is somewhere on a rock with a Bibby Ice tea, a family member and profound opinions about which cove is superior to which other cove (all coves are, apparently, either "authentic" or "ruined by tourists," with no middle category). The Croatian weekend is measured not in activities but in hours logged doing nothing productive near salt water, and anyone who fills their Saturday with errands is regarded with a mixture of pity and suspicion.

Winter weekends flip the register entirely. Coastal Croatia hibernates and inland Croatia takes over — hiking in Plitvice or the Zagorje hills, long lunches in konobas that haven't updated their menus since Tito, and a devotion to the *korzo*, the evening promenade where entire towns walk up and down the main street for no reason except to see and be seen. Nothing is achieved. Everything is observed. It is, I'd argue, more efficient socialising than any networking event I've endured in London.

What foreigners misjudge is the pacing. A "quick coffee" in Croatia is a two-hour commitment, and attempting to rush it marks you instantly as an outsider who has not yet surrendered to the rhythm. You don't get invited back if you're the person checking your watch. Slowness here isn't absence of plans — it's the entire plan.

## Peru: The Republic of Maximum Occupancy

Peru does not do "nothing." Peru does not believe in "nothing." A Peruvian weekend is a fully staffed production with a cast of forty relatives, several courses of food, at least one football match, and an emotional register that swings from tearful nostalgia to raucous laughter roughly every ninety minutes.

Saturday mornings belong to the market — not a leisurely browse but a full tactical operation, elbowing through stalls of fruit nobody outside the Andes has heard of, haggling as a competitive sport, and coming home with more food than any household could plausibly need, which is, of course, the point, because more people than expected will show up to eat it. Saturday afternoon is football, watched, discussed, and re-litigated with an intensity that makes English pub culture look positively restrained.

Sunday is the main event: *el almuerzo familiar*, the family lunch, which begins at a "civilised" eleven or noon and ends whenever the last uncle stops telling stories, typically somewhere around six. This is not a meal so much as a multi-generational summit with ceviche. Attendance is not optional. New partners are interrogated gently but thoroughly. Children run feral between courses. Grandmothers preside over all of it like small, formidable heads of state.

What Peru does exceptionally well is scale intimacy without losing warmth — forty people in one house feels less chaotic than it should, because everyone genuinely wants to be there. What it lacks, by Croatian standards, is silence. There is no equivalent of the Adriatic afternoon nap. There is always, always another course.

## The Verdict

If you want your nervous system to recover, Croatia wins outright — the entire culture is engineered around doing nothing with tremendous conviction, and there's something genuinely restorative about a country that has collectively agreed Sundays don't count. If you want to feel adored, exhausted and thoroughly fed by strangers-turned-family within one afternoon, Peru wins by a landslide; nowhere else have I been folded into a household this fast, this warmly, or this calorically.

The honest answer is you need both, in sequence: Peru to remember what unfiltered joy looks like, Croatia to recover from the sheer volume of it. Do them the other way round and you'll show up in Lima too rested to keep up.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a returning expat noted that in Croatia, being "too available" on weekends makes you look unemployed, not sociable; the coveted flex is being unreachable near water.*

> *expat.com, Lima thread — a relocated engineer said the hardest adjustment wasn't the food or the altitude, it was learning that "quick lunch" with a Peruvian family is a five-hour contractual obligation with dessert.*

> *InterNations Lima — a member wrote that missing one Sunday almuerzo without a very good excuse can generate weeks of gentle but relentless guilt from at least three separate aunts.*

## Conclusion

Croatia and Peru have arrived, from opposite directions, at the same conclusion: the weekend is sacred and the workweek is a regrettable interruption to real life. Croatia protects that sanctity with silence, salt water and the studied avoidance of anything resembling a schedule. Peru protects it with volume, food and an ever-expanding guest list that somehow never runs out of chairs. Neither will apologise for their version, and neither should. What both offer, in their own dialect, is the same rebuke to anywhere that treats leisure as an afterthought: rest is not what's left over after the week — it's the whole point, and everything else is scheduling around it.
