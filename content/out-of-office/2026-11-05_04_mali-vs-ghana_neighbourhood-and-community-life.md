# What Happens to "Community" When the State Doesn't Show Up

🇲🇱🇬🇭 Mali and Ghana, reviewed by a woman who has had opinions about neighbours on every inhabited continent

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country eventually answers the same question: who actually runs your street? In Ghana, the answer is a fascinating tangle of chiefs, land guards, and whichever funeral is currently monopolising everyone's weekend. In Mali, the answer, increasingly, is: the neighbourhood association, because the state has other priorities — like the fact that its own government, as of October last year, politely asked its allies to leave. I want to be upfront that this piece sits differently than most of what I write. Mali is not currently a place offering a bustling expat social calendar for me to be sardonic about. What it has instead is something more interesting and considerably more serious: communities that have been quietly running their own schools, clinics and street lighting because nobody else was going to.

## Mali: Where the Neighbourhood Association Is Doing City Hall's Job

Nearly half of Bamako's population lives in what urban planners bluntly classify as "precarious" informal settlements — roughly 40 unplanned neighbourhoods built on land still legally held by traditional chiefs, where residents pay for a plot but receive no formal title. About two-thirds of the buildings in the district are mud brick topped with metal sheeting or straw. Fewer than 2% of residents have a sewerage connection; wells supply roughly 65% of water needs. These aren't abstractions pulled from a development report for colour — they're the operating conditions under which "neighbourhood" gets defined here, day to day.

What's genuinely remarkable is what fills the gap. Take Samé, founded in 1958, roughly 4,000 residents, organised under traditional community rule through its own association, ACDSK — which didn't lobby the government to build the local school, health centre, pharmacy, mosques, standpipes and street lighting. It built them. That's not community spirit as a nice-to-have; that's community spirit as the entire municipal infrastructure budget, assembled by the people who'd otherwise have gone without.

And then there's cousinage — joking kinship, a genuinely structured social institution binding specific clans and ethnic groups through a historical "cathartic covenant," extending to actual family relations and to same-age-class peers, who share a mutual duty of solidarity alongside the licence to mock each other mercilessly. The Dogon and Bozo peoples, and the Diarra and Traoré families, are the textbook examples. People believe breaking the etiquette of these jokes invites supernatural consequence, which means the "insults" are taken exactly as seriously as they're delivered lightly — and this isn't nostalgic folklore, it's functioning, present-day conflict-resolution machinery between groups that might otherwise have far less patience for each other.

I have to be honest about the limits of this section, because pretending otherwise would be dishonest in a way I don't do even for comic effect: Mali is currently under a U.S. "Do Not Travel" advisory, and as of October 30th, 2025, the State Department ordered all non-emergency government personnel and their families out of the country, citing kidnapping, armed robbery, home invasion and carjacking risks that spike around local holidays. The English-language expat forums I'd normally mine for complaints about noisy neighbours have simply gone quiet — not because nobody's there, but because the people who might post aren't currently in a posting-things mood. That silence is itself the most honest data point in this entire piece.

## Ghana: A Society Organised Loosely Around Who Just Died

Ghana's neighbourhood life runs on a rhythm that will genuinely rearrange your calendar whether you consent or not: funerals. The country's own statistical service found that domestic tourism generated GH¢6.6 billion in 2023, and more than 80% of overnight domestic trips were made specifically to attend funerals or visit family — nearly 40% of overnight trips were funerals alone. That is not a cultural footnote. That is a measurable pillar of the national economy, complete with custom-printed cloth that's near-mandatory for family members, week-long event sequences stacking wake-keeping, burial and thanksgiving services, and families routinely borrowing against the donations collected at the funeral ground itself to cover costs that have, by every account, inflated well past what tradition originally called for.

Housing follows its own logic too: the compound house — a shared courtyard ringed by multiple households — remains the vernacular default, which means "neighbour" in Ghana often means someone you share a literal wall and water source with, not an abstraction two doors down. Expat enclaves cluster with striking specificity — Cantonments, Airport Residential, Labone, East Legon show up again and again across independent sources as the named, exclusive zones where the international community settles, complete with their own social infrastructure: a Goethe Institute hangout with a monthly artisan market, a running club, a women's association, international schools pitched at a premium. Frequent blackouts mean a UPS backup unit is standard household kit, and offices mark "Africa Wear Fridays" as routine, not performative.

Greeting etiquette carries weight well beyond small talk, especially in the north. NPR's own reporting describes ignoring a greeting — at any hour, including being roused at night by an elderly neighbour — as a genuine, understood insult. Elders and respected figures, which by default includes most foreigners, are greeted by squatting, producing the entirely real comic standoff of two people crouched toward each other, neither willing to rise first for fear it reads as disrespect. Addressing a chief, even one who shares your language, typically still requires going through an interpreter. And then there's the less charming infrastructure of neighbourhood power: "land guards," privately hired armed enforcers who physically occupy or defend disputed land on behalf of chiefs or claimants, a practice serious enough that the Asantehene, king of the Ashanti, has publicly threatened to strip the chieftaincy title from any chief caught using them. Community here isn't just who brings you food when someone dies. It's also, occasionally, who's willing to show up with muscle over a property line.

## The Verdict

I'm not going to pretend these two countries are playing the same game, and I refuse to manufacture false equivalence for the sake of a punchy verdict. Ghana's neighbourhood culture is vivid, funeral-anchored, and occasionally armed over real estate — a society organising itself loosely but functionally around chieftaincy, courtyard living, and death as a social occasion. Mali's neighbourhood culture, right now, is something closer to civic improvisation under genuine duress: associations building the infrastructure the state can't, kinship systems doing the conflict-resolution work courts might do elsewhere, all of it happening under conditions serious enough that most Western governments have told their own people to leave. If you're asking me which one makes a better holiday story, it's Ghana, easily. If you're asking me which one shows you more about what community actually is when nothing else is propping it up, it's Mali, and I don't think that's a close call either.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Accra — paraphrased: Ghana is "relatively safe though one needs to be aware of surroundings and not walk around waving your cell phone," in a city with an expat community large enough that "I don't feel like I know everyone or even half the people."*

> *NPR, Goats and Soda — paraphrased: a Peace Corps volunteer describes being greeted mid-sleep by an elderly neighbour and being expected to reply — ignoring a greeting, at any hour, reads as a real social insult.*

> *Expat.com Mali forum fragment — one of the only substantive replies found: a Malian poster briefly notes, in French, that Bamako has a safe residential area and decent French/English-language schools — a thin signal in an otherwise largely silent thread, reported here honestly rather than padded out.*

## Conclusion

Go to Ghana for the funerals, the compound-house gossip, and the thrill of a king publicly threatening his own chiefs over hired muscle. Approach Mali, if you approach it at all right now, with the seriousness the situation actually warrants, not the breezy curiosity I usually bring to this column — and recognise that "community" there currently means something closer to survival infrastructure than social calendar. Both countries will teach you that the state showing up is a privilege, not a given. Ghana just makes that lesson considerably more fun to learn.
