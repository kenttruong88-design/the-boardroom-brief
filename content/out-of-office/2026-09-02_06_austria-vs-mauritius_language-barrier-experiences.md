# One Country Can Speak English Perfectly and Refuses To, the Other Speaks Three Languages Just to Sell You a Mango

🇦🇹 Austria vs 🇲🇺 Mauritius — language barrier experiences

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Austrians rank third in the world for English proficiency and will still answer you in German out of sheer principle. Mauritians will greet you in Creole, pivot to French, land on English, and adjust all three mid-sentence depending on who else is listening, without ever making it your problem. One of these is a language barrier built on quiet stubbornness. The other isn't really a barrier at all — it's a moving target so fluid you barely notice you've been folded into it.

I've been coolly informed, in flawless English, that a Viennese waiter would "prefer" to continue in German. I've also been in a Port Louis market where a single transaction moved through three languages before I'd finished counting my change. Only one of these experiences made me feel unwelcome, and it wasn't the one in a country famous for hospitality.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇦🇹 Austria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn at least basic German — it signals effort in a way English fluency alone doesn't compensate for | Assume Austrian German is interchangeable with the German you learned in a Berlin classroom; regional dialect runs deep |
| Lean on English confidently in hotels, restaurants, and tourist-facing businesses | Expect Austrians over 30 to switch to English easily; many simply won't, regardless of ability |
| Recognise the job-market cost — foreign nationals face notably higher unemployment, partly language-driven | Take a German-only response personally; it's often more cultural reflex than unfriendliness |

**🇲🇺 Mauritius**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pick up a handful of Creole phrases — it dramatically shortens the social distance | Assume English, the official language, will actually be the language of daily life; it mostly isn't |
| Expect conversations to shift into Creole once multiple Mauritians are present, even if it started in English | Take the language switch personally; it's about comfort and rhythm, not excluding you |
| Use French for formal or business contexts — it sits alongside English as the working language of commerce | Expect one consistent language experience across the island; the code-switching is constant and situational |

## Austria: Fluent, Capable, and Not Always Willing

Here's the paradox that trips up every expat who assumes English proficiency equals English willingness: Austria ranks third globally on the EF English Proficiency Index, trailing only the Netherlands and Croatia. In hotels, restaurants, and anywhere used to dealing with tourists, you'll get by in English without a second thought. And yet the language barrier persists as, by most accounts, the single greatest challenge facing new arrivals — not because Austrians can't speak English, but because plenty of them would simply rather not, particularly once you're past the tourist-facing layer of the economy.

There's a generational split worth knowing about: younger Austrians tend to be far more comfortable code-switching into English, while a meaningful chunk of the population over 30 will stick to German regardless of your evident struggle. It isn't necessarily hostility — more a quiet cultural expectation that if you're living here, the effort should flow toward German, not away from it. And Austrian German isn't even the tidy, standardised version you might have studied; it comes loaded with regional particularities that can make textbook German feel almost like a different language once you're actually ordering a coffee in Graz.

The stakes are more than social. Unemployment among foreign nationals sits at roughly 9.1%, compared with 6.3% nationally, a gap largely attributed to the shortage of English-speaking roles outside a handful of urban, international-facing industries. English gets you fed, housed, and directed to the train station. It does not, on its own, get you fully inside the country's professional or social fabric — and Austrians, unlike some famously accommodating cultures, don't seem inclined to smooth that path for you. You're welcome to stay. You're just expected to meet them partway, in German, eventually.

## Mauritius: Trilingual by Default, Exclusionary by Accident

Mauritius operates on an entirely different linguistic logic: not one dominant language reluctantly shared with outsiders, but three languages in constant, situational rotation, none of them optional to understand if you actually want to follow what's happening around you. English holds the official title, the language of government and formal documents. French runs parallel as the language of business, media, and much of polite formal conversation. But the actual heartbeat of daily life — the market stall, the neighbourhood chat, the taxi ride — runs overwhelmingly on Mauritian Creole, spoken at home by roughly 90% of the population.

What's genuinely striking is the warmth built into the switching itself. Mauritians are, by and large, naturally trilingual, and the culturally embedded instinct is to greet you in whatever language seems most likely to put you at ease. The complication arrives the moment more than one Mauritian is present: even with the best intentions, conversation tends to drift back into Creole, the path of least resistance, the language everyone in the room actually dreams in. It's not a snub. It's gravity.

For newcomers willing to meet the island halfway, the payoff is real and immediate. Learning even a handful of Creole phrases — genuinely just the basics, market small talk, polite greetings — visibly shortens the social distance in a way that fluent French or English never quite manages on its own. Creole functions less as a barrier and more as the actual bridge: the thing that signals you're trying to be part of the rhythm rather than observing it from the formal-language sidelines. Business and bureaucracy will happily run in English or French. Belonging, such as it is, runs in Creole.

## The Verdict

Austria has the linguistic capacity to make your life easy and, past a certain age bracket, chooses not to always extend it. Mauritius has three languages simultaneously in play and somehow still manages to make the switching feel like inclusion rather than exclusion. If I have to navigate a genuine barrier, give me Mauritius's fluid, forgiving trilingualism over Austria's fluent-but-withheld English any day — one asks you to learn a little Creole and rewards the attempt instantly; the other asks you to learn German and reserves judgment regardless.

> *Expat.com Vienna forum — "Living and working in Vienna without speaking German?" — paraphrased: a long-term resident says fluent English got them through daily errands for years, but every promotion at work quietly required conversational German first.*

## Conclusion

Austria proves that proficiency and willingness are two entirely different things, and that a country can be objectively easy to navigate in English while still making you feel like a permanent guest. Mauritius proves the opposite: that genuine multilingual chaos, three languages deep, can feel more welcoming than one country politely declining to use the language it clearly knows. If you want a masterclass in linguistic gatekeeping dressed up as courtesy, book Vienna. If you want to watch a whole island effortlessly hold three languages at once and still make room for your terrible fourth one, go to Mauritius, and bring a notebook for the Creole.
