# Britain Wants an Address to Open an Account and an Account to Get an Address

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular silence that falls over a bank branch when a newcomer hands over a passport and a smile and is asked for a utility bill. The passport is fine. The smile is lovely. The utility bill, which would need to be addressed to a flat you cannot rent without a bank account, does not exist. I have watched grown professionals stare into this logical sinkhole with the glazed expression of a person reading terms and conditions.

Britain invented a great many things, including the queue and the bank statement as a form of personal identity, and it has perfected the art of demanding proof of a life you cannot yet prove you are living. The Philippines, for its part, has the opposite problem. It has banks, ID cards and a dense paper culture of its own, and a rather big diaspora sending money home. Both countries love a document. They just differ on which documents, and on how cheerfully they will tell you that yours is the wrong one.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇭 Philippines ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your passport, residence card and proof of address | Turn up with a single document and hope |
| Check whether a foreign-currency account is all you can open at first | Assume you get a full peso account on day one |
| Ask the bank about the minimum opening deposit | Open an account without knowing the balance penalty |
| Carry cash for everyday life | Rely on cards everywhere outside the cities |

| 🇬🇧 United Kingdom ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your employer or university for a letter confirming your address | Rely on a mobile phone bill as proof of address |
| Register with a GP and apply for a National Insurance number for official letters | Wait until the bank asks before starting the paperwork |
| Consider a basic or digital account to start | Assume the big banks will accept you immediately |
| Keep documents dated within three months | Present a statement from last winter |

## 🇵🇭 Philippines

I should say at the outset that the sources for this side are guides aimed at foreigners, not Filipinos, so the picture is the one a newcomer faces. Wise's guide for non-residents says that you usually need around 180 days of residence before you are treated as a resident alien and can open the same accounts as citizens, and that new arrivals may be limited to foreign-currency accounts at first. Most banks ask for a Philippine address, which makes opening an account from abroad difficult, though some, such as RCBC, offer online applications for which eligibility must be checked. You will want proof of your right to stay, such as an alien registration card, a passport as photo ID, a recent utility bill or rental contract and a minimum opening deposit that varies by bank. A reference letter from your existing bank can help.

The fees are mild by British standards. Account opening is usually free, monthly fees are usually free, and domestic transfers cost between nothing and ten pesos. The Wise tables quote a penalty of around 300 to 350 pesos for falling below the minimum balance, around 200 pesos to replace a debit card and about 600 pesos plus fees for an international transfer. Own-bank ATM withdrawals are usually free. None of that is the sort of number that ruins a Tuesday.

The landscape is also a concentrated one. A companion Wise guide says banking is dominated by five major banks: BDO Unibank, Metrobank, Land Bank, BPI and PNB. Cash is still widely used, and card acceptance is growing, mainly in cities and tourist areas. There are limits on the cash you can bring in, with more than 50,000 pesos restricted on entry and a customs form needed for more than 10,000 dollars.

The real texture of Filipino bureaucracy, I would suggest, is the queue and the stamp, and an unhurried belief that the correct document will eventually be found. I could not verify the details of remittance rules or local branch procedure from the pages I could read, so I will not pretend to. What I can say is that the system is tolerant if you arrive early, with originals, photocopies and some patience. The paperwork is a ritual, and the ritual is survivable.

## 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

The British version is the same ritual, performed with a straighter face. Expatica's guide, updated in July 2026, lays out the rules of proof of address: documents generally need to be dated within about three months, though some places accept six, and annual documents such as council tax or mortgage statements within twelve. Accepted items include utility bills, bank and credit card statements, council tax bills, a UK driving licence, a tenancy agreement and a mortgage statement. Official letters from HMRC, the DWP about a National Insurance number or the NHS about GP registration can also do the job. Mobile bills and handwritten letters from friends are often rejected.

Then comes the part everyone knows and nobody has fixed. Expatica spells out the catch-22: you need an address to open a bank account, and a bank account to secure accommodation. Its suggested way out is a collection of small hacks. Ask your employer or university for a letter. Apply for a National Insurance number, which triggers a letter from the DWP. Register with a GP. Open a basic or digital account, or a multi-currency account. Get added to the council tax or the electoral register, with your landlord's permission. Find a trusted person to vouch for you.

The Which? advice column, from 2017, shows how old the problem is. At that point, EU rules required UK banks to offer basic accounts to EU residents, with no overdraft and cash withdrawals by debit card, and twelve providers offered them. Nationwide required an EU resident applying from abroad to attend a face-to-face meeting within two months, and non-EU residents first had to secure a UK address. Barclays allowed an online application within 90 days, completed in person. Rules change, and the EU element no longer applies in the same way, but the shape is unchanged: a series of reasonable steps that, in sequence, are unreasonable.

What the British system lacks in flexibility it makes up for in predictability. Once you are in, you are in, and the app is pleasant. The point is to survive the first month. Think of it as a hazing ritual with a direct debit at the end.

## The Verdict

The Philippines wins on practicality and the UK wins on polish. If I were landing in Manila with a suitcase and a work visa, I would expect to spend an afternoon, plenty of photocopies and a good deal of patience, and I would walk out with an account. If I were landing in London, I would expect to spend three weeks on a sofa performing a masterclass in circular reasoning.

The British system is better once you are inside it and worse getting through the door. Its rules are documented, which helps, and absurd, which does not. The Filipino system is looser and more human, and it asks you for a visible commitment to the country before it opens up. Both want you to prove you exist. Only one of them has made proving it a prerequisite for having somewhere to live.

## Conclusion

Britain will ask for a bill you cannot have and a statement you have not earned. The Philippines will ask for an alien card, a passport and some time. Take originals and copies, ask your employer for a letter, get your National Insurance number early and keep your sense of humour in a safe place. The bureaucracy in both places is survivable. It is the circular logic of the British one that leaves a mark.
