# One Country Runs on Photocopies and Patience, the Other Runs on a Single Number You Need for Everything

🇪🇬 Egypt vs 🇧🇷 Brazil

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Cairo, opening a bank account meant multiple visits, a stack of photocopied documents thick enough to double as a doorstop, and a level of in-person negotiation with bank staff that felt less like banking and more like a slow, respectful diplomatic exchange. In São Paulo, nothing — not a phone plan, not a gym membership, not so much as a loyalty card at a pharmacy — happened without first reciting my CPF, the Brazilian taxpayer number that functions as a kind of universal key without which you basically don't exist.

Egypt's bureaucracy runs on paper, patience, and personal relationships built over repeated visits to the same branch. Brazil's runs on a single, powerful identification number that, once obtained, unlocks nearly everything else remarkably fast — except obtaining it in the first place, which is its own bureaucratic odyssey entirely.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇪🇬 Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring multiple photocopies of every document, always | Expect a single visit to complete any bureaucratic process |
| Build rapport with a specific bank staff member if possible | Assume online banking replaces the need for in-person visits |
| Keep both local currency and dollar accounts if eligible | Rush the process — patience genuinely speeds things up |
| Ask locals which branch is known for efficiency | Assume all branches of the same bank operate identically |

### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your CPF number immediately upon arrival | Try to open a bank account, phone plan, or lease without one |
| Use a cartório (notary office) for any official document needs | Expect government offices to move quickly, even with a CPF |
| Keep digital and physical copies of your CPF everywhere | Assume one government agency's process mirrors another's |
| Ask Brazilian friends to help navigate cartório specifics | Underestimate how often you'll need your CPF for small things |

## Egypt: Bureaucracy as a Relationship, Not a Transaction

Egyptian bureaucracy, particularly around banking, rewards something that took me a genuinely long time to accept: patience isn't just a virtue here, it's a functional requirement, and rushing accomplishes nothing except frustrating both parties. Opening an account meant multiple visits, an ever-expanding folder of photocopied documents — passport, residency permit, proof of address, and inevitably one more form nobody had mentioned until the third visit — and a process that unfolded across days rather than the single afternoon I'd naively expected.

What eventually made the difference wasn't any document I was missing, it was recognising that Egyptian bureaucratic culture runs substantially on personal rapport. Returning to the same branch, dealing with the same staff member, building a small but genuine relationship over repeated visits smoothed processes considerably compared to treating each visit as an anonymous transaction. This isn't corruption or favouritism in any meaningful sense, it's simply how trust gets established in a system that relies heavily on human judgment rather than pure procedural automation.

Currency considerations added another layer entirely. Maintaining both a local currency account and, where eligible, a dollar account became genuinely practical given currency fluctuation, and foreign residents who skipped this dual approach sometimes found themselves navigating awkward exchange situations later. The overall experience demanded a mindset shift: stop expecting efficiency, start expecting a process, and the frustration eases considerably once you do.

## Brazil: One Number to Rule Every Bureaucratic Interaction

Brazilian bureaucracy centres almost entirely around a single identifier: the CPF, a taxpayer registration number that, once obtained, becomes required for functionally everything — bank accounts, phone contracts, gym memberships, even some retail loyalty programs treat it as standard information to request at checkout. Arriving without one meant, for my first several weeks, an odd kind of bureaucratic invisibility, unable to complete transactions that Brazilians handle instantly by simply reciting eleven digits from memory.

Obtaining the CPF itself, ironically, becomes its own bureaucratic undertaking, involving Receita Federal registration and, depending on your situation, a visit to a cartório, Brazil's ubiquitous notary offices that handle an enormous range of official document authentication and processing. Cartórios operate with their own internal logic, queue systems, and document requirements that vary meaningfully by state and even by individual office, meaning advice from one region doesn't always transfer cleanly to another.

Once the CPF was actually in hand, though, the payoff was real and immediate — processes that had felt impossible suddenly moved with surprising speed, banking included. The lesson, learned slower than it should have taken, was to treat CPF acquisition as the single highest-priority task upon arrival, ahead of housing, ahead of a phone plan, because every other process quietly depends on it. Brazilian friends who helped navigate the specific cartório quirks in my city saved me weeks of confusion that official guidance alone never quite resolved.

## The Verdict

Egypt's bureaucracy is diffuse, relationship-based, and rewards patience distributed across many small interactions. Brazil's is concentrated around a single powerful identifier, front-loading the difficulty into one critical, occasionally maddening acquisition process before everything else moves comparatively fast.

I found Egypt's system more emotionally taxing day to day and Brazil's more front-loaded and stressful early, then genuinely easier. Suki's verdict: Brazil wins for efficiency once you clear the CPF hurdle, but Egypt wins for anyone who actually enjoys the slower, more human version of bureaucracy — because there's something to be said for a system where the bank teller eventually just recognises your face and waves you through.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Egypt — a newcomer described visiting the same bank branch four times over two weeks before finally getting an account opened, "each time missing one document nobody mentioned before."*

> *Reddit r/brasil — someone joked that they'd been asked for their CPF while buying a single bag of chips, and "at this point I'd give it to a stranger on the street if they asked politely."*

> *expat.com Sao Paulo — a longtime resident warned that cartório rules "genuinely vary by neighbourhood," and recommended calling ahead rather than assuming consistency.*

## Conclusion

Egypt and Brazil have both built bureaucratic systems that demand real patience, just distributed completely differently — Egypt spreads it across every interaction, Brazil concentrates it into one critical early hurdle. Move to Cairo and bring photocopies of everything, repeatedly. Move to São Paulo and get your CPF before you do anything else, genuinely anything else. Both systems will test you. Only one of them will eventually let you buy chips without producing a government ID.
