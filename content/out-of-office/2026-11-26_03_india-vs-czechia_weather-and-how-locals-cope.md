# India Survives the Heat and Czechia Survives Everything Else

🇮🇳 India vs 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

An Indian summer is a physical argument with the sun, and the sun has won since before there were maps. A Czech winter is a quieter fight, a grey and patient siege conducted in a valley where cold air likes to sit down and refuses to leave. One country gets weather as an assault. The other gets it as an atmosphere. Both have invented ways of coping that outsiders find either brilliant or insane.

I have spent a May in Delhi and a February in Prague, and my honest report is that neither prepares you for the other. The heat takes your energy. The cold takes your mood. The people who live there have worked out how to carry on anyway, and the rules they follow are worth stealing.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇮🇳 India ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stay hydrated and drink something salted or sweet in the heat | Run errands at 2pm in May |
| Plan journeys around the monsoon, not around the map | Assume a clear sky at 8am means a dry commute |
| Keep a light cotton wardrobe and a spare shirt | Wear synthetics in the humid season |
| Learn which neighbourhood floods first | Park low on a street with a drain problem |

| 🇨🇿 Czechia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy proper winter boots with grip before December | Trust fashion shoes on frozen pavement |
| Layer up and treat indoor heating as a luxury, not a given | Assume every office is warm |
| Take the Christmas-to-New-Year week off like everyone else | Schedule a meeting for 30 December |
| Join a sauna or a weekend ski trip | Hide indoors until March |

## 🇮🇳 India

Indian weather is a calendar with three or four acts, depending on where you stand. There is the long hot season, when afternoon temperatures in the north climb past anything a normal body understands. There is the monsoon, which arrives with drama and leaves the roads rebuilt as rivers. And there is a mild winter that Delhi spoils with smog and the south hardly notices. Which country you are in depends on which part you mean, and the biggest mistake an outsider can make is to say India has one climate.

What India does well is adapt in small, practical ways. The working day bends around the sun. Streets go quiet between lunch and four. Shops pull shutters, pavement vendors vanish and a nap becomes a piece of civil engineering. Drinks are sold to respond to the heat: nimbu pani, sugarcane juice, buttermilk, coconut water cracked open by a man with a very sharp knife. Cotton is not a fashion choice. It is survival.

The monsoon is a different skill. People carry umbrellas and a calm acceptance of delay, and anyone who has watched a Mumbai commuter wade through knee-deep water in office shoes will understand that complaining is optional and resilience is cultural. Air conditioning is the great equaliser for anyone who can afford it, and the great divider for everyone who cannot.

For an expat the surprise is that the extremes make the days feel organised. You always know what the weather is going to demand of you. There is a pattern to the heat, a rhythm to the rain and an unspoken rule about who is allowed to go outside when.

The social side of the weather matters as well. In north India the winter evening is a bonfire and a glass of tea, and the whole street comes out to stand near the flames and complain about the cold in the same breath. In the south the monsoon turns into a mood: pakoras, old songs and an excuse to leave work early. Weather is a topic of conversation, a reason for hospitality and a standing excuse for just about anything.

## 🇨🇿 Czechia

Czech weather is a more modest affair on paper and a more miserable one in practice. According to Expats.cz in February 2025, Prague sat at around minus 9 degrees Celsius after dipping to minus 12, and the article blames the city's valley topography for trapping cold air, with a lack of snow cover making things feel worse. Expat Exchange quotes an expat saying winter temperatures can drop to minus 10 and summers can reach 30. That is not Siberia. It is a long, wet, grey cold that gets into your bones and stays through breakfast.

The coping tools are very Czech. The Christmas week is a national pause, with most people taking the seven days between Christmas and New Year off, according to an Expat.com winter guide. Weekends turn into rituals: cross-country skiing between villages and pubs, a mountain chata with a fire going and a beer in hand, a sauna session to defrost. Expats.cz mentions saunas and chata fires as the standard Czech responses, alongside pohoda, the idea of a relaxed, unhurried contentment.

There is also a more mundane reality. Roads need salting, and the municipal Prague roads authority reportedly keeps 73 salt spreaders on first-class roads and 51 smaller machines on secondary ones. Offices are often chilly. A survey cited by Expats.cz found one in three employees complaining about office cold, and 34 per cent of offices missing the 21 to 23 degree standard for demanding work. A US diplomatic expat writing in a Talesmag post report in January 2026 gives the plain version: winters are cold and surfaces are slippery, so bring good walking shoes.

That slipperiness is the true Czech winter hazard. The pavement turns to glass, the old tram stops become skating rinks and everyone around you walks with the grim confidence of someone who has done this since birth. It is a place where the weather is endured, not conquered.

The Expat.com winter guide, written in 2012 by a poster calling themselves Czechthatouton, is refreshingly unsentimental. It says January and February are the coldest months of the year and that winter is not the author's favourite time, which is a very Czech way of delivering bad news. There is no attempt to sell the season as a fairytale. There is simply a calendar, a list of things to do and a promise that spring will eventually come, which it does, with a ferocity of cherry blossom that makes the whole ordeal feel earned.

## The Verdict

India wins, and it is partly because the heat is honest. A 45-degree afternoon announces itself, and the response is obvious: shade, water, a ceiling fan and a nap. Czech winter is sneakier. It is a low-grade, months-long mood drain, and the only available medicine is a sauna and a beer.

That said, the Czech coping culture is much better than anyone gives it credit for. The weekend skiing, the week off at Christmas, the chata and the pohoda are all excellent solutions to a hard problem. If I had to be gloomy somewhere for three months, I would pick the country that built a whole leisure culture around it.

So the honest score is India by a margin for sheer endurance and Czechia by a margin for craftsmanship. Heat is something you survive. Cold is something the Czechs have turned into an art form, and they do it without complaining, which is infuriating, and admirable, and slightly unfair to the rest of us.

## Conclusion

Both countries have worked out that the weather is not an excuse. It is a schedule. In India you plan around the sun and the rain. In Czechia you plan around the dark and the ice. If you can accept that the climate is going to run your life for part of every year, you will be fine, and you will probably be better at coping than your neighbours back home.

Pack accordingly. Take lightweight cotton and a hat to India, and proper boots and a good coat to Prague. Find the right local ritual for the season, whether that is cold sugarcane juice or a hot sauna. And if anyone tells you the weather is just small talk, tell them you have been to both places, and that it is the entire conversation.
