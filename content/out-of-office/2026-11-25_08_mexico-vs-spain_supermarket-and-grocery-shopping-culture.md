# Mexico Sells You One Egg and Spain Sells You a Leg of Ham

🇲🇽 Mexico vs 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a theory that two countries who share a language will share a shopping basket. It is nonsense, and the person who first said it had clearly never watched a Mexican cashier hand a bag to a teenager who is working for tips alone. Mexico and Spain share Spanish, a love of tortilla in two entirely different senses and a ferocious dedication to lunch. After that, the supermarkets go their separate ways.

I have bought food in sixty countries and the supermarket remains the truest interview a country gives. It tells you what is cheap, what is sacred, how far people trust strangers with their eggs and what time you are allowed to be hungry. Mexico runs on warmth, improvisation and a thousand tiny transactions. Spain runs on timetables, ham and the quiet certainty that the shop will shut.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇲🇽 Mexico ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shop the mercado for fruit, vegetables and fresh meat | Buy everything at the big chain because it is easier |
| Tip the bagger five to ten pesos per bag | Walk past them as if they were part of the shelving |
| Learn the shop names: carnicería, frutería, panadería | Wander in and point at things like a tourist |
| Take your own bags to the market | Expect the vendor to supply them |

| 🇪🇸 Spain ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan your shop around opening hours and the midday break | Turn up at three in the afternoon expecting the small shop to be open |
| Ask at the counter for ham, cheese and fish by weight | Grab the pre-packed slices and miss the point |
| Bring your own carrier bag and pay for any you forget | Assume the till will hand you one free |
| Visit the municipal market on Saturday morning | Leave the weekly shop until Sunday and find a shutter |

## 🇲🇽 Mexico

Mexico does groceries on two tracks, and you need both. The supermarket track is huge and corporate. Walmart de México has more than 3,100 stores, the vast majority of them the discount banner Bodega Aurrera, Chedraui has 728, Costco has 43 warehouses and the convenience chain OXXO has over 24,700 shops, according to an expat guide updated in October. Home delivery usually needs a minimum basket of 300 to 500 pesos, and baggers often work for tips alone, which is why the rule of thumb is five to ten pesos per bag. It is an economy of small gestures.

The second track is the mercado, and this is where Mexico earns my respect. A woman in a market guide wrote that mangoes in season cost about a dollar for around five pounds, eggs are sold by weight at roughly ten for a dollar and a chicken vendor will fillet and mince six drumsticks while you wait for about fifty pesos. Another expat who lives in Guanajuato says most produce costs 50 to 60 per cent less than in California, that she can buy a single egg, a single stick of butter and a handful of fruit and that she shops on foot most days. Two people living there often spend 200 to 250 dollars a month mixing supermarkets and markets.

The limitation is variety. That same writer notes the Mexican shop may carry only one brand of plain peanut butter, and imported cheese, alcohol and packaged luxuries are best sought in the chain stores. But the freshness is obscene, the vendors know your name and the rhythm of shopping every day for what you will eat tonight is a quiet revolution against the weekly mega-shop. I love it. I also cannot stop adding up the pesos.

The Mexican chain stores are also worth a look for their sheer cultural range. Bodega Aurrera is the people's discounter, Chedraui is the mainstream department-store-style grocer, and the warehouse clubs serve the middle class with bulk packs of everything. The convenience shop OXXO is practically a national organ, selling beer, snacks, bill payment and, one assumes, hope. The result is a country where the same family might buy tortillas at a street stall, vegetables at a market, rice at a discounter and imported cheese at a club. It is an economy of choice, and it is deeply Mexican. Nobody has a single supermarket. They have a route.

## 🇪🇸 Spain

Spain takes the opposite approach. It trusts its supermarkets to be brilliant, orderly and efficient, and mostly they are. The big chains are clean, well stocked and relentlessly sensible, the own-label products are famously good and a shopping trip is a short, structured event that ends with a receipt and a feeling of mild competence. I will not give you figures I do not have, but I will say that a Spanish supermarket is the kind of place where you can plan a week of meals from the shelves and nobody tries to sell you a membership.

The magic is at the counter. Spain still believes in the deli, the fishmonger and the man who will slice a leg of cured ham with the care of a surgeon. You ask for a quarter kilo of this and a hundred grams of that, the person behind the glass advises you with the confidence of an academic and you go home with food that has a history. The municipal market is not a tourist attraction. It is a working weekly institution, where the old ladies know exactly which stall to trust and will shoulder you out of the way if you dither.

Then there are the hours. Small shops close for the long midday break, many places are shuttered on Sunday and a Saturday afternoon errand can end in a locked door. The system punishes the unprepared and rewards the planner, which is why Spanish households tend to stock up with a quiet military precision. The bag rule is the same as everywhere in Europe: bring your own or pay. Do not forget your passport, your patience or your sense of occasion. The shop will not wait for you.

There is also the matter of language, which is not as simple as it looks. The Mexican shopper learns that a tienda sells different things from an abarrotes, that a carnicería will cut your meat to order and that the pan dulce vanishes by noon. A Mexico News Daily writer lists the shop names ending in ría as the first thing to learn, adding that you should pick the cleanest, busiest butcher, bring your own bag and use the regular shops for imported goods. In Spain the vocabulary is similar and the manners are different, and it is that tiny difference that gets foreigners into trouble at the till.

## The Verdict

Mexico wins for the sheer joy of it, and that is a judgement of character, not of efficiency. A country where you can buy one egg, where the vendor fillets a chicken to your order and the teenager packing your bag is treated as part of the economy has a warmth that the Spanish system, for all its competence, does not quite reproduce.

Spain wins on predictability and on the finest raw ingredients of the Mediterranean, but it loses points for the shutters and for a grocery culture that is largely about knowing the rules in advance. Mexico forgives you for not knowing. Spain simply closes.

My ruling is Mexico for flexibility, Spain for the ham. If I had to live in one, I would pick the country that lets me buy a single lime without a lecture. I would then fly to the other every few months and spend a very large amount of money on a very small amount of pig. A balanced life requires nothing less.

## Conclusion

Both countries have a deep suspicion of the American habit of buying a month of food in one go. They would rather you chose the day's dinner with your own hands, from someone who can tell you where it came from. That is a lovely idea and it is slightly exhausting.

So go to the mercado early in Mexico and bring change. Go to the fishmonger on Saturday in Spain and bring patience. In both places, say hello before you ask for anything. And if you find yourself at a till without a bag, remember that the cashier has seen this before. They are not angry. They are just waiting to see whether you learned anything.
