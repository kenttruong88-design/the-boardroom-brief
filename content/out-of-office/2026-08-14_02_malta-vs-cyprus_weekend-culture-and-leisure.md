# Two Sun-Baked Rocks, One Very Long Lunch

🇲🇹 Malta 🇨🇾 Cyprus
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Malta and Cyprus are both small, both scorched, both former British colonies that kept the driving-on-the-left habit and a faint, lingering obsession with tea. And both have built weekend cultures around the same central, unbothered principle: nothing that can be done on Monday should ever be attempted on Saturday. I've spent enough weekends on both rocks to know the difference between "relaxed" and "closed for reasons nobody will explain to you," and these two islands sit on opposite ends of that spectrum.

Malta turns its weekends into theatre — fireworks, brass bands, a village festa that will keep you awake until 3am whether you asked for it or not. Cyprus turns its weekends into a nap. Both are valid life philosophies. Only one of them will let you buy milk on a Sunday.

## Do's & Don'ts: Malta

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the village festa calendar before booking a quiet weekend anywhere near a church | Assume "small island" means quick to cross — traffic on a festa Saturday can eat two hours |
| Take the ferry to Gozo for an actually slower pace | Expect Sunday grocery shopping to be simple; many smaller shops shut entirely |
| Embrace the fireworks, even at midnight, even on a Tuesday that isn't technically the weekend | Complain about the noise to a local — they will look at you like you've insulted their grandmother |

## Do's & Don'ts: Cyprus

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book Sunday lunch (mezze, obviously) well in advance — it's the actual national sport | Plan anything ambitious before noon; the country wakes up slowly and resents being rushed |
| Head to the mountains in summer when the coast gets unbearable | Expect much beyond cafés and tavernas to be open on a Sunday |
| Learn to enjoy silence — Cypriot weekends are built around long, unhurried stillness | Mistake the quiet for boredom and fill it with plans nobody else wants |

## Malta: A Festa for Every Excuse, and No Regrets About It

Malta does not do quiet weekends, not really, not anywhere near a village with a patron saint — which is every village, because there are more churches here than there are actual reasons to build them. Between May and September you cannot throw a stone without hitting a festa: marching bands, garish string lights strung across entire streets, statues of saints carried through town on shoulders while fireworks detonate close enough to singe your eyebrows. Nobody asks permission. Nobody apologises for the 1am pyrotechnics. This is simply what a Maltese weekend is, and if you moved here expecting Mediterranean tranquility, you were misinformed by a brochure with an agenda.

What I love about it is the total lack of self-consciousness. A festa isn't performed for tourists — it barely acknowledges you're there. It's a genuine, slightly unhinged community celebration that happens to also be spectacular. Compare that to the sanitised, ticketed "cultural events" you get in bigger countries, and Malta's chaos feels almost radical.

The flip side is logistics. The island is seventeen miles long and every festa weekend turns it into a single, furious traffic jam, because apparently nobody in Malta has considered that fireworks and narrow medieval streets built for donkeys don't accommodate modern cars well. And Sunday retail hours remain stubbornly, almost proudly, inconvenient — smaller shops close, and if you didn't stock up on Saturday, you're having cereal for dinner. Malta gives you spectacle in exchange for convenience. Most weekends, that's a fair trade. Some weekends, you just want milk.

## Cyprus: The Art of Doing Absolutely Nothing, Beautifully

Cyprus, by contrast, treats the weekend as a sacred pause rather than a performance. There's no village-wide fireworks display competing for your attention — there's a table, usually outdoors, usually under a vine, usually covered in more mezze plates than any group of humans could reasonably finish, and the expectation that you will sit at it for four hours minimum. Sunday lunch here isn't a meal, it's an institution with its own gravitational pull, and skipping it to "get things done" marks you out as either a tourist or someone in serious need of Cypriot intervention.

The pace is the point. Cafés fill early with people who have nowhere to be and no interest in pretending otherwise. Coastal towns like Limassol get busy in a lazy, beach-towel sort of way rather than a nightlife sort of way — the real party crowd sticks to Ayia Napa, and everyone else is perfectly happy watching the sea do very little, very slowly. Head up into the Troodos Mountains in August and you'll find an entirely different weekend culture: hiking trails, mountain tavernas, and locals who drive an hour uphill purely to escape their own coastline's heat.

What catches expats off guard is how little happens on a Cypriot Sunday, retail-wise. Sunday trading exists in patches but it is not the default, and if your idea of a weekend involves shopping centres and errands, you will find yourself staring at a lot of shuttered storefronts wondering if there's been some kind of national holiday you weren't told about. There hasn't. Everyone's just at lunch. Still.

## The Verdict

Malta gives you spectacle; Cyprus gives you stillness. If your idea of a good weekend involves fireworks exploding over a 400-year-old church while a brass band plays something vaguely triumphant at ear-splitting volume, Malta wins, decisively, and I say that with real affection. If your idea of a good weekend is four hours at a table with people you like and absolutely nowhere else to be, Cyprus wins, and honestly it might be the healthier choice, though nobody who's had a Maltese festa will ever admit that out loud. I'll take the fireworks. I like my Sundays with a bit of danger in them.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/malta — a resident joked that you can plan your entire summer around avoiding whichever village is hosting a festa that weekend, because the traffic diversions alone will ruin your Saturday.*

> *Internations Limassol — an expat admitted she initially found Cypriot Sundays "aggressively boring" before realising everyone else had simply mastered doing nothing productively.*

> *expat.com Malta — someone new to the island said they got caught out twice in one month by shops closing at 1pm on a Sunday with zero warning posted anywhere.*

## Conclusion

Both islands agree on one thing: the weekend is not for productivity, whatever your employer might think. Malta just expresses that through noise, colour, and a saint's statue on someone's shoulders, while Cyprus expresses it through silence, mezze, and a lunch that refuses to end. Choose Malta if you want your weekend to remember you. Choose Cyprus if you want to forget you have a job at all, at least until Monday drags you back to reality with considerably less charm than either island offered.
