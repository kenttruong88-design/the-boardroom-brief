# One Country Makes You Collect Six Signatures Before You're Allowed a Bank Account, the Other Makes You Prove You Exist Before It Lets You Log In

🇪🇹 Ethiopia vs 🇳🇴 Norway
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nothing exposes a country's relationship with its own institutions faster than trying to open a bank account in it. Ethiopia hands you a paper chain of approvals, stamps, and patient waiting that would make a Habsburg civil servant weep with pride. Norway hands you a flawless, entirely digital system that is so dependent on one single ID number that, until you have it, you functionally do not exist to the state at all.

Both systems are, in their own way, deeply logical. Neither is designed with your first month in the country in mind, and I say that as someone who has been mildly humiliated by both.

## Do's & Don'ts: Ethiopia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring more original documents and photocopies than you think could possibly be needed | Expect any single visit to a bank branch to resolve your account opening in one go |
| Get a local sponsor or employer letter sorted before you even approach a bank | Assume foreign currency accounts work the same as birr accounts — rules differ sharply |
| Budget real days, not hours, for the account-opening and registration process | Lose your patience with staff — the paperwork chain is the system, not personal obstruction |
| Keep every stamped document afterward — you will be asked to produce them again | Rely on card payments as a default; cash still dominates far more transactions than expected |

## Do's & Don'ts: Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Prioritise getting your D-number or personal number the moment you arrive | Expect to function normally without BankID — it gates almost everything digitally |
| Book Skatteetaten and bank appointments early — slots fill up fast in bigger cities | Assume a foreign credit history or ID transfers any weight here — it generally doesn't |
| Use Altinn early — it's the hub for nearly all official interactions once registered | Ignore mail from the tax office; digital systems assume you check them promptly |
| Expect efficiency once you're "in the system" — Norway is genuinely fast after that point | Underestimate how slow the pre-registration limbo period can feel before you're recognised |

## Ethiopia: A Bureaucracy Built on the Belief That Trust Must Be Earned in Triplicate

Opening a bank account in Ethiopia as a foreign resident is an education in exactly how many humans a modern financial system can still involve. The National Bank of Ethiopia maintains genuinely strict foreign-exchange and account-opening controls — a legacy of currency management concerns that long predate any individual applicant's paperwork — meaning foreign residents typically need an employer letter, a residence permit, sponsor documentation, and often a formal introduction from an existing account holder or institution before a bank will even seriously process an application.

The process rewards patience in a way modern digital bureaucracies have mostly engineered away. Multiple branch visits are standard, not exceptional. Documents get physically stamped, re-checked, and occasionally re-requested in a form ever-so-slightly different from the one you brought the first time. The Ethiopian Investment Commission's own guidance for foreign residents and investors acknowledges the multi-step nature of the process candidly, framing it less as an obstacle and more as due diligence — which, to be fair to the system, it genuinely is, given the currency controls it's protecting.

What surprises newcomers most is how much of daily Ethiopian financial life still runs on cash and informal trust networks even once an account exists — mobile money and digital banking are growing fast in Addis Ababa specifically, but the assumption that card payments will cover most transactions is a Western import that doesn't survive contact with reality much outside the capital's newer commercial districts.

The emotional register of Ethiopian bureaucracy is worth naming honestly: it is slow, it is document-heavy, and it will test the patience of anyone used to instant digital onboarding. But it is not capricious. Every stamp exists for a traceable reason, and staff I've dealt with have been, without exception, courteous about a process they didn't design and can't speed up on your behalf.

## Norway: A Bureaucracy So Digital It Forgets You Need to Exist On Paper First

Norway's bureaucratic reputation as clean, efficient, and almost entirely digital is, once you're inside the system, completely deserved. Altinn handles nearly every interaction with the state through one unified portal, tax filing is famously so automated that most residents barely have to do anything, and once your identity is properly registered, Norwegian bureaucracy runs with a smoothness that makes almost every other country on this list look faintly medieval.

The catch — and it is a genuine, well-documented catch — is the gap before you're "in the system" at all. New arrivals need either a D-number (temporary) or a full personal number (permanent) from Skatteetaten before almost anything else becomes possible, and that registration itself requires an in-person appointment, often with a waiting period, at exactly the moment a new arrival has the least patience and the most urgent need for a working bank account. Until that number exists, you cannot get BankID, and until you have BankID, vast portions of Norwegian digital life — banking, signing a lease, certain employer processes — remain functionally closed to you, creating a strange limbo where a country famous for frictionless bureaucracy briefly makes you feel like you don't exist.

Once through that gate, though, the system genuinely delivers on its reputation. Bank accounts open quickly once ID is sorted, tax administration requires minimal manual input, and Altinn's centralisation means you're rarely bouncing between disconnected agencies the way you would almost anywhere else. Skatteetaten's own guidance is refreshingly clear and available in English, a small courtesy that shouldn't be remarkable and yet, compared to plenty of other bureaucracies, absolutely is.

Norway's bureaucracy isn't slow. It's binary — closed until you're registered, remarkably open after — and the honest advice for anyone moving here is to treat that first appointment as the single most urgent task on your entire relocation list.

## The Verdict

Ethiopia's bureaucracy is slow because it doesn't trust paper until paper proves itself, repeatedly, in person. Norway's bureaucracy is fast because it doesn't trust anything until a single digital number exists — but that number takes real, sometimes maddening, time to acquire.

I'm calling this one for Norway, narrowly, and only because the wait, once you're through it, is genuinely finite and the system afterward is close to flawless. Ethiopia's process demands more patience for longer, and never fully hands you the digital convenience Norway eventually does. But anyone who's sat in Skatteetaten limbo without a D-number, unable to even open a bank account to receive a salary, knows Norway's efficiency has its own bureaucratic purgatory built right into the front door.

## What Nobody Warned You About

## Conclusion

Ethiopia and Norway have built two entirely different bureaucratic philosophies — one that makes you prove yourself through paper and patience, one that makes you prove yourself through a single digital gate before unlocking near-total convenience. Neither country is going to speed up for you, and neither will apologise for the wait. Bring documents to Addis Ababa. Bring your passport and a very early appointment to Oslo. In both cases, bring more patience than you think you'll need — you'll need it.
