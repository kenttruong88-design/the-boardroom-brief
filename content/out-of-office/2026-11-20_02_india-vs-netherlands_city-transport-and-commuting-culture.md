# India Commutes by Negotiation and the Netherlands Commutes by Bicycle and Smugness

🇮🇳 India vs 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

An Indian commute is a spectator sport with a contact element. You choose between a train carriage that defies the physics of human volume, an auto-rickshaw whose driver has opinions about your destination, a bus that stops when it feels like it and a metro that is clean, cool and utterly silent, like a cathedral for people who have given up on the street. All of them arrive eventually. Few of them arrive on the schedule the app promised.

A Dutch commute is a religion with one sacrament: the bicycle. Everyone, from the prime minister's staff to the nine-year-old with a violin case, pedals through weather that would cancel a small war. The infrastructure is so good that it makes the visitor feel faintly insulted. Somebody has thought of everything, and that somebody is a civil servant in The Hague who cycles to work in the rain and has never once complained.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Agree on the fare or insist on the meter before you move | Get in first and argue at the destination |
| Leave generous buffer time for traffic | Trust the first estimate you see on the app |
| Learn the local train and metro lines for your route | Assume a road journey is the fastest option |
| Carry small notes and keep valuables close | Flash a phone in a crowded carriage |

**Netherlands**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a bike, a good lock and a second good lock | Leave a bicycle unlocked, even for a minute |
| Ride in the red cycle lane and signal your turns | Walk in the cycle lane while looking at your phone |
| Use an OV-chipkaart or contactless payment and check out | Forget to tap out and pay the penalty |
| Dress for rain every single day | Cancel plans because it is drizzling |

## India

The first thing a newcomer to an Indian city learns is that transport is not a service but a negotiation. In many cities you will bargain with an auto driver who quotes you a price that has more to do with your accent than your distance, and ride-hailing apps have taken some of the guesswork out, though not the surge pricing or the cancelled booking at the worst possible moment. The decision about how to travel is a daily strategic calculation involving time, temperature, budget and mood.

Where India genuinely shines is in its urban rail. The metro systems in Delhi and several other cities are efficient, air-conditioned and affordable, and they have changed the commute for millions. Mumbai's suburban trains are legendary: relentlessly crowded, astonishingly punctual by the standards of the circumstances, and the backbone of a city that would otherwise seize. Regular riders develop an unwritten etiquette that is fascinating to watch: who stands where, who helps whom aboard, who calls out the station before you do.

Then there is the road. Traffic in major cities can turn a ten-kilometre journey into a ninety-minute lesson in patience. Lane markings are suggestions, horns are a language and the pedestrian is a brave, improvisational creature. For an expat accustomed to rules, the chaos can be overwhelming. For one who adapts, there is a kind of liberation in it: the system is so fluid that there is almost always another way through.

Most corporate expats resolve the whole problem by hiring a driver, which makes the commute a time to think, reply to messages or simply stare out of the window at a city doing everything at once. It is comfortable, it is common, and it means you never quite learn the place the way a train commuter does.

Cost matters, too. A metro ride in an Indian city is cheap enough that nobody thinks twice, and an auto fare, once agreed, is a fraction of what a taxi costs abroad. The real price is time and stamina: a bad day on the roads can swallow three hours and leave you wrung out before the working day has started. Smart commuters guard their routes jealously and share the secrets only with friends.

## Netherlands

The Netherlands built its cities for bicycles and then wondered why everyone was so healthy. Dedicated cycle lanes, priority traffic lights, enormous bike parking structures near stations and a culture in which a bicycle is simply how you get places make the commute feel effortless and slightly competitive. Expat guides will tell you that the first week is humbling, because you will be overtaken by someone in a business suit eating a sandwich.

Public transport sits alongside it. The OV-chipkaart, the national travel card, works across trains, buses and trams, and you check in and out every time. Forget to tap out and the system will cheerfully charge you for a journey you did not make. It is the one place where the Dutch efficiency has a trap door, and every expat falls through it exactly once. Expat columnists write about the same experience in different words: the system is excellent, the ticketing is baffling, and the trains are reliable until they are not.

Weather is the quiet tax on the whole arrangement. A Dutch rain is not dramatic; it is horizontal, persistent and rather personal. You will ride in it, you will arrive damp and nobody will care. Winters add darkness at four in the afternoon and a wind that makes a cargo bike feel like a sail.

What you gain is independence. Nobody drives you, nobody negotiates with you, and a ten-minute ride on a bicycle replaces the car journey that would take thirty. For families, it is a revelation: the children cycle to school alone and nobody calls the authorities.

Practicality extends to everything that rolls. Bicycles carry children, shopping, furniture and, on one memorable occasion I witnessed, a small sofa. Bike theft is the great Dutch shame, so a serious lock is a rite of passage. Indian newcomers often say the surprise is not the cycling but the silence: no horns, no haggling, just the soft ticking of a thousand freewheels.

## The Verdict

India gives you movement. The Netherlands gives you freedom. A Dutch commute is better by every measurable standard: safer, cheaper, healthier, more predictable and almost infuriatingly pleasant. The country has solved a problem that most of the world has decided is unsolvable.

But India's commute has something the Dutch version lacks: stories. Nobody has ever described an uneventful bicycle ride to a dinner party. The train in Mumbai, the auto in Bengaluru and the shared taxi in Delhi each produce a narrative before breakfast.

I say the Netherlands wins, comfortably, and I say it knowing that the Dutch will be unbearable about it. The only consolation for the rest of us is that they will be unbearable on a bicycle, in the rain, with their hair ruined.

## Conclusion

Move from Delhi to Amsterdam and you will spend the first month marvelling that the trains leave at the time on the board. Move from Amsterdam to Delhi and you will spend the first month marvelling that anyone arrives anywhere at all.

Both cities work. One works by planning and one works by improvisation. After a year in the Netherlands you will forget how to haggle and learn how to queue. After a year in India you will forget how to wait. Choose which skill you would like to lose.
