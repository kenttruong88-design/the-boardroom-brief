# America Fills the Weekend With Plans; New Zealand Fills It With Mud and Mild Smugness

🇺🇸 United States vs 🇳🇿 New Zealand

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The American weekend is a logistics operation. There is brunch, which must be booked eleven days ahead and consumed alongside eleven strangers who have also booked. There is the youth-sports tournament two states away, the Costco run that swallows a Saturday whole, and the Sunday evening dread that has been marketed so successfully it now has its own streaming playlists. Leisure in the United States is something you schedule, optimise, photograph and then feel faintly guilty about not having enjoyed enough.

New Zealand's approach to the weekend is to look at that whole apparatus and ask why you would not simply go and stand in a river. The country has a population smaller than the greater Los Angeles area and a landscape designed by someone who got carried away, and the result is a national leisure culture built on boats, boots, beaches and a deeply held belief that being outdoors in drizzle is character-building. I have done weekends in both, and I can tell you that one of them is relaxing and the other one is fun, and which is which depends entirely on your tolerance for silence.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇺🇸 United States ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book brunch and anything remotely popular well ahead | Turn up at a trendy spot on Sunday at 11am and expect a table |
| Accept that errands are a legitimate weekend activity | Plan a big day without allowing for the drive |
| Have a standing answer for "what are you up to this weekend" | Admit you plan to do nothing at all, loudly |
| Take the Monday holiday seriously, it is a three-day cult | Schedule anything on a long-weekend Friday afternoon |

| 🇳🇿 New Zealand ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Own proper waterproofs and use them without complaint | Cancel an outdoor plan because it is raining |
| Bring a plate when invited to a barbecue | Arrive empty-handed and expect applause |
| Check the tides and the weather app before any walk | Treat a "short track" as a stroll in trainers |
| Be home and fed before the restaurants stop serving | Turn up at a country-town café at 4pm hoping for lunch |

## 🇺🇸 United States

Let me be fair to America first, because it deserves it. No country on earth does the weekend as spectacle better. Friday night football under floodlights in a small town, a tailgate that begins before breakfast, a farmers' market that doubles as a social network, a city that will sell you a good coffee, a bad cocktail and a live band within a three-block walk on any given Saturday. The sheer density of options is the point. You can spend a Saturday in the United States being a different person four times over, and nobody will comment.

The catch is that the choice is largely commercial, and mostly requires a car. The weekend in a typical American suburb is a series of destinations connected by parking lots. You do not wander, you arrive. Time-use data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics has shown for years that the average American spends a hefty slice of leisure time in front of a screen, and having watched it happen across multiple living rooms I can confirm the data is not being unkind. Leisure is also oddly performative: the hike must be geotagged, the dinner photographed, the hobby monetised into a side hustle by Sunday afternoon.

Then there is the calendar. Weekends in America have a competitive tinge. Children's sports eat Saturdays, and a long Monday holiday can turn an entire region into a single traffic jam. And Sunday, the supposed day of rest, now begins at about 4pm with the low hum of an inbox you swore you would not open. The upside is flexibility: nothing is ever properly closed. Wanting a pharmacy, a burrito or a haircut at nine on a Sunday night is a reasonable request, and someone will sell it to you. For an expat who values convenience over serenity, it is hard to beat.

The deeper truth about the American weekend is that it is relentlessly social and yet strangely lonely. You can fill a Saturday with activities and still not have a conversation that lasts longer than a transaction. Friends meet at venues, not in homes, and even a barbecue can feel like a rehearsed performance. Expats from smaller, slower countries often tell me that the first month feels thrilling and the third feels like being rented to by the hour. I do not think this is a flaw in Americans. It is simply what happens when leisure is sold to you as a product, rather than handed to you as a habit.

## 🇳🇿 New Zealand

New Zealand does not do spectacle. It does fresh air. The weekend revolves around the outdoors, the sea, the bach (a holiday cottage that is usually held together by family history and sandflies), and a general expectation that you will be doing something that involves sunscreen or a thermos. Locals will think nothing of driving two hours to a beach, walking for four, and turning up at a friend's barbecue with a salad in a Tupperware. It is wholesome to a degree that can make a worldly person feel faintly corrupted.

It is also, and I say this with love, quiet. An American in Waipukurau told Expat Arrivals she missed having things to do on the weekends, described the place as pretty dead for entertainment, and said the nearest bigger town was a good forty minutes away. Reader comments collected by the NZ Herald add a line that every newcomer eventually learns the hard way: restaurants stop serving at about nine. Outside Auckland, Wellington and Queenstown, your Saturday night options are a pub, a pub, and a different pub, and the kitchen at all three is shutting.

What you get in exchange is space. Nobody is queuing for a trailhead; the road to the beach is empty; the mountain is, frankly, free. And there is a rhythm to the week that Americans would find almost medically suspicious: work finishes, people go home, and Sunday is allowed to be a day. Weekends are cheap, physical and surprisingly social, built on clubs, school fields and the unspoken rule that you will help someone move a couch. But the price is dull towns, early closing and a slight judgment if you would rather be at a cinema than on a hillside. Pack accordingly.

One more thing about New Zealand, and it is a warning delivered with affection: the weekend is a team sport, and you will be recruited. A new neighbour will ask if you play anything. A colleague will mention a working bee, a fundraising sausage sizzle, or a swim at the local pool that starts at seven. Say yes at least twice, and your social life is sorted for the year. Say no every time, and you will be gently, politely and permanently left alone with your Netflix account and a view of the paddock. It is an extraordinarily effective system for integrating outsiders, and it asks only that you turn up.

## The Verdict

New Zealand wins, narrowly, and only if you have a particular temperament. If you are a person who needs stimulus, the United States will feed you endlessly and you will arrive at Monday tired but entertained. If you are a person who has been quietly dying inside under the weight of a calendar, New Zealand will hand you a pair of boots and tell you to go outside until you feel better, and it will work. I think it works.

But let us not be romantic. Kiwi leisure is cheap because there is nothing to spend money on in the evenings, and the thrill of a tramp wears thin by your fourth consecutive Saturday in a town where the best nightlife is a quiz. America is the country for people who like options. New Zealand is for people who like air. I fall into the second camp on weekends and the first camp the rest of the week, which tells you everything you need to know about me and nothing about which country you should move to.

## Conclusion

Move to America if you want your weekend to be an event, and move to New Zealand if you want it to be a pause. Either way you will spend Sunday evening watching the light go, wondering whether you made the right choice. In the United States you will be wondering while sitting in traffic. In New Zealand you will be wondering at a beach, completely alone, with a flask of tea going cold. One of these is better for your blood pressure. The other has a drive-through.
