# Britain Ranks You by Which Supermarket You Shop At. Colombia Just Wants to Know If You Can Handle the Plaza

🇬🇧 UK 🇨🇴 Colombia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Britain has turned grocery shopping into a covert class survey, where the choice between Waitrose, Sainsbury's, Tesco, and Aldi tells anyone paying attention roughly everything they need to know about your income, your postcode, and possibly your politics. Colombia hasn't bothered with any of that subtlety, because the real test isn't which chain you loyalty-card yourself into, it's whether you can hold your own in a plaza de mercado, haggling over mangoes with a vendor who has clocked you as a tourist before you've even opened your mouth.

I've stood in a Waitrose in Chiswick and watched a woman's expression flicker with something close to relief when she spotted a fellow shopper's tote bag from the same store, a silent handshake of mutual class recognition. I've also stood in Bogotá's Paloquemao market at 7am, surrounded by fruit I couldn't name, being gently but firmly steered toward the good pineapples by a vendor who had decided, unprompted, that I needed guidance. Only one of these experiences involved anyone actually caring whether I got a good deal.

## Do's & Don'ts: UK

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Sign up for a loyalty card — Nectar and Clubcard prices are real savings | Assume the cheapest own-brand is beneath you; Brits secretly love a bargain |
| Bring your own bags — the charge adds up and nobody's impressed | Chat with the cashier at length; a brisk "thanks, bye" is the norm |
| Shop the yellow-sticker reduced section near closing time | Judge someone's Aldi shop — it's increasingly a badge of savviness, not shame |

## Do's & Don'ts: Colombia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit a plaza de mercado for the freshest, cheapest produce | Expect fixed prices at a market stall — a little negotiation is normal |
| Bring cash — many market vendors don't take cards | Rush the interaction; a bit of small talk with the vendor is expected |
| Ask vendors for recommendations — they take real pride in their produce | Assume supermarkets like Éxito replace the market experience entirely |

## UK: A Loyalty Card Is a Personality Trait

British supermarket culture is, beneath its polite surface, an elaborate and largely unspoken class system. Where you shop is legible in a way few Brits will admit out loud but everyone quietly clocks. Waitrose carries a certain cachet, all wicker baskets and artisanal sourdough; Marks & Spencer sits comfortably in the middle, reliable and a little smug about its "this is not just food" tagline; Tesco and Sainsbury's are the vast, unbothered middle ground most of the country actually shops at; and Aldi and Lidl, once mildly stigmatised as the budget option, have undergone a genuine cultural rehabilitation, now openly praised even by people who could easily afford Waitrose, because being seen to know a bargain has become its own status marker.

The loyalty card ecosystem is where British thrift gets genuinely serious. Tesco Clubcard and Sainsbury's Nectar prices create a two-tier system where the "regular" price on the shelf is almost performative, since anyone with the app scans past it to the real, discounted figure. Brits who don't have a loyalty card are, functionally, choosing to pay a tax for their own disorganisation, and everyone knows it.

Then there's the reduced-item hunt, a genuinely nationwide sport played out in the early evening as supermarkets mark down items nearing their sell-by date. Watching otherwise reserved, queue-respecting Britons develop a sudden competitive edge around the yellow-sticker trolley at 7pm is one of the more entertaining spectacles retail has to offer, a rare crack in the famous British reserve.

Social interaction at the till, meanwhile, remains resolutely minimal. A "hiya, you alright?" from the cashier is a script, not an invitation to conversation, and anyone who mistakes it for genuine interest in their day will get a slightly startled response. Efficiency and a certain emotional distance are the house style, and self-checkout machines, despite their well-documented tendency to demand "unexpected item in the bagging area" at the worst possible moment, have been embraced specifically because they let shoppers avoid even that minimal human interaction.

## Colombia: The Market Is a Relationship, Not a Transaction

Colombian grocery shopping operates on an entirely different social contract, one where the plaza de mercado remains the beating heart of how most people actually source their food, supermarket chains like Éxito and Carulla notwithstanding. These markets aren't nostalgic throwbacks kept alive for tourists, they're genuinely where Colombians of every income bracket go for fresher produce, better prices, and a level of product knowledge no supermarket employee could match.

The relationship between vendor and customer is the whole point. Regulars build actual rapport with "their" fruit seller, their meat vendor, their arepa stand, over months and years, and that relationship translates into better cuts, better prices, and produce set aside before it even hits the public display. Haggling exists but operates gently, more a conversational dance than aggressive negotiation, and a foreigner who tries too hard to drive a hard bargain, or conversely, who pays the first price offered without a flicker of engagement, both mark themselves as outsiders who haven't learned the rhythm.

Cash remains essential in this world. Many market vendors, especially smaller stalls, don't take cards, and turning up without cop pesos in hand is a rookie mistake that ends with an awkward walk to find an ATM while your selected mangoes wait, unpaid for, at the stall. The supermarkets, by contrast, function much more like their global counterparts, air-conditioned, card-friendly, orderly, and genuinely useful for packaged goods, imported items, and the sort of shopping that doesn't require a relationship, just a trolley.

What nobody quite prepares foreigners for is how much genuine pride Colombian vendors take in their produce. Ask about a fruit you don't recognise and you'll likely get an enthusiastic, unprompted tutorial, complete with ripeness tests and serving suggestions, delivered with none of the transactional brevity a British till worker would offer. The market isn't just where Colombians buy food. It's where a huge amount of everyday social life happens, several times a week, whether you were looking for company or not.

## The Verdict

Britain wins on systemised efficiency and the quiet thrill of a genuinely good loyalty discount. Colombia wins on everything a supermarket, however well-run, simply cannot replicate: relationship, freshness, and the sense that buying dinner is also a small daily act of community.

Give me British efficiency for the boring weekly stock-up and Colombian markets for anything that should actually taste like something. And if you're judging me by my Aldi bags in one country and my haggling technique in the other, know that I've made my peace with both forms of scrutiny.

## Conclusion

The UK has turned the supermarket into a quiet, endlessly legible status symbol, one loyalty card swipe at a time. Colombia has kept the market as something closer to its original purpose: a place where food, relationship, and community all get bought together, cash only, no app required. Britain will save you money if you play the loyalty game right. Colombia will feed you better if you're willing to learn a vendor's name. Do both properly and you'll never look at a shopping trolley the same way again.
