# India Has Twenty-Two Languages and Singapore Has One Sentence That Ends in Lah

🇮🇳 India vs 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

An Indian moving to Singapore is in the unusual position of being told that the language problem is English, and then discovering the English is a separate language that has been wearing English's clothes. Singlish is a creole of English, Malay, Hokkien, Mandarin and Tamil, held together by particles like *lah*, *lor* and *meh*, and it can turn a simple "can?" into a complete legal agreement.

A foreigner moving to India is in the opposite position. They are told that everyone speaks English, and so they do, in the boardroom. Step outside, and you are in one of twenty-two scheduled languages, any of which may be spoken by the person selling you a SIM card. The two countries are both multilingual, and both are lying about it in very different ways.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn Hindi numbers and a few survival phrases | Assume the whole country speaks Hindi |
| Learn to read the local script on signs | Rely on English signage outside the big cities |
| Use ride apps to bridge the driver conversation | Argue fares in a language you do not share |
| Ask which language your colleagues prefer | Guess from someone's appearance |

**Singapore**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn *can*, *cannot*, *lah* and *lor* by listening | Perform Singlish at people on day one |
| Speak slowly and simply in heartland shops | Assume English means identical English |
| Respect Mother Tongue pride | Treat Tamil as an Indian import |
| Ask people to repeat, politely | Pretend to understand |

## India

India's language map is a headache with a federal budget. According to the 2011 Census figures cited on Wikipedia, Hindi is the first language of about 43.6 per cent of Indians, and around 57 per cent counting second and third languages. English is the first language of almost no one but is used, in some form, by around ten per cent. There are twenty-two scheduled languages plus English as an additional official language, and individual states have their own priorities: Karnataka's sole official language is Kannada and Maharashtra's is Marathi.

For an expat that means your language problem depends on your postcode. Bengaluru's international crowd, as Citizen Matters found, chooses between Kannada, Hindi, Tamil, Telugu and English, and many residents are not native Kannada speakers anyway. One interviewee put it neatly: you do not know who speaks what. A Nigerian who has lived there five years described language as a barrier but not a big one, especially in academia. That is the middle-class expat's typical experience: the office, the campus and the gated community all run in English.

Step outside and it changes. The Expat.com forum's thread on language barriers in India is full of the same pattern: big cities are easier, villages harder. A resident of Goa recommends learning "kitna", the word for "how much", and the Hindi numbers, and says Hindi reached roughly thirty to forty per cent after two years. A resident of Vadodara notes that street-level people have basic English but keep speaking Hindi or Gujarati regardless, and recommends learning to read local scripts. Another, in Shimla, says most people speak English, though locals may address an Indian companion in Hindi even if you are the one with the question.

The workplace adds its own layer. In a big Indian company, English may be the contract language, Hindi the corridor language and a regional tongue the language of the canteen, and you will not know which is in play until someone laughs at a joke you did not understand. That is not exclusion; it is a country that has never needed a single mother tongue. Learning even a modest number of phrases changes how you are received, from client to colleague.

The punchline is that Indians will be gracious about this. Nobody expects you to be fluent. They will switch languages mid-sentence, rescue you with gestures and recruit a bystander as an interpreter. The barrier is real, but it is a porous one, held together by hospitality.

## Singapore

Singapore has four official languages, English, Mandarin, Malay and Tamil, and Malay is the national language, mostly for ceremonial purposes. Expatica reports, on 2020 census figures, that about 48 per cent of residents speak English at home, just under thirty per cent Mandarin, about nine per cent Malay and around 2.5 per cent Tamil, with other Chinese varieties filling most of the rest. Every child studies a Mother Tongue. The working language is English and the street language is something else.

That "something else" is Singlish. Tabla's cheerful guide for new Indian arrivals calls it, roughly, the Hinglish of Singapore, with particles and the all-purpose "can". The Indian arrival has an edge here, because Indian English and Singlish share an elastic cheerfulness about grammar. The shock is accent and speed, and the surprise that Mandarin is the language of the lift and the kopitiam.

The expat forum evidence is nicely contradictory. An Algerian newcomer on Expat.com complained that Chinese was the main language and he struggled to order water at cafes outside the malls. A long-term resident replied that every Singaporean speaks English, though not necessarily Chinese, that he had spent fifteen years in the heartlands without trouble, and that the problem was probably Singlish. A ten-year resident said he had never had difficulties at all. A fair reading is that the mall and the office are fluent, the hawker stall is brisk, and difficulties tend to arise among recent arrivals from China or Tamil Nadu serving in jobs where English is not required.

The story of the reverse direction is instructive. Tabla profiled a Singaporean Chinese engineer who spent seventeen years in Chennai, learned conversational Tamil and some Hindi, and said English worked for engineers but not for drivers and site workers. His mix of English and Tamil he calls, with a shrug, Tamilish. It is a reminder that the barrier is not about intelligence or effort, but about which rooms you want to be let into. The corporate room speaks English. The real room speaks whatever the cook speaks.

Indians will also find Tamil and Hindi food terms in Little India and conversations in Tamil in surprising places, which is both a comfort and a reminder that Singapore's Indian community is Singaporean first.

## The Verdict

Singapore wins on practicality. You arrive, you speak English, you are understood, you are mildly puzzled by the particles and you are fine by Friday. The government built an English-first country, and the result is that a newcomer can go for months without noticing that other languages exist.

India wins on warmth and loses on predictability. It asks you to build relationships in a language you do not have, and it forgives you when you fail. A Hindi phrase offered badly is received like a bouquet.

There is one more difference worth stating. Singapore treats language as policy: a Mother Tongue for every child, English for everything else, and a slogan or two on the side. India treats language as identity, and the argument over which one should dominate has shaped elections. A newcomer who does not grasp that will mistake a language choice for a convenience when it is often a statement. Pick your words as carefully as you pick your business card.

If you want to be understood, move to Singapore. If you want to understand something, go to India and stay long enough to be corrected by a taxi driver. Both are acceptable ways of being a foreigner.

## Conclusion

Language is the part of an international move that nobody budgets for, because it looks like a solved problem on the job spec. Both of these countries will humble you.

In India you will learn that "English-speaking" describes a social class. In Singapore you will learn that "English" describes a family of dialects, one of which says *lah*. Learn the numbers, learn to listen, and never, ever tell a local that you speak the local language fluently. They will test you on the spot, and they will be right.
