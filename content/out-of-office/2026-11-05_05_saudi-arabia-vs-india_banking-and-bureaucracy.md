# Your Money Can Go In. It Cannot, Under Any Circumstances, Come Out.

🇸🇦🇮🇳 Saudi Arabia and India, reviewed by someone who has had a bank account frozen in a language she didn't speak and lived to complain about it

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two of the world's most digitally sophisticated payment cultures, and both have found spectacular, completely unrelated ways to make the actual bank account underneath all that sophistication an ordeal. Saudi Arabia will let your salary pile up invisibly behind a frozen account the moment your residency paperwork lapses — money flows in, nothing flows out, a very literal rich-on-paper-broke-in-practice arrangement. India will let you pay a roadside chai vendor by scanning a QR code in under four seconds, then make opening the bank account that powers that QR code a weeks-long saga involving a bank employee who comes to your actual home. I have been bureaucratically humiliated by both, and I'd like to walk you through precisely how.

## Saudi Arabia: Your Salary Keeps Arriving. You Just Can't Touch It.

The mechanic at the centre of Saudi banking bureaucracy is almost elegant in its cruelty: the moment your Iqama, your residency ID, expires, your bank account automatically freezes — Saudis and expats alike, no targeted punishment implied. Deposits still land. Your employer's salary transfer doesn't know or care that your paperwork lapsed. But withdrawals, ATM card renewals, anything that moves money *out*, simply stop. If the Iqama isn't renewed within 180 days, the bank closes the account outright after settling dues. On a final exit visa, the account closes entirely, and you need your exit paperwork in hand just to extract what's left. Named, on-record cases make the hardship concrete: Abdulrahman Yousif, a Syrian advertising employee in Jeddah, waited six months for renewal while bills piled up because his salary arrived by transfer he couldn't touch; Saleh Al-Haj, Sudanese, waited four months because his sponsor was travelling, with rent pressure mounting the entire time. A Labor Ministry spokesman confirmed employees harmed by a sponsor's delay can file a formal complaint — which tells you the problem is common enough to need an official remedy.

Long-running expat forum threads treat this with the weary, communal fatalism of people who've all independently learned the same hard lesson: drain your account *before* expiry, because extraction afterward is genuinely difficult, involves multiple branch visits, Arabic-only phone support, and system outages conveniently blamed for every delay. Reported workarounds — presenting a passport to force a release, leaning on an employer letter, transferring to a friend's account — work inconsistently, and posters explicitly warn each other that money "may be lost" chasing them.

Here's what makes this genuinely worth watching rather than just enduring: the system is reforming, fast, and in directions that cut against the stereotype. SAMA, the central bank, confirmed Saudi Arabia hit a 79% cashless-transaction rate in 2024, beating its own 2025 target early, with over 280 fintech companies operating and 70-plus graduating from its regulatory sandbox into full licences. And as of September 2025, SAMA now lets the Ministry of Interior's "Visitor ID," issued via the Absher app, count as valid identification for opening a bank account — meaning a short-term tourist, business traveller or pilgrim can now, in some respects, open an account more easily than a long-term expat historically could under the Iqama-and-sponsor system. The Iqama fee structure itself remains a real cost of staying legal: 650 riyals annually for a worker's renewal, 500 for a dependent, employer levies scaling up to 9,600 riyals a year, with Absher's own processing fee a comparatively modest 51.75 riyals. Gender-segregated banking, once standard, has been substantially dismantled since 2018's broader social reforms. This is a bureaucracy actively renovating itself while you watch — which doesn't make the frozen-account wait any shorter, but does mean the complaint you're filing today may genuinely not apply in two years.

## India: A Bank Visits Your House. The QR Code Does Everything Else.

India's contradiction runs in the opposite direction from Saudi Arabia's: the plumbing is dazzlingly modern, the fixtures are aggressively analogue. UPI, the Unified Payments Interface, now accounts for the overwhelming majority of the country's digital transaction volume — 228 billion transactions across calendar 2025, with December alone logging 21.63 billion transactions worth ₹27.97 trillion. Nearly every roadside chai stall, auto-rickshaw and vegetable cart has a QR code taped somewhere visible. By some reporting, over 99% of transactions by volume now happen digitally. This is, genuinely, one of the most frictionless everyday payment cultures on earth.

And then you try to open the bank account underneath it. Foreigners typically open what's called an NRO account at a forex-licensed bank, and the onboarding ritual is wonderfully, specifically physical: a human bank representative commonly comes to your home or office to collect the paperwork in person. Your signature must match your passport signature exactly, and a mismatch causes real, reported friction. First deposits are ideally made by cheque rather than cash, because cash deposits draw genuine scrutiny, and a bounced cheque triggers its own penalty. Experience is wildly visa-dependent: some posters describe the process as faster than opening an account in France, debit card and chequebook arriving within a week; others call the paperwork "exhausting" and simply hire a chartered accountant to push it through for them. HDFC, ICICI and Axis get recommended by name repeatedly, with Axis specifically praised for handling forex remittances, extra paperwork included.

The identity-document layer deserves its own paragraph of appreciation. India runs on the twin pillars of PAN and Aadhaar, except foreigners and NRIs occupy a genuinely confusing carve-out: the Indian government has had to formally clarify, more than once, that Aadhaar is not mandatory for an NRI filing taxes, applying for a PAN card, getting a driving licence, or buying a SIM — other documents suffice, provided you can produce them. PAN remains mandatory for large cash deposits and stock market investment regardless. And then there's stamp paper: a huge range of Indian official documents require a specific, purchasable, denomination-specific physical sheet of paper to be legally valid — a genuinely surreal artefact of bureaucratic design that exists, as one academic paper memorably put it, despite sometimes not being "worth the paper it's written on." Bank hours run a civilised Monday to Friday, 9 to 4:30, Saturday mornings only — a schedule seemingly designed by people who have never needed to bank urgently on a Saturday afternoon in their lives.

## The Verdict

Saudi Arabia's bureaucracy punishes you passively — just let a document lapse and the system does the rest, freezing your money without a single human needing to intervene, which is somehow more unnerving than if someone had actively decided to do it to you. India's bureaucracy punishes you actively and with great ceremony: a human being comes to your home, examines your signature against your passport like a forgery investigator, and may still ask you to produce a specific denomination of paper before anything becomes official. I find Saudi Arabia's version more alarming and India's version more entertaining, which is my way of saying I'd rather be annoyed by a bureaucracy with a face than frightened by one without. Saudi Arabia is also, genuinely, reforming fastest — the Visitor ID rule is proof the frustration has a shelf life. India's stamp-paper economy, by contrast, feels like it's going to outlive us all.

## Conclusion

Both countries will cheerfully let you believe banking has been solved, right up until the specific moment it hasn't. Saudi Arabia's freeze is a trap you walk into by accident, usually via a sponsor's paperwork delay that has nothing to do with you. India's onboarding is a trap you walk into on purpose, eyes open, because the alternative is not banking at all. Keep your Iqama current. Keep your signature consistent. And keep, always, a small reserve of patience that neither country's otherwise genuinely impressive digital infrastructure will spare you from spending.
