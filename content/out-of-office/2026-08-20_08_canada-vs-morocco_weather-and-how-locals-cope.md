# Canada Trains You to Fear Winter From Birth. Morocco Just Makes You Nap Through the Worst of It

🇨🇦 Canada 🇲🇦 Morocco
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Canada treats winter as a lifelong training regimen, an entire culture organised around the assumption that the sky will, for roughly five months of the year, actively try to kill you if you're unprepared, and everyone from toddlers to grandparents is drilled accordingly. Morocco treats extreme heat with something closer to ancestral wisdom than dread: slow down, seek shade, eat later, and let the worst hours of the day simply pass while everyone quietly waits them out.

I've stood on a Winnipeg street corner in January where the air itself felt like a personal insult, watching locals stroll past in full technical gear with the casual unbotheredness of people who've simply accepted this is their life for a third of the year. I've also spent a July afternoon in Marrakech watching an entire medina fall into an unofficial, unspoken siesta, shutters down, streets empty, everyone reappearing, refreshed, the second the sun conceded some ground. Both countries have made peace with extremity. Only one of them makes it look relaxing.

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Layer properly — cotton kills, technical fabrics save you | Underestimate windchill; the number on the app matters more than the temperature |
| Keep an emergency kit in your car from November through March | Assume all of Canada has the same winter — Vancouver and Winnipeg barely compare |
| Invest in real winter boots, not fashion ones | Walk on ice like it's pavement; learn the shuffle, it saves hips |

## Do's & Don'ts: Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Rest during peak afternoon heat, like everyone else does | Schedule strenuous activity or errands for midday in July or August |
| Drink water constantly, even when you don't feel thirsty | Assume coastal cities like Essaouira share the Sahara's extremes |
| Dress in loose, breathable, covering fabric — it works better than exposed skin | Wear dark, tight clothing expecting it to be cooler; it isn't |

## Canada: Winter as National Curriculum

Canadian winter preparedness isn't really optional cultural trivia, it's closer to a survival curriculum absorbed from childhood, refined over decades, and passed down with an almost religious specificity. "Dress in layers" isn't a vague suggestion here, it's an exact science, with cotton widely understood as a genuine liability, since it traps moisture against skin and accelerates heat loss, while proper technical base layers, insulating mid-layers, and windproof outer shells form a system Canadians assemble as automatically as putting on shoes.

The windchill factor looms larger in the collective consciousness than the actual temperature reading, because Canadians have learned, often the hard way, that minus fifteen with a brutal prairie wind behaves nothing like minus fifteen on a still day. Weather apps here report windchill prominently for exactly this reason, and locals plan entire days, whether a walk to the store is safe, whether exposed skin needs covering, around that adjusted number rather than the raw thermometer reading.

The country's sheer geographic range means "Canadian winter" isn't one experience but several wildly different ones. Vancouver's rain-soaked, relatively mild winters bear almost no resemblance to Winnipeg's or Saskatoon's prairie extremes, where wind has thousands of unobstructed kilometres to build momentum before hitting exposed skin, or to the Maritime provinces' damp, bone-deep cold. Newcomers who've heard vague warnings about "Canadian winter" without regional specificity often arrive catastrophically underprepared for whichever version they've actually landed in.

What genuinely surprises outsiders is the sheer normalcy of it all. Canadians don't treat surviving winter as an achievement, they treat it as Tuesday. Ice skating on a frozen pond, cross-country skiing to work, the specific practised shuffle-walk that prevents slips on black ice, all of it delivered with an unbothered competence built from a lifetime of practice. The emergency car kit, blankets, snacks, a shovel, kept in the trunk from November through March isn't paranoia, it's basic infrastructure, treated with the same unremarkable seriousness as a spare tyre.

## Morocco: Heat as Something to Be Outlasted, Not Fought

Morocco's relationship with extreme heat, particularly the punishing summer temperatures that hit interior cities like Marrakech and Fez, and the genuinely dangerous highs found in pre-Saharan regions, runs on a wisdom that's less about active defence and more about graceful surrender to the day's rhythm. The entire daily schedule bends around the sun's worst hours, roughly noon to 4pm, during which much of ordinary life simply pauses.

Shops in the medina shutter, streets that were bustling at 10am empty out almost entirely, and locals retreat indoors or into whatever shade is available, not out of laziness but out of hard-earned practicality. Physical activity, errands, and anything strenuous get pushed to early morning or the cooler evening hours, when the medina reawakens with a second wind that often runs later into the night than visitors expect, dinners starting at 9 or 10pm being entirely normal in peak summer.

Clothing wisdom here inverts what many visitors instinctively assume. Loose, long, breathable fabric, often covering most of the body, actually performs better in extreme dry heat than minimal, tight, or dark clothing, since it blocks direct sun exposure while still allowing airflow. Traditional djellabas exist for genuinely practical thermal reasons as much as cultural ones, and Moroccans who've grown up in this climate treat the tourist instinct to strip down to minimal clothing in extreme heat with a kind of bemused concern, since it's frequently the less effective strategy.

Water consumption becomes a constant, almost automatic background habit rather than a response to feeling thirsty, since by the time thirst registers in extreme dry heat, mild dehydration has often already begun. And crucially, Morocco's climate isn't monolithic any more than Canada's winter is: coastal cities like Essaouira and Casablanca enjoy a genuinely moderating Atlantic influence that keeps summer temperatures far more manageable than the furnace conditions found in Marrakech or the pre-Saharan south, a distinction visitors researching "Moroccan summer" from a single data point frequently miss entirely.

## The Verdict

Canada wins on sheer engineered preparedness, a population that has turned surviving genuinely dangerous cold into a smoothly executed, decades-practised system. Morocco wins on rhythm and grace, on a culture that doesn't fight extreme heat so much as reschedule life around it and come out the other side rested rather than depleted.

If I had to choose which extremity to endure, I'd take Morocco's forced afternoon nap over Canada's windchill-adjusted commute every time. There's something to be said for a culture that responds to brutal weather by lying down for a few hours rather than layering up and pushing through. Canada, I respect your resilience. Morocco, I respect your nap schedule considerably more.

## Conclusion

Canada has engineered an entire cultural apparatus to survive winter through preparation, technical fabric, and sheer stubborn resilience. Morocco has cultivated an entirely different survival strategy for extreme heat: patience, rhythm, and the wisdom to simply stop fighting the day and rest through its worst hours. Both approaches work. Only one of them involves a nap as the primary defence mechanism, and frankly, that one has the better idea.
