# One Country Will Organise A Street Patrol For You, The Other Will Nod At You Once And Call It Friendship

🇲🇾 Malaysia vs 🇳🇿 New Zealand

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Malaysia has a government ministry actively funding neighbourhood committees to the tune of RM20 million a year, whose stated job includes everything from crime prevention to teaching grandmothers how to spot a scam text. New Zealand has a landscape so staggeringly beautiful that nobody has gotten around to organising anything, because why would you, when the view is doing most of the social work already. One country treats "community" as infrastructure to be built, funded, and monitored. The other treats it as something that happens quietly, eventually, usually over a Facebook Marketplace trade, if you're patient and don't take it personally.

I have lived in both kinds of place, and I can tell you the uncomfortable truth nobody puts in the relocation brochure: Malaysian neighbourliness is loud, fast, and slightly nosy in the best way — someone will know your business within a week and bring you soup anyway. New Zealand neighbourliness is a long game that rewards the patient and quietly punishes anyone expecting the open-armed welcome their cousin got in Bali. Expect a volunteer patrol in one country and expect, frankly, to be ignored at the bus stop in the other. Both will eventually give you something real. Only one of them will make you fill out a form for it.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Malaysia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join or at least attend your local Rukun Tetangga (RT) event — these government-backed committees run everything from health screenings to scam-awareness workshops | Assume RTs are just security patrols — many now run economic programmes, eldercare, and even AED/CPR training |
| Lean into gotong-royong (communal work days) — ground-floor residents growing herbs in shared garden beds is a normal, welcomed sight | Expect every condo building to have this spirit automatically — one senior advocate publicly described apartment neighbours living for years as "total strangers" |
| Use WhatsApp or Telegram groups — they're the default modern replacement for the old kampung notice board | Overlook the religious and ethnic diversity angle — RTs are explicitly framed as spaces that work "regardless of race, religion or political beliefs" |
| Expect warmth to come with directness — multiple expats describe "someone to assist you" as a baseline expectation, not a bonus | Expect the same density of expat community everywhere — Johor Bahru has noticeably fewer expats and a thinner scene than KL or Penang |

**New Zealand**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say yes to small, mundane interactions — car parks, cafés, and Facebook Marketplace trades are genuinely where friendships start | Expect fast intimacy — one widely reported line from a British expat described feeling like "a watered-down version" of themselves socially after the move |
| Give it real time — multiple accounts describe needing months to "open up" to the kindness once they recognised it | Mistake Kiwi reserve for coldness — it's frequently a difference in directness and banter style, not an absence of warmth |
| Appreciate understatement — Kiwis are "welcoming and approachable" once you adjust your expectations for the pace | Expect British-style banter — UK expats specifically flagged a "lack of interest" in the back-and-forth teasing they were used to at home |
| Initiate rather than wait — the easiest wins come from everyday errands, not planned events | Assume anonymity like a big European city — small-town New Zealand notices newcomers; it just doesn't announce that it has |

## Malaysia: Community As Civic Infrastructure, Not Accident

What makes Malaysian neighbourhood life structurally different is that it has an actual government department behind it. Rukun Tetangga (RT) committees were originally set up for neighbourhood safety and crime prevention, but per the Ministry of National Unity's own stated plans, they're now being repositioned as a "Penggerak Masyarakat Progresif" — a progressive community mobiliser — with a mandate that stretches well beyond patrol duty. Budget 2024 allocated RM20 million specifically to strengthen RT's role as what the ministry calls a social reference point. This isn't abstract policy language; it shows up in daily life. The Star's feature on RT committees nationwide documents a monthly community policing programme in Petaling Jaya's SS20 Damansara Kim running since 2008, alongside health screenings, anti-scam talks for seniors, and AED/CPR training. In Taman Desa Kulai, Johor, the local RT runs a donated-goods recycling scheme for families in need and a volunteer patrol credited with reducing house break-ins, plus river clean-ups. In Ipoh's Waller Court, one of the city's oldest public housing estates, elderly residents receive food aid and smart emergency devices with fall-detection features, organised entirely at the neighbourhood level.

The RT chairman quoted in that same feature, James Lee, describes these committees as platforms that bring people together "regardless of race, religion or political beliefs" — a line that matters more than it might read on paper, given Malaysia's genuinely layered ethnic and religious makeup. But it would be dishonest to present this as universal. A prominent gerontologist writing in The Star bluntly described apartment residents who'd shared the same building for years as "total strangers," and argued that the kampung spirit needs active reconstruction in cities rather than assumed presence — proof that Malaysia's famous warmth is a cultural default, not a guarantee, and that it requires the RT infrastructure precisely because density and modern condo living have started to erode it on their own. For expats, the practical reality lands somewhere in between: forum accounts describe "a lot of expats from all over the world" in cities like Kuala Lumpur, with one poster noting flatly that "there is always someone to assist you," while a Reddit thread on Johor's expat scene notes it has "definitely much less expats" than KL or Penang, with a far thinner, more localised community to plug into.

The kampung spirit that the gerontologist worried was eroding in the cities is still, by contrast, fully intact in Malaysia's smaller towns and village-adjacent suburbs, where gotong-royong — the communal work session, historically used for anything from harvest to house-building — still gets called on for genuinely mundane things: clearing a monsoon drain before the rains hit, repainting a community hall, setting up decorations for Hari Raya or Deepavali regardless of who in the street actually celebrates which one. Nobody organises it through the RT exactly; it's closer to a standing social expectation that simply predates the ministry's involvement and will presumably outlast it too.

## New Zealand: Warmth That Arrives On Its Own Schedule

New Zealand's community culture runs on a different clock entirely, and the gap between expectation and reality is the single most consistent theme in every account I found. British expats, culturally primed for a certain kind of quick, teasing social rapport, have specifically reported struggling with what one widely syndicated piece summarised as "poor banter and local 'lack of interest' in others" — with one expat's description of the emotional toll distilled into a single, striking line: feeling like "a watered-down version" of themselves since the move. That's not a complaint about unfriendliness exactly. It's a complaint about a different operating system for friendliness, one that doesn't announce itself the way British or European sociability does.

The flip side of that same coin is Dani Kuchel's account, writing for a relocation blog about her move from Berlin: she describes realising "how welcoming and approachable Kiwis are" specifically through unplanned, low-stakes encounters — shops, cafés, car parks — and credits a Facebook Marketplace trade with producing a friendship she calls close and genuine, adding, not quite believing it herself, "I'm not exaggerating." Critically, she also notes she needed time to adjust after Berlin's relative anonymity, framing New Zealand's kindness as something that required her to actively open up to it rather than simply receive it on arrival. That's the throughline across nearly every account: New Zealand's community culture isn't cold, it's slow-release, built from small, repeated, unglamorous interactions rather than institutional programming or instant gregariousness. There's no RT-equivalent handing you a welcome packet. There's just a stranger at a car boot sale who, eventually, becomes somebody who matters.

## The Verdict

Malaysia wins on structure, speed, and sheer civic intent — when a government is funding your neighbourhood's scam-awareness workshops, you will be integrated into something whether you try or not. New Zealand wins on depth once you've earned it, but "once you've earned it" is doing a lot of work in that sentence, and plenty of expats never stick around long enough to collect. If you need community now, Malaysia hands it to you, warts, nosiness and all. If you can wait a year for something quieter and more durable, New Zealand eventually delivers. I don't have the patience for "eventually." Give me the gotong-royong and the soup.

## Conclusion

Here's the sting, delivered with love: Malaysia will make you feel like part of the neighbourhood before you've unpacked your boxes, and New Zealand will make you feel like a mild inconvenience at the dairy for approximately eight months before anyone admits they like you. Both outcomes are real community. One just comes with a government subsidy and a WhatsApp group, and the other comes with a Facebook Marketplace listing and an unreasonable amount of patience. Choose accordingly, and for heaven's sake, stop calling either of them "exotic."
