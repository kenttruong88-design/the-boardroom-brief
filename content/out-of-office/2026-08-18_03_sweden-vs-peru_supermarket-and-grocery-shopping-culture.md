# Sweden Queues for Groceries With Its Feelings. Peru Just Haggles.

🇸🇪 Sweden vs 🇵🇪 Peru — Supermarket and Grocery Shopping Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Sweden has turned grocery shopping into an exercise in emotional regulation — you take a numbered ticket, you wait your turn without a flicker of impatience, and you would rather starve quietly than cut in line, because the queue is basically a small democratic institution and everyone has agreed to respect it or die. Peru treats the supermarket as merely one option among several, and frankly the least interesting one, because the real theatre — the real relationship between buyer and seller — happens at the mercado, where prices are a conversation, not a barcode.

I've queued in Stockholm with the quiet dread of someone who forgot their ticket number, and I've haggled in a Lima mercado over three soles of quinoa purely for the sport of it. Both experiences taught me something. Only one of them was fun.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take a number at the deli or fish counter and actually watch it | Push your trolley past someone mid-browse; personal space is basically sacred ground |
| Bring your own bags — plastic ones cost money and cost you a judgmental look | Chat up the cashier; efficient silence is the entire point of the transaction |
| Learn the difference between ICA, Coop, and Hemköp pricing before committing to a "home" store | Assume Systembolaget keeps the same hours as the supermarket — alcohol runs on its own strict schedule |

### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go to the mercado for produce, meat, and genuine social contact; save the supermarket for packaged goods | Expect fixed prices at a market stall — a little friendly negotiation is expected, not rude |
| Bring small bills; vendors rarely have change for anything large | Show up right at closing hoping for a deal; some vendors will simply ignore you out of principle |
| Ask the vendor what's freshest that day — they will tell you, unprompted, at length | Assume Wong and Plaza Vea are interchangeable; regulars have fierce, unshakeable loyalties |

## Sweden: The Sacred Queue and the Silent Trolley

A Swedish supermarket is, on the surface, indistinguishable from any other clean, well-lit Northern European grocery store — but spend twenty minutes inside one and you start to notice the invisible choreography everyone else seems to have memorised at birth. The queue is not merely a line; it is a small covenant. At any counter with a ticket machine — the fish counter, the deli, the bakery — you take your number and wait with a stillness that borders on meditative, and heaven help you if you try to make eye contact with staff before your number is called, because that reads as an attempt to jump the queue through sheer force of personality, and Swedes will notice, disapprove silently, and never mention it again.

The checkout itself is a masterclass in efficient, low-affect transaction. Small talk is not merely absent, it's faintly suspicious — the cashier scans your items, states the total, and the entire exchange concludes without either party performing warmth they don't feel. This isn't coldness, exactly; it's a cultural agreement that shopping is a logistics problem, not a social occasion, and dragging emotion into it is a bit like bringing a guitar to a tax audit.

What nobody warns you about is Systembolaget, the state alcohol monopoly, which operates on its own separate, stricter hours and closes well before the supermarket does, turning a casual Friday wine run into a genuine time-management crisis if you've left it too late. Plan your week around it or resign yourself to drinking whatever's already in the cupboard.

## Peru: The Mercado Is the Main Event

Peruvians will use a supermarket — Wong, Plaza Vea, Metro — for the boring stuff: detergent, packaged snacks, the odd emergency ingredient. But the actual grocery shopping, the kind that matters, happens at the mercado, a sensory assault of colour, shouting, and produce so fresh it was arguably still growing that morning. Every stall has a specialty and a personality, and building a relationship with your preferred vendors — the one who always saves you the good avocados, the one who throws in a free bunch of cilantro because you've been loyal for six months — is not a nice bonus, it's the entire point of the system.

Haggling here isn't aggressive; it's closer to a friendly duet. You ask the price, the vendor states something slightly inflated, you counter, they counter back with mock offence, and you land somewhere in the middle with both parties satisfied and, more importantly, having enjoyed the exchange. Refusing to engage in this — just paying the first price silently, supermarket-style — actually confuses vendors, who may wonder if you're upset with them specifically.

What nobody prepares you for is the sensory calibration required. The smell of a Peruvian mercado's meat and fish section is not for the faint-hearted, the noise level rivals a football match, and the sheer density of stalls selling nearly identical produce means loyalty, not proximity, decides where you shop. Show up without cash, without patience for a bit of banter, or without an appetite for chaos, and you will retreat gratefully to the sterile hush of Plaza Vea within the week.

## The Verdict

Sweden wins on systems — a country that has engineered grocery shopping into a frictionless, almost therapeutic routine, which is its own quiet achievement. Peru wins on soul — nowhere else does buying vegetables double as a social event, a negotiation, and genuinely enjoyable theatre. If you want your grocery run to be over in eleven minutes with zero human interaction, go to Sweden. If you want it to take ninety minutes, involve three separate conversations, and end with a free herb bundle, go to Peru. I know which one I actually look forward to.

## Conclusion

Sweden's supermarket is a machine built for dignity through efficiency; Peru's mercado is a stage built for relationship through negotiation. Neither would tolerate the other's approach for more than a week — a Swede haggling would feel like an act of aggression, and a Peruvian queuing silently for a fish ticket would feel like being ignored on purpose. Pick your grocery run accordingly, and for heaven's sake, learn the local rules before you either cut a Swedish queue or insult a Peruvian vendor by refusing to negotiate. Both are unforgivable, in their own country, for entirely opposite reasons.
