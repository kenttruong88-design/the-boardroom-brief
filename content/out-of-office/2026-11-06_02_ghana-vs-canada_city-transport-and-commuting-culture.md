🇬🇭 Ghana vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every city teaches you its own commuting language within the first week, whether you want to learn it or not. In Accra, that language is spoken through a conductor's hand slapping the side of a van. In Toronto, it is communicated entirely through a death stare, delivered in total silence, because God forbid anyone actually say something.

Toronto's own transit authority publishes an etiquette guide. Accra's tro-tro network has no schedule, no app, and no official guide of any kind, and somehow runs on time more often than the TTC does during an ice storm. One of these systems is organised chaos that works. The other is organised order that occasionally stops working entirely, and everyone pretends not to notice.

## Do's & Don'ts: Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the hand signals — a stirring-finger gesture for Kwame Nkrumah Circle, calling out "ewomu" when you're near your stop | Expect a fixed schedule, a published route map, or a vehicle that leaves before it's full |
| Run two or three ride-hailing apps at once and compare live surge pricing before you book, the way locals actually do it | Pay the first Bolt quote you see; a Yango check can save you more than half the fare |
| Keep small notes on you to pass forward hand-to-hand to the mate | Assume sidewalks exist; much of the city simply doesn't have them, so walking is its own negotiation |
| Treat the tro-tro as a genuinely social space — shared destinations, shared commentary, the occasional shared umbrella | Snatch your phone out mid-ride near an open window; motorbike-riding phone thieves are a real, named risk, not an urban legend |

## Do's & Don'ts: Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stand right on escalators, let people off before you board, and read the room instead of asking it out loud | Block a doorway with your backpack on a packed streetcar; Toronto commuters will silently resent you for the rest of your natural life |
| Budget real time for winter; TTC routes do grind to a halt in ice storms, and this is treated as normal, not emergency | Expect anyone to say "excuse me" before the death glare arrives; the glare is the communication |
| Build in the national average of roughly 26 minutes each way, longer again if you're in Toronto specifically | Assume a short commute; Toronto's average sits at 33.3 minutes, the longest of any Canadian city, and 15.7% of its workers commute an hour or more |
| Consider GO Transit's regional rail for longer hops — and read its actual, crowd-sourced Etiquette Book if you want to understand the national psyche | Mistake Canadian politeness for warmth; it is, specifically, a non-confrontation protocol, not an invitation to chat |

## Ghana: a system built by consensus, not by schedule

The tro-tro is not public transport in the Western sense of a timetable, a route map, and a dependable arrival window. It is a privately-run minivan network that nonetheless manages, through sheer collective improvisation, to move most of Accra around for a fraction of the price of a taxi — roughly $0.25 a ride, according to one Talesmag post from a longtime resident, against $1 to $5 for a cab. The system runs on a vocabulary all its own: the "mate," or conductor, collects fares passed hand-to-hand from passenger to passenger down the length of the van, calls out the destination, slaps the vehicle's side panel to signal the driver to stop, and will occasionally surrender his own seat when the van fills up. There's a hand-signal grammar for destinations — a finger-stirring gesture means you're headed to Kwame Nkrumah Circle — and locals say "ewomu," Twi for "it's near," to flag they're approaching their stop. No schedule exists because no schedule is needed; vehicles simply leave when full, which is a more honest contract with your time than any printed timetable has ever offered.

One obroni's (foreigner's) first tro-tro ride, recounted on MyJoyOnline, captures the sensory overload precisely: crossing the street by jumping a five-foot-deep open gutter while a colleague caught her on the far side, motorbike riders ferrying a baby strapped to its mother with nobody wearing a helmet, and a stranger's warning to step back from the curb because phone-snatchers on motorbikes specifically target people standing too close to the road. Total cost for three rides across the city: about 20 cedis, or roughly $2. That is not a system failing to modernise. That is a system that modernised around a different set of priorities entirely — cost and flexibility over predictability — and it shows in how the ride-hailing layer has been bolted on top of it. Commuters in Accra now routinely run Bolt, Yango, and Accra Cab simultaneously, switching apps mid-commute based on live surge pricing, a habit documented, not invented: one reported Bolt fare from Kasoa to Ridge Hospital rose 50% at peak demand, prompting an immediate switch to Yango, which came in 55% cheaper for the identical route. No sidewalks, no passenger rail, and absolutely no patience for anyone who thinks that's a contradiction rather than a workaround.

## Canada: a system of rules, politely unenforced out loud

Canada's commuting problem is not a lack of infrastructure. It is an abundance of infrastructure that still, somehow, cannot keep up with the number of people standing on top of it. Toronto's average commute runs 33.3 minutes each way, the longest of any Canadian city according to the 2021 census, with 15.7% of its workers commuting an hour or more each direction. Drivers lost roughly 100 hours to rush-hour traffic in 2025 alone — four full days — a figure that's actually risen from the year before, per TomTom's Traffic Index, with average rush-hour speeds crawling along at under 19 kilometres an hour.

What makes it distinctly Canadian isn't the congestion — every major city has congestion — it's the etiquette architecture built up around it, documented so thoroughly it's become its own minor journalism beat. BlogTO has run multiple pieces over the past decade cataloguing "worst people on the TTC," from a rider waiting fifteen minutes at Sherbourne station with seventy others for one overcrowded streetcar, to forty-plus commuters watching a packed 504C bus sail straight past their stop without even slowing down. CBC's own etiquette explainer describes the city's preferred enforcement mechanism bluntly: not verbal confrontation, but "the death glare." You will know you've done something wrong on Toronto transit. Nobody will tell you what. You are simply expected to feel it, the way you'd feel a sudden drop in room temperature.

Then there's the seasonal failure mode nobody in a brochure mentions: ice storms and extreme cold routinely grind TTC routes to a halt, a recurring enough phenomenon that BlogTO has covered it under headlines like "Toronto wakes up to commuter chaos as icy roads grind TTC routes to a halt." GO Transit, the regional rail operator, has gone so far as to publish its own crowd-sourced Etiquette Book, assembled directly from commuter complaints — a genuinely telling institutional artifact. This is a country formalising politeness into an actual PDF because the alternative, apparently, was too many silent death glares going unresolved.

## The Verdict

Ghana's system has no schedule and somehow feels more honest than Canada's, which has every schedule imaginable and still loses four days a year to traffic it never quite admits to. Accra's chaos is loud, visible, and priced in cedis you can actually track, cost swing and all; Toronto's order is silent, glacial, and still somehow leaves you standing at Sherbourne station with seventy strangers who are all furious and none of whom will say so out loud. I'll take the honest chaos. At least the tro-tro tells you, through a slap on its own side panel, exactly what it thinks of your stop request. The TTC just lets you freeze at the platform wondering if the death glare you're receiving is about your backpack or your general existence.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *MyJoyOnline — a first-time tro-tro rider describes jumping a five-foot-deep open gutter to cross the street, and being warned by a stranger to step back from the curb so a passing motorbike rider couldn't snatch her phone.*

> *Expat.com forum, Accra — a prospective resident asking whether car-free life in Accra is realistic is told tro-tros are cheap but "often crowded and uncomfortable," with taxis costing several times more for the same trip.*

> *BlogTO, reporting Toronto commuter complaints — one rider waited fifteen minutes at Sherbourne station alongside roughly seventy others for a single overcrowded streetcar, while a packed bus elsewhere sailed past forty waiting passengers without stopping.*

## Conclusion

Both systems will eventually get you where you're going, which is really all transport is obligated to do. But only one of them will teach you a new vocabulary, give you a fare system you can calculate in your head, and let a stranger genuinely look out for your phone. The other will teach you to read a facial expression as a legal notice. Accra wins on honesty, cost, and sheer improvisational nerve. Toronto wins on infrastructure spend and absolutely nothing else — not even, by its own commuters' account, on actually getting you there on time.
