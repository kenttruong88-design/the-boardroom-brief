# Ireland Will Treat You Eventually. Moldova Will Treat You If You Brought Cash

🇲🇩 Moldova vs 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Healthcare is the one topic on which every expat becomes an expert within a fortnight. You do not choose to learn it. A child gets a fever, a tooth gives out, a form arrives in an unfamiliar language, and suddenly you are conducting a seminar on the national health system, with yourself as the only attendee.

Moldova and Ireland make a fascinating pair, because they sit at opposite ends of the European spectrum on wealth, and yet both have a reputation for a system that works better on paper than it does on a Tuesday afternoon. One is a small, poorer country that has inherited a post-Soviet model. The other is a wealthy one with a two-tier system that baffles even its own residents.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Moldova**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check what the public insurance actually covers | Assume everything is free because you are insured |
| Keep cash and contacts for private clinics | Rely on the public system for anything urgent and complicated |
| Ask local friends which doctors they trust | Choose a clinic from an advert |
| Keep your own copies of every test result | Leave the records with the clinic |

**Ireland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register as ordinarily resident and ask what you qualify for | Assume you have a medical card |
| Consider private health insurance, especially for hospital care | Rely on the public system for non-urgent treatment |
| Keep receipts, since tax relief is available on some medical costs | Throw them away |
| Know the numbers 112 and 999 for emergencies | Drive yourself to hospital in a crisis |

## Moldova

I must be open that I did not read a source on Moldovan healthcare for this piece, so this section is general knowledge and opinion, and you should check the detail with a local. The broad picture, as I understand it, is a public system funded through compulsory health insurance, with chronic funding pressure, strained facilities and a long-standing practice of informal payments and private care for anything where speed matters.

That last point is the cultural centre of gravity. In countries with a post-Soviet medical heritage, the public system exists, and so does a parallel economy of private clinics and personal connections. People who can pay do so. People who cannot rely on family, friends and the cousin who knows a surgeon. It is not corruption in the cartoon sense. It is adaptation to scarcity, and it shapes how Moldovans think about doctors.

The upside is a certain directness and a deep respect for the physician. Doctors are trusted, sometimes excessively, and a consultation is often thorough and personal. Many Moldovan professionals are also well trained, and a long list of them have taken their skills abroad, including to Ireland, which is one reason the two countries are connected in healthcare at all.

The downside is equipment, queues and the quiet, universal advice that if you can afford the private route, you take it. The expat who arrives expecting Western European standards across the board will be disappointed, and the expat who arrives expecting nothing will be pleasantly surprised by the best clinics.

If you are an outsider moving there, build a trusted network quickly. A good private clinic, a pharmacy you trust and a friend who speaks the language are worth more than any insurance policy. Keep your own records. And accept that the system works through people more than through procedures.

## Ireland

Ireland is a different animal. According to Expat.com's guide, the public system is run by the Health Service Executive, and expats count as ordinarily resident after living in Ireland for at least a year. About 2.52 million people, or 46 percent, hold private health insurance, according to Health Insurance Authority figures from October 2025. There are 21 private hospitals, mostly around Dublin, and the Drug Payment Scheme caps household prescription spending at 80 euros a month.

The European Health Insurance Card covers public care only, not private. Non-EU nationals may also need proof of private insurance for visas or residence permits, and some medical expenses attract tax relief if claimed within four years. It is a system with generous rules and complicated edges, and the edges are where newcomers get cut.

The voice of experience comes from a Galway expat on Expat Exchange, who wrote: "Private health insurance is absolutely essential. The private hospitals are OK provided it is not an emergency." That sentence is the unofficial Irish health policy. Almost half the population has private insurance because the public system's waiting lists make it a necessity for anyone who wants treatment on a human timescale.

The same expat described being "left on a trolley for 30 hours" after a fall with a broken collar bone and ribs. The trolley is a national symbol in Ireland, the shorthand for emergency department overcrowding, and I mention it with sympathy and without surprise. Trolley counts are published every day, and nobody finds that strange.

Still, the quality of care, once you reach it, is high, and the doctors and nurses are often outstanding. The Irish system's problem is access, not competence. For a newcomer, the practical lesson is that you must engage with the structure early: register, work out your category, get insured and keep your documents straight.

A word about the human side, because medicine is also about trust. In Moldova, the relationship with a doctor is personal and often long, and people go back to the same name for years. In Ireland, the GP is the gateway to everything, and the relationship is similar in spirit but squeezed by volume. Newcomers should register with a family doctor early and keep going, because continuity is the closest thing to a shortcut the system offers. That advice works in both countries, which tells you something: whatever the structure, the people inside it respond to being known.

It also helps to be honest with yourself about risk. If you have a chronic condition, a pregnancy or children, the gaps in either system matter far more than they do to a healthy single person on a short posting. Ask about specialists, medication supply and costs before you accept the job. A relocation package that ignores healthcare is a relocation package that has not been read, and the person who finds that out in a waiting room is rarely in the mood to be philosophical.

## The Verdict

Ireland wins on standards and Moldova wins on speed, if you can pay for it. That sounds like a joke, but it is truer than it should be. The Irish system has far better infrastructure and far worse waiting times. The Moldovan system has a more modest infrastructure, and a private sector that moves faster for those with the means.

If I were unwell and urgent, I would want to be in a well-run Irish hospital, preferably with insurance. If I were unwell and impatient, I would want a private Moldovan clinic and a friend with a phone. The truth is that wealth, not geography, decides your experience in both.

My advice is cynical but sound: whichever country you pick, buy the insurance, keep the records and do not rely on the public system for anything you cannot wait for. And if you are being wheeled to a corridor in Galway, bring a book.

## Conclusion

Every health system is a promise, and every expat eventually finds out which part of it is kept. Ireland promises care and delivers it slowly. Moldova promises a framework and delivers it through people.

You cannot fix either, and you cannot opt out. So read the small print, buy the insurance and remember that in both countries, the best medical plan is a good relationship with a good doctor. Everything else is paperwork, and paperwork never set a bone.
