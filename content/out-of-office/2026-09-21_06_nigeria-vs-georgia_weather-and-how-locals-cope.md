# One Country Battles Heat With Sheer Willpower. The Other Just Puts On a Second Sweater and Complains Beautifully.

🇳🇬🇬🇪 By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office

Lagos does not have weather so much as it has a single, unrelenting climatic mood: hot, then hotter, then briefly and violently rained on, then hot again. Tbilisi, meanwhile, gets actual seasons — snow-capped mountains visible from the old town in winter, blistering summers in between, and a population that has turned complaining about all of it into something close to a national art form.

I've sweated through Lagos traffic in a blouse that surrendered by 9am, and I've shivered through a Tbilisi January wondering why nobody had mentioned the wind coming off the Caucasus. Both countries have opinions about how you should be coping. Only one of them is right, and it's not the one you'd expect.

## Do's & Don'ts: Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry an umbrella year-round — it doubles as heat shade and rain cover | Assume "rainy season" means occasional showers; it means daily deluges |
| Dress in breathable fabrics and plan errands for early morning | Schedule outdoor plans for midday during harmattan or peak heat |
| Stock up on dust masks or scarves during harmattan season | Ignore the harmattan haze — it affects visibility and breathing both |
| Keep a generator or backup power plan for AC during outages | Rely solely on grid power to survive the hottest months |

## Do's & Don'ts: Georgia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Layer heavily for Tbilisi winters — the wind cuts harder than the temperature suggests | Trust a mild-looking forecast; mountain wind changes everything fast |
| Head to Batumi or the coast if you want summer relief from inland heat | Assume the whole country shares Tbilisi's climate — it doesn't |
| Embrace khinkali and hot wine culture as the local winter coping strategy | Skip the traditional bathhouses — they're a genuine seasonal ritual |
| Check regional forecasts separately if travelling to mountain areas | Pack for Tbilisi weather and head to Svaneti without rechecking |

## Nigeria: Heat as a Permanent Condition, Not a Season

Nigeria doesn't really do "seasons" in the four-part sense much of the world takes for granted. Lagos runs a near-constant hot and humid climate punctuated by a dramatic rainy season, roughly April through October, when the skies open with a force that turns streets into rivers within twenty minutes and then, just as abruptly, clears to blazing sun again. The rest of the year skews dry, capped by harmattan — the dusty, cooler wind that rolls down from the Sahara between November and February, coating everything in a fine reddish haze and dropping visibility enough to genuinely affect flights and driving.

Locals have built an entire coping infrastructure around this reality that visitors chronically underestimate. Generators aren't a backup plan; in a country where grid power remains unreliable, they're the actual plan for keeping AC and fans running through the hottest stretches. Umbrellas serve double duty as sun shade and rain shelter, carried as reflexively as a phone. Clothing choices lean hard into breathable fabric, and the savvier residents structure their entire day around the heat — errands and outdoor work happen at dawn or dusk, with the punishing midday hours reserved for being anywhere with functioning air conditioning.

What's remarkable, and what visitors consistently fail to prepare for, is harmattan's dual nature: it's genuinely cooler, a welcome relief after months of humidity, but it arrives with a dust load thick enough to trigger respiratory irritation and turn the sky a persistent, hazy beige. Nigerians navigate it with scarves, masks in the dustiest stretches, and a fatalism that would read as complaint anywhere else but here is simply accurate weather reporting. Nobody in Lagos is surprised by any of this. The only people caught off guard are the ones who didn't ask before they packed.

## Georgia: Four Seasons, One National Sport of Complaining About Them

Georgia, by contrast, gets the full seasonal cycle, and it wears each one with theatrical intensity. Tbilisi winters are genuinely cold — not Siberian, but sharp, windy, and deceptive, because a forecast that looks merely chilly on paper can feel considerably harsher once the wind funnels down off the Caucasus mountains and straight through whatever coat you optimistically decided was "probably enough." Summers swing the other direction entirely, with inland heat that sends locals fleeing to Batumi and the Black Sea coast in numbers that make July inland feel like a ghost town by comparison.

The coping mechanisms here are less about infrastructure and more about ritual. Winter is met with khinkali, hot wine, and a bathhouse culture — the sulfur baths of Tbilisi's Abanotubani district — that treats seasonal discomfort as an excuse for communal indulgence rather than something to simply endure. Georgians don't so much tough out winter as they schedule an entire social calendar around softening it, and there's something genuinely appealing about a culture that responds to cold weather with dumplings and steam rooms rather than grim silence.

The complication for visitors is Georgia's dramatic internal climate variation — a mild Tbilisi forecast tells you almost nothing about conditions three hours away in the mountainous Svaneti region, where snow can arrive early and stay late regardless of what the capital is doing. Locals instinctively check regional forecasts separately; travellers who pack based purely on Tbilisi's mild reputation regularly find themselves badly underdressed the moment they head toward the mountains, discovering the hard way that "Georgia" isn't one climate but several, loosely governed by altitude and proximity to a sea nobody bothered to mention.

## The Verdict

Nigeria's heat is relentless but honest — it never pretends to be anything other than what it is, and the coping infrastructure, generators and all, has been battle-tested by necessity into genuine competence. Georgia's weather is milder on paper but sneakier in practice, with wind and altitude doing damage that the raw temperature numbers don't warn you about. If I have to choose which country's weather I'd rather negotiate with, I'll take Georgia — not because it's easier, but because any climate that responds to a cold snap with hot wine and dumplings understands something about coping that generators, however necessary, simply can't replicate.

## Conclusion

Weather, it turns out, is never really just weather — it's a preview of how a country organises itself around hardship. Nigeria copes through infrastructure and fatalistic pragmatism; Georgia copes through ritual, food, and communal indulgence. Neither approach is soft, and neither country wants your pity. Pack an umbrella for Lagos, a proper windproof coat for Tbilisi, and in both places, take the locals' advice over the forecast app every single time. The forecast doesn't know what harmattan dust or Caucasus wind actually feels like. The locals do, and they've been complaining about it, accurately, for generations.
