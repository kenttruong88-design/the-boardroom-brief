# Zambia Will Ask You for Proof of Everything; France Will Ask You for Proof That Your Proof Exists

🇿🇲 Zambia 🇫🇷 France
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have opened a bank account in Lusaka in under a week with a passport, a reference letter, and a smile. I have also spent four months in France in a bureaucratic loop so recursive it should have its own philosophy department: I needed a bank account to get my Titre de Séjour, and I needed my Titre de Séjour to get a bank account. Both countries have real bureaucracies. Only one of them has weaponized the concept into an art form.

## Do's & Don'ts: Zambia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a passport, proof of address, and a reference letter to any bank | Assume every bank has identical foreign-account requirements — call ahead |
| Expect a in-person process — online-only account opening is rare | Expect instant approval; allow several business days minimum |
| Use a local reference or employer letter to speed things along | Assume a tourist visa alone will satisfy most banks' account criteria |
| Keep photocopies of every document — originals sometimes get held | Get frustrated by manual paperwork — the system runs on physical files |

## Do's & Don'ts: France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Start your Titre de Séjour and bank account processes simultaneously | Wait for one bureaucratic step to finish before starting the next |
| Use a récépissé (proof of pending application) as an interim document | Assume every bank branch interprets the rules the same way — some are flexible |
| Book OFII and prefecture appointments the moment you're eligible | Expect phone or email follow-ups to move anything faster |
| Keep certified photocopies of absolutely everything, always | Throw away any official letter — you will need it again, guaranteed |

## Zambia: Paper-Based, but Predictable

Zambian banking bureaucracy has a reputation, largely earned, for being slow and heavily manual — but it is, crucially, predictable manual slowness rather than the recursive kind. Expat Arrivals' guide to banking in Zambia lays out a process that, while requiring patience, follows a comprehensible linear sequence: passport, proof of address, often a reference letter from an employer or existing account holder, submitted in person, processed over several business days. It's old-fashioned. It is not, however, designed to trap you.

GlobalBankDirectory's breakdown of opening an account as a foreigner in Zambia notes something genuinely refreshing by comparison to more "developed" bureaucracies: individual bank branches retain real discretion, and a good relationship with a branch manager or a solid reference letter can meaningfully speed up an otherwise slow process. This is a system that still runs on human judgment rather than pure procedural rigidity — which cuts both ways, since it also means outcomes can vary meaningfully bank to bank, branch to branch, even teller to teller.

What genuinely trips up newcomers isn't the requirements themselves but the paper-heaviness of the whole operation: physical files, photocopies, occasionally documents that get "held" for verification in a way that feels alarming until you realize it's routine. An Internations Lusaka thread captures the general sentiment well — bring more documentation than you think you'll need, expect a in-person process from start to finish, and treat the whole thing as a multi-day errand rather than a same-day task. Slow, yes. But it moves in one direction, and it eventually arrives.

## France: A Bureaucracy That Requires Itself to Function

French bureaucracy's reputation is not exaggerated, and the specific expat-banking bottleneck is its purest expression: many French banks require a Titre de Séjour (residence permit) to open a full bank account, and the residence permit application process, in turn, frequently expects proof of a stable financial situation — sometimes explicitly, sometimes just implicitly through the paperwork officials expect to see. An Expat Forum thread titled, simply, "Bank won't open account without Titre de Séjour" reads like a support group, with dozens of replies describing the exact same catch-22 across different prefectures and different years.

The workaround, once you find it (usually from another expat, rarely from an official source), is the récépissé — a temporary proof-of-pending-application document issued after you've submitted your residence permit paperwork but before it's approved, which some banks will grudgingly accept in lieu of the full Titre de Séjour. bestcitiesinfrance.com's own 2026 guide to opening accounts as a foreigner is refreshingly candid that success depends heavily on which specific branch and which specific advisor you get — the rules exist, but their application is inconsistent enough that "try a different branch" is genuinely standard advice.

A widely shared Substack essay on "Reflections on Bureaucracy and Daily Life in France" nails the emotional texture of the experience better than any official guide: it's not that any single step is unreasonable, it's that the system seems to have been designed by several different departments who have never once spoken to each other, each convinced their own paperwork requirement is the sensible baseline everyone else should be building around. You are not navigating one bureaucracy in France. You are navigating several, simultaneously, each with its own conflicting idea of what "proof" means.

## The Verdict

Zambia's banking bureaucracy is slow, manual, and paper-heavy — but it is fundamentally sequential: you produce a document, it gets processed, you move forward. France has built something more insidious: a bureaucracy that requires you to already possess the thing you're applying for in order to apply for it, a closed loop that only opens through informal knowledge (the récépissé workaround) that no official pamphlet will hand you upfront. I'll take Zambia's honest slowness over France's philosophical trap every single time — at least in Lusaka, patience is eventually rewarded rather than recursively punished.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat Forum For People Moving Overseas — a new arrival in France recounted being turned away from three separate bank branches before finding one advisor willing to accept a récépissé, and called it "the single most French experience of my life, and I've eaten a lot of cheese."*

> *leavingamerica.substack.com — a longtime American resident of France wrote that surviving French bureaucracy "isn't about following the rules, it's about finding the one human in the system willing to bend them slightly in your favor."*

> *Internations Lusaka community — an expat described being asked to produce a physical reference letter twice, at two different branches of the same bank, and simply resubmitted the same photocopy both times "because arguing would have taken longer."*

## Conclusion

Every bureaucracy is, at its core, a country's institutional trust issues made visible on a form. Zambia's says "prove it, slowly, in person, and we'll believe you." France's says "prove it, but also prove the thing that proves it, and good luck finding out which office believes which document today." I've made peace with both, eventually — but only one of them made me question the nature of proof itself while standing in line at a Paris branch that had, that day, apparently changed its own rules without telling anyone, including itself.
