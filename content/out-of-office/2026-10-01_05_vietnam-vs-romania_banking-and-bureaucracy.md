# One Country's Bank Will Approve You in an Afternoon. The Other Will Make You Return Four Times for the Same Stamp.

🇻🇳🇷🇴 Vietnam & Romania
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nothing exposes a country's actual relationship with the state quite like watching how it handles a foreigner trying to open a bank account. Vietnam, for all its reputation as a place where things move fast, treats banking with a brisk, almost suspiciously efficient pragmatism once you have the right paperwork. Romania, a European Union member state with every modern banking app you could want, somehow still requires you to physically appear, in person, with an original stamped document, for processes that could plainly be handled by email.

I've opened accounts in both, and I can tell you precisely which experience left me sobbing quietly in a car park afterward. Spoiler: it wasn't the one in Hanoi.

## Do's & Don'ts: Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your temporary residence card (TRC) sorted first — most banks won't budge without it | Don't expect every branch of the same bank to apply the rules identically — ring ahead and confirm |
| Bring more documentation than you think you need, including your passport's original visa page | Don't assume English will be spoken fluently at smaller branches outside major cities |
| Use a local fixer or your employer's HR team if offered — it genuinely speeds things up | Don't wire large sums internationally without checking the receiving bank's reporting threshold first |
| Expect same-day or next-day account opening once your paperwork is actually in order | Don't lose your bank book (sổ tiết kiệm) — replacing it is a far bigger headache than opening the account was |

## Do's & Don'ts: Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your CNP (personal numeric code) sorted at the local town hall before attempting anything else | Don't expect a single visit to suffice for anything involving ANAF, the tax authority — budget for at least two |
| Bring physical copies of everything, even documents you've already submitted digitally | Don't assume a stamped document from one office will be accepted without question at another |
| Be early — queue numbers run out, and "early" means well before opening time at busy branches | Don't lose your patience with the clerk; they didn't design this system, and your frustration changes nothing |
| Keep every receipt and stamped form indefinitely — you will be asked for it again, probably at the worst time | Don't assume online banking apps (several are genuinely excellent) replace the need for in-person bureaucracy entirely |

## Vietnam: Fast, Once You've Cleared the Gate

Vietnamese bureaucracy has a reputation, not entirely deserved, for being chaotic. What's actually true is narrower and more interesting: the gate to entry — getting your temporary residence card, your work permit, your visa sponsorship sorted — can be genuinely slow and occasionally opaque, heavily dependent on your employer's competence and connections. But once you're through that gate, with the correct stamped documents in hand, the actual transactional bureaucracy moves with startling speed.

Opening a bank account at Vietcombank or Techcombank, once your TRC is in order, is often a same-day affair — fill in the forms, provide your documents, and walk out with a functioning account and a debit card, sometimes within the hour. This stands in stark contrast to the initial paperwork gauntlet, which can take weeks of waiting on an employer's HR department or an agency to push things through the right channels. The system essentially front-loads all its friction into the entry process and then gets out of your way.

What foreigners consistently underestimate is how much depends on personal relationships and local knowledge rather than the written rules alone. A clerk who likes you, or who's been pre-briefed by your company's HR liaison, can smooth over ambiguities that would otherwise stall you for days. This isn't corruption so much as a system that still runs substantially on social lubrication rather than pure procedural rigidity — which is either charmingly human or deeply frustrating, depending entirely on whether you're the one benefiting from it that day.

## Romania: Efficient Technology, Medieval Process

Romania presents a genuinely strange contradiction: its digital banking apps are excellent, its fintech scene is lively, and yet huge swathes of necessary bureaucracy still require you to show up physically, with a stamped original document, to an office that keeps old-fashioned hours and issues paper queue numbers that run out before 9am. You can transfer money instantly on your phone and then spend four hours the same week queuing at ANAF because a single stamp from a different office wasn't accepted at face value.

The root of this isn't incompetence; it's institutional caution layered over decades, a system still metabolising its transition from a heavily centralised bureaucratic state. Every office wants its own verification, its own stamp, its own physical proof, because for a very long time, trusting another office's paperwork wasnual. The result for a foreigner trying to get a CNP, register for tax purposes, or open certain types of account is a process that feels almost comically redundant: submit the document, get it stamped, submit a copy of the stamped document elsewhere, get that stamped too.

The silver lining is that once you're fully registered — CNP in hand, tax status settled — day-to-day banking in Romania is as modern and frictionless as anywhere in Western Europe. Revolut, Banca Transilvania's app, contactless everything; the country has leapfrogged ahead on consumer banking technology even while its administrative entry process remains stuck in an earlier decade. The contrast is the whole story: brilliant interface, brutal onboarding.

## The Verdict

Vietnam's system is slow at the gate and fast once you're through it. Romania's system is fast at the gate (EU freedom of movement helps enormously) and then slow, repetitively so, at every subsequent administrative step. If I had to pick which country's bureaucracy I'd rather navigate with a hangover, it's Vietnam, purely because the actual bank visit, once properly papered, takes less time than it takes to finish your coffee.

I'm calling it for Vietnam. Not because Vietnamese bureaucracy is "better" in some abstract sense, but because it fails you once, upfront, honestly, rather than nickel-and-diming your patience across six separate stamped visits to buildings that all claim the others got it wrong.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/VietNam — paraphrased: opened my account in 40 minutes once my TRC finally came through after six weeks of waiting. The waiting was the hard part, not the bank.*

> *Internations Bucharest — paraphrased: went to ANAF four times for the same registration. Each time a different clerk wanted a slightly different stamp. Nobody could tell me which one was actually correct.*

> *expat.com Hanoi — paraphrased: my company's HR assistant came with me to the bank and suddenly everything that was "impossible" became very possible within ten minutes.*

## Conclusion

Both countries will test your patience, just at different stages of the process and for different reasons. Vietnam front-loads the pain into getting legally established, then rewards you with genuine efficiency once the paperwork gods are satisfied. Romania spreads the pain out indefinitely, demanding you return again and again for stamps that logically should have been issued the first time. If you're the kind of person who can tolerate one brutal slog followed by smooth sailing, go to Hanoi. If you'd rather have your frustration distributed in smaller, recurring doses forever, Bucharest awaits, queue number in hand.
