# Bagging Rights: A Tale of Two Tills

🇿🇦 South Africa vs 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country reveals its true character at the till, and I don't mean the big stuff like democracy or healthcare — I mean whether a stranger will pack your shopping bags, or silently judge you for not packing them fast enough yourself. South Africa and the Netherlands have built two of the most philosophically opposed grocery cultures on the planet, and neither nation has the faintest idea how strange it looks to the other.

In South Africa, the supermarket is a small, air-conditioned theatre of service, staffed by packers, car guards, and trolley attendants who treat your shopping trip like a hospitality event with a security detail attached. In the Netherlands, it's a stark lesson in self-sufficiency, where you weigh your own peppers, bag your own bread, and have exact change ready before the cashier finishes scanning your toilet paper. One country wants to help you. The other wants you to help yourself, quickly, and get out of the way.

## Do's & Don'ts

### South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep a few rand coins loose for the car guard and the bag packer | Assume "service with a smile" means it's free — tip accordingly |
| Download the Checkers Sixty60 or Pick n Pay ASAP app before you even unpack your suitcase | Leave your trolley unattended in the parking lot — someone will "watch" it whether you ask or not |
| Compare Woolworths, Checkers and Pick n Pay prices weekly — specials move fast | Expect every branch of the same chain to stock the same thing; branches vary wildly by suburb |
| Bring your own bags or budget a few rand for them at checkout | Walk to a distant car park alone at night with full bags — plan your route, not just your purchases |

### Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Weigh and label your own loose fruit and vegetables before the till | Forget this step — the cashier will send you back, queue and all |
| Carry a Dutch Maestro/debit card or cash | Assume your foreign Visa or Mastercard credit card will work — many tills flatly refuse it |
| Bring your own bag, or budget up to €0.50 for one | Dawdle at the register — bag your own shopping fast or face the sighs of the entire queue |
| Shop around Dirk, Lidl or Aldi for the weekly basics, Albert Heijn for the extras | Expect supermarkets open late on Sundays everywhere, especially outside the big cities |

## South Africa

Walk into a Woolworths, Checkers or Pick n Pay anywhere from Sandton to Stellenbosch and you will be processed, gently, by an entire ecosystem of humans whose job is to make your fifteen-minute grocery run feel like a minor event. Someone will watch your car in the parking lot, unprompted, and expect a few rand for the privilege when you return — not a scam, a genuine, PSIRA-surveyed national institution: roughly 89% of South Africans tip car guards, mostly R2 to R10, per a BusinessTech reader survey and the 2018 PSIRA report. Inside, a packer will bag your groceries without asking, and you'll feel like a monster if you don't tip them too. New arrivals find this baffling, then charming, then — around month three — load-bearing to their entire week.

The chains themselves run a quietly vicious turf war dressed up as loyalty. Checkers has spent the past few years aggressively poaching Pick n Pay and Woolworths' upper-income shoppers with its Sixty60 delivery app and glossy in-store upgrades, while Woolworths holds the premium high ground — think a South African Whole Foods, at Whole Foods prices — and Pick n Pay anchors the middle with its No Name house brand and a Smart Shopper points system so slow to pay out that collecting enough points to redeem feels like a personal finance strategy in itself. Shoprite and Spar round out the value end. Expats quickly learn there's no single "normal" supermarket; there's a hierarchy, and which rung you shop at says something about you, whether you meant it to or not.

Then there's the specials culture, which borders on religious devotion. Weekly catalogues drop, prices swing 19% to 36% on staples like cheese and coffee, and seasoned shoppers plan entire weeks around which chain has toilet paper on special. Layer onto this the very real, very practical safety calculus — smash-and-grabs, parking-lot vigilance, never flashing cash at an ATM beforehand — and the South African supermarket trip stops being an errand and becomes a small, well-rehearsed operation. It is, improbably, one of the friendliest hostile environments you will ever push a trolley through.

## Netherlands

The Dutch supermarket, by contrast, offers you no help, no ceremony, and absolutely no patience for your indecision. You will weigh your own apples on a machine, print your own sticker, and if you forget — and you will forget, at least once, loudly, in front of a queue of visibly irritated Dutch pensioners — you will be sent back to do it properly while everyone behind you recalibrates their opinion of foreigners downward. Expatica's own grocery guide treats this as baseline etiquette, not a quirk: cashiers do not bag for you, so you bag fast, you pay fast, and you move. The till is not a social space. It is a conveyor belt with judgment attached.

Payment brings its own trap: a large share of stores, Albert Heijn included, simply will not take a foreign Visa or Mastercard credit card, so new arrivals without a Dutch Maestro card or cash in hand get the particular humiliation of holding up a line while a cashier explains that the machine has declined them again. DutchReview's 2026 breakdown of the market lays out how locals survive this system financially: Dirk and Lidl for weekly staples, Jumbo and Plus for the middle ground, Albert Heijn reserved for top-ups, all tracked via apps and the weekly Reclamefolder, because — per Consumentenbond's own tracking — shrinkflation is rampant and nobody trusts a steady price anymore.

What expats miss most, per Holland Times' summary of Reddit sentiment and trade data, is variety: thin international aisles, fewer vegetarian substitutes, and an absence of home-country staples — which is why dedicated international supermarkets in the Netherlands have climbed 11% in five years. Specialist shops fill the gaps, a Toko for trassi, Gall & Gall for anything over 15% ABV, turning one errand into three. Add the bike panniers, the bottle-deposit returns, and early closing hours, and you get a system built entirely for people who already know what they're doing.

There is a quieter divide worth naming too: queue psychology. In South Africa, a slow till is a shared grievance — everyone sighs together, a packer might apologise on the cashier's behalf. In the Netherlands, a slow till is a personal failure, yours specifically, and the silence radiating from the trolley behind you is not imagined.

## The Verdict

If you want a supermarket trip that doubles as a small act of community — tipping, chatting, someone watching your car, someone else bagging your bread — South Africa wins outright, and no amount of Dutch efficiency will ever replicate that warmth, because it isn't trying to. If you want to get in, get out, and never make eye contact with another human being, the Netherlands has built the most ruthlessly optimised grocery system in Western Europe, and you will either love it or spend eighteen months silently fuming at a weighing scale.

My honest verdict: South Africa wins on humanity, the Netherlands wins on sheer logistical spite, and both countries would be horrified to shop in the other's supermarket for a week. The Dutch would find South Africa's tipping expectations an affront to their cultural operating system. South Africans would find the Netherlands' till-side silence borderline sociopathic. Neither is wrong. Expats, caught in the middle, simply adapt — and eventually mistake the local system for common sense, which is the surest sign you've actually moved somewhere.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Cape Town — paraphrased: Woolworths has the best produce and feels almost gourmet, but it's genuinely pricey; Pick n Pay and Checkers are reliable workhorses where you can find everything, just without the polish.*

> *Holland Times, summarising Reddit r/Netherlands sentiment — paraphrased: expats repeatedly flag the lack of variety and the thin international aisles in Dutch supermarkets, a complaint Dutch shoppers themselves rarely share, since the stores already stock exactly what they personally need.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Johannesburg — paraphrased: specialty import shops like Thrupp's are worth the markup purely for finding hard-to-get items from home, because the regular chains simply won't carry them.*

## Conclusion

Grocery shopping is the most honest cultural test there is, because nobody performs for the camera at the till — they just reveal, trolley by trolley, what they actually value. South Africa values the human chain around the transaction: the guard, the packer, the shared sigh at a slow queue. The Netherlands values the transaction itself, stripped of ornament, weighed to the gram and paid for in exact change. Neither system is broken. Both are, in their own way, perfectly rational responses to the countries that built them.

What nobody tells you before you move is that you'll eventually defend whichever system you landed in, fiercely, as though you invented it yourself. Give it a year. You'll be explaining car-guard tipping etiquette to a bewildered Dutch cousin, or lecturing a South African friend on the sanctity of weighing your own peppers. That's not integration. That's Stockholm syndrome with a loyalty card.
