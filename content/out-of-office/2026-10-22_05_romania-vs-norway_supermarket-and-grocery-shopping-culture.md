# One Country Will Haggle You Over a Kilo of Tomatoes, the Other Will Make You Drive to Sweden for a Bottle of Wine

🇷🇴 Romania vs 🇳🇴 Norway

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Grocery shopping is the single most honest cultural audit you can perform on a country, because nobody is performing for you. Nobody fixes their face for the till the way they fix it for a dinner party. In Romania, that honesty looks like a woman in a headscarf refusing to let you leave her stall without "just one more" bunch of dill, thrown in free, because apparently you look like you need feeding. In Norway, it looks like a cashier at Kiwi scanning your four items — bread, milk, a block of Jarlsberg, one depressed-looking cucumber — and the total arriving somewhere in the 220-kroner range, while your face does something involuntary.

I have now bought groceries in both, repeatedly, under duress, and I can report that these two countries have built, from the same basic human need to eat, two entirely different civilizations. Romania still runs on relationship, haggling, and a produce aisle that smells like actual dirt. Norway runs on regulation, rationing-by-price, and a Sunday so sacred the supermarkets themselves observe it like a day of mourning. Neither one is quaint. Both are, in their own way, slightly insane.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Romania:**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic numbers in Romanian before you hit the piață — bargaining is smoother in the local tongue | Expect fixed, printed prices at a produce stall; everything is a conversation |
| Bring your own bags and small cash to both markets and Mega Image, Profi, or Kaufland | Assume a hypermarket on the edge of town has the same prices as the city-centre Lidl |
| Ask vendors which fruit is actually in season — they will tell you, bluntly | Buy tomatoes in January and expect them to taste like anything |
| Enjoy the ritual of greeting a stallholder by name once you're a regular | Rush the transaction — haggling without small talk reads as rude |

**Norway:**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stock up before Sunday — nearly everything, including most supermarkets, is shut by law | Wander into a town expecting a Sunday grocery run to save you |
| Bring ID everywhere — Vinmonopolet checks age hard for anything over 4.7% ABV | Expect to buy wine, spirits, or strong beer at a normal supermarket till |
| Compare Rema 1000, Kiwi, and Coop prices — they genuinely differ on staples | Be shocked when a basic basket costs double what it did at home |
| Consider a Sweden border run if you live near Kongsvinger or Strömstad | Assume "harryhandel" border shopping is some fringe eccentricity — it's a national pastime |

## Romania

Romania's grocery culture is a country arguing with itself in real time, and it is glorious to watch. On one side: the piață, the open-air or covered market that has existed in some form since before anyone currently alive was born, where a grandmother will sell you peppers she grew herself and will absolutely remember if you were rude to her last Tuesday. On the other: an extremely aggressive, extremely fast hypermarket boom, with Kaufland, Lidl, Profi, and Mega Image multiplying across the country so quickly that Romania-Insider has reported modern trade — the supermarkets and hypermarkets — now accounts for more than half of all retail, and Digi24 has reported traditional piețe losing so much ground that supermarkets alone now capture around 40% of the national food budget.

This is not a quiet transition. It's a tug-of-war, and both sides are winning something. The piață survives because Romanians, bless them, still care whether their tomatoes have flavor, and because the ritual of the market — the haggling, the free herbs thrown in "for the house," the vendor who insists her cheese is better than her neighbor's and is, infuriatingly, correct — is not something a scanner at self-checkout can replicate. Meanwhile Kaufland and Lidl keep opening stores at a clip that would make a Western European planning board weep, because convenience, refrigeration, and a receipt you can return something with are genuinely appealing when you're juggling a job and a commute.

What foreigners get wrong, almost universally, is assuming one of these is "the real Romania" and the other is an embarrassing modern intrusion. Wrong on both counts. The piață is not a tourist museum piece — locals use it weekly because it's often cheaper and better for produce. The hypermarket is not a betrayal of tradition — it's where the same people buy everything else. The trick, which expat.com forum threads confirm newcomers take months to learn, is figuring out which items belong where: meat and dairy from the supermarket chain you trust, vegetables from whichever stallholder has decided to adopt you. Treat it as one system with two counters, not a referendum on authenticity.

## Norway

Norway's grocery culture can be summarized in one sentence: the state has decided you should drink and eat less, and it has built an entire retail infrastructure to make that decision for you whether you like it or not. Vinmonopolet, the state alcohol monopoly, is the purest expression of this — a chain of government-run shops with limited hours, hard age checks (18 for anything under 22% ABV, 20 above it, per Vinmonopolet's own published rules), and zero competition, because nowhere else in the country can legally sell you a bottle of wine or spirits. Beer under 4.7% sneaks into ordinary supermarkets, which is the only mercy on offer.

Then there are the hours. Norwegian shop-opening law has, for decades, kept most retail — supermarkets very much included — closed on Sundays, a rule that has been debated, tweaked, and litigated (The Local has covered the periodic "should we overhaul Sunday closing" arguments) but which still, broadly, holds. You learn to shop on Saturday with the grim efficiency of someone provisioning for a siege, because Sunday will offer you nothing but a closed Kiwi and your own poor planning.

And then there's the price, which is the part nobody warns you about loudly enough. Norway has been repeatedly flagged — including by Lifeinnorway.net — as having among the most expensive groceries in Europe, a fact so well internalized by Norwegians themselves that Statistics Norway has literally tracked "grensehandel," the cross-border shopping trips Norwegians take into Sweden specifically to buy cheaper meat, alcohol, and sweets, as its own official economic statistic. When your own national statistics agency has a dedicated category for "citizens driving to a different country to buy groceries," you have built something structurally strange. Rema 1000, Kiwi, and Coop compete fiercely on price precisely because everyone is this aware of how much they're being charged.

## The Verdict

If you're asking which country treats grocery shopping as a genuine pleasure, Romania wins, no contest — a piață visit is a social event with a side of vegetables, and even the hypermarkets are cheap enough that nobody's doing trigonometry over a basket of cheese. If you're asking which country has built the more fascinating, more self-aware, more quietly furious retail system, that's Norway, hands down — a nation so committed to controlling its population's vices and spending that it tracks, by law, how many of its own citizens flee across a land border for cheaper bacon. Romania will feed you better for less money and make you feel human while doing it. Norway will hand you a receipt that reads like a threat and a Vinmonopolet opening-hours schedule you'll need to memorize like scripture. I know which fridge I'd rather stock. I also know which country's till receipts I'd frame and sell as modern art.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/Norway — newcomers routinely post photos of basic grocery receipts, stunned that four normal items cleared 200 kroner without a single luxury product in the bag.*

> *Internations Oslo community — longtime expats describe the specific panic of realizing, mid-Sunday, that every grocery store and the Vinmonopolet are closed, and that nothing short of a gas station will save the dinner plan.*

> *expat.com Romania forum — first-time piață shoppers ask, repeatedly, whether it's rude to simply pay the sticker price, because the vendors seem almost offended when nobody tries to negotiate.*

## Conclusion

Here is the uncomfortable truth underneath all of this: both countries have built their grocery systems around something they value more than convenience, and both systems will frustrate you precisely because that value isn't yours. Romania prizes relationship and freshness over speed, so you wait, you chat, you occasionally get overruled on how many onions you "actually need." Norway prizes control and equality over abundance, so you plan, you stockpile, you accept that your Friday-night wine run is a government-scheduled event. Neither is exotic — that word is for people who've never had to actually feed themselves somewhere unfamiliar. They're just two entirely rational answers to the same dull Tuesday problem, built by people who care about different things than you do. Go shop in both before you decide whose logic you prefer. Bring cash for one and a calendar for the other.
