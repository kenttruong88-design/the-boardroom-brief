# Canada Gives You a Health Card and No Doctor. China Gives You a Doctor and Asks Who Is Paying for Dinner

🇨🇦 Canada vs 🇨🇳 China

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every relocation brochure promises "excellent healthcare" in the way a restaurant promises "fresh ingredients": technically true, wildly misleading. In Canada the system is public, principled and, for a growing number of residents, a waiting room with ambitions. In China it is a sprawling, loud, astonishingly fast machine that works superbly if you have a translator, a deposit and a friend to sleep in a plastic chair beside your bed.

The two systems fail in opposite directions. One makes you wait for access. The other gives you access and makes you do the admin. Knowing which kind of suffering suits you is the entire decision, and nobody in HR will say it out loud.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a provincial family-doctor waitlist the week you land | Assume a health card equals a doctor |
| Check your province's rules on any initial waiting period | Skip private cover for drugs, dental and eyes |
| Bring a book to a walk-in clinic | Plan a day around seeing only one clinician |
| Use the provincial health line before going to the ER | Treat every ailment as an emergency |

**China**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn enough Chinese or bring an interpreter | Walk into a local hospital expecting English |
| Carry proof of insurance and a deposit | Assume treatment starts before payment |
| Use the international wards if your employer's plan covers them | Be surprised when people enter the exam room mid-consultation |
| Arrange a friend or colleague to stay with you if admitted | Expect hospital meals or full nursing |

## Canada

Canadian healthcare is a paradox wearing a maple leaf. The coverage is universal, the principle is admirable, and the practical experience of finding a family doctor has become a national sport. WelcomeAide's March 2026 guide for newcomers is blunt about it: popular walk-in clinics can take one to three hours, non-urgent ER visits can run four to eight hours or more, and your provincial health card, free to use at a walk-in, may land you with a different doctor every time. The Expat Arrivals guide to Ottawa notes that tens of thousands of residents there lack a primary-care provider at all, and that most locals carry private cover, usually through an employer, because the public plan excludes prescriptions, dentistry and optometry.

The rules also vary by province, which is a Canadian way of saying "good luck". Alberta usually has no wait. British Columbia's plan starts on the first day of the third month after you establish residence. Quebec may impose up to three months. On Ontario, the sources I read flatly disagree about whether the three-month wait still exists, which tells you everything about how well-advertised these rules are. Take out private cover for the gap, wherever you land.

The stories from patients are the part brochures omit. In an r/canada thread about people diagnosed in emergency rooms, one user said he waited eleven years for a family doctor and had testicular cancer found in the ER. Another got a one-week wait for a haematology referral, gave up, went to emergency, spent about six hours in the waiting room plus another six on a hallway bed, and left with a leukaemia diagnosis. A third had been trying to get an Ontario family doctor for four years. Someone who moved provinces in 2021 got no doctor until this year and had a neurology appointment by phone with a physician five hours away. These are Canadians rather than expats, and Reddit is not a clinical trial. But a system that produces this many near-identical anecdotes has a pattern, not a rumour.

Be fair to it. The same threads include praise for an ER that diagnosed leukaemia the same day. Care, once you are inside, is competent and often excellent. The problem is the door.

## China

China's problem is the opposite. The door is wide open, the queue is a wall of people, and nobody will hold your hand once you are through it. The Expat Exchange guide, sponsored by an insurer and read with that in mind, says bluntly that it is imperative to speak Chinese or bring someone who does. International wards exist, with interpreters, in places such as Peking University Hospital Shenzhen, but the wider system runs in Mandarin and expects you to keep up. Privacy is an optional extra: people may walk into exam rooms without warning.

Ikky in China, a practical guide from June 2024, describes a national system covering roughly ninety percent of the population, with community centres, specialist institutions and hospitals, and warns that hospitals may demand proof of insurance or a deposit before they will treat you. The multilingual United Family hospitals in Beijing and Shanghai are the safe choice for anyone with a corporate plan.

What the guides understate is how cheap and how fast it can be. An American on Expat.com described being admitted in January 2023 with walking pneumonia, then a blood clot after two weeks in bed, at a brand-new hospital not set up for foreigners. The uninsured bill came to about $750 for two weeks, excluding medication, and medication cost roughly the same. The catch: a companion was needed around the clock in the first week and from six in the morning until six in the evening in the second, because meals were not provided and nurses mainly checked vitals and changed IV drips. The writer, who estimated the US bill at around $30,000, called the experience very positive. Another member in a small town said care there was as good as or better than in Chengdu, but that it took about three years of study before they could attend hospital alone.

Costs reported on the Expat Exchange guide are almost comical to anyone from Toronto: a mole removal with a biopsy for about 1,200 yuan, three crowns and a root canal in Xi'an for $1,200 in total, and a month of diabetes, blood pressure and cholesterol medication for about 400 yuan. Speed, price and access are real. So is the need for a human being willing to sleep in a chair.

A word on logistics, because they matter more than the headline. The Ikky guide stresses that Chinese hospitals expect payment or proof of insurance up front, and the same Expat Exchange guide reports that six of the seven women one expat knew had delivered by Caesarean, with English-speaking maternity staff at the VIP Birthing Center in Guangzhou and Shanghai East International in Pudong. Canada, by contrast, asks you to pay in waiting rather than in deposits. Either way you are buying a different set of risks, so choose which risk you can live with before you sign the lease.

## The Verdict

Canada is the better system if you never get sick. China is the better system if you do, provided you are well prepared. That is not a joke. It is the least comfortable sentence in this article.

In Canada your health is guaranteed in principle and rationed in practice. In China your health is purchasable in practice and rationed only by your language skills and your bank card. If you are young and healthy, Canada will cost you little and ask for nothing. If you need a scan next Tuesday, a Chinese hospital will give you one and a bill the size of a pair of trainers.

My pick, for a family relocating on a corporate plan with international cover, is China, but only if you have built the support network in advance. For a solo traveller with no insurance and no Mandarin, it is Canada, and may the odds be ever in your favour.

## Conclusion

Both countries will keep you alive. Neither will make it painless. One makes you wait, one makes you pay, and both reward the people who read the rules before they get sick.

So do the boring thing. Register for the doctor waitlist, buy the insurance, find the interpreter, pack the charger. A relocation is a gamble, and health is the one table where you should never play without a chip stack.

Then go and enjoy the country. That is, after all, what the healthcare is for.
