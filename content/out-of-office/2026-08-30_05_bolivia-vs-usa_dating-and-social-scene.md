# One Country Dates Through a Chaperone System With Extra Steps, the Other Dates Through a Subscription Service

🇧🇴 Bolivia vs 🇺🇸 USA — Dating and Social Scene

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Bolivian dating still runs largely on family involvement, group outings, and a courtship pace that would strike most Americans as either charmingly old-fashioned or maddeningly slow, depending on how many swipes they've already burned that week. American dating, meanwhile, has been almost entirely absorbed by apps — a system so thoroughly gamified that an entire generation now finds meeting someone the old-fashioned way, at a bar, on a train, in a bookshop, to be borderline suspicious behaviour.

I have been taken to meet a Bolivian family's entire extended household on what I genuinely believed was still date number two, complete with grandmother's assessment of my table manners. I have also matched with someone in New York, exchanged four hundred text messages over three weeks, and met them in person exactly once before the conversation quietly evaporated into the algorithmic void. Only one of these processes ended with me being fed.

## Do's & Don'ts: Bolivia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to meet the family relatively early if things get serious — it's the norm, not a red flag | Rush physical intimacy — courtship here tends to move at a genuinely slower pace |
| Join group outings (grupos de amigos) — much early dating happens within a friend circle | Assume dating apps dominate outside major cities — in-person introductions still lead |
| Show up properly dressed for a date, even a casual one — effort is noticed and appreciated | Be surprised by chaperoned or group first dates, especially in smaller cities |
| Be patient with a slower relationship timeline — commitment is taken seriously before it's declared | Publicly flaunt a new relationship before it's been introduced to close family and friends |

## Do's & Don'ts: USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect most dating to start on an app, even in smaller cities and towns | Assume a match automatically leads to a real conversation — ghosting is a widely accepted norm |
| Be direct about intentions early — Americans generally appreciate clarity over ambiguity | Expect family involvement early — many relationships stay private for months |
| Suggest a specific, low-pressure activity for a first date — coffee, a walk, a casual drink | Take "let's hang out sometime" as a firm commitment — it frequently isn't |
| Use "DTR" (define the relationship) conversations — they're a genuinely normalised American ritual | Be shocked by "situationships" — a semi-official relationship category with real cultural staying power |

## Bolivia: Family First, Apps a Distant Second

Bolivian dating culture, particularly outside the largest cities, still runs substantially through social and family networks rather than through an app's algorithm. Meeting someone through a shared friend group, a family event, or a neighbourhood connection remains the dominant pathway into a relationship, and the pace once a connection is made tends to be markedly slower and more deliberate than American dating norms — early relationships are often conducted partly in groups, with friends and cousins folded into outings that an American dater would consider strictly one-on-one territory.

Family involvement arrives earlier and carries more weight here than most Western daters expect. Bringing a partner home to meet parents, aunts, and grandparents isn't reserved for a serious, months-deep commitment — it can happen relatively early, and is generally read as a sign of genuine intent rather than a terrifying escalation. This has a real stabilising effect on the culture: because family opinion matters, relationships that progress tend to be taken seriously by both people involved from a fairly early stage, and the casual, low-commitment "situationship" so common in American dating has much less cultural traction here.

Urban centres like La Paz and Santa Cruz have absorbed dating apps into the mix over the past decade, particularly among younger, more internationally connected Bolivians, but even there the apps tend to function as an introduction mechanism rather than a full replacement for social dating — matches are more likely to lead quickly to a group setting or a family-adjacent context than to an extended, app-only text relationship. Presentation and effort also matter more visibly here than in much of the anglophone dating world: showing up underdressed or visibly disengaged for even a casual date reads as a real lack of interest, not quirky authenticity.

## USA: Swipe First, Ask Questions Never

American dating has undergone one of the most complete technological transformations of any major social ritual in the developed world — dating apps are now the dominant, often default starting point for new relationships across nearly every age group and region, to the point that meeting someone organically, without a shared app match preceding it, has taken on a slightly nostalgic, almost quaint quality among younger Americans. This shift has brought real efficiency — a much wider pool of potential partners, filterable by nearly any preference — alongside a set of genuinely new social pathologies that didn't exist a generation ago: ghosting (disappearing from a conversation with zero explanation) has become so normalised it barely registers as rude anymore, and "situationships" — a deliberately undefined, semi-committed relationship status — have become a recognised, almost institutional category rather than a temporary awkward phase.

American dating culture does retain a countervailing directness that surprises visitors from more indirect cultures: the "DTR" conversation — explicitly asking a partner to define the relationship — is a normalised, expected ritual rather than an anxiety-inducing taboo, and stating your intentions clearly, even bluntly, is generally read as mature rather than presumptuous. This directness, though, coexists uneasily with the ambiguity the apps have introduced; you can have a startlingly honest conversation about relationship expectations with someone you've been talking to for exactly six days.

Family involvement, by contrast, tends to arrive much later in American relationships than in Bolivia — meeting parents is frequently treated as a significant, sometimes anxiety-inducing milestone reserved for a relationship that's already been privately established for months, a sequencing that would strike a Bolivian family as almost secretive.

## The Verdict

Bolivia's dating culture is slower, more communal, and more thoroughly integrated with family from the outset, producing relationships that feel more deliberate but come loaded with real social scrutiny from day one. America's is faster, more individualistic, and radically more efficient at generating first dates, at the direct cost of a commitment culture that's become genuinely allergic to labels. Bolivia wins on depth and intention. America wins on sheer volume of options, for whatever that's actually worth to you at 11pm on a Tuesday.

## Conclusion

Bolivia offers a dating culture that still assumes a relationship is a family and community event from fairly early on, slower but arguably sturdier for it. America offers unmatched efficiency and choice, at the cost of a commitment vocabulary that's had to invent entirely new, deliberately vague words just to describe what's actually happening between two people. Go to Bolivia to meet the grandmother early. Go to America and just try, for once, to get a clear answer to a text.
