# Karachi Gives You a Park With a Gate and Britain Gives You a Park With Weather

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

A park is the most honest piece of public property a city owns. It tells you who the city expects to spend a Sunday there, whether they are allowed to bring a dog, and how much they trust each other with a bench. Pakistan and the United Kingdom have a deep, tangled history, a large shared diaspora and a pair of utterly different attitudes to a patch of grass.

I have sat in a Karachi park at sunset watching three generations of one family eat from foil containers, and I have sat on a damp bench in a British park eating a sausage roll in a drizzle with a stranger who said nothing and meant everything. Both were perfect. Neither was the same.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇰 Pakistan ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go in the evening, when the heat breaks and families arrive | Plan a midday stroll in the dusty season |
| Bring food and share it, because picnicking is the point | Show up with a single sandwich and a laptop |
| Use the jogging tracks in the larger parks | Assume every park is open to every activity |
| Check if the park allows dogs before you bring one | Expect most parks to be dog-friendly |

| 🇬🇧 United Kingdom ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry a waterproof, even in the sun | Trust a blue sky before 11am |
| Say hello to dog walkers, because they will say it first | Treat a dog on a lead as an invitation to pet it |
| Take your rubbish home or find a bin | Leave a barbecue mess on the grass |
| Use the free parks as your living room in summer | Expect to pay for a lawn |

## 🇵🇰 Pakistan

According to Culture Trip, Karachi has parks that work as an oasis in a dusty, dry city. Bin Qasim Park covers 130 acres and overlooks a 90-metre-high Port Fountain. Nisar Shaheed Park spans 26 acres with a one-kilometre jogging track. Aziz Bhatti Park covers eight acres and has a natural lake, and Safari Park, which dates from 1970, lets you drive a car inside it. That last detail tells you everything about the national relationship with green space: it is a place where the whole family goes together, usually by vehicle, and stays until it is dark.

Expat Exchange describes parks in Pakistan as a popular destination for families, who picnic, jog and play sport. The word to underline is families. This is not a solo activity or a quiet retreat. It is a social event with food, relatives, children running between benches and someone's uncle in charge of the barbecue. The park is the living room that most flats cannot supply.

There are caveats. Public green space in a megacity under pressure is hard to maintain, and not all of it is welcoming. Culture Trip notes that Hill Park in Karachi is dog-friendly, and adds, sadly, that most parks in the city are not. That is a quiet but telling detail, because dog ownership is one of the things that expats from Britain miss first.

Climate also shapes the park. In the heat, daylight hours are for staying in, and the evening is for coming out. A park at six in the evening is a different place from the same park at noon. The culture of the park is the culture of the cool hour, which is worth remembering if you are new in town and wondering why the place is empty at lunchtime.

There is a practical side expats should know about. Facilities such as jogging tracks, fountains and lakes make larger Karachi parks into proper day trips, and many open late into the evening because that is when people can actually use them. Food vendors, ice-cream carts and balloon sellers gather at the gates. It is a mini-economy built around leisure, and the park is its marketplace as much as its lawn.

## 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

Britain has a more relaxed legal relationship with its green space. Most urban parks are free, open and used by everyone, from joggers and dog walkers to students with a bottle of something cheap on a warm Friday. A park is a civic right, something a town council provides because a Victorian once decided that workers deserved fresh air. You do not pay, you do not book, and you are rarely told off for sitting down.

The British park is also a social leveller, at least in intent. A pensioner feeding the ducks and a banker running a lunchtime loop share the same path and a very British nod. Dogs are everywhere and are the major social lubricant. Owners who would never speak to each other in a lift will happily exchange life stories in the name of their spaniels.

The weather runs the whole operation. A British summer park day is a gamble. The sun comes out and the entire population appears on the lawn, half-dressed and wildly optimistic, as if the sky might change its mind. By four it has changed its mind, and everyone goes home or to the pub. That quick turn from tragedy to comedy is the British outdoor experience.

Raja Omer Shabbir, writing in the Daily Times in 2020 about living as a minority abroad, says that after moving to the UK he found English counted for little in socialising. He does not mention parks, but the point carries over. A park is where British social rules show up without a script. Nobody tells you how to behave. You work it out by watching the person with the dog.

British parks come with their own small institutions: the bandstand, the park run on Saturday morning, the café run by a very serious woman who will not be rushed, the pond with its self-appointed duck committee. Beyond the city, Britain also has an unusual network of footpaths that let walkers cross farmland and moor, which turns the whole countryside into a long, wet public park. For a newcomer from a megacity short on open space, that is a quiet revelation, and it is the thing most British-Pakistani families I know say they would miss.

## The Verdict

I will say Pakistan, with a footnote the size of a municipal budget. A park that has family at its core, with three generations, a picnic and a place to run, is a better idea of public space than a lawn people merely walk across. The Karachi model gets people together, and that is the whole point of a park.

The UK wins on access and on the quiet, democratic sense that green space belongs to everyone. It also wins on dogs, which is not a minor thing. You can walk a dog in nearly any park in Britain, and in Karachi, per Culture Trip, you mostly cannot.

But I am picking the country where the park is a party, because a bench in the drizzle is lovely, and a family picnic in the cool of the evening is something else. One is an act of solitude. The other is an act of belonging. If you have to move to a country and choose how you spend a Sunday, choose the one with the food.

## Conclusion

A park does not care what passport you carry. It cares whether you will use it kindly. In Pakistan, that means bringing food and family and waiting for the cool hour. In the UK, that means a waterproof, a nod to the dog walker and the patience to wait for the sun.

So move with both in mind. Take the evening picnic habit with you wherever you go. Learn the British art of enjoying a patch of grass in the rain without complaint. And if you find a park in either country that is empty and beautiful, sit down quietly and tell nobody, because that is how a good park becomes a crowded one.
