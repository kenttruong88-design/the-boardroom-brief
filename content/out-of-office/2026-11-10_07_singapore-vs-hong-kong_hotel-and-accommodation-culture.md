# Singapore Gives You a Room. Hong Kong Gives You a Cupboard and Charges You for the View

🇸🇬 Singapore vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two city-states, two harbours, two skylines that look like someone lost an argument with a tall building. They also happen to be the two places in Asia where a business traveller can burn through a monthly rent in a single week of hotel stays and call it a conference. In Singapore, the hotel is a polished machine with a lobby that smells of orchids and a spreadsheet. In Hong Kong, the hotel is a negotiation between your luggage and the available square footage.

It is tempting to treat them as interchangeable. They are not, and the difference is in the experience of the room itself. Singapore sells smoothness. Hong Kong sells density, and then asks you to admire how well it has been packed. The sourcing for this one is partly dated, so I will note the age of each figure and let you adjust your outrage accordingly.

**Singapore**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |

| Compare total trip cost, not only the room rate | Forget that Singapore's average daily rate hit $314 in early 2024 |
| Treat the hotel as a place to work as well as sleep | Plan on a sprawling bargain; space is not cheap |

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|

## Singapore: The Hotel as Efficient Appliance

According to the Skift piece from September 2024, Singapore's hotel occupancy in the first quarter of that year was 77%, against 73% for Hong Kong, and its average daily rate reached $314. The article describes Singapore's luxury hotel sector as having record-breaking revenue per available room, though it gives no figure, and, citing a Global Asset Solutions report, positions the city for high-profile events and luxury travellers. That is the pitch: a place that has decided to be expensive and excellent at once.

Back in 2017, ECA International data reported by TTG Asia ranked Singapore the fourth most expensive location in Asia-Pacific for business travel, with a typical business trip costing US$472 a day excluding travel to and from the city, covering a four-star hotel, meals, laundry, transport and incidentals. That study is nine years old and should not be used for budgeting, but the shape of it matters: Singapore's costs were driven heavily by the hotel itself, because without the hotel it dropped to seventh in the regional ranking.

What this tells the relocating executive is that the room is the main event. Singapore's hotels tend to be consistent in quality and polished in service, and you pay for the consistency. The city is compact and logical, so the hotel works as a base rather than a destination. If you arrive with a laptop and a suit, you can function; if you arrive with opinions about square footage, you may be disappointed.

The real lesson from the cost studies, dated as they are, is that a hotel bill is a proxy for a city's whole economy. Rooms are expensive where land is scarce, labour is skilled and demand is stubborn. Both cities tick every box, which is why a trip to either feels like paying a membership fee for being in the right place at the right time. The business traveller's consolation is that the expense account is somebody else's problem. The relocating executive, who will be paying for the temporary accommodation while the flat is arranged, has a different view.

## Hong Kong: The Art of Fitting Everything Into Nothing

Hong Kong's hotels carry the burden of the city's most famous constraint: land. The Skift article I read does not give room sizes, and my attempt to retrieve a Frommer's accommodation guide failed, so I will not pretend to a precision I cannot support. What the material does say is more interesting. Hong Kong's occupancy in the first quarter of 2024 was 73%, and a Global Asset Solutions report cited by Skift characterises the market as having a softer corporate segment and more budget-conscious visitors than Singapore.

Historically, though, Hong Kong has been the expensive one. The 2017 TTG Asia study found Hong Kong ranked second most expensive in Asia-Pacific for business travel, behind only Tokyo, and that its hotel costs were 13% higher than Singapore's. Singapore's average business trip cost was 8% cheaper than Hong Kong's. Shanghai's hotel rates, for comparison, were 32% lower than Hong Kong's. The picture was of a city that made you pay for every metre, and that is a reputation that sticks long after the data ages.

For the practical reader, the lesson is to treat any fixed belief about which city is pricier as a hypothesis. Data from 2017 says Hong Kong, a softer market in 2024 may have changed the picture, and I cannot give you a verified current comparison from these pages. What does not change is the experience of arriving: a hotel in Hong Kong is a vertical object in a dense district, a place where you can step out of the lift and into a street that has not slept since Thursday. You are not buying a room. You are buying the position.

One more thing, and it is a matter of temperament rather than fact. Singapore's hotels invite you to relax into their efficiency, and Hong Kong's invite you to ignore them and go outside. If you are a person who likes to return to a calm, well-ordered room after a long day, Singapore will feel like a favour. If you are a person who regards a hotel room as the place between meals, Hong Kong will feel like freedom. Know which of those you are before you book, and do not pretend otherwise to your assistant.

## The Verdict

Singapore wins for the stay; Hong Kong wins for the location. If you want to sleep well, work calmly and be served by a system that appears to have been designed by someone who reads manuals for pleasure, book Singapore. If you want to be in the middle of an overwhelming city, accept a smaller room, and think of the hotel as a charging station, book Hong Kong.

I will say this plainly: the old assumption that Hong Kong is simply the pricey one is out of date enough that you should check before you assume it. The 2017 gap of 13% on hotel costs has presumably moved, and a market with 73% occupancy and a softer corporate segment is not one that can dictate terms as confidently as it once did. Shop around. Negotiate. And if a room feels small, know that it was probably always going to.

## Conclusion

Hotels are where a city shows you its priorities without having to say them aloud. Singapore's say: we have anticipated your needs and billed you in advance. Hong Kong's say: you are here, which is what matters, and the rest is a rounding error.

Choose the one that matches your mood and your expense policy. Then bring a good book, because in either city, the thing you will end up looking at for longest is the ceiling.
