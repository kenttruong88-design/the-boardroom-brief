# India Will Carry Your Bags and Your Family Tree, and Czechia Will Charge You for the Towel Rail

🇮🇳 India vs 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

An Indian hotel is a performance of service. Doors are opened, bags are lifted, tea appears from nowhere, and a man whose name you will never learn knows your room number, your breakfast order and possibly your mother's maiden name. Hospitality is not a department. It is a personality trait, and it will be applied to you with great force whether you asked for it or not.

Czechia is not unhelpful, but it is a different philosophy. The Czech hotel is a quieter, more transactional affair, with a pension on every corner, a plain breakfast buffet and a receptionist whose warmth is directly proportional to how well you pronounced the street name. Moving between the two is like switching from a brass band to a string quartet, and the second one is not going to take your suitcase.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**India**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Tip porters and housekeeping generously | Treat tipping as optional |
| Ask for what you need, politely and clearly | Expect staff to say no directly |
| Check the room before accepting it | Take the first room offered without a look |
| Embrace the chatty, helpful front desk | Rush a conversation that is clearly a gift |

**Czechia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether the price is per room or per person | Assume a headline price covers everyone |
| Confirm air conditioning is in your own room | Assume it works in August |
| Learn a few Czech greetings | Expect staff to speak your language |
| Consider a family-run pension for value | Ignore anything without a star rating |

## India

Indian hotels, from a grand palace to a budget lodge, are built on the idea that the guest is a visitor to be looked after. The phrase is cliché and also true: the culture of atithi, the guest as a near-divine presence, runs deeper in hospitality than any training manual. Staff are numerous, available and attentive, and a hotel without someone to carry your bag would be considered a shabby operation.

The upside is comfort. You rarely have to do anything for yourself. Laundry is collected, breakfast is made to order, and a request for a missing adaptor produces three adaptors and an apology for the inconvenience. For a business traveller, the system is a small marvel, and many expats arriving from colder, more self-service cultures find it both luxurious and faintly embarrassing.

The catch is that the service has its own grammar. Direct refusal is uncomfortable, so a polite yes may mean maybe, and a maybe may mean no. The sophisticated visitor learns to read the wobble in a head tilt. Tipping is an expected part of the arrangement, and the staff's generosity of attention usually rests on a sensible expectation of reward. Rooms may not match the photographs, and a quick inspection before accepting is normal, not rude.

There is also variety. Indian accommodation ranges from heritage palaces to boutique homestays to humble guesthouses with an owner who will feed you dinner and insist you are family. Expats relocating from India are often shocked, not by any one thing in Europe, but by the cumulative absence of fuss. Nobody is waiting to help. That absence is called efficiency, and it is a hard adjustment for people raised on service.

## Czechia

Czech accommodation is practical. Prague has everything from grand historic hotels to modest pensions to apartment rentals, and the sector is well geared towards tourism, but the style is cooler. A guide published by Living Prague, written by an English expat, offers the kind of advice you will not find in a brochure. Check whether prices are quoted per room or per person, because a bargain "per person" can double. Expect roughly a thousand crowns more per night for each additional star. Be sceptical about the word "deluxe," which may mean around ten percent more space. And make sure any air conditioning is in your own room and individually controlled, rather than a vague promise about the building.

That is a perfect summary of the Czech approach: precise, a little sceptical, and unwilling to oversell. A pension is often a small, family-run place with simple rooms, a decent breakfast and a host who will be courteous without being theatrical. Staff in tourist areas are used to foreign guests, but the service culture is less effusive. You are a customer, not a visiting dignitary.

For Indian travellers, the practical question is food. An old thread on the MyCzechRepublic forum, started in 2006 by a couple from Asia, asked about affordable rooms, vegetarian dishes and halal food. The answers were frank for their time: Asian restaurants are common, a strictly vegetarian restaurant was one of the few, and halal was said to be nearly impossible. Twenty years later, the picture is far better, and a 2026 blog aimed at Indian travellers describes staying in Vinohrady near Náměstí Míru and recommends several vegetarian places, with a tip to learn a Czech phrase for "I'm vegetarian; no meat, fish or broth." The page reads like marketing, so verify details.

Czechia is also friendlier on price than Western Europe, and a good pension in a central district is a sensible choice if you do not need the theatre of a five-star lobby.

## The Verdict

India wins on service, and it is not close. Nobody does welcome like an Indian hotel at its best, and the sense of being genuinely looked after is something most Western chains have forgotten how to produce. The first night in a Czech pension, you will notice the silence, and you may miss the fuss.

Czechia wins on honesty and value. The price is the price, the room is the room, and there is a charming absence of pretence. You pay for what you get and get what you pay for, and a good family-run pension can feel more human than a hotel with forty uniformed staff.

I give the title to India for hospitality and the practical points to Czechia. If I am sick, tired or arriving at midnight, I want the Indian hotel. If I want a quiet bed, a decent breakfast and no one asking about my plans, I want the Czech one. Choose your mood, and book accordingly.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Living Prague accommodation guide — an English expat warns that prices are usually quoted per room, so a "per person" offer deserves suspicion, and that each extra star adds roughly a thousand crowns a night.*

> *MyCzechRepublic forum (2006) — a couple from Asia asking about vegetarian and halal food were told that strictly vegetarian restaurants were rare and halal almost impossible, advice that is two decades old and thankfully outdated.*

> *AllBlogs, Indian traveller guide (2026) — a first-person account of staying in Vinohrady suggests learning one Czech phrase explaining that you eat no meat, fish or broth, because "vegetarian" alone may still arrive with bacon.*

## Conclusion

Hospitality is a cultural language, and you only hear how loud it is when you move to a quieter country. India says welcome with its entire staff. Czechia says it with a tidy room, a fair bill and a nod at breakfast.

Pack both expectations. Tip your Indian porter like you mean it, and read your Czech booking confirmation like a contract. Learn the phrase for vegetarian. Check the air conditioning. And if you ever feel lonely in a very neat Prague pension, remember that somewhere in India, a man is carrying a stranger's suitcase up three flights of stairs for the pure joy of being useful.
