# One Country Freezes You Solid, the Other Slow-Cooks You

🇫🇮 Finland vs 🇸🇧 Solomon Islands — Weather and How Locals Cope

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country insists its weather builds character. Finland builds character through darkness and cold so total it reorganises your entire personality around vitamin D supplements. The Solomon Islands builds character through humidity so relentless that "dry" becomes a season you remember fondly rather than one you're currently experiencing. Both places have figured out how to live inside genuinely difficult climates. Only one of them has invented an entire national mood around it.

I have shivered through a February in Helsinki that made me question the structural integrity of my own bones, and I have sweated through a Honiara afternoon that made me question why anyone invented long trousers. Climate, it turns out, is the one thing you truly cannot negotiate your way around, no matter how good your visa is.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Finland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a proper winter coat before you land, not after your first frostbitten commute | Assume "layer up" is optional advice rather than survival instruction |
| Book a light therapy lamp alongside your apartment | Schedule anything important for the darkest weeks of December without a backup plan |
| Learn to love sauna culture — it's not optional, it's infrastructure | Underestimate how disorienting perpetual summer daylight is either |
| Respect the ice — studded boots aren't a fashion choice | Compare Finnish winter to "a bit of snow back home" out loud |

**Solomon Islands**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan outdoor work for early morning before the humidity peaks | Expect air conditioning everywhere — plan around fans and shade instead |
| Track the wet season (November–April) when scheduling anything outdoors | Assume "hot and humid all year" means every day feels identical |
| Respect cyclone warnings even if the island seems calm | Pack a European wardrobe — natural fibres or you'll regret it by lunchtime |
| Hydrate constantly, more than feels necessary | Underestimate how much heat drains your energy for the first month |

## Finland: Darkness as a Design Choice

Finns don't complain about winter, which took me an embarrassingly long time to understand isn't stoicism — it's acceptance so complete it's stopped registering as a hardship. From late November through January, large parts of the country experience genuine polar night conditions, and even Helsinki, which gets some daylight, compresses it into a grim six-hour smear that barely counts. The average temperature makes "layer up" sound like the understatement it is; this is a country where studded boots exist as a serious purchasing decision, not a novelty.

What actually gets newcomers isn't the cold, though. It's the psychological toll of the dark, which arrives with a name — kaamosmasennus, roughly "polar night depression" — and a socially accepted response: light therapy lamps sold in ordinary electronics shops next to phone chargers, as unremarkable a household item as a kettle. Every expat I've spoken to in Helsinki eventually bought one, usually around week three, usually after a well-meaning Finnish colleague asked, with total sincerity, whether they'd "sorted their lamp yet."

And then, just as you've built your entire emotional infrastructure around darkness, summer arrives and does the exact opposite: near-perpetual daylight that makes sleep an act of willpower. The genius of Finnish coping culture is the sauna, which isn't a spa indulgence here but a genuine seasonal reset button, used year-round with a devotion that borders on religious. Once you stop resisting the sauna and start scheduling your week around it the way locals do, Finnish winter stops being an ordeal and starts being, improbably, kind of soothing. It just takes one truly miserable December to get there.

## Solomon Islands: Humidity as a Permanent State

The Solomon Islands doesn't really have a "cold season" to look forward to, which is either liberating or exhausting depending entirely on your relationship with sweat. Average temperatures hover around 27°C year-round, but the real story is humidity, which sits at 75 to 85 percent along the coast and simply refuses to negotiate. The wet season, November through April, delivers roughly 70 percent of the year's rainfall, some months dumping 300 millimetres versus the dry season's comparatively restrained 100. "Dry season" here is a relative term, the tropical equivalent of Finland's "warmer week in February."

Cyclones are the wildcard locals genuinely respect, even though the islands' low latitude spares them the worst of the Southwest Pacific's fury compared to, say, Fiji or Vanuatu. They still form, mostly November to May, and the calm, glassy morning that precedes one is exactly the kind of weather that makes newcomers assume warnings are overcautious. They are not. Ask literally anyone who's lived through a season here.

The coping mechanism locals have perfected isn't technological so much as rhythmic: work happens early, before the mid-morning humidity spike turns any physical task into an endurance event, and the middle of the day slows to something closer to a controlled shuffle. Air conditioning exists but is treated as a luxury rather than a baseline expectation, which is precisely the adjustment that breaks newly arrived expats who assumed the tropics simply came with central cooling as standard. They do not. You will learn to love a ceiling fan the way you once loved central heating, and you will mean it.

## The Verdict

Finland, and it isn't remotely close. Cold is a problem you can dress your way out of; humidity is a problem that lives inside your clothes regardless of what you're wearing. Finland at least gives you agency — buy the coat, get the lamp, book the sauna, and you've genuinely solved most of winter's misery. The Solomon Islands' climate simply is what it is, permanently, and your only real strategy is rescheduling your life around a heat that never actually leaves. Give me a season I can dress for over a climate I can only endure.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a Helsinki-based consultant paraphrased: nobody mentioned that by February you'll be genuinely grateful for a Tuesday with visible sunlight, and you'll mention it to strangers unprompted.*

> *TheLocal.fi — paraphrased forum comment: one newcomer said buying a light therapy lamp felt embarrassing until every single Finnish coworker admitted to owning one too.*

> *Expat Arrivals community, Pacific Islands thread — paraphrased: a contractor working in Honiara said the biggest adjustment wasn't the heat itself but accepting that "cooling off" simply means "slightly less soaked in sweat."*

## Conclusion

Both climates will eventually reorganise your habits whether you consent or not — Finland teaches you to chase daylight like a hobby, the Solomon Islands teaches you to treat shade as a strategic resource. Neither is "exotic," they're just weather systems doing exactly what geography built them to do, with locals who long ago stopped finding either remarkable. You will find them remarkable for approximately one season. Then you'll buy the lamp, or the fan, and get on with it, same as everyone else did before you.
