# The Philippines Exercises to Be Seen, Canada Exercises to Be Forgiven

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country believes it has a healthy relationship with exercise, and every country is lying. The Philippines treats fitness as a public performance, staged under fluorescent mall lighting and filmed from three angles. Canada treats it as a private moral debt, repaid in January and written off by March.

I have joined gyms in more countries than I care to count, and I can tell you that the building matters far less than the national attitude walking through the door. Move from Manila to Toronto, or the other way round, and you are not changing gym. You are changing religion.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇭 Philippines ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the evening zumba crowd in the plaza at least once | Assume the instructor will go easy on the newcomer |
| Bring a small towel and a change of shirt for the humidity | Wear your good gym kit to a crowded outdoor session |
| Book a trainer, because they are far cheaper than at home | Skip the sunscreen on a morning run |
| Ask the basketball court regulars if you can join a game | Walk onto the court and expect to be passed the ball |

| 🇨🇦 Canada ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the municipal rec centre, which is cheap and excellent | Sign a long contract on the first day of a sales push |
| Buy proper winter running gear before the first frost | Run outside in cotton and call it character |
| Wipe down the machine and re-rack your weights | Treat the changing room as a place for conversation |
| Read the cancellation terms before you sign anything | Assume you can quit with a polite email |

## 🇵🇭 Philippines

Fitness in Metro Manila has become a lifestyle, an identity and, if the local commentary is right, a status symbol. Walk through Bonifacio Global City on a weekday morning and you will see a parade of runners, cyclists and people in matching outfits doing something with kettlebells. The gym is the place where a young professional shows that she has arrived, and the photograph is part of the workout.

The part I love is what happens outdoors. In the evening, barangay plazas and covered courts turn into open-air dance classes, with a stereo the size of a fridge and a hundred neighbours following one enthusiastic instructor. Nobody is embarrassed. Nobody is wearing anything technical. It is the most democratic form of exercise I have encountered, and it costs close to nothing.

Basketball is the national obsession, and even the smallest street has a hoop strung between two poles. Boxing gyms, sparring clubs and martial arts studios fill in the rest. For an expat, labour is the quiet bargain here: personal trainers are widely available and priced well below what you would pay in a Western capital, so you can afford to be coached rather than merely hopeful.

The weather writes the rules. Heat and humidity push serious training indoors, into air-conditioned gyms, or into the cooler hours around dawn and dusk. Typhoon season cancels the rest. Expats who insist on a lunchtime run in April learn quickly that the sun has no interest in their schedule.

One warning: the social side can be intense. People will comment on your body, kindly and frankly, in a way that would start a lawsuit in Vancouver. Take it as the national love language, shrug, and keep lifting.

Class culture deserves a mention too. Zumba, aerobics and boxing-based workouts are the social glue of local fitness, and there is often a shared warm-up where the whole room is expected to cheer. A shy newcomer will be pulled into the front row within minutes. Resistance is theatrical and entirely futile, and by the third song you will have forgiven everyone involved.

## 🇨🇦 Canada

Canada's gym culture is polite to the point of being sedated. People nod, plug in headphones and avoid your eyes. The big chains, the budget operators and the local independents all offer rows of machines and a vague sense that you should be somewhere else. It is perfectly pleasant, and perfectly silent.

The real treasure is the public network of recreation centres. Municipal pools, rinks, climbing walls and fitness floors are cheap, well run and open to anyone, and community groups such as the YMCA widen the range further, often with subsidised access for newcomers. If you are an expat on a budget, this is where you belong, and you will feel like you have discovered a secret that the locals keep failing to appreciate.

Then there is the weather, which Canada uses as a national personality. Skiing, skating, hockey and winter running are not niche hobbies but ordinary Saturdays. The country takes real pride in being outdoors in a temperature that would close Manila's airport out of sheer shock. The official guidance on movement is sensible and sober, and the people mostly follow it, though they do so wearing three layers and carrying a thermos.

The commercial side deserves caution. Memberships are sold hard in January, contracts can be fiddly to leave, and the cancellation rules are the sort of thing you read once, carefully, with a pen. Treat the signature as a legal act rather than a fitness decision.

Newcomers from warmer countries tend to underestimate the winter. A treadmill is not a sign of weakness in January, it is a survival strategy. Many Filipino families in Canada find their feet through community centres, church groups and sports leagues, where badminton and basketball keep the old habits alive. A gym membership is rarely the first thing you need. A good coat usually is.

## The Verdict

Canada wins on infrastructure and the Philippines wins on joy, and I will not pretend that is a draw. A Canadian rec centre is a small miracle of public planning. A Manila plaza at dusk is a small miracle of human nature. Only one of those can be enjoyed without paying anyone a monthly fee and without being filmed.

If you want to get fit quietly, with good facilities and nobody asking about your weight, Canada is the place. If you want to get fit loudly, with a crowd, a beat and a trainer you can actually afford, the Philippines will carry you across the finish line.

My own rule is simple. I would train in Canada and I would exercise in the Philippines. Training is what you do alone with a plan. Exercise is what you do because the whole street is doing it and leaving would be rude.

The sting is that both countries are better at selling fitness than at sustaining it. One sells it as status and the other sells it as redemption, and in both cases the real exercise is resisting the sofa.

The two cultures also differ on privacy. In Manila you will be watched, advised and sometimes applauded. In Toronto you will be left completely alone, which some people find liberating and others find like exercising inside a library. Choose according to how much encouragement you need and how much you can tolerate.

## Conclusion

Move between these two countries and your body will notice before your head does. In Manila it sweats, in Toronto it shivers, and in both it is asked to perform for an audience that already has an opinion.

My parting advice is to adopt the best of each. Take the Filipino plaza spirit to Canada and start a free class in the park, even if it is only you and a speaker. Take the Canadian rec centre habit to Manila and insist on cheap, public, well run facilities for everyone.

And whichever way you travel, do not buy the annual contract on the first day. The gym is counting on your optimism, and your optimism has a very poor record.
