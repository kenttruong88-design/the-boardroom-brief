# One Country's Gym Is a Field With Shouting. The Other's Is a Library With Weights.

🇮🇪 Ireland vs 🇯🇵 Japan — Fitness and Gym Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ireland doesn't really have a "gym culture" so much as a GAA culture that occasionally overlaps with a treadmill. Fitness here happens communally, outdoors, in the rain, organised around hurling and Gaelic football clubs that double as the actual social infrastructure of most towns — you don't join a gym, you join a club, and the club decides your fitness regime, your Friday nights, and occasionally your marriage prospects. Japan takes the opposite approach entirely: gyms are hushed, orderly spaces where grunting is a social transgression, machines are wiped down with an intensity bordering on ritual, and conversation is kept to the absolute minimum required for survival.

I've stood on the sideline of a GAA match being screamed at by a sixty-year-old woman for standing in the wrong spot. I've also worked out in a Tokyo gym so silent I could hear my own shame at breathing slightly too loud on the rowing machine. Both are forms of physical culture. Only one involves being yelled at by anyone's grandmother.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a local GAA club if you want to actually integrate into a town | Expect indoor, climate-controlled comfort; most training happens outside, rain or shine |
| Show up to support even if you're not playing — spectating is half the culture | Be surprised by shouted feedback from the sideline; it's encouragement, mostly |
| Embrace the pub afterward; it's considered part of the training cycle | Assume a "gym" is the main fitness outlet; for most towns, the pitch is |

### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wipe down every machine before and after use, without exception | Grunt, drop weights loudly, or make unnecessary noise — it draws real disapproval |
| Check tattoo policies before visiting — some gyms and onsen still restrict them | Skip the indoor shoes rule; most gyms require separate footwear for the floor |
| Follow posted machine time limits during busy hours | Chat loudly with a workout partner; most Japanese gyms are near-silent by unspoken agreement |

## Ireland: Fitness as Community, Rain Included

The GAA club is the single most important piece of fitness infrastructure in Ireland, and it isn't close. Nearly every town, no matter how small, has one, and the club functions as gym, social club, and unofficial civic government rolled into a muddy field with two sets of goalposts. Training happens outdoors, year-round, in weather that would shut down fitness culture almost anywhere else on earth — Irish GAA players train through horizontal rain with a stoicism that borders on the absurd, because the alternative, cancelling, simply isn't considered.

What makes this system so effective isn't the training itself, it's the social contract underneath it. You don't join a GAA club purely to get fit; you join to belong, and fitness happens as a side effect of showing up every week to a community that will notice, genuinely, if you don't. This produces a level of adherence that gym memberships, with their notorious January drop-off, could only dream of — nobody ghosts their local hurling team, because the social cost of doing so is far higher than the social cost of skipping a solo gym session nobody's tracking.

The sideline culture deserves its own dissertation. Spectating a GAA match means witnessing genuinely unhinged levels of vocal commitment from parents, grandparents, and passersby, all offering tactical advice at a volume that suggests the match outcome is a matter of personal survival. It's chaotic, warm, occasionally terrifying if you're the one being shouted at, and completely central to why the culture works — fitness here is inseparable from spectacle, from community, from the pub afterward where the match gets replayed conversationally in even more detail than it was played physically.

## Japan: Fitness as Quiet Discipline

Japanese gym culture couldn't be further from a muddy pitch full of shouting relatives. Walk into a Tokyo gym at peak hours and you'll find it strangely, almost eerily, quiet — dozens of people working out with focused intensity, headphones in, conversation minimal, machines wiped down before and after use with a thoroughness that borders on ceremonial. Noise, in general, is treated as an imposition on others; dropping weights loudly, grunting through a heavy set, or chatting at volume with a workout partner will draw the kind of silent disapproval that, once you've experienced it, you go to considerable lengths to avoid repeating.

This isn't coldness, it's an extension of a broader cultural value around not imposing your presence on shared space, and it produces gyms that feel, frankly, more pleasant to train in than the sweaty, echoing arenas common elsewhere — nobody's shouting encouragement at strangers, nobody's hogging the squat rack while filming a form check for social media, and the overall atmosphere is one of quiet, parallel discipline rather than communal spectacle.

There are practical wrinkles for newcomers. Many gyms, and virtually all onsen and public baths, maintain restrictions around visible tattoos, a holdover from associations with organised crime that persists even as attitudes slowly shift, and it's worth checking a facility's specific policy before showing up ink visible and getting turned away at reception, quietly but firmly. Indoor-only shoes are non-negotiable at most gyms, machine time limits get enforced during busy hours without exception, and the overall experience rewards those who observe rather than announce their fitness journey. It is, in its own restrained way, just as demanding as Irish GAA training — the discipline is simply internal rather than externally shouted at you from a sideline.

## The Verdict

Ireland wins on community and sheer emotional intensity — nothing replicates the feeling of an entire town caring, loudly, about your hurling form. Japan wins on focus and respect for shared space — nowhere else will you train with this level of collective consideration and quiet discipline. If you want fitness that doubles as your entire social life, Ireland will absorb you completely, mud and all. If you want fitness as a private, respectful discipline free of shouted commentary, Japan is unmatched. Just don't grunt in a Tokyo gym, and don't expect silence on an Irish sideline.

## Conclusion

Ireland turns fitness into belonging, loudly, in the rain, with your business known to the entire parish by Sunday. Japan turns fitness into a private discipline practised in respectful silence alongside strangers who will never ask your name. Both work. Neither will let you fake it — Ireland will notice if you stop showing up, and Japan will notice if you make too much noise while you're there. Pack accordingly: boots for one, indoor shoes and a tattoo cover-up for the other.
