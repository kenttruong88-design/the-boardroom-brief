# One Country Burns Winter in Effigy. The Other Throws a Party for Every Single Saint, Personally

🇱🇹 Lithuania vs 🇵🇭 Philippines

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Lithuania celebrates the changing of the seasons with pagan bonfires so old that the Christian veneer painted over them centuries ago still doesn't quite cover the original intent, and everyone's fine with that arrangement. The Philippines, meanwhile, has essentially organised its entire national calendar around the premise that literally every town deserves its own dedicated, patron-saint-sanctioned excuse for a multi-day party, and has been doing so with unwavering commitment since Spanish colonial priests first introduced the concept and clearly underestimated how enthusiastically it would be embraced.

## Do's & Don'ts: Lithuania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a Joninės (Rasos) bonfire on June 24th if you're anywhere near one | Expect Christmas-level commercialism around folk festivals — these stay genuinely local |
| Wear or accept a flower crown if offered during Midsummer celebrations | Treat Užgavėnės masks as costume-party fun only — they carry real symbolic weight |
| Try the pancakes during Užgavėnės — eating them is part of the seasonal ritual | Assume every public holiday is a major event; some are quieter, more reflective observances |
| Ask locals about the pagan roots behind Christian-named holidays — most enjoy explaining | Schedule tight travel plans around February's Užgavėnės — small towns shift schedules |

## Do's & Don'ts: Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend a barangay fiesta if invited — outsiders are genuinely, warmly welcomed | Skip the food offered at a fiesta — sharing home-cooked dishes is central to the tradition |
| Expect processions, brass bands, and street parades as standard fiesta elements | Assume one town's fiesta date applies nationally — each barangay has its own patron saint |
| Bring a small gift or contribution if staying with a local family during fiesta season | Underestimate how loud and long these celebrations run — multi-day is standard, not exceptional |
| Use fiesta season to reconnect if you have Filipino heritage — it's a genuine homecoming tradition | Treat it as purely tourist spectacle — for locals, it's faith, family, and identity combined |

## Lithuania: Bonfires, Fern Flowers, and a Calendar That Never Fully Left Paganism Behind

Lithuania was the last country in Europe to formally Christianise, and its festival calendar still carries the fingerprints of that late conversion more visibly than almost anywhere else on the continent. Rasos — also known by its Christian overlay name Joninės, St. John's Day — falls on June 24th and remains, in practice, a midsummer pagan festival wearing a saint's name as a formality. Bonfires, torchlit processions, flower crowns, and the enduring folk tradition of searching the forest at midnight for a mythical blooming fern flower said to bring fortune to whoever finds it: this is not a sanitised heritage reenactment for tourists. Lithuanians actually do this, in genuine numbers, every year, with a sincerity that would embarrass most Western countries' relationship with their own folk traditions.

Užgavėnės, the pre-Lenten carnival occurring roughly seven weeks before Easter, offers an entirely different but equally vivid seasonal ritual: an effigy representing winter is ceremonially burned, colourfully masked and costumed processions wind through towns, and pancakes — round, golden, symbolically standing in for the returning sun — are eaten in genuine quantity as part of the observance rather than as festival-adjacent snacking. The masks aren't decorative flourishes; they carry real symbolic weight tied to driving out winter and darkness, and locals take the tradition seriously enough that treating it as simple costume-party fun tends to land oddly with anyone who actually grew up with it.

What distinguishes Lithuania's festival culture from more commercialised European folk traditions is its genuine, unforced locality. These aren't staged for international tourism boards; they persist because communities have simply kept doing them, generation after generation, largely regardless of whether anyone outside the country is paying attention. For visitors, that authenticity is the reward — and also occasionally the inconvenience, since small towns reorganise their entire schedule around these dates with limited concession to anyone's travel itinerary.

## Philippines: A Patron Saint for Every Barangay, and a Party to Match

If Lithuania's festival calendar is defined by a handful of deeply rooted seasonal observances, the Philippines runs on the opposite principle entirely: near-constant, hyperlocal celebration, with every barangay — the smallest administrative unit, roughly a village or ward — maintaining its own dedicated fiesta in honour of its own specific patron saint. Introduced during Spanish colonial rule and enthusiastically, permanently absorbed into Filipino cultural identity ever since, the barangay fiesta has become something considerably larger than its religious origins: a manifestation of community, faith, and belonging rolled into one multi-day celebration featuring processions, brass bands, elaborately decorated streets, and food in genuinely staggering abundance.

The food component deserves particular emphasis because it's not peripheral to the tradition — it is, in many ways, the tradition's actual social mechanism. Families prepare special home-cooked dishes specifically for fiesta, and the custom of visiting neighbours and relatives to share these dishes functions as the primary way community bonds get renewed and strengthened year over year. Fiestas are also, notably, almost always free to attend, and the open, generous hospitality extended to outsiders and returning overseas Filipino workers alike — the fiesta as genuine "balik-bayan," a homecoming incentive — means visitors rarely need a formal connection to be genuinely welcomed in.

What surprises first-time visitors most isn't the religious procession element, which reads as fairly familiar to anyone who's encountered Catholic festival traditions elsewhere. It's the sheer scale and duration: these aren't tidy single-afternoon events. Multi-day celebrations, entire streets given over to decoration and parade routes, and a level of civic investment that treats the local fiesta as genuinely equal in importance to any national holiday — because in the barangay where it's happening, for that week, it functionally is.

## The Verdict

Lithuania offers depth: a small number of genuinely ancient, deeply meaningful seasonal rituals practised with real sincerity rather than performed for an audience. The Philippines offers breadth: an almost inexhaustible calendar of hyperlocal celebration, where somewhere in the archipelago, on any given week, a barangay is very likely mid-fiesta. If you want a handful of profound, atmospheric cultural experiences, Lithuania's bonfires and Užgavėnės masks deliver something genuinely rare in modern Europe. If you want to never run out of an excuse to eat exceptional home-cooked food with total strangers who insist on treating you like extended family, the Philippines wins, decisively, and will not accept your polite refusal of a second plate.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Curiosity Magazine — paraphrased: a writer exploring Lithuanian folk tradition noted that the persistence of pagan-rooted holidays like Rasos reflects genuine cultural continuity rather than tourist-oriented revival, since the country Christianised later and less completely than its neighbours.*

> *RichestPH — paraphrased: a contributor described barangay fiestas as functioning less like tourist events and more like an annual community reunion, where sharing food with visiting strangers is treated as a point of local pride rather than an inconvenience.*

> *Discover Philippines blog — paraphrased: a writer cautioned first-time visitors not to underestimate fiesta duration, noting that what looks like a single-day event on a calendar often stretches into a multi-day affair with no expectation that guests will leave early.*

## Conclusion

Neither of these festival cultures exists to be consumed as spectacle, however photogenic both undeniably are. Lithuania's bonfires and Užgavėnės masks are a nation quietly, persistently holding onto a pre-Christian identity that never fully disappeared. The Philippines' relentless barangay fiesta calendar is a nation that took a colonial religious import and turned it into the single most effective community-bonding mechanism I've encountered anywhere in sixty countries of eating other people's food at their expense. Show up to either with genuine curiosity rather than a camera looking for content, and you'll leave with considerably more than photographs — you'll leave, in the Philippines' case, with a full stomach and at least one new aunt you didn't previously know you had.
