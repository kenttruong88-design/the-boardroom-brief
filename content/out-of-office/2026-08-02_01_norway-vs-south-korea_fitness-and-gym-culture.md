# Norway Thinks the Gym Is a Moral Failure. South Korea Thinks Your Body Needs a Portfolio.

🇳🇴 Norway · 🇰🇷 South Korea
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Norwegians do not "work out." They "go outside," which apparently absolves them of ever setting foot in a building with a squat rack, because why would you lift a barbell indoors when you could be dragging a sled up a mountain in horizontal sleet, smiling, calling it Tuesday. South Koreans, meanwhile, have turned the human body into a KPI. There is a spreadsheet somewhere in Seoul tracking your body fat percentage against your last relationship, and someone is losing.

I have shivered through both. I have been handed a thermos of black coffee on a Norwegian mountainside by a stranger who assumed, correctly, that I was dying, and I have been sized up by a Gangnam personal trainer within four seconds of walking into his gym, like a horse at auction. Neither culture asked if I wanted to participate. Both assumed I would eventually thank them.

## Norway: Where Fitness Is a Weather Report

Norway does not have a fitness culture so much as a national refusal to stay inside. The concept is called *friluftsliv* — "open-air life" — and it is less a hobby than a founding constitutional principle, unwritten but enforced by mild social shame. Statistics Norway's leisure surveys consistently show the majority of adults report regular outdoor physical activity, and the country's public health guidance treats a brisk walk in the rain as roughly equivalent to a prescription. Gyms exist — SATS is everywhere, gleaming and Scandinavian-minimalist — but ask a Norwegian about their "workout routine" and you'll get a faintly confused look, the same one you'd get asking them about their breathing routine. It's not a routine. It's Tuesday.

What nobody tells you is how much equipment this requires. You cannot simply decide to enjoy nature in Norway; you must first acquire the correct base layer, the correct mid-layer, the correct shell, the correct boots, and at least one item from Norrøna that costs more than your flight there. Cross-country skiing is taught practically in utero — toddlers are strapped into skis before they can reliably use cutlery — and by adulthood it's less a sport than a birthright you'd be embarrassed to admit you're bad at. I once mentioned, at a dinner party in Oslo, that I found downhill skiing "quite hard." The table went silent in the specific way a British table goes silent when you mention supporting the wrong football club.

Then there's the cold-water swimming, which has gone from eccentric habit to full-blown wellness trend, dutifully followed by the mandatory sauna, because Norwegians have collectively decided that near-hypothermia followed by near-heatstroke is the path to inner peace. I've done it. It works, infuriatingly. But the real fitness culture here isn't the swim or the ski — it's the sheer, bottomless Norwegian tolerance for discomfort, treated not as suffering but as a kind of moral hygiene. If you're not slightly wet and slightly cold, are you even living correctly?

## South Korea: Where the Gym Has Sales Targets

If Norway treats fitness as a spiritual obligation to the outdoors, South Korea treats it as a personal branding exercise with a hard deadline. The 24-hour *heljang* (헬스장) is a fixture of every neighbourhood, often stacked three floors up above a fried chicken restaurant, which tells you everything about the beautiful contradiction of Korean life. Walk in as a foreigner and within minutes a personal trainer will materialise, assess you with the clinical detachment of a customs officer, and pitch you a PT package that costs more than your rent. Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency data shows fitness centre usage climbing steadily for over a decade, and the industry report from Statistics Korea confirms it's now a genuine economic sector, not a niche.

The cultural centrepiece, and I say this with total sincerity, is the "body profile" photoshoot — a ritualised, months-long body transformation culminating in a professional shoot, usually in minimal clothing, oiled, lit, and filtered within an inch of its life. It is treated the way Westerners treat a milestone birthday: something you simply do, eventually, whether you want to or not. I watched a colleague in Seoul train for four months, cut carbs to near-zero for the final two weeks, and produce a photoshoot she then never showed anyone. The point wasn't the photo. The point was proving to herself she could do the photo.

And then, gloriously, there's hiking — not the gentle Sunday stroll Westerners imagine, but a full paramilitary operation involving trekking poles, moisture-wicking everything, and a demographic skew so heavily toward retirees in head-to-toe North Face that Bukhansan on a Sunday looks like a Gore-Tex convention. Don't underestimate them. They will lap you.

## The Verdict

Norway wins on sustainability — nobody burns out on "going for a walk," and the outdoor habit tends to last a lifetime rather than a January. South Korea wins on sheer intensity and, unexpectedly, on inclusivity of the older generation, who dominate the hiking scene with a ferocity Norway's gentle wanderers can't match. But if I'm honest about which culture would actually get *me* to the gym consistently, it's Korea — nothing motivates quite like a trainer texting you at 7am asking why you skipped leg day. Norway just lets you disappear into a fjord and never really checks in. Freedom, yes. Also a little bit sad.

> *Reddit r/korea — an expat recounting their PT aggressively upselling supplements mid-session, then guilt-tripping them for "not taking their transformation seriously."*

## Conclusion

Pick your poison: a culture that will drag you outside into weather that wants you dead and call it self-care, or a culture that will put your body on a spreadsheet and text you about it. Norway's version costs less and asks less of your ego. Korea's version gets faster results and requires you to occasionally be photographed oiled under studio lighting. Neither will let you simply exist in your body unbothered — that particular luxury, it turns out, is the one thing money and geography can't buy you anywhere.
