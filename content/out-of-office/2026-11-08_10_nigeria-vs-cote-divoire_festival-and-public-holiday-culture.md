# Nigeria Will Give You A Public Holiday For Every Religion It Has; Côte d'Ivoire Will Give You A Festival That Forgives Its Entire Village In One Weekend

🇳🇬 🇨🇮 *By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Public holidays reveal what a nation is actually trying to hold together. Nigeria's calendar is a careful, deliberate balancing act — Christian holidays, Muslim holidays, secular national days, all stacked with the diplomatic precision of a country that knows exactly how religiously and ethnically split it is, and has decided the calendar itself must do some of the unifying work. Côte d'Ivoire's most famous cultural event does something stranger and, frankly, more interesting: it designates an entire festival, the Fête des Masques in Abissa, specifically for public truth-telling and ritual forgiveness, as though the whole country collectively agreed that grudges need a scheduled outlet or they'll fester.

I have spent an Eid in Lagos watching an entire street block itself off for communal prayer and then, two hours later, a Christmas market two neighbourhoods over as though the city had simply decided to believe in everything at once. I have also stood in Grand-Bassam watching the Abissa festival's masked dancers while a local calmly informed me that, yes, people genuinely use this week to tell their chief exactly what they think of him, and no, he's not allowed to be offended.

## Nigeria: A Calendar Built To Hold A Country Together

Nigeria's public holiday calendar is, on paper, a fairly conventional list — Wikipedia's own listing covers the expected national and religious days — but the lived experience of it is considerably more interesting than the list suggests. This is a country split close to evenly between a Muslim-majority north and Christian-majority south, and the federal calendar's studied inclusion of both major faiths' key holidays, observed nationally rather than regionally, functions as a quiet, continuous act of political balance. Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Kabir sit on the same official calendar as Christmas and Good Friday, both genuinely, visibly celebrated in mixed cities like Lagos, where you'll see Eid prayer grounds and Christmas markets within blocks of each other, often within the same month.

TheCable's coverage of Independence Day celebrations captures the other half of the equation — a secular national holiday that, unlike the religious observances, belongs to everyone regardless of faith, and gets treated with a genuine, grassroots patriotic energy that cuts across the country's usual fault lines. October 1st isn't just a day off; it's one of the few calendar fixtures that doesn't require choosing a side.

What catches most newcomers off guard isn't the holidays themselves but their practical bleed. Official single-day listings understate the real disruption — travel, business, logistics all slow for days around Eid and Christmas specifically, as extended families travel between states for the kind of multi-day gathering the official calendar doesn't quite capture.

Nigeria's federal structure adds a further wrinkle most newcomers never anticipate: individual states can and do declare their own additional holidays on top of the national calendar, often tied to a state's founding anniversary, a significant local religious observance, or a gubernatorial decision that arrives with only days of formal notice. A business plan built purely around the official national list will, with some regularity, get blindsided by a state-specific closure nobody outside that state's own civil service saw coming — which is exactly the kind of granular local knowledge expat business guides consistently flag as something a purely desk-researched calendar will never catch.

## Côte d'Ivoire: A Festival That Builds Forgiveness Into The Schedule

Côte d'Ivoire's standard public holiday calendar — Independence Day, Easter, Eid, Christmas, Assumption, tracked cleanly on sites like Calendarific — follows a familiar Francophone West African pattern and won't surprise anyone who's lived in the region. What genuinely sets the country apart, and what every serious cultural account of it eventually circles back to, is the Fête des Masques — the Abissa festival, centred in Grand-Bassam — which does something no ordinary public holiday does: it institutionalises grievance.

TakeYourBackpack's first-person account of attending Abissa describes a festival built around a specific, deliberate social function: for one scheduled week, the normal hierarchy of chiefs, elders, and ordinary villagers is formally suspended, and people are not just permitted but culturally expected to voice grievances, criticise authority, and air disputes openly, through masked performance, music, and ritualised confrontation — before the whole community collectively, formally, moves on. It's less a party with cultural trimmings and more a release valve with exceptional music attached.

Afrik.com's coverage of Abidjan's broader festival calendar situates Abissa within a city that treats large-scale cultural events as genuine civic infrastructure rather than tourism add-ons — Abidjan positions itself, deliberately, as a regional festival hub, and the sheer density of cultural programming reflects a national comfort with public, collective celebration that goes well beyond the standard holiday list.

What's easy to miss, watching the masked dancers and drumming from the outside, is how deliberately structured the forgiveness mechanism actually is. It isn't a free-for-all shouting match; specific roles, orders of speaking, and ritual sequences govern who airs what grievance and when, with elders moderating the process to ensure it resolves rather than escalates. Anthropological interest in Abissa has grown specifically because it represents a rare example of a culture building institutionalised, scheduled conflict resolution directly into its festival calendar, rather than leaving grievance and reconciliation to handle themselves informally, badly, year-round.

## The Verdict

Nigeria wins on sheer calendar diplomacy — a country this religiously and ethnically complex, running a shared public holiday schedule that genuinely, visibly honours multiple faiths side by side, is a quiet institutional achievement most newcomers don't appreciate until they've lived through an Eid and a Christmas in the same city. Côte d'Ivoire wins on sheer cultural imagination — a festival that doesn't just celebrate but functions, built explicitly to let a village say the unsayable once a year and then genuinely move forward. Nigeria's calendar holds a nation together through inclusion. Côte d'Ivoire's holds a village together through catharsis. Both, in their own ways, are doing something far more sophisticated than "day off work."

Western calendars, by comparison, look almost embarrassingly thin next to either model. A bank holiday back home marks a date, not a function — nobody expects it to actively do anything beyond grant a day's rest. Nigeria's calendar does real diplomatic work every single year, quietly managing a fault line most countries that complex would struggle to hold. Abissa does real psychological work, on a schedule, with witnesses. Neither nation would describe their holidays in these terms out loud, because they don't need to; the function simply runs, year after year, whether or not an outsider ever notices what it's actually for.

## The Parting Shot

Nigeria's calendar will teach you that a fractious nation can still agree on how to celebrate; Côte d'Ivoire's will teach you that sometimes the healthiest thing a community can do is schedule the argument. Show up for both, and for heaven's sake, if you're ever handed a mask in Grand-Bassam during Abissa, do not take whatever gets said to you personally. It's not about you. That's rather the point.
