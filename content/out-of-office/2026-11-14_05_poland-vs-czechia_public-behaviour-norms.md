# Poles Queue Like It's a Contact Sport. Czechs Queue Like It's a Secret

🇵🇱 Poland vs 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Central Europe is where the British idea of the orderly queue goes to be quietly dismantled. We think we invented the thing, and we are very smug about it, and then we arrive in Warsaw or Prague and discover that the line is a negotiation, a rumour or an optical illusion. I have stood in both, and I can report that the two nations have taken the same historical trauma and turned it into entirely different manners.

Poland and Czechia sit next to each other, share a language family and a communist past, and still manage to disagree on how far apart two strangers should stand. If you are moving between them, you will not need a phrasebook so much as a recalibrated sense of personal space.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Poland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stand diagonally behind the person at a ticket window | Hover directly at their shoulder |
| Keep a respectful distance at cash machines | Crowd the person in front |
| Assert your place in the line, calmly | Stay silent when someone says they were ahead of you |
| Expect shops to open a second till and serve the back first | Be outraged by it |

**Czechia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take a number at banks and post offices | Stand in a line that does not exist |
| Stand on the right of escalators and pass on the left | Block the left lane |
| Say *s dovolením* when you need to pass | Push through silently |
| Keep your voice and your phone quiet in banks and hospitals | Take calls in post offices or libraries |

## Poland

The best account of Polish queueing I have found is In Your Pocket's affectionate essay, and it reads like an anthropologist's field notes. Its thesis is that Polish queue behaviour was shaped by communist-era shortages, when joining a line was a survival skill, and the techniques survive. Common tactics include the claim that "I was standing in front of you", or the appeal of age: "I'm older than you, let me go first." You have to be ready to hold your ground, politely and firmly, or be moved backward by nothing more than confidence.

The geometry is odd too. At train-station ticket windows, the essay explains, the queue forms horizontally, so you stand diagonally behind the person being served, not in a straight line. At cash machines, people keep a large distance, and nobody admits to being in the line at all. And shops, it notes, may open a second till and serve the back of the line first, which can feel like betrayal and is, in fact, efficient.

A reader named Maria Donely, commenting on the piece, wrote: "Trust me, Polish queue tricks become a subtle folklore compering to American ways." I love the typo, and I love the sentiment. The Polish queue is not rude. It is a craft, with its own folklore, and the people who practise it are often cheerful about it. They would tell you the system works, and the evidence is that it does.

What surprised me is how warm the surrounding culture is. Poles will cut a line and then help you with your bags. They will negotiate your place at the window and then offer you their seat on the tram. The social contract is not that nobody pushes. It is that everyone knows the rules of pushing, and you are expected to learn them quickly.

My advice is to adopt the confidence, not the elbows. Say clearly where you stood. Hold the spot. Smile. Within a month you will be doing it too, and you will feel faintly ashamed, and then you will go home to a country where nobody queues properly and miss it.

## Czechia

The Czech approach, according to the Expats.cz writer Ryan Scott, is more mysterious. Banks and post offices use take-a-number machines, which solves the problem by removing the line entirely. At bus and tram stops, he notes, the line often turns into a crowd. And at stations, asking to cut in can work, depending on how much time pressure the other person is under.

His own experience is a gem of expat comedy. He admitted, "I have received a few ugly glares for seemingly standing in the wrong place." He was also unsure whether the problem was that "some people seem lost and so take the wrong ticket or multiple tickets" at the number machine. The Czech queue is not hostile, but it is unspoken. There are rules, and nobody will explain them, and you will be silently judged for breaking them.

His earlier piece on public behaviour is a short manual to the same world. Czechs stand on the right of escalators and pass on the left, saying *s dovolením* as they go. Seats are offered to the elderly, pregnant women and small children. Mobile phones are discouraged in post offices, banks, hospitals and libraries, with signs showing a phone in a red circle. Loud sniffling on a train, he found, was a mistake, because shouting, whistling and loud noises generally earn disapproval.

That is the heart of Czech public manners: restraint. People will not confront you, but they will notice. A Czech tram is quiet in a way that can feel hostile to someone from a talkative culture. Nobody is angry. They are simply managing the shared space by minimising their presence in it.

If Poland is a negotiation, Czechia is a library. The rules are strict, the penalty is a look, and the whole system depends on everyone pretending not to look. For a newcomer, the Czech version is easier to survive, because nobody physically moves you. It is harder to master, because nobody tells you when you have failed.

A final note for anyone who has to share a commute with both cultures. The Polish queue rewards speaking up, and the Czech queue rewards keeping quiet, so the same instinct that makes you a good citizen in one will make you faintly ridiculous in the other. Switch cities and you must switch posture. Do it badly and nothing dramatic happens: you will get an extra glare in Prague or an extra elbow in Warsaw. Do it well and you will blend in within a month, which is the only form of local knowledge that cannot be bought and never appears in a guidebook.

## The Verdict

Poland wins on character, Czechia wins on calm. I know which I would rather have on a Monday morning. Give me the Czech tram, with its hush and its take-a-number machine and its unspoken order, because I do not have the energy at eight o'clock to defend my place against a lady of seventy.

But I know which I would rather have for a good story. The Polish queue is theatre. It has folklore, drama and a cast of characters, and I have never left one without a tale. The Czech queue is a quiet and efficient exercise in not being noticed, and nobody has ever told a good anecdote about a take-a-number machine.

For a first posting I would pick Czechia, because it will forgive you more. For a second, I would pick Poland, because by then you will be ready to fight for your place.

## Conclusion

Manners are just rules that nobody wrote down. In Poland, the rules are performed loudly and contested daily. In Czechia, they are obeyed silently and enforced by glare. You will break both sets within your first week, and you will be forgiven by one and judged by the other.

Stand a little closer in Warsaw. Stand a little farther in Prague. And in both cities, if somebody says they were here first, check your ticket before you argue.
