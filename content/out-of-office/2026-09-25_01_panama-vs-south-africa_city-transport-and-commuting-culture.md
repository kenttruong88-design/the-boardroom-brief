# Panama Built a Metro to Kill the Diablo Rojo. Johannesburg Just Lets the Minibus Win Every Single Time.

🇵🇦 Panama 🇿🇦 South Africa
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Panama City did something almost nobody in Latin America has managed: it looked at a chaotic, privately-run, gloriously over-decorated bus system, admitted it was a public safety liability, and replaced great chunks of it with an actual metro. Johannesburg looked at its own chaotic, privately-run, entirely undecorated minibus taxi system and shrugged, because fourteen million daily trips a year don't reroute themselves just because a government would find it tidier. I have commuted badly in both cities. In Panama I stood on a Metro platform so clean it felt like an apology for something. In Johannesburg I sat in a Toyota Quantum with eighteen strangers while the driver negotiated a fare dispute, overtook on a pavement, and answered two phone calls, all before I'd worked out where the seatbelt would have gone if there'd been one.

Both systems are, technically, "informal transport getting formalised," which is the tourism-board euphemism for "we built something on top of chaos without actually removing the chaos underneath." The difference is how much of the chaos survived contact with the formalisation, and — spoiler — in one of these cities the chaos absolutely won.

## Panama: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a Metro card (Metrobús works on it too) and top it up in advance | Try to pay cash on the Metro — it doesn't exist as an option, and the machines have moods |
| Stand right, walk left on the escalators — Panamanians take this more seriously than you'd expect | Expect the Metrobús schedule to be a genuine promise rather than a rough suggestion |
| Use the Metro for anything crossing the city at rush hour — it beats surface traffic every time | Hail one of the last surviving Diablo Rojo buses expecting a route map; ask a local first |

## South Africa: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the hand signals — one finger up means "town," and yes, everyone actually uses them | Get into a minibus taxi during a fare or route dispute; wait for the next one, no exceptions |
| Take the Gautrain between Joburg, Pretoria, and the airport — it's genuinely excellent | Assume Uber and minibus taxis peacefully coexist; the turf tension is real and occasionally violent |
| Sit near the front if you're new to the system — easier to signal your stop, easier to exit fast | Walk to a taxi rank alone after dark, whatever your Google Maps ETA insists |

## Panama: A Metro That Actually Arrived On Time, Historically Speaking

Panama City opened Latin America's first metro system outside Mexico City, Santiago, and a handful of others back in 2014, and — this is the part that still mildly stuns me — it worked, and it kept expanding, with Line 2 following and a Line 3 pushing out toward Panamá Oeste. That's not nothing in a region where infrastructure promises routinely die in committee. The Metro is air-conditioned, which in a city that sits at a permanent, punishing tropical simmer is not a luxury, it's a mercy. It's also cheap, fast, and — critically — trusted, which is the rarest currency in public transit anywhere.

The catch is that the Metro doesn't cover everything, and the gaps are filled by the Metrobús network and whatever remains of the old "Diablo Rojo" buses — retired American school buses, repainted by hand in wild, competitive murals of saints, footballers, and the occasional cartoon character, that used to run the entire city on a system best described as "vibes and horn honks." The government has spent a decade trying to phase them out entirely in favour of the standardised Metrobús fleet, and mostly succeeded, which secretly makes me a little sad, because a Diablo Rojo screaming down Vía España at 3am playing reggaeton at a volume that violates several conventions is, objectively, more fun than a beige bus with a card reader. Panama chose safety and predictability over spectacle. I understand it. I still miss the spectacle. The Metro itself, though, is the real story: it's proof a mid-sized Latin American capital can build heavy rail, run it well, and have citizens actually prefer it to driving, which is the entire point and which most cities twice its size have never managed.

## South Africa: The Minibus Taxi Is the Actual Government of the Road

Johannesburg has a proper rapid rail line — the Gautrain, sleek, punctual, air-conditioned, connecting Sandton, Pretoria, and OR Tambo airport with a competence that feels almost imported from somewhere else. It is also, brutally, a system built for a narrow band of commuters on a narrow band of routes, because it was never designed to replace the minibus taxi industry, which moves the overwhelming majority of South Africans to work every single day and answers to essentially nobody but itself.

The minibus taxi system is privately owned, route-associated rather than centrally scheduled, negotiated in cash, navigated by hand signal, and — depending entirely on which rank, which association, and which day you've picked — either a shockingly efficient way to get fifteen people to Soweto in twenty minutes or the site of a violent turf dispute between rival taxi associations that's shut down an entire route for a week. This isn't hyperbole for colour; taxi violence over routes and permits is a documented, recurring feature of the industry, and it coexists uneasily with a newer generation of Uber and Bolt drivers who've learned the hard way which ranks to avoid entirely. And yet — the minibus taxi industry also functions as the closest thing millions of South Africans have to affordable, accessible, go-anywhere transport in a country where the state-run alternative (Metrorail) has spent years plagued by cable theft, arson, and chronic underinvestment. It's not that South Africa failed to build a metro. It built one, mostly, and it's called an eighteen-seater Toyota Quantum, self-organised into an entire informal economy that moves a nation.

## The Verdict

Panama wins on comfort, safety, and the simple dignity of a schedule you can trust, and if you're picking somewhere to actually commute daily without a story to tell about it, take the Metro every time. But South Africa's minibus taxi network is, against every instinct European transit planners have, one of the most resilient pieces of self-organised infrastructure on the continent — nobody funded it properly, nobody regulated it kindly, and it still moves more people than the state-run alternative ever managed. Panama built order from the top down. South Africa built order from the bottom up, out of sheer necessity, and it's held for decades against enormous pressure. I'd rather be comfortable in Panama. I'd rather be impressed, however uneasily, by Johannesburg.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/panama — paraphrased: assumed the Metro card would also work seamlessly on Metrobús routes across the city, discovered the network still has real gaps in Panamá Oeste where you're back to informal buses with no fixed schedule at all.*

> *Internations Johannesburg — paraphrased: took an Uber to a taxi rank to "see what it was like," driver refused to drop off directly at the rank itself, only explained afterward that Uber drivers avoid certain ranks entirely over safety and turf issues.*

> *Quora — paraphrased response to "is it safe to take a minibus taxi as a foreigner": technically yes if you go with someone who already knows the hand signals and the route, absolutely not as a solo first attempt, and the fare negotiation alone will mark you as new before you've said a word.*

## Conclusion

Somewhere between a spotless Metro platform in Panama and a shouted fare dispute in a Joburg taxi rank, you'll learn the actual difference between a transport system and a transport economy. Panama gave its citizens a system. South Africa's citizens built themselves an economy, informally, out of necessity, and dared the state to catch up. Neither one is finished. Both of them will get you where you're going — just budget very differently for how much you'll have to negotiate to get there, and never, in either city, act like you know the route better than the driver does.
