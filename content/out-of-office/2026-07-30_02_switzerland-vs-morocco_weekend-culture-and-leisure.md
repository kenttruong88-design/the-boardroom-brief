# One Country Legislates Silence, the Other Legislates Couscous

🇨🇭 Switzerland vs 🇲🇦 Morocco
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Switzerland has, in places, actual municipal ordinances governing how loudly you're allowed to enjoy yourself on a Sunday. Morocco has an unwritten but ironclad national understanding that Friday afternoon belongs to couscous, family, and the mosque, in that order of visibility if not importance. One country weaponises silence as leisure; the other weaponises abundance. Both will make you feel, at some point, that you're doing your weekend wrong.

I've been fined — well, glared at with the intensity of a fine — for using a washing machine after 10pm on a Swiss Sunday. I've also sat through a four-hour Friday lunch in Fes that started as couscous and ended as a nap, a tea ceremony, and a second nap, with nobody once checking a watch. Both experiences taught me that "weekend" is a culturally loaded word, and assuming your version travels is how you end up either fined or fed against your will.

## Do's & Don'ts: Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan your grocery shopping before Saturday evening — most shops shut and stay shut through Sunday | Run a lawnmower, drill, or washing machine on a Sunday; neighbours will complain, and they'll be within their rights |
| Embrace hiking as the default Sunday activity — the trail network exists precisely for this | Expect restaurants in small towns to be open; many close entirely on Sundays and Mondays |
| Book huts and cable cars in advance during peak weekend season | Assume "quiet hours" are a suggestion — in many cantons they're municipal law |

## Do's & Don'ts: Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept a Friday couscous invitation as the honour it is | Schedule anything demanding between roughly noon and 3pm on Friday — the country pauses for prayer and lunch |
| Visit a hammam at least once, respectfully, ideally with a local guide the first time | Wear a swimsuit expecting privacy norms from a European spa — hammam etiquette is different and modesty matters |
| Expect weekend souks to be louder, busier, and more social than the weekdays | Assume Saturday-Sunday is the universal weekend — Friday carries more cultural weight than either |

## Switzerland: Leisure as Regulated Silence

The Swiss weekend is not, as outsiders often assume, an absence of rules — it's the opposite. It's one of the most carefully legislated leisure environments on earth, and understanding that is the only way to enjoy it rather than resent it. Sunday quiet hours (Ruhetag, more or less) are real, enforced by neighbours with the moral authority of people who have never once been wrong about anything, and they cover everything from lawn care to recycling glass bottles into a bin. You will be informed, directly, if you violate this. I was.

But the payoff for accepting the silence is one of the best-organised outdoor leisure systems in the world. The Swiss don't experience Sunday as boredom; they experience it as a 65,000-kilometre invitation. Yellow-signed hiking trails, precisely timed to the minute, connect villages to alpine huts to train stations with a punctuality that borders on the spiritual. Families genuinely spend entire Sundays walking, not performing wellness for an audience, just walking, eating bread and cheese at 2,000 metres, and going home tired in the good way.

The trap for newcomers is treating the Sunday closures as an inconvenience to route around rather than a structural feature to plan for. Miss your Saturday grocery run and you'll spend Sunday eating crackers while your Swiss neighbours are somewhere above the treeline, entirely unbothered, having correctly assumed you'd figure it out eventually.

## Morocco: Leisure as Communal Excess

Morocco's weekend doesn't start on Saturday — culturally, it starts on Friday, and it starts with couscous. Friday is the day of communal prayer, and the tradition of a large family couscous lunch afterward is close to universal, observed by households that otherwise vary enormously in religious practice. If a Moroccan family invites you to Friday couscous, they are not offering you a meal; they are offering you an afternoon, possibly the whole day, and declining without a genuinely good reason will read as a small insult.

Where the Swiss weekend contracts into private, quiet, individual leisure, the Moroccan weekend expands into public, loud, collective leisure. Souks get busier, not quieter. Extended families gather. The hammam — the public bathhouse, still a weekly ritual for many Moroccan families regardless of income — becomes a genuinely social institution, part hygiene, part gossip network, part multi-generational bonding that has no real Western equivalent. Going once, with a local who can walk you through the etiquette (modesty, the right time of day, what you bring), is one of the more quietly profound things you can do as a visitor.

The friction point is pacing. Moroccan Friday afternoon effectively removes itself from the schedule of anyone trying to get something done — offices thin out, some shops close, traffic patterns shift around mosque times. Fighting this is pointless. The efficient move is to build your Friday around the pause rather than through it.

## The Verdict

Switzerland has engineered leisure into something almost administratively perfect — silence as a public good, nature as infrastructure. Morocco has kept leisure defiantly human — loud, communal, organised around food and family rather than a rulebook. I trust the Swiss trail network with my life. I trust a Moroccan grandmother's couscous with my soul. Only one of those is a fair fight, and it isn't close: give me the couscous.

## Conclusion

Switzerland's weekend rewards planning and punishes spontaneity; Morocco's rewards spontaneity and punishes overplanning. Show up to Zurich expecting Saturday-night shopping and you'll be disappointed. Show up to Fes on a Friday with a tight schedule and you'll be gently, deliciously derailed. Neither country is wrong. But only one of them will feed you until you can't move, and frankly, that's the weekend I want to be having.
