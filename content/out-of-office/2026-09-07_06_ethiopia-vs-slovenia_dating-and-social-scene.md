# One Country Still Asks the Family First. The Other Won't Even Make Eye Contact.

🇪🇹 Ethiopia · 🇸🇮 Slovenia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ethiopia's dating scene, particularly outside the increasingly Westernized bubble of central Addis Ababa, still runs substantially through family involvement, community reputation, and a courtship pace that would make a Western dating app feel like a car crash by comparison. Slovenia's dating scene, by contrast, runs through a level of social reserve so pronounced that foreigners frequently mistake basic Slovenian politeness for outright disinterest, sometimes for months. One of these countries wants to meet your relatives before your second date. The other might not make eye contact until your third mutual friend group hangout.

I have been quietly but thoroughly vetted by an Ethiopian family before anyone used the word "dating" out loud, and I have spent an entire Ljubljana winter convinced a perfectly friendly Slovenian colleague hated me, only to discover months later this was just Tuesday-level Slovenian warmth. Only one of these was actually about me.

## Do's & Don'ts: Ethiopia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect family involvement to increase, not decrease, the more serious a relationship becomes | Assume Addis Ababa's cafe-and-app dating scene represents how courtship works nationwide |
| Take a coffee ceremony invitation seriously — it's a genuine social and often courtship ritual, not just a drink | Rush physical affection in public; norms remain notably conservative outside specific urban social circles |
| Understand that community reputation carries real weight in how a relationship is perceived and discussed | Underestimate how differently dating operates across Ethiopia's dozens of distinct ethnic and religious communities |

## Do's & Don'ts: Slovenia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Be patient — Slovenian warmth is real but arrives slowly, often after repeated low-key group encounters | Mistake initial reserve for rejection; Slovenians are famously slow to open up, not unfriendly |
| Join hiking or outdoor social groups — Slovenia's dating and friendship scene runs heavily through shared activity | Push for early emotional disclosure; oversharing too soon reads as overwhelming, not endearing |
| Let a friendship develop naturally before assuming romantic interest either way — the line is often genuinely blurry | Expect loud, expressive courtship; Slovenian affection tends to be understated and shown through consistency |

## Ethiopia: Courtship as a Community Project

Dating in Ethiopia resists easy generalization more than almost anywhere else on this list, largely because the country contains dozens of distinct ethnic and religious communities — Amhara, Oromo, Tigrayan, and many others, spanning Orthodox Christian, Muslim, and Protestant traditions — each with its own norms around courtship, family involvement, and the pace at which a relationship is permitted to become public. What holds fairly consistently across most of them, though, particularly outside the more cosmopolitan pockets of central Addis Ababa, is that dating is rarely treated as a purely private matter between two people. Family awareness, and often approval, tends to enter the picture earlier and more heavily than most Western visitors expect, sometimes well before either party would describe things as "serious."

Addis Ababa itself has developed a genuinely modern, urban social and dating scene over the past decade — cafes, rooftop bars, and a small but real dating-app culture exist and are used, particularly among younger, educated, and diaspora-connected Ethiopians. But even here, the pace tends to run slower and more deliberately than the swipe-and-meet rhythm common elsewhere, and public displays of affection remain notably restrained by broader international standards, a norm that holds even among couples who are, by local standards, quite progressive.

The coffee ceremony deserves special mention as a courtship institution in its own right: an elaborate, unhurried ritual of roasting, grinding, and serving coffee over three rounds that can stretch well past an hour, traditionally hosted in a family or social setting. Being invited to one, particularly by someone's family, carries weight that a casual coffee shop meeting simply doesn't — it signals a level of welcome and seriousness that outsiders frequently miss entirely, mistaking it for simple hospitality rather than the quietly significant social gesture it often is.

## Slovenia: Warmth on a Very Long Delay

Slovenia's reputation for reserve is not exaggerated, and anyone arriving expecting the more overtly expressive social warmth of neighbouring Italy or Croatia will need a genuine recalibration period. Slovenians, by broad cultural consensus even among Slovenians themselves, are slow to open up to new people — initial interactions can read as polite but distant, and it can take months of repeated, low-stakes contact (through work, shared hobbies, or mutual friends) before anything resembling real warmth becomes visible. This is not coldness; it's a cultural preference for earning trust incrementally rather than performing friendliness upfront, and once that trust is established, Slovenian friendships and relationships tend to be genuinely deep and durable.

The practical dating scene reflects this rhythm closely. Ljubljana has a small but functional bar and cafe culture, and dating apps exist and are used, particularly among younger urban Slovenians, but the broader social pattern still favours meeting through existing social or activity-based circles over cold introductions. Slovenia's famously outdoor-oriented culture — hiking, cycling, skiing, all deeply embedded in national identity — functions as a major social and dating infrastructure in its own right; a hiking club or climbing group is, in practice, one of the more reliable ways to meet people, since shared activity provides the low-pressure repeated contact Slovenian social norms seem to require before anything opens up.

Foreigners, especially those from more overtly expressive dating cultures, frequently misread this reserve as disinterest and give up too early, which is, according to more than one Slovenian I've discussed this with, exactly the wrong response. The advice that comes up consistently is patience bordering on stubbornness: keep showing up, keep the interactions low-key and consistent, and the warmth — which is real, and once unlocked is unusually sincere — eventually arrives on its own schedule, not yours.

## The Verdict

Ethiopia offers structure, meaning, and a courtship process that treats a relationship as something a whole community has a stake in — which is either deeply reassuring or a lot of pressure, depending on your temperament. Slovenia offers almost the opposite: minimal external involvement, but a social climate so reserved that simply getting a first date requires more patience than most people bring to the entire relationship elsewhere.

I'll give the edge to Ethiopia, mostly because a coffee ceremony beats months of ambiguous Slovenian silence every time. At least you know where you stand, even if where you stand involves an aunt evaluating your prospects over the second pour.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a respondent describing the modern Addis dating scene noted that even confident, urban young Ethiopians will often still introduce a partner to family "earlier than most Western daters would consider normal, and treat it as completely unremarkable."*

> *Internations Ljubljana — one member wrote that it took an entire year of the same weekly hiking group before a Slovenian acquaintance invited her over for coffee, "which, I've since learned, is basically Slovenian for declaring undying friendship."*

> *expat.com Addis Ababa forum — a foreign resident wrote that being invited to a coffee ceremony by a Ethiopian family early in a relationship should be read as a much bigger signal than it initially appears.*

## Conclusion

Ethiopia will keep folding family and community into even the earliest stages of romance, treating a relationship as a shared project rather than a private one. Slovenia will keep making you wait, politely and at length, for warmth that turns out to be entirely genuine once it finally shows up. Bring patience to both, but bring considerably more of it to Ljubljana — the coffee ceremony, at least, gives you something to do while you wait.
