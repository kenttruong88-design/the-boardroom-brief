# The Line Is Sacred in One Country and Theoretical in the Other

🇮🇷 Iran vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have queued in sixty countries, which sounds like a brag and is actually a diagnosis. Somewhere around relocation number nine, I stopped being surprised by what a line of humans could do to me — cut in front, collapse into a scrum, strike up a conversation about my shoes — and started taking notes instead. Iran and Canada sit at opposite poles of this particular comedy, and if you've been posted between them, the whiplash is not imagined.

Canada queues like it's a civic religion: you arrive, you stand, you do not speak above a library murmur, and you apologise to the floor if you breathe near someone's personal bubble. Iran treats the queue as a theoretical construct — present in principle, the way "bedtime" exists for a toddler — while compensating with a warmth and physical closeness that will either delight you or send you back to your hotel room. Neither is wrong. Both will test you.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇮🇷 Iran

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet everyone you recognise, even briefly, before getting to business | Rush straight into a request without the social preamble — it reads as cold |
| Accept that "no" during *taarof* often means "ask me again" | Take a first refusal at face value and walk away insulted |
| Expect close physical proximity with people of your own gender | Assume closeness is flirtation — it almost never is |
| Let honking, traffic noise and crowded sidewalks become background hum | Interpret every honk as aggression directed at you personally |

### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Find the line, join the back of it, and never ask where it "really" starts | Cut ahead because you assumed nobody was technically "in" the queue |
| Keep roughly an arm's length of space when talking to near-strangers | Stand close enough to read someone's phone screen while queuing |
| Say "sorry" liberally, even when the collision was clearly their fault | Raise your voice in public to resolve a dispute |
| Keep small talk to weather, traffic, or sports | Open with questions about salary, religion, or who someone voted for |

## 🇮🇷 Iran: The Queue Is a Suggestion, the Conversation Is Not Optional

Let's be honest about what a "queue" means in Iran: a rough cluster of intent, not a line. At a bakery, a bank, or a Tehran metro window, the people nearest the front aren't necessarily the ones who arrived first — they're the ones who committed hardest. This isn't rudeness. It's a different operating system, one where social obligation outranks chronological order, and where the smart move is to hold your position with quiet confidence rather than physically assert it.

What replaces the orderly line is relentless, mandatory sociability. Forum regulars on expat.com's Iran board are blunt about it — one poster, farzan, notes that greeting and chatting with people you know is simply expected "unless you are in a hurry," while another, Pelindali, insists the deeper custom is reciprocal generosity: you keep offering until the other person's refusal turns sincere rather than ceremonial. That's *taarof* in miniature — a civility ritual where the first "no, please, after you" is theatre, and only the third one counts. Newcomers who take the first no at face value spend months mysteriously behind on social debts they didn't know they owed.

Noise and proximity follow their own logic too. Tehran's traffic has been described, accurately, as a cacophony — motorcycles threading pavements, taxis honking at pedestrians as communication rather than insult, drivers texting through intersections where crossing is, per expat blogger Pontia Fallahi of My Persian Corner, "a skill to be learned" rather than a right of way. Shared taxis mean strangers sit thigh-to-thigh for a whole route and sometimes become friends by the end of it. Personal space contracts dramatically with people of your own gender — hands held, cheeks kissed, shoulders draped — while staying formal, by design, between genders in public, where overt affection is a line nobody in authority wants you testing. The result is a public sphere that's loud, warm, physically unbothered, and governed by rules nobody writes down or explains. You absorb them through repeated minor humiliation.

## 🇨🇦 Canada: Order Enforced by Passive-Aggressive Silence

Canada's relationship with the queue borders on devotional. Simon Fraser University's own orientation guidance for international students doesn't hedge: Canadians believe whoever arrived first should be served first, full stop, and grow visibly uncomfortable — not shouty, just uncomfortable, in that particularly Canadian register — when someone skips ahead. An Ipsos Reid national survey found 84% of Canadians think their compatriots are growing *less* patient about lining up, with clinic registration and retail checkout tied as the two most despised waits. Quebecers loathe hospital queues most; British Columbians are the most forgiving. Everyone, nationwide, wants more staff and more self-checkout machines, which tells you everything about a culture that would rather automate a problem than confront a person about it.

That instinct — automate or absorb, never confront — is the real Canadian tell. The preferred distance for a conversation or a queue is roughly an arm's length, the SFU guidance notes, and privacy is sacrosanct even in crowded spaces. Bump into someone on the sidewalk and you'll both apologise, sometimes for something unambiguously their fault. Raising your voice to resolve a public dispute is a small act of social self-harm; Canadians would rather sigh pointedly or complain to a friend later than tell a queue-jumper off to their face. Small talk stays on weather, commutes and hockey scores — money, politics and religion get the same caution Iranians reserve for a stranger's shoes in their home. The public realm looks, from outside, eerily calm. It is not calm. It is just extremely well mannered about its rage.

## The Verdict

If you want a culture that tells you, kindly but firmly, exactly where you stand — physically, socially, chronologically — Canada wins on predictability every time. The line is real, the distance is measured, the apology pre-loaded. You'll never be confused about your place, even as you suspect everyone around you is seething.

Iran wins on something Canada can't manufacture: texture. The queue might be fictional, but the warmth isn't, and the closeness that unsettles a first-timer is the same closeness that gets you invited to a stranger's dinner table within a week. Canadian order is comfortable. Iranian disorder is, frankly, more alive.

My sympathies go to whoever relocates directly between the two. You'll spend your first Tehran month standing too far back in bakery scrums, and your first Toronto month apologising to furniture. Both phases pass. Neither is dignified.

## Conclusion

Neither country hands you a rulebook, which is, infuriatingly, the point of culture. Canada's rules stay unspoken because everyone already agrees on them and finds saying them aloud slightly vulgar. Iran's shift with relationship, context and how many times you've been offered tea. Learn to read a Canadian sigh and you'll survive Toronto. Learn to say no three times and mean it the third time, and you'll survive Tehran. Get it backwards — apologise profusely in a bakery scrum, or charm your way to the front of a post office line — and you'll be, politely or otherwise, put back in your place. Pack patience either way; you'll just spend it in different currencies.
