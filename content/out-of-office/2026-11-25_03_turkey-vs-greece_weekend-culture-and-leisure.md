# Turkey Eats Breakfast for Three Hours and Greece Calls It a Light Snack

🇹🇷 Turkey vs 🇬🇷 Greece

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two countries share a sea, a coffee, a cuisine they argue about at funerals and an absolute conviction that nobody should do anything useful on a Saturday. The Turks call the coffee Turkish. The Greeks call it Greek. Both agree that you should not drink it quickly, and both will explain why with the energy of a legal dispute.

I have spent more weekends than is medically wise on both shores, and I would like to retire the word 'laid-back', because it implies a lack of effort. Nobody is lazy here. The weekend in Turkey and Greece is a professional discipline, with rituals, rankings and a firm hierarchy of who sits where. The difference is what the ritual is built around. One is a table covered in small plates before noon. The other is a table that never really clears until the sun does.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇹🇷 Turkey ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat kahvalti, the long weekend breakfast, as the main social event | Show up expecting a quick coffee and a pastry |
| Accept the second and third glass of tea | Say no firmly and look offended |
| Have your main meal at lunchtime if you plan to sleep early | Book an eleven o'clock dinner and wonder why the place is dead |
| Take a ferry or a walk by the water after eating | Rush back to a screen because the plates are cleared |

| 🇬🇷 Greece ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Linger over a single coffee in the kafeneio or cafe for as long as you like | Ask for the bill after the first drink |
| Share plates and order for the table | Insist on your own separate dish |
| Plan Sunday around a long family lunch | Expect to get anything done in the shops after midday |
| Take a name day seriously and bring something | Wander in empty-handed because it was a casual invitation |

## 🇹🇷 Turkey

Turkey takes breakfast so seriously that it is basically a second job. The Saturday and Sunday kahvalti is a spread of cheeses, olives, eggs, honey, clotted cream, tomatoes, fried dough and a rotating cast of jams, delivered in a cascade of tiny plates until the table disappears. It is not eaten, it is attended. An expat in Alanya noted that life in Turkey runs on a different rhythm entirely: shop hours, working hours, sleep and sunlight all move around, and he now eats lunch out rather than dinner because he goes to bed early. In summer he wakes his wife at sunrise so they can walk or swim before the coffee and conversation, which is the most Mediterranean sentence I have read this year.

The hospitality is real and sometimes overwhelming. A travel writer in Istanbul was invited into a cafe by the owner for free Turkish coffee and then tea, with no charge and no sales pitch, and I have had that experience so many times I stopped being surprised. Tea flows constantly. The shopkeeper pours it, the neighbour pours it, the man fixing your boiler pours it, and refusing is an act of mild aggression. People are friendly. Several expats note, however, that they find it hard to turn that friendliness into real friendship, and that bureaucracy works wonderfully once you pay someone who knows the system to handle it for you.

The weekend sprawls outdoors. Families on the Bosphorus, picnics under plane trees, ferries crossing at sunset, a whole nation of strollers eating sunflower seeds and arguing about football. It is warm, social and delightfully noisy. And the sun, as ever, gets a vote: in summer, the day starts early and ends late, with the afternoon surrendered entirely to rest.

What strikes most expats in Turkey is how the day itself stretches. Shops open late and close late, the evening meal can start at nine and the city is still awake at midnight, but the hours are strangely flexible. People tell you they adapt in months, not days, because opening times, working hours, sleep cycles and sunlight all change how you plan a day. The weekend is not a different state so much as the same life with more tea in it. A walk by the water, a game of backgammon in a corner cafe, a ferry to the islands, and the slow realisation that you have not checked your phone for two hours. It is not an accident. It is a social design.

## 🇬🇷 Greece

Greece's weekend runs on a different engine, which is leisure as a philosophical position. The coffee is the unit of time. A frappe can sit in front of you for two hours and nobody will so much as glance at the bill, because the table is rented by the hour in everything but price. The taverna lunch is the Sunday cathedral: grandmothers, cousins, someone's boyfriend, a dog, a fish nobody ordered, and a conversation about politics that ends in pudding. Time is not spent in Greece. It is simply slow-poured.

The caveat is that the quality of any given meal is a lottery. One travel writer in 2012 found the Piraeus tavern she picked bland: undressed cabbage, flat hummus, tired sardines, with the grilled haloumi the only star. That is one plate on one day, and anyone who has eaten properly in the islands knows the ceiling is stratospheric. But the lesson holds: Greece is about the occasion, not necessarily the cooking. You go for the people at the next table, the sea, the long afternoon, the sense that nothing is expected of you except staying.

It is also a country that closes. Shops shut at lunch, many do not reopen on Sundays, and the idea of nipping out for a screwdriver on a Sunday is cute. Name days, saints' days, long weekends and holidays all pull the calendar loose from any sensible work structure. This is frustrating for about a week and then it becomes the best thing about the place. You stop resenting the shutters and start admiring the nerve.

Greece, meanwhile, has made a quiet art of the pause. The idea that you can sit with one coffee, watch the world, and nobody will move you along is precious and increasingly rare in a continent of timed tables and booking apps. The ritual of the Sunday family lunch, the neighbourhood kafeneio where old men argue about politics and the sudden, spontaneous trip to the sea because the weather turned are not tourist inventions. They are how people live. The risk, of course, is that you start to believe it. You will return to your job in a grey office and find that your sense of urgency has been confiscated at customs.

## The Verdict

I give it to Turkey, narrowly, and I say that as someone who adores Greece. Turkey's weekend feels more generous. You are pulled into it. The table grows, the tea refills, strangers become hosts, and the sheer energy of a city like Istanbul on a Saturday morning is a gift that no island can match.

Greece is more serene, more beautiful and more self-contained. It is also, on the evidence, more indifferent to your presence. A Greek weekend is a gorgeous private performance that you are permitted to watch. A Turkish weekend wants you to sit down and pass the cheese. If you want to be left alone with a view and a very long coffee, Greece. If you want to be fed until you feel a moral obligation to lie down, Turkey.

Both will make you worse at your job and better at your life. In my professional opinion that is the only trade worth making. I just wish the Greeks would open a shop on a Sunday.

## Conclusion

Both of these countries have worked out that the point of a weekend is not to recover from the week. It is to remind yourself that the week was an unreasonable imposition. The Turks do it with breakfast, the Greeks with lunch, and neither is interested in your productivity metrics.

My advice is to book nothing, eat everything and take the afternoon off from being a person with plans. If someone offers you tea, say yes. If someone offers you a name day party, say yes and bring wine. And if you try to buy a screwdriver on a Greek Sunday, the sea will still be there, which is more than anyone can say for your plans.
