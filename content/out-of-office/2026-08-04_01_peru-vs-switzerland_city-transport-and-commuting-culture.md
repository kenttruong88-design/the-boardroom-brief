# Punctuality Is a Personality Trait in One of These Countries, and a Rumour in the Other

🇵🇪 Peru vs 🇨🇭 Switzerland — City Transport & Commuting Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have been flung across a Lima intersection by a combi driver who treated the concept of "lane" as a Western imperialist construct, and I have stood on a Zurich platform watching locals visibly age with fury because a tram was ninety seconds late. Both experiences taught me something profound about humanity: we will build entire moral universes around how we get to work.

Switzerland has turned commuting into a religion, complete with sacred texts (the timetable, laminated, memorised, worshipped) and a clergy (SBB conductors, who will look at you with the quiet disappointment of a disappointed uncle if your ticket is wrong). Peru has turned commuting into a live-action bargaining simulator where the bus doesn't really stop, the fare is a suggestion, and the destination is more of a vibe than a guarantee. One of these systems runs on 99.5% punctuality. The other runs on faith, elbows, and a driver's cousin hanging out the door yelling the route like an auctioneer having a breakdown.

## Do's and Don'ts

### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Have small coins ready — drivers hate breaking large bills mid-swerve | Expect the combi to fully stop; it will slow to a menacing crawl and that's your window |
| Learn the hand signal for your stop before you need it | Assume the posted route number means the bus still goes there |
| Use the Metropolitano for anything remotely time-sensitive | Trust a taxi without agreeing the price before the door closes |
| Download a ride-hailing app for late nights | Sit near the door if you're precious about personal space |

### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a Halbtax card immediately if you're staying more than a month | Talk loudly on the phone in the "quiet" carriage — you will be silently judged into next week |
| Validate your ticket before boarding, always | Assume you can wing it without a ticket — inspectors are relentless and unimpressed by excuses |
| Trust the connection times exactly as printed, down to the minute | Eat something smelly on the train unless you enjoy being a pariah |

## Peru: Organised Chaos, Deeply Organised

Lima's combis are not public transport in the way a Swiss person understands the phrase. They are a decentralised, driver-owned, commission-based transit network that has evolved, through decades of total absence of regulation, into something that actually works — provided you accept that "working" means "eventually, probably, with elbows." The driver's assistant, the *cobrador*, hangs out the open door bellowing the route at a volume and speed designed to be intelligible only to people who already know it, which is either the single most exclusionary transport system on Earth or a very effective Darwinian filter for tourists who haven't done their homework. I fall into the latter category more often than I'd like to admit.

The Metropolitano, Lima's dedicated busway, is the one genuinely civilised option — fast, air-conditioned, and running on a card system that doesn't require you to negotiate your own survival. Use it. The Línea 1 metro is limited but reliable on the routes it covers. Everything else is a glorious improvisation: motorbike taxis (*mototaxis*) that treat traffic laws as a rough first draft, and a general driving culture where the horn is used not as a warning but as a form of continuous ambient communication, roughly translating to "I exist, and so do my intentions, which are unclear even to me."

What nobody tells you is that Lima traffic isn't actually dangerous in the way it looks — it's dangerous in the way white-water rafting is dangerous, which is to say: everyone involved is highly skilled at not hitting you, right up until the one time they aren't. You adapt fast. Within a month you'll be crossing six-lane avenues with the calm of a local, making eye contact with an oncoming bus like it's a mutual understanding rather than a threat. This is either character growth or brain damage. Time will tell.

## Switzerland: The Timetable Is Law and You Are Beneath It

The Swiss transport system is, by any global measure, an act of civic genius — trains connecting to buses connecting to boats with connection windows measured in single-digit minutes, all of it running with a punctuality rate that would make a Peruvian combi driver weep with shame or possibly just laugh in your face. This is not an exaggeration for effect. I have made a ferry connection in a village of four hundred people because the bus arrived at the dock exactly, to the minute, as printed in 2019.

But precision has a cost, and the cost is joy. Swiss commuting culture treats the train as a solemn shared space where eye contact is an act of aggression, phone calls are a war crime, and the correct emotional register at all times is "neutral, possibly slightly resentful." God help you if the 8:14 becomes the 8:15 — I have watched grown adults sigh with the wounded gravity of people who have just learned a family member has disappointed them. There is no room for spontaneity here. The system doesn't want your charm. It wants your validated ticket and your silence.

And it is, infuriatingly, worth it. You will never miss a meeting because of a train in Switzerland. You will occasionally miss a train by four seconds and be made to feel, by the collective disapproving energy of an entire platform, like you personally destabilised the Confederation.

## The Verdict

Switzerland wins on function, obviously — nobody is choosing Lima's traffic for its emotional warmth over Zurich's trains for their surgical precision if the metric is "will I get to my meeting." But Peru wins on something the Swiss system has engineered out entirely: the sense that you're participating in something alive. A combi ride is chaotic, occasionally terrifying, and completely, gloriously human — a driver, his cousin, and forty strangers negotiating survival together at 40 kilometres an hour. A Swiss train is a machine you are lucky to be inside of, and it will never once acknowledge your existence. I'd rather be shouted at by a cobrador than ignored by a nation.

## Conclusion

Peru will teach you to negotiate, improvise, and trust strangers with your literal body. Switzerland will teach you that a society can optimise a system so completely that the humans inside it become an afterthought. Neither is wrong. Both are, in their own ways, insane. If you want a commute that respects your time, move to Zurich and learn to love silence. If you want a commute that respects your capacity for chaos, move to Lima and learn to love elbows. Just don't try to bring Swiss expectations to a Peruvian combi — I've watched that particular delusion die in real time, somewhere on Avenida Arequipa, at rush hour, with a cobrador shouting the fare into your face like it's your fault for being confused.
