# Spain Gives You a Doctor, Colombia Gives You a Waiting-Room Hierarchy, and Both Beat a Hospital Bill

🇪🇸 Spain · 🇨🇴 Colombia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nothing reveals a country's real character like the moment you are lying on a trolley in its corridor wondering what your insurance actually covers. I have been ill in more countries than most people have visited, and I can report that the quality of the care matters rather less than the quality of the paperwork surrounding it. Spain and Colombia are both countries where the medicine is perfectly competent and the administration is a performance art. They simply choose different theatres.

Spain runs a public system built on a 1986 General Health Law and, since a 2018 royal decree-law, nominally open to every resident. Colombia built something stranger in 1993: a compulsory insurance market in which private-ish companies called EPS stand between you and the doctor like a bouncer with a clipboard. One country trusts the state to look after you. The other trusts a spreadsheet. Neither is entirely wrong, and both will keep you waiting.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇪🇸 Spain**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm which autonomous community runs your care; the system is decentralised and the rules differ | Assume the public system will take you automatically on arrival; one expat in Granada waited about a year |
| Buy private cover from a licensed Spanish insurer if your visa demands it | Rely on a foreign policy for a residence application; the consulate wants a company licensed to operate in Spain |
| Use the pharmacy first for minor ailments; many drugs need no prescription | Expect antibiotics over the counter; they need a prescription |
| Learn that 112 is the emergency number before you need it | Treat a private emergency visit as a luxury; it is often quicker |

**🇨🇴 Colombia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Enrol with an EPS; Law 100 of 1993 made health insurance compulsory | Imagine you can float outside the system with no cover |
| Ask about a Plan Complementario or Medicina Prepagada if you want shorter queues | Assume the cheapest EPS tier comes with a speedy appointment |
| Check which clinics and providers your EPS actually contracts with | Walk into the nearest shiny clinic and expect it to accept your plan |
| Keep every authorisation and paper record | Expect the system to remember you between visits |

## 🇪🇸 Spain: The Welfare State With a Pharmacy Habit

Start with the architecture, because it explains the mood. The Sistema Nacional de Salud grew out of the 1986 General Health Law, and in 2018 Real Decreto-Ley 7/2018 restored access for all residents, undocumented migrants included, after a 2012 model that tied care to employment or insurance status. It is run by the autonomous communities, which means Spain has seventeen slightly different opinions about how a hospital should work. Spend a month in Andalucía and a month in Catalonia and you will meet two distinct bureaucracies wearing one flag.

For the foreigner, the practical truth is that residency paperwork and healthcare paperwork are fused. Spanish consulates ask visa applicants for medical insurance from a company licensed in Spain, and the numbers people quote are almost insultingly modest: a Granada couple reported paying about €188 a month between them for full cover with no copay, a Salamanca expat about €150 for a Sanitas plan. Compare that with what an American pays to be told "it's probably a virus" and you will understand why they weep at the pharmacy counter. One expat reported custom orthotics at about $45 against roughly $300 back home.

Private care is where the foreign visitor finds the speed. The Granada expat who couldn't get into the public system for roughly a year found private enrolment "not difficult" and an emergency visit at a Vithas hospital done in under two hours, which they described as unheard of in the States. Meanwhile births told a more mixed story: a Barcelona delivery assigned through Social Security meant no pain relief and disappointing breastfeeding support, while a Granada state-hospital birth was warm and well midwifed. Spain is a lottery of postcodes, so choose your postcode like a grown-up.

The pharmacy, though, is the national treasure. Spaniards treat their farmacia like a first-line clinic, selling many drugs without a prescription, though not antibiotics, which is the one rule I will actually defend. Ten per cent of the population uses private insurance, health spending sits well below the OECD average, and somehow the doctors-per-head figures are among Europe's best. It works because everyone mutters about it and uses it anyway.

## 🇨🇴 Colombia: The Insurance Market in a Linen Suit

Colombia's story begins with a statistic that should embarrass richer nations: coverage went from roughly 21 per cent before 1993 to about 97 per cent by 2023. Law 100 of 1993 made insurance compulsory and created the EPS, the Entidades Promotoras de Salud, which sell service packages and contract providers, with the Superintendencia Nacional de Salud supervising the lot. Workers and employers pay in through the contributory track, while the poorest are covered by a subsidised track assigned through SISBEN. It is genuinely impressive social engineering, and it feels like it.

What it feels like to the expat is a system with a velvet rope. The basic EPS tier gets you care. A Plan Complementario or, higher still, Medicina Prepagada gets you priority service, the sort of tier where the receptionist knows your name rather than your authorisation number. Anyone who tells you Colombian healthcare is simply "cheap" has met the prepagada end of it and not the queue at seven in the morning for a specialist referral. Cheap is a price. Fast is a tier.

I cannot offer you a charming forum anecdote here, because the Colombia expat page I tried to read refused to load, and I do not invent testimony for your entertainment. What I can say is that the official architecture rewards the organised. Know which EPS you belong to, know which clinics it contracts, keep your papers, and treat every authorisation as a small legal document. The people who thrive in Colombian healthcare are the ones who treat it as a project rather than a service.

The honest strength is that the country committed, loudly and legally, to insuring nearly everyone, and the staff I have met in its clinics were frequently better trained than the buildings suggest. The honest weakness is that your experience depends on who pays for your tier. In Spain, your postcode decides your care. In Colombia, your plan does.

## The Verdict

Spain wins, but only on points, and only if you buy private cover and stop pretending the public system is waiting breathlessly for you. The value is absurd: a couple's full coverage for less than one American dentist's weekend, a pharmacy that behaves like a neighbour, and an emergency room that can see you before your coffee has gone cold. The public system is excellent and also slow to admit foreigners, so the smart move is the sensible hybrid that every long-term expat quietly arrives at.

Colombia loses nothing in ambition and gains plenty in ingenuity, but it asks more of you. You will be navigating an insurer, a tier, a provider network and a referral chain, all in Spanish, all in person. If you are the kind of person who enjoys an administrative puzzle, you will do fine and may even feel smug. If you are the kind of person who wants to hand over a card and be treated, Spain is your country.

My sting is this: neither system is the problem. The problem is the foreigner who arrives assuming healthcare is a thing that happens to you, rather than a thing you set up in advance with the same care you give your tax return. Do the paperwork before you need the doctor, not from the trolley.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat Exchange, "15 Expats Talk about Health Insurance and Healthcare in Spain" — a Granada expat says they weren't eligible for the public system and waited about a year, but private enrolment was easy and a Vithas emergency visit took under two hours, which they called unheard of in the US.*

> *Expat Exchange, "15 Expats Talk about Health Insurance and Healthcare in Spain" — a Tenerife expat says a dental procedure cost about 12 per cent of the US price, with a same-day, English-speaking dentist. Other answers on the page disagree about whether residents can use the public system at all.*

## Conclusion

I have eaten my way through sixty countries and been ill in a fair few of them, and the rule holds everywhere: healthcare is a logistics problem wearing a white coat. Spain has solved the logistics for people who arrive with a licensed policy and a sense of humour. Colombia has solved the logistics for the nation and left the individual to solve it again, in person, with documents.

Pick Spain if you want to be looked after. Pick Colombia if you want to be impressed by what a country can build when it decides everyone gets covered. And in either case, learn the word for pharmacy before you learn the word for goodbye, because you will say the first one considerably more often than the second.
