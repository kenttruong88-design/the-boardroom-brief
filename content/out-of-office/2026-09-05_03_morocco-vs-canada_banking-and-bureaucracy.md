# One Country Makes You Wait in a Room, the Other Makes You Wait in a Queue for the Room

🇲🇦 Morocco × 🇨🇦 Canada
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have opened a bank account in Casablanca that required four visits, two stamps I never saw explained, and a branch manager who eventually just decided he liked me and pushed it through out of what I can only describe as personal generosity. I have also opened a bank account in Toronto that required a Social Insurance Number, a proof-of-address utility bill I did not yet have because I did not yet have an apartment, and a phone tree so polite it nearly apologised me into giving up entirely.

Bureaucracy, wherever you find it, runs on one of two engines: relationships or rules. Morocco runs on the former with a warmth that can bend the system for you if you're patient and polite. Canada runs on the latter with a rigidity so relentlessly courteous you'll thank the system for rejecting your form.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇲🇦 Morocco**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a rapport with one specific branch employee | Expect the same process to work identically at another branch |
| Bring more original documents and photocopies than seem necessary | Assume a stamped form means the process is finished |
| Be patient and warm — relationships genuinely move things faster | Show visible frustration; it rarely speeds anything up |
| Ask directly what's missing rather than guessing | Assume online banking replaces the need for in-person visits |

**🇨🇦 Canada**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your Social Insurance Number arranged before anything else | Try to open an account without a permanent address first |
| Book appointments in advance — walk-ins can mean long waits | Expect a human to override the system for you, ever |
| Keep every reference number from every phone call | Assume "no reply in 5 business days" means it's been forgotten |
| Use online banking apps — they're genuinely excellent once set up | Expect the same efficiency from the in-branch experience |

## Morocco: Bureaucracy as a Relationship, Not a Transaction

Opening a bank account in Morocco is less a procedure and more a negotiation you didn't know you'd entered. You'll be told you need your passport, a residency document, proof of income, and two photos — and then, upon arrival, told you actually also need a letter from your employer, a specific stamp from a different office across town, and ideally a personal introduction from someone the branch manager already trusts. None of this is written down anywhere official. It is simply known, the way certain restaurant menus are "known" to have off-menu items if you ask the right waiter the right way.

What redeems this entirely is the human warmth threaded through it. Once a bank employee decides they like you — and this can happen astonishingly fast, sometimes over nothing more than a shared joke about the weather or a compliment on their office plant — doors open that seemed permanently shut an hour earlier. I've watched a manager personally walk a stack of paperwork to another department rather than make a customer come back a third time, simply because he'd taken a shine to them. This is not corruption; it's a system that runs on trust between people rather than trust in forms, and once you're inside that trust, it moves remarkably fast.

The frustration, if you're coming from a rules-based culture, is the sheer unpredictability. The same document requirement can differ from branch to branch, week to week, clerk to clerk. Ask two different employees the same question and you may get two confidently delivered, entirely contradictory answers. The trick — and every long-term expat in Casablanca or Rabat will tell you this — is to stop asking "what is the rule" and start asking "who can help me," because in Morocco, the second question is always the more useful one.

## Canada: Bureaucracy as an Unbreakable, Politely Delivered System

Canada, by glorious contrast, has built a bureaucracy so procedurally consistent that you could open an account in Halifax using the exact same steps you'd use in Calgary, down to the letter. This is either deeply reassuring or soul-crushingly rigid depending on your mood and how badly you need that account opened today. The Social Insurance Number is the sun around which every other administrative task orbits — get it, and doors open in predictable sequence; don't have it yet, and you will discover just how many things quietly require it that nobody mentioned up front.

The politeness is real, and it is also, somehow, the obstacle. A Canadian customer service representative will apologise to you with such warmth and sincerity while explaining that no, actually, they cannot help you, that you'll leave the call feeling oddly comforted despite having achieved absolutely nothing. "I'm so sorry about that, that must be frustrating" is doing an enormous amount of load-bearing work in a system where the actual answer is "the rule does not bend, for you or for anyone."

Once you're through the initial setup gauntlet, though, Canadian banking rewards you with genuinely excellent digital infrastructure — apps that work, transfers that clear same-day, fraud protection that actually protects. The system's rigidity, once it has your data correctly filed, becomes a strength rather than a wall. The hard part is purely the gate at the front: proving who you are, where you live, and why you deserve to exist in the system at all, in an order so specific that missing one document sends you back to the very start, politely, with a form letter thanking you for your patience.

## The Verdict

Morocco will exhaust you with inconsistency and then, unexpectedly, save you with kindness. Canada will comfort you with consistency and then quietly grind you down with a system that has never once made an exception for anybody, including, I suspect, itself. If you need speed and don't mind playing the relationship game, Morocco can surprise you. If you need certainty and can survive weeks of paperwork before the machine finally accepts you, Canada delivers reliably — just don't expect anyone to break a rule on your behalf, however nicely they apologise for it.

Given a straight choice, I'd rather charm my way through Morocco's uncertainty than be politely stonewalled by Canada's perfect, immovable system. At least in Rabat, someone might actually just like you enough to help.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations Casablanca — paraphrased: a longtime expat said the moment they stopped filing complaints and started bringing the branch manager mint tea from the shop next door, their account issues resolved within the week.*

> *r/PersonalFinanceCanada — paraphrased: a newcomer described spending six weeks unable to open a full bank account because their SIN and proof-of-address documents kept arriving in the "wrong" order relative to what each institution required.*

> *Quora, "How long does it really take to open a bank account in Morocco?" — paraphrased: a respondent said the honest answer depends entirely on who you're speaking to that day, not on any published timeline.*

## Conclusion

Somewhere between Morocco's warm chaos and Canada's cold precision lies the fantasy bureaucracy nobody has built yet — fast, human, and consistent all at once. Until that exists, you're choosing your poison: charm your way through unpredictability, or endure a flawless system that will never once bend for you, however politely it explains why not. I know which one lets me leave with a friend as well as a bank account, and it isn't the one with the maple leaf on the form.
