# Your Dominican Neighbours Will Know Your Business by Tuesday. Your Croatian Ones Knew It in 1987.

🇩🇴 Dominican Republic vs 🇭🇷 Croatia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular species of expat who moves abroad "for the community" and then spends eighteen months furious that the community has opinions about them. I have met this person in fourteen countries. I have, on two unfortunate occasions, been this person. The Dominican Republic and Croatia are the two places most likely to cure you of the fantasy, because both have neighbourhood life in industrial quantities — they simply dispense it through completely different valves.

In a Dominican barrio, community is a sound system. It arrives at 7am as merengue from the colmado, continues as a domino game on the pavement at 4pm, and concludes at 1am as an argument about baseball you did not start but are now somehow part of. In a Croatian stari grad, community is a filing cabinet. It is silent, it is thorough, and it contains a record of every time you have left your bins out on the wrong day since the Habsburgs. One will adopt you. The other will observe you. Neither will leave you alone, and anyone who promised otherwise was selling you a villa.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇩🇴 Dominican Republic**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the name of whoever runs your nearest colmado — they are your bank, courier and news agency | Treat the colmado as a mere shop and walk in without greeting everyone present |
| Say "buenas" to every human you pass, including the ones asleep in plastic chairs | Complain about music volume before you've lived there a month — you'll lose, loudly |
| Accept the plate of sancocho when it appears; return the dish with something in it | Return an empty container. That is a declaration of war |
| Keep a stash of small change and patience for the apagón (power cut) chats | Assume a closed door means privacy — it means "knock louder" |

**🇭🇷 Croatia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend the building meeting (sastanak stanara) at least once so they know your face | Hang laundry on the street-facing façade in a protected old town |
| Greet the elderly lady on the ground floor. She is the building's actual government | Drill, hammer or hoover between 2pm and 5pm — that is sacred sleep time in many buildings |
| Learn the rules for the shared stairwell cleaning rota, whether written or implied | Assume summer party rules apply in October. The islands go quiet and stay quiet |
| Bring a small gift (coffee, a cake) when introducing yourself | Mistake reserve for hostility — it thaws, slowly, over rakija |

## The Dominican Republic: A Neighbourhood With the Volume Permanently at Eleven

The unit of Dominican neighbourhood life is not the house, the street or the parish. It is the colmado — the corner shop-cum-bar-cum-social-club that sits on roughly every third corner of every barrio from Santo Domingo to Santiago. The colmado sells rice, cooking gas, single cigarettes, phone credit and Presidente beer so cold it hurts your teeth. It also delivers, on a motorbike, within eleven minutes, to people who have not given an address, because the delivery boy knows who you are. You told nobody. Everybody knows anyway.

This is the bargain. In a Dominican barrio you will never be lonely and you will never be anonymous. The woman across the street will notice you have been coughing and send her son over with a ginger tea. The man next door will notice your new girlfriend and have a view. Doors stay open because the heat demands it, and an open door is read, correctly, as a social invitation. Plastic chairs migrate onto the pavement at dusk and a nightly parliament convenes to discuss politics, prices, the national baseball league and, eventually, you.

The noise is the thing foreigners cannot get over, and it is also the thing they are wrong about. The speaker on the pickup truck selling avocados, the neighbour's bachata, the rooster with a personality disorder — these are not antisocial. They are the neighbourhood proving it is alive. Complaining about it marks you, instantly and permanently, as someone who has mistaken a Caribbean city for a Surrey cul-de-sac.

The flip side is solidarity of a kind wealthier countries have forgotten. When the power fails — and the apagón remains a regular guest in many areas — the street pools generators, candles and gossip. When someone dies, the whole block turns up for the nine nights of prayers. When you are ill, you will be fed whether you like it or not. Gated expat compounds in Punta Cana exist precisely so people can avoid all this, and I consider every one of them a monument to missing the point.

## Croatia: Community Life, Administered

A Croatian neighbourhood does not throw its arms around you. It assesses you from behind a lace curtain, forms an opinion, and files it. Then, somewhere between six months and six years later, it invites you for coffee, and you realise you have been accepted into something older and sturdier than anything a Dominican street party could offer.

The engine room is the apartment building. Most urban Croatians live in flats, many of them the socialist-era blocks of Zagreb's Novi Zagreb or Split's Split 3, and each building functions as a small, fractious republic. There is a building representative (predstavnik stanara), a reserve fund everyone pays into, and meetings where the fate of the lift, the façade and the hallway lightbulb are debated with the intensity of a Dayton negotiation. Skip these meetings and decisions will be made about you, without you, and billed to you.

In the old stone towns of Dalmatia — Trogir, Korčula, the backstreets of Split — neighbourhood life is vertical and intimate. Houses share walls that are older than most countries. Your neighbour can hear you sneeze, and will say "nazdravlje" through the stone. Laundry lines crisscross alleys a metre and a half wide, and there is a strict, unwritten grammar about whose sheets hang where. The afternoon rest hour is observed with genuine seriousness: drill a shelf at 3pm and expect a delegation.

Then there is the seasonal split personality. From June to September the coast belongs to tourists, and locals retreat into a defensive crouch. In November, the islands empty to a few hundred souls, and the community that remains becomes extraordinarily tight — the same twelve people in the same café, the same fisherman bringing the same neighbour the same fish. Winter on a Croatian island is the most intense neighbourhood experience in Europe. Arrive in October and you will find out, very quickly, whether you are a resident or merely a summer.

## The Verdict

The Dominican Republic wins on warmth, and it isn't close. There is no country I have lived in where a stranger becomes a neighbour faster, or where a neighbour becomes family with less paperwork. If you are the kind of person who wants to be known — who wants somebody to notice when you haven't come out of the house for two days — a Dominican barrio will do it better than any therapist.

But Croatia wins on depth, and that matters more than people moving abroad at forty-five like to admit. Dominican community is instantly available and slightly interchangeable; you are welcomed because welcoming is the culture. Croatian community is earned, and once earned it is load-bearing. The neighbour who took four years to say more than "dobar dan" will be the one who drives you to hospital at 3am and waters your tomatoes for a month without being asked.

So: go Dominican if you want a party. Go Croatian if you want a pension. And if you want neither — if what you actually want is a nice view and no one to talk to — please stay home and stop moving into other people's neighbourhoods.

## Conclusion

Neighbourhood life is the most honest test of whether you actually live somewhere or are merely staying there on an extended basis. The Dominican Republic tests you with noise and generosity; Croatia tests you with silence and procedure. Both are tests of the same thing — whether you are willing to be part of something you don't control.

Most expats fail both. They buy the view, install the blackout blinds, order everything online and wonder why, three years in, nobody knows their name. That isn't the neighbourhood's failing. It's yours.

The colmado owner will remember you for a decade. The lady on the ground floor in Split will remember you for considerably longer. The only question is whether what they remember is worth remembering. Take the sancocho. Go to the meeting. Stop hiding behind the gate.
