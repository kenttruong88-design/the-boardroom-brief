# The Dutch Don't Go to the Gym, They Just Go to Work. Peru Will See You at the Park at 6am, Fully Committed

🇳🇱 Netherlands 🇵🇪 Peru
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The Netherlands has quietly solved fitness by refusing to treat it as a separate activity at all. Cycling isn't exercise here, it's transport, which means the entire population gets a daily cardio session without ever setting foot in a gym or owning a single piece of activewear. Peru, particularly in Lima's wealthier and increasingly health-conscious districts, has gone the opposite direction entirely: deliberate, structured, often communal outdoor fitness, parks full of people doing serious sunrise workouts before a day that usually starts brutally early.

I've watched a Dutch grandmother cycle past me in Amsterdam carrying two children, a bag of groceries, and what appeared to be a small piece of furniture, all while checking her phone, and I've also stood in Lima's Parque Kennedy at 6am watching a bootcamp class that looked more disciplined than anything I've ever paid a personal trainer to inflict on me. Neither country calls what they're doing "working out." Only one of them means it.

## Do's & Don'ts: Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a proper bike and use it for everything — it's the actual fitness plan | Wear full cycling lycra for a five-minute commute — locals will notice |
| Expect cyclists everywhere, always, in every weather | Assume gym membership is the default route to fitness — it's a minority habit |
| Use bike lanes correctly — they're taken more seriously than car lanes | Walk in the bike lane; you will be bell-rung at, repeatedly, without mercy |

## Do's & Don'ts: Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a park bootcamp or running group — they're social as much as physical | Expect gyms to be cheap; quality fitness clubs in Lima carry real prestige |
| Exercise early — 6am park sessions beat both heat and traffic | Underestimate altitude effects if training outside Lima, in Cusco or Arequipa |
| Try outdoor group classes — aerobics and Zumba culture runs deep | Assume rural areas mirror Lima's structured fitness scene |

## Netherlands: Fitness by Accident, Nationwide

The Dutch relationship with fitness is almost aggressively unglamorous, because it isn't really a relationship with fitness at all, it's a relationship with the bicycle, which happens to double as one of the most effective cardiovascular interventions any country has ever accidentally implemented at national scale. Cycling infrastructure here isn't a nod to sustainability, it's the actual dominant mode of transport, with dedicated lanes, priority signalling, and a driving culture that treats cyclists as the default road user rather than an inconvenience to be tolerated.

This produces a genuinely strange effect for anyone arriving from a car-dependent or gym-dependent culture: Dutch people, across every age bracket, from schoolchildren to eighty-year-olds, get meaningful daily exercise simply by living their normal lives. Nobody frames the school run, the grocery run, or the commute to work as a workout, because framing it that way would be like calling breathing a cardio routine. It just is what happens when you get from one place to another.

Formal gym culture exists, certainly, and chains like Basic-Fit have expanded aggressively, but gym membership occupies a genuinely secondary role in the national fitness picture compared to most Western European or North American countries. The bicycle has already done the baseline work; the gym, for those who use one, is for supplementary strength training or a specific aesthetic goal, not the primary fitness intervention.

What visitors consistently underestimate is the sheer weather-agnosticism of it all. Rain, wind, the particular horizontal sleet that Dutch winters specialise in, none of it meaningfully reduces cycling volumes. There's a well-worn local saying that there's no such thing as bad weather, only bad clothing, and Dutch cyclists genuinely live by it, pedalling through conditions that would send a fair-weather cyclist elsewhere running for the car. The result is a population with an enviable baseline fitness level that nobody had to consciously choose. It was simply built into the infrastructure decades ago and never removed.

## Peru: Fitness as Deliberate, Social Ritual

Peru, and Lima specifically, has taken the opposite path: fitness here is intentional, structured, and increasingly a significant marker of aspiration and social identity, particularly among the growing urban middle and upper-middle classes. Parque Kennedy, the Malecón along the Miraflores coastline, and countless smaller neighbourhood parks fill before sunrise with runners, bootcamp groups, and outdoor aerobics classes that carry a discipline and intensity that would put many Western gym-goers to shame.

The timing isn't incidental. Lima's traffic becomes genuinely punishing by mid-morning, and the heat, while rarely extreme by tropical standards, still makes a 6am session vastly more appealing than an afternoon one. This has produced a real dawn fitness culture, one that's as much about community as calories: the same faces show up to the same park sessions week after week, building the kind of low-stakes social bonds that Dutch cyclists get for free just by sharing a bike lane, except here it's earned through actual shared sweat.

Gym culture, where it exists, carries genuine prestige. A membership at one of Lima's higher-end fitness clubs, Bodytech among the most recognisable, signals a certain socioeconomic bracket, and the gyms themselves lean into this with genuinely impressive facilities that wouldn't look out of place in Miami or Madrid. This creates a visible split between the structured, aspirational gym-and-bootcamp culture of wealthier Lima districts and the far more informal, resourceful fitness habits found elsewhere in the country, where a park, a rope, and sheer willpower do most of the work.

Altitude adds its own layer for anyone training beyond Lima's sea-level comfort. Cusco and Arequipa sit high enough that even modest exercise hits differently, and Peruvians training or competing across these regions build a genuine physiological toughness that flatland fitness culture simply doesn't require. What nobody quite prepares visitors for is how social the whole ecosystem is. A Peruvian bootcamp class isn't a solitary grind with headphones in, it's a genuinely communal event, instructor included, and skipping it feels less like missing a workout and more like missing a standing appointment with friends.

## The Verdict

The Netherlands wins on sheer population-level efficiency: a fitness plan nobody has to think about, sustained for decades, embedded directly into the national infrastructure. Peru wins on intentionality and community, on making fitness a genuinely social, aspirational, disciplined choice rather than an accident of urban planning.

If I had to bet on which population will still be effortlessly fit in fifty years without trying, it's the Dutch, purely by default. But if I wanted the version of fitness that actually feels like it means something, that comes with a community and a 6am alarm you set on purpose, Lima's parks have the edge. Just don't skip leg day. The Andes are watching.

## Conclusion

The Dutch built fitness into their infrastructure so thoroughly that nobody has to think about it, which is either the most elegant public health intervention in Europe or proof that convenience beats willpower every time. Peru built fitness into its social calendar instead, turning a workout into a standing community appointment nobody wants to be the one to miss. One country got fit by accident. The other got fit on purpose, and got a park full of friends out of the deal too.
