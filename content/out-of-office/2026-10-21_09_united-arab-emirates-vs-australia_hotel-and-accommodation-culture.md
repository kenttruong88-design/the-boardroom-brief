🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates 🇦🇺 Australia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The UAE will house a newly arrived executive in a serviced apartment with a pool, a gym, and a concierge before their visa paperwork has even finished processing — corporate relocation there runs on a logic of visible, immediate comfort as a recruitment tool. Australia will make that same executive compete against forty other applicants, in person, with a folder of references, pay stubs, and a cover letter addressed to a rental property, for a one-bedroom flat with a shared laundry and no guarantee of success.

I have been handed keys to a serviced Dubai apartment within forty-eight hours of landing, with the water already running and a welcome basket on the counter. I have also stood in a queue of thirty strangers outside a Sydney rental inspection, all of us pretending not to notice we were sizing each other up as rivals. Both countries will house you eventually. Only one of them makes you fight for it.

## Do's & Don'ts: United Arab Emirates

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate your housing allowance as part of your employment package before you arrive, if possible | Assume accommodation is separate from your job offer; for many corporate roles, it's deeply intertwined |
| Expect to pay rent via post-dated cheques, often one to four per year, as the local norm | Assume monthly rent payments are standard; many UAE landlords still expect large lump-sum cheques |
| Register your tenancy contract (Ejari in Dubai) properly — it's a legal requirement, not optional paperwork | Skip formal registration to save a step; it affects your ability to get utilities connected and resolve disputes |
| Use a serviced apartment as a deliberate short-term bridge while you find longer-term housing | Treat a serviced apartment's comfort as representative of typical UAE rental costs; it's a premium product |

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend rental inspections promptly and bring a complete application (references, payslips, rental history) | Show up underprepared to an inspection; competitive markets can see dozens of applicants for one property |
| Budget for a bond (often four weeks' rent) plus the first rent payment, both due early | Assume a verbal agreement secures a property; written applications and formal approval are standard |
| Research the specific state's tenancy laws; rules on bonds, notice periods, and rent increases vary by state | Assume national consistency; Australian tenancy law is set state by state, not federally |
| Treat a rental reference from a previous landlord as genuinely important leverage | Burn a landlord relationship casually; reference checks are taken seriously in competitive rental markets |

## United Arab Emirates: comfort first, paperwork later

Corporate relocation to the UAE, especially Dubai and Abu Dhabi, is often built around a compressed, high-comfort onboarding sequence: many employers either provide housing directly or a generous housing allowance as a distinct line item in a compensation package, and serviced apartments — fully furnished, hotel-adjacent, with pools, gyms, and often daily housekeeping — are a common short-term landing spot while longer leases get sorted. This reflects the UAE's broader economic model, built heavily around attracting mobile international talent quickly, with accommodation treated as recruitment infrastructure rather than something new arrivals are expected to hunt for themselves.

Longer-term UAE renting operates on its own distinct logic, most strikingly the post-dated cheque system: many landlords still expect rent paid in large instalments — one, two, or four cheques covering the entire year — rather than the monthly rhythm common elsewhere, which can be a serious cash-flow shock for newcomers used to paying rent in small, regular amounts. Formal tenancy registration (Ejari in Dubai, and equivalent systems elsewhere in the Emirates) is a genuine legal requirement, not bureaucratic theatre, and skipping it causes real downstream problems with utility connections and dispute resolution.

What UAE accommodation culture doesn't really have is the adversarial, competitive-application atmosphere common in tighter Western rental markets — availability has historically been relatively abundant across the broader market, even if headline developments command serious premiums, and landlords are generally more focused on securing reliable long-term tenants than running a competitive bidding process.

## Australia: the rental application as a job interview

Australian rental markets, particularly in Sydney, Melbourne, and Brisbane during periods of low vacancy, have developed an openly competitive, almost adversarial application culture that regularly stuns newcomers from markets where housing is handed over, not fought for. A single advertised rental inspection can draw dozens of attendees, each required to submit a formal application — rental history, payslips, references, sometimes a personal cover letter — competing directly against each other for one property, with property managers able to pick the strongest file rather than simply the first acceptable one.

Financially, the upfront burden is real: a bond (commonly equivalent to four weeks' rent, sometimes more depending on the state) plus the first rental payment are typically both due before move-in, on top of whatever moving costs a relocation already involves. Newcomers without an existing Australian rental history are at a structural disadvantage in competitive markets, since property managers weight local references heavily — meaning the very first rental is often the hardest one to secure, a genuine catch-22 for new arrivals.

Crucially, tenancy law in Australia is set state by state rather than nationally — bond limits, notice periods, rent-increase rules, and tenant protections all vary across New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, and the rest, so advice that's accurate in Sydney can be flatly wrong in Perth. This decentralised structure means expats moving between Australian cities, not just into Australia from abroad, have to re-learn the local rules each time.

Furnishing norms add one more layer of friction for movers crossing between the two systems. UAE rentals, especially in the serviced and mid-to-upper segments popular with relocating professionals, are very often offered fully furnished, down to kitchenware and linens, reflecting a market heavily oriented toward a transient international workforce that doesn't want to buy a sofa for an eighteen-month posting. Australian rentals are overwhelmingly unfurnished as the default — tenants are expected to arrive with, or promptly buy, their own furniture, appliances in some cases included but often not, and a newcomer assuming Dubai-style turnkey convenience will instead find themselves furniture shopping within days of signing a twelve-month lease, an unexpected cost and logistical scramble that UAE relocation guides rarely prepare people for precisely because it's so far outside that market's own norm.

## The Verdict

The UAE wins, unambiguously, on day-one comfort — nowhere else will a company hand you keys to a serviced apartment with a working pool before your bank account even exists. Australia wins on long-term fairness and transparency, in the sense that a defined, documented application process, however competitive and stressful, treats every applicant the same way, with real legal protections once you're in. If you want to be cushioned immediately and sort the complexity out later, the UAE delivers that softness. If you want a system that's harder to enter but genuinely protects you once you're inside it, Australia's bureaucratic rigour, as exhausting as the inspection queue feels, earns its keep.

## Conclusion

What's actually different here isn't generosity versus stinginess — it's where each system decided to put the friction. The UAE removes friction at the front door, housing you fast and sorting the fine print later, because attracting talent quickly matters more to that economy than protecting against every edge case upfront. Australia puts the friction right at the front door, making you prove yourself before you're ever handed a key, in exchange for a tenancy once secured that's genuinely well-protected by law. Arrive in either place expecting the other's rhythm and you'll either be pleasantly stunned by how fast the UAE moves, or quietly demoralised by how many rental inspections it takes to get a "yes" in Australia. Pack patience for one. Pack your references, properly organised, for the other.
