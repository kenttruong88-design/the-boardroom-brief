# Mumbai Will Switch Languages Mid-Sentence. Bandar Seri Begawan Will Switch Scripts.

🇮🇳 India vs 🇧🇳 Brunei

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The myth every relocation guide repeats about both these countries is "English is widely spoken, you'll be fine." I have been, technically, fine in both. I have also stood in a Vadodara street market functioning like a deaf person communicating entirely through mime, and ordered food in Bandar Seri Begawan in the wrong language entirely while the waitress relayed my order to the kitchen in a third one. "Fine" is doing a tremendous amount of unexamined work in that sentence.

Both countries have genuine, deep English fluency somewhere in their systems. Neither will reliably hand it to you at street level, and the reasons why are completely different, which is the part nobody tells you before you book the flight.

## Do's and Don'ts: India 🇮🇳

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to read basic numerals and addresses in the local script; it stops auto-rickshaw drivers taking liberties | Assume English fluency is uniform; EF's index scores Telangana at 548 and Uttar Pradesh at just 418 |
| Expect Hinglish — English and Hindi genuinely interwoven mid-sentence — as the real urban professional register | Expect street-level transactions (markets, autos, delivery) to run smoothly in English outside the big cities |
| Use a translation app's camera feature for signage and menus outside metro areas; it's a real daily tool | Rely on a local companion as your "translator" and assume locals will still address you directly, not them |

## Do's and Don'ts: Brunei 🇧🇳

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect English to work fine for business, exams, and official matters — it's genuinely entrenched | Expect Standard Malay in the street; Brunei Malay is the real vernacular and diverges in meaningful ways |
| Learn to recognise Jawi (Arabic script) on signage; Brunei promotes it actively alongside Malay | Assume a Malay greeting gets you a Malay reply; code-switching into Javanese, Mandarin, or dialect is common |
| Treat the small expat community as your real social adjustment challenge, not the language itself | Confuse "kerajaan" with its Malaysian/Indonesian meaning; in Brunei Malay it means "government," not "kingdom" |

India's English story is really twenty-eight different stories wearing one national statistic. The EF English Proficiency Index 2025 ranks the country 74th globally, a "moderate" score of 484, but the real information is in the internal spread: Telangana scores 548, Uttar Pradesh scores 418 — a gap wider than the difference between many entire countries — and at the city level, Kochi hits 577 against Thiruvarur's 328. Listening is the weakest skill nationally, which tracks with what every visitor eventually discovers: reading an English sign is one thing, understanding rapid spoken English layered with regional accent and code-switched Hindi is another entirely. That code-switching — "Hinglish" — is the actual operating register of urban professional India: boardroom decks in English, the argument about the decks in Hindi, sometimes within the same breath. Street level is a different country altogether. One expat.com forum poster, "Laura," in Goa, found language "isn't much of an issue in the bigger cities" and leaned on her Indian husband as, in her words, "my own personal translator" — except another poster in Shimla, "matshidisolp," noted that even with a local companion present, shopkeepers would often address him in Hindi rather than her directly, a small but telling erasure. The most vivid account came from "deafdrummer," posting from Vadodara in Gujarat: "Most of the people I speak to on the streets don't know English beyond basic addresses and numbers," forcing a kind of functional communication "like a completely deaf person," heavy on mime, with reading local script flagged as "essential" just to avoid an auto-rickshaw driver taking a profitable detour. The throughline across every account: trying, even badly, reads as respect and gets rewarded; assuming English will simply appear does not.

Brunei's situation inverts the problem entirely — the fluency is real, official, and still somehow doesn't solve the daily experience. Malay has been the official language since the 1959 Constitution, and English was deeply entrenched during nearly a century of British protectorate rule through 1984; under the SPN21 education policy since 2009, maths and science are taught in English from the very first year of primary school. On paper, this should make Brunei a breeze. In practice, Brunei Malay — not Standard Malay — is the actual street vernacular, spoken by roughly 266,000 people and diverging from Standard and Malaysian Malay in genuine false-friend traps: "kerajaan" means "government" in Brunei Malay, not "kingdom," as it does elsewhere. A University of Brunei Darussalam study found listeners could correctly guess, with 74% accuracy, whether an English speaker was ethnically Malay or Chinese from just ten seconds of speech — the accent markers run that deep even within English itself. An online discussion forum studied academically found nearly half of all posts, even in English-medium spaces, were partly or fully in Malay anyway, confirming code-switching as the actual default rather than the exception. Jawi, the Arabic-based script, appears deliberately alongside Malay on official signage, a cultural choice Brunei pursues more enthusiastically than its neighbours. One Indonesian expat blogger's account captured the layered reality perfectly: she ordered food in Indonesian, the waitress replied in Brunei Malay, then relayed the order to the kitchen in Javanese — prompting the entirely reasonable aside, "Why the heck did she not speak to me in Javanese in the first place?" An ExpatExchange thread on Brunei's toughest adjustments found the real friction wasn't grammar at all: "it can be difficult to find a sense of community and make friends," with the tiny expat population — roughly 7,500 Indians, 80,000 Indonesian workers, and a small Western professional contingent — making isolation, not incomprehension, the actual barrier.

India's own professional-English register has a specific texture worth naming directly: Hinglish isn't sloppy code-switching, it's a stable, rule-governed register that functions as its own dialect in urban offices, which is exactly why a foreigner who's "fluent in English" can still feel lost in a Mumbai meeting conducted in a language that is, technically, English the whole time. Brunei's layering runs just as deep in the other direction — the country's roughly 430,000 residents include around 80,000 Indonesian migrant workers whose own home languages (Javanese among them) sit underneath the official Malay-English bilingual system entirely unofficially, meaning the "third language" you actually encounter in a given interaction is frequently neither of the two the government lists.

The honest contrast: India's language barrier is a genuine, regionally lopsided skills gap you can measure, state by state, and work around with effort and a translation app. Brunei's "barrier" barely exists linguistically — English is everywhere it needs to be — and the real wall is social smallness dressed up, confusingly, as a language problem.

## The Verdict

If you're going to struggle, better to struggle with a real, honest skills gap you can actually close through effort — which makes India, despite the regional lottery, the more rewarding long-term language project. Brunei will hand you fluent English on day one and then quietly withhold the community that makes fluency worth having. I'm calling this one for India: a hard-won "I ni ce"-equivalent moment of connection with a Vadodara shopkeeper beats fluent small talk with nobody to have it with.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Expat.com, India language-barriers thread, "deafdrummer," paraphrased: outside basic addresses and numbers, most street-level interactions in Vadodara happen with no shared English at all — reading local signage becomes "essential" just to avoid being misled.*

> *ExpatExchange, Brunei country page, paraphrased: the real challenge for many expats isn't Malay at all — "it can be difficult to find a sense of community and make friends" in a country with such a small foreign population.*

> *Magdalene.co, Indonesian expat blog, paraphrased: ordering in one language and getting an answer relayed through two others is a normal Brunei experience — "Why the heck did she not speak to me in Javanese in the first place?"*

## Conclusion

Neither country will hand you the easy fluency the brochure promised. India will make you regionally humble about exactly how much English actually travels once you leave the metro. Brunei will make you fluent and then painfully aware that fluency was never the problem you needed solved. Learn the numbers, learn the script, and in Brunei specifically — make a friend before you make a sentence. You'll need the friend considerably more.
