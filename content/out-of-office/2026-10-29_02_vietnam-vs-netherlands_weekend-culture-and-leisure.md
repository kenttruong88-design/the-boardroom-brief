# One Country Schedules Its Joy Three Weeks Out. The Other Just Shows Up and Starts Eating.

🇻🇳 Vietnam 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ask a Vietnamese colleague what they're doing this weekend and you'll get an answer involving at least four relatives, a motorbike, and possibly an ancestor altar that needs attending to before anyone eats. Ask a Dutch colleague the same question on a Tuesday and they will open an app, consult a grid of color-coded blocks, and tell you they are, regrettably, fully booked until the 14th of next month — for brunch.

Both of these are, somehow, considered relaxing. I have spent weekends in both countries doing what passes locally for leisure, and I can report that one nation treats free time as a sacred, slightly chaotic family inheritance, and the other treats it as a quarterly performance review you have to apply for in writing. Neither is wrong. Both will make you feel, at some point, profoundly unprepared.

## Do's & Don'ts: Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the Sunday family lunch invitation even if you've met these relatives once | Assume "just coffee" means thirty minutes — it means ninety and a motorbike tour |
| Learn to love a plastic stool at 6am; that's where actual Saigon happens | Schedule anything that requires a government office on a Saturday — they're shut |
| Treat the cafe as your second office; everyone else already has | Mistake a "maybe" for a yes, or a yes for a hard commitment |
| Build slack into weekend plans for sudden floods, Tet prep, or a national football match | Expect your Vietnamese colleague to explain family leave in detail — just say thank you and move on |

## Do's & Don'ts: Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Put every social plan, including coffee, directly into your agenda app | Ask a Dutch friend to "hang out sometime" — it needs a date, a time, and ideally a calendar invite |
| Buy a bike. Not for exercise — for basic weekend citizenship | Expect weekend spontaneity; most people are booked three to four weeks out |
| Show up for vrijmibo (Friday drinks) if you want any shot at a social life | Take bluntness about your free Saturday personally — it's not an insult, it's logistics |
| Accept that cycling is both your commute and your hobby, often on the same bike | Assume a cancelled plan will be rescheduled quickly — the agenda is a one-way street |

## Vietnam

Vietnamese weekends don't begin with a plan so much as a summons. Family obligation sits at the absolute centre of how time off gets spent, and it's non-negotiable in a way that would send a Dutch HR department into a tailspin. Vietcetera, reporting on workplace norms, notes that a colleague requesting early leave for "việc gia đình" — family business — needs no further explanation, and pressing for one is considered rude. That same deference extends straight into the weekend: Saturday is for the altar, the elderly parents, the cousin's wedding you didn't RSVP to because RSVPing isn't really how it works here. You go. Everyone goes. It isn't optional, and nobody pretends it is.

What's left over after family duty gets absorbed almost entirely by the cafe. Vietnam doesn't have a coffee culture so much as a coffee economy — industry data compiled by Saigoneer puts the national cafe count somewhere between 317,000 and half a million, depending on whose count you trust, generating well over a billion dollars a year. That's not a statistic about caffeine. That's a statistic about where an entire society conducts its actual social life: on a plastic stool, phone face-down, watching traffic do something vaguely balletic for three hours while nobody is in any hurry to leave. Weekend mornings in Hanoi or Saigon are built around this ritual with an intensity that makes a London flat white seem like a drive-through transaction.

Nightlife runs on a completely different clock to the one you're used to — loud, cheap, and usually improvised rather than booked. Markets double as weekend entertainment, haggling is the sport, and an expat post report filed through the U.S. Consulate in Ho Chi Minh City described the social circle for foreigners as affordable but genuinely small, with the wine shop functioning as the de facto expat embassy. That tracks with what I've seen on forums too: a long-running expat.com thread titled, with admirable honesty, "Friends to hang out on weekend" shows newcomers proposing pool games and asking strangers if they're "up to next weekend?" — a slightly plaintive energy that tells you everything about how hard-won a non-family social weekend can be here if you've arrived without one built in already.

The result is a culture where leisure is abundant but almost entirely communal and improvised — glorious if you have the family network, mildly lonely if you don't, and essentially impossible to plan three weeks out, because nobody is trying to.

## Netherlands

The Dutch weekend is, by contrast, a document. It exists before it happens, in an app, with other people's names attached to specific hour-long blocks. IamAmsterdam's own expat portal states this plainly for newcomers: most people pin down appointments — even a casual coffee — well in advance, and a last-minute invitation is as likely to be declined as accepted. This isn't rudeness. It's just that Dutch leisure time is bounded, respected, and treated as something you don't get to improvise your way into uninvited, which is a profoundly different emotional contract than the Vietnamese "just show up, there's always room."

Cycling is the connective tissue of all of it. CBS, the national statistics office, found that in a typical week the average Dutch resident cycles more kilometres for touring, sport, and leisure combined (roughly 8.3 km) than for commuting to work (about 3.1 km) — meaning the bike you ride to the office on Tuesday is, structurally, the same bike doing the emotional labour of your Saturday. Teenagers cycle furthest of any age group, at nearly 33 km a week, which should humiliate anyone who's ever claimed they're "too tired" to cycle to brunch.

Social life clusters around two fixed rituals: vrijmibo, the Friday-afternoon office drink that functions as an unofficial extension of the working week, and the weekend itself, which gets allocated between hobbies (cooking and reading top the national surveys), sport clubs that cheerfully absorb beginners — hockey, padel, climbing — and whatever's already in the agenda. The catch, and it is a real one, is integration. DutchNews columnist Molly Quell wrote in October about the small library of Reddit threads from newly arrived expats struggling to break in, quoting one blunt Dutch reply to a lonely newcomer: "We don't know you, you might just be unlikable for all we know." That is, structurally, the Dutch weekend in one sentence — not unwelcoming exactly, just entirely unwilling to make room for you without an appointment.

## The Verdict

If you want a weekend that feels like belonging to something, go to Vietnam. If you want a weekend that respects your time down to the minute and will never, ever ghost you on a confirmed plan, go to the Netherlands. The Vietnamese model has a massive asterisk: it works beautifully if you have family or a found-family cafe crew, and it can be quietly brutal if you don't, judging by how many expat forum posts amount to strangers begging strangers for a pool game. The Dutch model has the opposite asterisk: the plan, once made, is sacred — but making it in the first place, as a foreigner with no existing network, can feel like applying for weekend citizenship and getting waitlisted.

My verdict, with zero diplomacy: Vietnam wins on warmth, the Netherlands wins on reliability, and if you want both you'll need to marry into a Dutch cycling club and a Vietnamese family simultaneously, which sounds exhausting and is probably the single most relaxing life available on Earth.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/VietNam (via third-party mirror) — paraphrased: a returning Viet Kieu describes Saigon's social scene as "alive and vibrant," perfect for either a quiet life or, in another commenter's words, nonstop "benders with very little responsibility" — but warns not to stay more than two years without a real plan.*

> *expat.com Ho Chi Minh City forum, thread no. 591545 — paraphrased: a newcomer posts looking for weekend company and specifies they're "not looking for a date and not an ATM machine," while another user offers a pool-and-fresh-air meetup and simply asks, "anyone up to next weekend?"*

> *Reddit r/Netherlands, quoted in DutchNews.nl — paraphrased: a Dutch commenter tells a struggling newcomer that nobody owes them a friendship just because they moved there, bluntly suggesting they might just not be likable — which, several expats note in the same piece, is the most Dutch sentence imaginable.*

## Conclusion

Here's the sting: both countries have quietly solved the same problem — how to make leisure meaningful instead of just empty hours — and arrived at opposite answers that each expose the other's weakness. Vietnam gives you belonging without boundaries, which is wonderful until you need a boundary. The Netherlands gives you boundaries without belonging, which is efficient until you need belonging. Neither will apologise for this, and neither should. So stop asking which country "does weekends better," because that's a brochure question. Ask instead which kind of lonely you can live with — the kind with a cafe stool and eventual uncles, or the kind with a beautifully organised, thoroughly empty Saturday agenda. Pick accordingly, and stop pretending either one is "exotic." It's just Tuesday somewhere, forever.
