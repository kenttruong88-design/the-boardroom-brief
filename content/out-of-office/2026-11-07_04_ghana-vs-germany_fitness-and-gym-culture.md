# Germans Will Judge You for Wearing Swimwear in the Sauna. Ghanaians Will Judge You for Not Having a Gym Membership at All.

🇬🇭 Ghana 🤝 🇩🇪 Germany
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Accra's gym boom is a genuinely recent, genuinely interesting social phenomenon — ModernGhana's own business reporting describes a city where commercial gyms have gone from a niche expense to something close to an aspirational status marker, almost overnight, powering what's being called a new urban fitness economy. InsightGhana's coverage asks, pointedly, whether this is a real lifestyle shift or a trend riding social-media aesthetics — and the honest answer, from the outside, looks like both at once.

Germany, meanwhile, has had its fitness and body-culture norms settled for the better part of a century, and settled in a direction that still regularly startles newcomers: FKK (Freikörperkultur, "free body culture") means public nudity in saunas, some parks, and certain lake areas isn't fringe — it's often the *rule*, with swimwear in a sauna treated as the actual breach of etiquette, not the nudity itself. One country is sprinting to build a fitness culture from scratch. The other inherited one so settled nobody there even registers how strange it looks from outside.

## Ghana, Deep Dive

What's happening in Accra's gym scene right now is close to a live case study in how fast a fitness culture can be built from a standing start. ModernGhana's business-press coverage frames it plainly: commercial gyms have become a genuine growth sector, with new facilities opening at pace and membership increasingly read as a marker of urban, aspirational, middle-class identity rather than a niche health pursuit. InsightGhana's own framing — explicitly asking whether this is "trend or true lifestyle shift" — captures the uncertainty fairly: it's too early to say this is fully embedded, but the direction is unmistakable.

For an incoming expat, the practical upshot is that gym quality and pricing vary enormously and quickly — a facility that was the best option eighteen months ago may have been superseded, and Expat.com's own Accra-specific thread on "Gym/Health Club in Accra" functions as a genuinely useful, continuously updated resource precisely because the landscape keeps shifting. The social dimension matters more here than in many Western gym cultures: being seen at a reputable gym, posting about it, and participating in group classes carries status weight that's discussed fairly openly rather than treated as embarrassingly vain.

It's also worth separating the gym-specific boom from Ghana's older, deeper informal fitness culture — street running groups, church-organised sports, and outdoor calisthenics have existed for a long time and carry their own social currency, independent of the commercial gym trend. A newcomer who only engages with the gym boom is seeing half the picture; the other half is happening on the street, for free, and has been for decades. Early-morning group runs in particular function as a genuine social institution in several Accra neighbourhoods, often organised informally through church or workplace networks rather than through any app or gym membership — arguably a more reliable way to meet people quickly than a paid facility membership, even as the commercial gym sector gets most of the press coverage.

## Germany, Deep Dive

Germany's FKK tradition is old enough — dating to early-20th-century naturist movements — that it's stopped being a "culture" in the sense of something actively practised with intent and become something closer to ambient infrastructure: it's simply how certain spaces work, and the people using them aren't making a statement by being nude any more than they'd be making a statement by wearing shoes. The Local's reporting on foreigners' reactions to this is consistently, almost comically, about the newcomer's discomfort rather than any local's — Germans themselves report genuine confusion at why this surprises anyone.

The specific etiquette trap, repeated across IamExpat's sauna guide and Expat.com's own "casual nudity" thread, is that wearing swimwear into a sauna is the actual faux pas. It's read as unhygienic (textiles are considered to trap sweat and bacteria against the wooden benches) and, more subtly, as a kind of unwelcome moral commentary on an environment where nobody asked for modesty to be introduced. Sauna areas are near-universally mixed-gender in Germany, and the entire design assumption is that this is a wellness space, not a sexualised one — treating it as the latter, through stares or obvious discomfort, is the actual violation of the norm. This extends to designated FKK sunbathing zones within larger public parks, where the nude and clothed areas are often simply adjacent, unmarked by any barrier beyond social convention, and newcomers who wander across the invisible line without realising it is, apparently, a rite of passage most long-term residents remember fondly and will happily recount in detail.

Separately, and less discussed by outsiders fixated on the nudity angle, is how rigid German gym contracts are. LiveInGermany's expat-facing guide is blunt that 12-to-24-month lock-in contracts are standard, with cancellation terms that can be genuinely difficult to navigate — a very different kind of German rigidity from the sauna's liberal body norms, and one that catches out far more newcomers in practice than nudity ever does.

## The Verdict

Ghana is building its fitness culture in real time, in public, with all the visible status-signalling and inconsistency that comes with something still forming. Germany's fitness culture is so settled it's stopped noticing itself — liberal about bodies in a way that still shocks outsiders, and simultaneously bureaucratically rigid about contracts in a way that shocks them again, differently, a few weeks later. If you want a gym scene that's dynamic, aspirational, and still writing its own rules, Accra. If you want one that's figured out exactly how it feels about nudity but will trap you in a contract for two years regardless — Germany, and good luck with the cancellation letter.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *The Local DE's reporting on foreigners and FKK — newcomers across multiple accounts describe the sauna-swimwear reversal as the single most disorienting adjustment, more than the nudity itself.*

> *LiveInGermany's expat gym guide flags cancellation-notice periods as a recurring, specific source of newcomer frustration — the contract terms, not the fitness culture itself, are what actually trips people up long-term.*

## Conclusion

Both countries, in completely different registers, ask you to recalibrate what "normal" means around your own body and your own money. Ghana asks you to perform fitness as a visible, social, aspirational act, still being actively defined. Germany asks you to drop the performance entirely in the sauna while signing a document that will own eighteen months of your gym-going life. Neither one is going to apologise for making you uncomfortable in the attempt — and honestly, by month three in either country, you'll have stopped noticing what used to seem strange, which is rather the point of moving abroad in the first place.
