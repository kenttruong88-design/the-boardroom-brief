# Fireworks Are Optional. Committee Meetings Are Not.

🇳🇴 Norway vs 🇯🇵 Japan — Festival and Public Holiday Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Norwegians treat a public holiday like a hostage negotiation with the sun: they've been deprived of daylight for so long that the second it appears, they will burn something, drink something, and jump into freezing water wearing very little, and you will not stop them. Japan, meanwhile, treats a festival like a military operation with better costumes — every float route, every chant, every folding of every yukata has been rehearsed since approximately the Heian period, and improvisation is treated as a personal betrayal of the neighbourhood association.

I have stood in both crowds. In one, a man handed me a beer at 6am and called it breakfast. In the other, a woman corrected the angle of my obi with the quiet fury of someone defusing a bomb. Neither country will apologise for this. Neither should.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a bonfire on Sankthansaften even if you weren't invited — nobody was "invited" | Expect the bonfire to be small; some are the size of a shipping container |
| Bring your own drink to 17 May celebrations; it's a marathon, not a bar tab | Wear black — everyone else is in a bunad and you'll look like you're at a funeral |
| Wave a flag even if it isn't yours — the crowd will not check your passport | Assume shops are open on a "red day"; they are aggressively not |

### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book Obon or Golden Week travel four months out, minimum | Try to "wing it" on shinkansen tickets during a national holiday — you will stand for three hours |
| Follow the crowd's lead at a matsuri; there is a correct way to walk beside the mikoshi | Touch the float or the shrine unless invited — it is not a photo prop |
| Learn one shrine greeting; it buys enormous goodwill | Assume every festival is tourist-friendly; some are fiercely local and mean it |

## Norway: The Nation That Peaked at Midnight

Norway has two settings: dormant and radiant, and the entire public holiday calendar exists to manage the transition. Sankthansaften, the midsummer bonfire festival, is the closest thing this country has to a national exorcism — five months of darkness get burned off in a single night, often literally, on pyres tall enough to be visible from Sweden. Nobody organises this centrally. Every town, every fjordside hamlet, every group of teenagers with access to a pallet stack builds their own, and the unspoken competition over whose fire is bigger has apparently been running since the Vikings, who at least had the excuse of appeasing gods rather than just outdoing the neighbours.

Then there's 17 May, Constitution Day, which every outsider assumes will be a restrained Scandinavian affair — flags, maybe a band — and is instead the single most sincere display of national joy I have witnessed anywhere on earth. Children's parades. Adults crying. An entire population in folk costume that cost more than a used car, because a bunad is a heirloom, not a rental. There is no cynicism in it whatsoever, which is almost unsettling for those of us raised on irony as a coping mechanism.

What nobody warns you about is the closure. A Norwegian "red day" isn't a suggestion — supermarkets shut, pharmacies shut, entire towns go dark, and if you didn't stock up the night before, you will eat crispbread for dinner and like it. The flip side is that Norwegians have made peace with idleness in a way that should embarrass the rest of the productivity-obsessed world; a red day is not an opportunity to "get things done," it is a legally sanctioned excuse to sit in a field. I respect a country that protects boredom this fiercely.

## Japan: Choreography as a Love Language

Japan's festivals are not spontaneous, and pretending otherwise is the fastest way to embarrass yourself. A matsuri — the local shrine festival, the summer fireworks display, the autumn harvest procession — has typically been planned by the same neighbourhood committee for generations, with routes, timings, and even the specific chant of the mikoshi bearers passed down like liturgy. This is not bureaucracy for its own sake. It's devotion expressed through logistics, which, if you think about it, is a more honest form of love than most Hallmark card sentiment.

Golden Week and Obon, meanwhile, are less "holiday" and more "coordinated national migration." Tens of millions of people move simultaneously to ancestral hometowns or tourist hotspots, and the transport system — famously, insanely punctual on a normal Tuesday — simply absorbs the chaos and keeps running, which tells you everything about the engineering priorities of this country. Try this in most nations and the trains would collapse into a metaphor for societal breakdown. In Japan, they just add more staff to bow at you while you queue.

The thing expats never adjust to is the emotional register. A matsuri looks ecstatic — taiko drums, yukata, candy apples — but underneath it is profoundly formal, a structured expression of gratitude to a deity, ancestor, or harvest, and treating it purely as spectacle (filming too aggressively, wandering into the mikoshi's path, showing up drunk and loud) reads as a genuine violation, not a cultural misstep you can charm your way out of. Respect the choreography or stay home.

## The Verdict

Norway wins on raw, unfiltered joy — a nation that spends most of the year in darkness and then loses its collective mind with total sincerity the moment the sun returns is, frankly, the more relatable species of human. Japan wins on craft — nothing on earth is staged with the devotion of a Kyoto matsuri, and the payoff is a spectacle so precise it makes Norway's bonfires look like a bar fight that got out of hand (affectionately). If you want to feel something, go to Norway on 17 May. If you want to witness centuries of institutional memory expressed through fire and drumming, go to Japan in August. Just don't confuse the two, and for the love of all that's holy, don't touch the mikoshi.

## Conclusion

Somewhere between a Norwegian field fire and a Japanese shrine procession is the entire argument for why people travel at all — not for the beach, not for the food, but for the moment you realise an entire culture has organised itself around a feeling you didn't know you were missing. Norway will hand you a beer and set something on fire. Japan will teach you the correct angle to bow at a float you're not allowed to touch. Both will ruin you for a normal bank holiday back home, where the biggest tradition is a barbecue nobody really wanted to host. Pick your intensity. Bring layers either way.
