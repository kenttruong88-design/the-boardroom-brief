# One Weekend Starts on Friday Afternoon With a Siren, The Other Starts With a Bike Ride

🇮🇱 Israel 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Israel's weekend is a tightly choreographed shutdown — Shabbat descends Friday afternoon with an actual siren in religious neighbourhoods, shops shutter, buses stop running in many cities, and an entire country collectively exhales into family dinners and beach afternoons until Saturday evening brings it all roaring back. The Netherlands' weekend is a looser, more individualistic affair: bike to a market, brunch too long, cycle somewhere scenic, and treat Sunday with the gentle, unhurried reverence the Dutch reserve for actual leisure, no sirens required.

I've been caught genuinely off guard in Jerusalem by a Friday afternoon siren announcing Shabbat's arrival, scrambling to find an open shop before everything closed. I've also spent a baffling first Dutch Sunday wondering why absolutely nothing urgent seemed to be happening anywhere, before realising that was, in fact, the entire point.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Israel**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shop and run errands before Friday afternoon — most things close for Shabbat | Assume stores, banks, or public transport will run normally on Saturday in most cities |
| Enjoy Tel Aviv's beaches and secular nightlife, which largely continue through Shabbat | Expect the same openness in Jerusalem, where Shabbat closures are far stricter |
| Plan around the Sunday–Thursday work week when scheduling international calls | Book a Friday evening meeting assuming it'll run like any other weekday |
| Respect quiet in religious neighbourhoods during Shabbat hours | Drive through ultra-Orthodox areas on Shabbat without being prepared for strong objections |

**Netherlands**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Rent or borrow a bike for the full weekend cycling-market-park circuit | Expect weekend errands to be fast; Dutch Saturdays are often as busy with shopping as weekdays |
| Book brunch reservations ahead — it's a genuine weekend institution, not a quick meal | Rush brunch; it's meant to be slow, social, and take up a chunk of the morning |
| Embrace "niksen" (doing nothing) as a legitimate Sunday activity | Fill every weekend hour with plans — unstructured downtime is culturally valued, not wasted time |
| Check Sunday opening hours before planning errands — some shops close or run reduced hours | Assume every Dutch city has the same Sunday shopping hours as Amsterdam |

## Israel: The Weekend That Arrives With a Siren

Israel's weekend runs on a rhythm unlike almost anywhere else, dictated by Shabbat — the Jewish day of rest beginning at sundown Friday and ending Saturday evening. In religious neighbourhoods and smaller cities, this isn't a soft suggestion; sirens genuinely sound to mark Shabbat's arrival in places like Jerusalem, public transport shuts down almost entirely, shops close, and the streets empty into a hush that feels, to a newcomer, almost eerie after the noise of a normal Israeli Friday morning.

Tel Aviv tells a different story within the same country, a deliberately secular bubble where beaches stay packed, bars and restaurants continue operating, and Shabbat is observed more as a personal choice than a civic shutdown — the contrast between Tel Aviv's Friday night beach parties and Jerusalem's silent streets, less than an hour apart, is one of the more disorienting cultural whiplashes available anywhere in the world. Locals navigate this fluently, knowing exactly which city, which neighbourhood, and which crowd will be doing what, while visitors frequently get caught out assuming the whole country operates like whichever city they landed in first.

The structural quirk that trips up every newcomer is the work week itself: Israel runs Sunday through Thursday, with Friday and Saturday forming the weekend, which means Thursday night functions as the Israeli "Friday night" for going out, and international calls scheduled for a Sunday assuming it's a day off will be met with genuine confusion. Plan grocery runs, bank visits, and errands for before Friday afternoon without exception — the collective Israeli instinct to stock up before Shabbat is so ingrained that supermarket queues on Friday mornings rival any holiday rush elsewhere.

## Netherlands: The Weekend as Deliberate Slowness

Dutch weekends run on an entirely different philosophy: not shutdown, but deliberate, almost meditative slowness, built around cycling, markets, and a genuine cultural commitment to doing less. Saturday mornings in Amsterdam, Utrecht, or Rotterdam typically open with a trip to the local market — fresh stroopwafels, cheese stalls, flowers by the armful — followed by a long bike ride somewhere scenic, because the Netherlands has built its entire urban geography around the assumption that a bicycle is the correct way to experience a weekend, not just commute through one.

Brunch deserves special mention as a near-sacred Dutch weekend institution: reservations are often necessary at popular spots, and the meal itself is expected to stretch languidly across hours rather than the rushed thirty-minute version common elsewhere. This unhurried instinct extends into the concept the Dutch have more or less gifted the world's self-help industry — "niksen," the deliberate art of doing absolutely nothing, treated not as laziness but as a legitimate, even admirable, way to spend a Sunday afternoon, guilt-free.

What catches visitors off guard, especially those expecting a quiet "day of rest" akin to Israel's Shabbat, is how much Dutch weekends still involve activity and commerce — Saturday shopping remains genuinely busy in most Dutch cities, markets run at full tilt, and only Sundays bring the quieter, more variable retail hours (and even those vary meaningfully by city and store). The Dutch weekend isn't a shutdown of society; it's a shift in society's priorities, toward leisure, family, bikes, and the specific, protected pleasure of a very long brunch.

## The Verdict

Israel's Shabbat offers something the Netherlands doesn't: an actual, enforced, collective pause — a weekend that happens whether you planned for it or not, which has its own strange beauty once you stop fighting it. The Netherlands offers something gentler and more sustainable long-term: a weekend built around genuine rest without total societal shutdown, bikes instead of sirens, brunch instead of silence. For sheer cultural distinctiveness, Israel's Shabbat wins outright. For a weekend I'd actually want to live inside every single week, the Dutch approach — bike, market, niksen, repeat — takes it without much contest.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Reddit r/israel — paraphrased: a new resident describes being genuinely startled by the Shabbat siren their first Friday in Jerusalem, having had no idea it was coming or what it signified until a neighbour explained afterward.*

## Conclusion

Israel and the Netherlands have built two of the most distinctive weekend cultures on the planet, for opposite reasons: one through religious and civic structure, the other through a deliberate, almost philosophical embrace of slowness. Visit Israel and you'll learn to plan your errands around a siren. Visit the Netherlands and you'll learn to stop planning altogether, at least for one guilt-free Sunday. Both, in their own way, are trying to protect something modern life keeps trying to steal back: actual rest. Neither country is willing to give it up without a fight.
