# One Country Will Let You Bid for a Shoebox; the Other Will Just Rent You the Whole Building

🇳🇱 Netherlands 🇹🇷 Turkey
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have watched a grown Dutch professional cry, genuinely cry, over losing a 40-square-meter Amsterdam apartment to a bidding war he didn't know he was in until it was over. I have also, in Istanbul, been handed the keys to a three-bedroom apartment with sea views inside forty-eight hours by a landlord who mostly wanted to know if I liked tea. Housing markets are not neutral; they are personality tests disguised as spreadsheets, and these two countries could not have failed mine more differently.

## Do's & Don'ts: Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your BSN and a Dutch bank account sorted before house-hunting | Wait until you land to start searching — the good listings vanish in hours |
| Use a registered relocation agent if your budget allows it | View a listing without a signed contract ready — someone else will beat you |
| Expect to over-offer above asking rent in major cities | Assume "furnished" means more than a bed and a fridge |
| Budget for a broker's fee on top of your first month and deposit | Skip the housing registration (inschrijving) — it blocks almost everything else |

## Do's & Don'ts: Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate rent directly — it is expected, not rude | Accept the first listed price without at least one counter-offer |
| Get any verbal agreement in writing before handing over a deposit | Assume informal, handshake-based rental terms will hold up later |
| Ask about aidat (building maintenance fees) up front | Forget to check whether utilities are included or separate |
| Lean on personal introductions and local contacts to find listings | Rely solely on international listing sites — local networks move faster |

## Netherlands: Bidding Wars for a Room With a Radiator

The Dutch rental market in 2026 is less a housing sector and more a extreme sport with worse odds. Pararius's own expat guide to the "Dutch rental crisis" doesn't hedge on the scale of the problem: chronic undersupply, especially in Amsterdam, Utrecht, and Rotterdam, has turned ordinary apartment-hunting into a competitive bidding process where listings can receive dozens of applications within hours of going live, and paying above the asking rent — once considered gauche — is now simply the price of entry.

NL Times' coverage of expats "beating the odds" to grab an apartment despite the housing crunch reads like a heist movie: successful renters describe having bank statements, employer letters, BSN numbers, and signed intent-to-rent documents pre-assembled and ready to fire off the literal instant a listing appears, because hesitation of even a few hours means losing out to someone who didn't hesitate. Relocation agencies exist specifically to manage this arms race on your behalf, for a fee, and Trustpilot reviews of services like Cityhomes are a genuinely useful barometer of just how desperate the market has gotten — five-star reviews thanking an agency for "finding anything at all" are not uncommon.

What makes this particularly galling to newcomers is the paperwork-before-humanity sequencing: Dutch landlords and agencies want your BSN (citizen service number), Dutch bank account, employment contract, and often a guarantor lined up before you're even permitted to view most listings, let alone bid on one — a bureaucratic gauntlet that assumes you've already been in the country for months before you're allowed to actually live anywhere sensible. It's an oddly circular system for a country this efficient at everything else: you need housing to get settled, and you need to already look settled to get housing.

## Turkey: Handshake Deals and a Genuine Human on the Other End

Turkish rental culture, particularly outside the most internationally saturated pockets of Istanbul, still runs substantially on relationship and negotiation rather than algorithmic bidding wars. Expat Arrivals' guide to Istanbul accommodation is refreshingly calm compared to its Dutch equivalent: yes, finding a good apartment takes legwork, but the process is fundamentally human — you meet the landlord, you negotiate rent in person, sometimes over tea, and the final price is very rarely the first number quoted.

This negotiability cuts both ways. expat.com's Istanbul housing threads are full of cautionary tales about verbal agreements that evaporated the moment a better tenant came along, deposits handed over without documentation, and aidat (building maintenance fees) that weren't mentioned until move-in day. The flexibility that makes Turkish rental culture feel warmer and less mechanically brutal than the Dutch system also means fewer institutional guardrails protecting you if a landlord decides to renegotiate reality after the fact — get everything in writing, every single time, no matter how friendly the tea session felt.

What Turkey does uniquely well is speed and personability once you're plugged into the right local network. Personal introductions — a colleague's cousin, a doorman (kapıcı) who knows every empty unit on the block, a real estate agent (emlakçı) working purely on local reputation — routinely outperform international listing sites, and turnaround from "I need an apartment" to "here are the keys" can be a matter of days rather than the months-long Dutch bidding gauntlet. The tradeoff is that this system rewards being embedded and connected far more than it rewards having a strong bank statement, which is either liberating or terrifying depending on how recently you moved to the country.

## The Verdict

The Netherlands has built an efficient, transparent, thoroughly documented housing market that is nonetheless a genuine ordeal to survive as a newcomer — everything is above board and almost nothing is available. Turkey has built a warmer, more negotiable, more human system that will move fast for you precisely to the extent you're willing to build real relationships and paper-trail your own protection along the way. If efficiency without mercy is your speed, the Netherlands will reward your spreadsheet. If you'd rather negotiate over tea and take your chances on the handshake, Istanbul will have you moved in before Amsterdam's bidding war for that shoebox even closes.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a respondent asked how hard it is to find an apartment in Amsterdam as a foreigner and was told flatly: "Have your paperwork ready before you even start looking, or you will lose to someone who does — every single time."*

> *Internations Amsterdam community — a member described viewing eleven apartments and losing all eleven bidding wars before finally landing one, calling the experience "genuinely worse for my morale than my actual visa process."*

> *expat.com Istanbul forum — a British expat recounted a landlord verbally promising a two-year lease at a fixed rent, then raising it within four months, and warned future arrivals to "get the lease terms notarized, tea or no tea."*

## Conclusion

Housing will tell you more about a country's soul than its tourist board ever will. The Netherlands' rental market is a masterclass in institutional efficiency that somehow produces a deeply inefficient, soul-crushing experience for the actual humans trying to use it. Turkey's is the opposite: personal, negotiable, occasionally under-documented, and far kinder to your nervous system if you're willing to do the relationship-building work. I'll take the tea over the bidding war, every time — just bring a notary along for the second cup.
