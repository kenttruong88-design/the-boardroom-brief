🇬🇭 Ghana 🇧🇪 Belgium
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ghana doesn't really have "a" festival calendar — it has dozens of them, each ethnic group and region running its own cycle of durbars, yam festivals, and ancestral remembrance days that can shut down an entire town for a week while the chief's palanquin makes its rounds. Belgium, meanwhile, has a public holiday calendar so fragmented by region and language that you can live an hour from someone and have a completely different set of days off, and nobody will think to mention this to you until you've already shown up to a closed office.

Having navigated both, I can tell you the fundamental difference isn't enthusiasm — Belgians throw themselves into their festivals (ask anyone who's survived Carnival in Binche) with real commitment. It's structure. Ghana's festivals are organised around lineage, land, and ancestry; Belgium's holidays are organised around a linguistic and regional settlement that took centuries of mutual suspicion to negotiate. One is a celebration of continuity. The other is a celebration of compromise. Both, somehow, involve enormous amounts of beer.

## Do's & Don'ts: Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn which festival belongs to which ethnic group and region before you reference it casually | Call a regional festival "the Ghanaian festival"; Homowo, Odwira, and Aboakyer are distinct traditions, not interchangeable |
| Dress respectfully and, where invited, in appropriate cloth for a durbar — it's an honour to be included | Treat a durbar as a photo-op spectacle; it's a living ceremonial and ancestral event, not a tourist show |
| Expect travel disruption around major festivals, as diaspora and locals alike return home for them | Schedule tight regional travel during peak festival season without checking local calendars first |
| Join the noise-making bans and customs seriously (some festivals restrict drumming or noise before the main event) | Dismiss a pre-festival ban on drumming/noise as superstition; it's taken as seriously as any civic law |

## Do's & Don'ts: Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether a holiday is federal, regional (Flanders/Wallonia/Brussels), or purely local before planning around it | Assume a holiday observed in Antwerp is observed in Liège; regional calendars genuinely differ |
| Embrace carnival season fully if you're anywhere near Binche, Aalst, or Stavelot — it's taken very seriously | Underestimate carnival; some towns effectively shut down and locals plan their whole year around it |
| Expect shops and offices to close for bridge days ("pont") clustered around a Thursday holiday | Assume a Friday after a Thursday holiday is a normal working day; many Belgians simply take it anyway |
| Learn at least the basic French/Dutch split in festival names; it affects which calendar applies to you | Refer to a Flemish festival by a French name (or vice versa) in the wrong region; it lands badly |

## Ghana: festivals as ancestral law

Ghanaian festivals are not holidays in the Western sense of a day off work — they are living civic and spiritual institutions, each tied to a specific people's history, harvest cycle, or ancestral covenant. Homowo, celebrated by the Ga people, commemorates surviving a historic famine and literally translates to "hooting at hunger" — it involves sprinkling kpokpoi (a corn-and-palm-nut dish) at doorways and on the ground as an offering, a custom that looks, to the uninitiated, like food being wasted, and is in fact one of the most meaningful acts of remembrance in the calendar. Odwira, observed by Akan groups including the Ashanti, is a purification and harvest festival tied to royal ancestry, often featuring a durbar of chiefs in full regalia that functions as both ceremony and a rare public display of traditional political authority.

What surprises outsiders most is the sheer organisational weight behind these events: diaspora Ghanaians plan international travel around them months in advance, local governments adjust schedules, and in some towns there are formal bans on drumming and loud noise in the days before a major festival, strictly enforced, because the silence itself is considered part of the ritual preparation. Treating any of this as a loosely scheduled cultural festival — the kind you can wander into with a camera and wander out of unchanged — misunderstands what's actually happening, which is a community actively, deliberately maintaining its connection to specific ancestors, specific land, and a specific historical memory.

## Belgium: public holidays as a map of old arguments

Belgium's holiday calendar is a quiet monument to the country's defining feature: it is not one country so much as three regions and three language communities who agreed, eventually, to share a king and a football team. Ten public holidays are federal and observed nationwide — Easter Monday, Labour Day, Ascension, and the rest — but regional community holidays (Flemish Community Day, French Community Day, German-speaking Community Day) are observed only in their respective regions, meaning a Fleming and a Walloon genuinely do not share the same full set of days off, despite living in the same small country.

Carnival is where Belgian festival culture gets properly serious. Binche's carnival — UNESCO-recognised — centres on the Gilles, costumed figures in wax masks and ostrich-feather headdresses who throw oranges into crowds in a ritual nobody fully explains to outsiders and everybody takes completely seriously. Aalst's carnival leans satirical and deliberately provocative, built around floats that mock current events with a bluntness that occasionally causes genuine diplomatic headaches. These aren't quaint local colour — in their towns, carnival season effectively overrides the normal calendar, with businesses closing and entire extended families reorganising their year around it.

The Belgian habit of taking a "pont" (bridge day) — an extra day off connecting a Thursday holiday to the weekend — is unofficial but near-universal, and newcomers who schedule important meetings for that Friday will find the office conspicuously, collectively empty, with no formal announcement that this was ever going to happen.

Death and remembrance deserve their own mention, because both cultures treat it with far more public ceremony than many newcomers expect, just differently. Ghanaian funerals are, famously, major festival-scale social events in their own right — elaborate, expensive, often week-long celebrations of a life, complete with specific "fantasy coffins" carved to reflect the deceased's profession or passions, live music, and attendance numbering in the hundreds, treated as a celebration of a life fully lived rather than a solemn, hushed affair. Belgium's equivalent commemorative culture is quieter and more privately observed, though All Saints' Day (Toussaint/Allerheiligen) on 1 November is a genuine nationwide public holiday specifically for visiting and tending family graves, a moment of collective, if understated, remembrance that briefly fills every cemetery in the country with flowers, mostly chrysanthemums, and families standing together in a silence that would look almost austere next to a Ghanaian funeral's drumming and dancing.

## The Verdict

Ghana's festivals win on depth — there is nothing in Belgium's calendar with the ancestral, civic weight of Homowo or Odwira, and that's not a criticism of Belgium, it's just a different kind of country with a different kind of history. Belgium wins on sheer commitment to the bit — nowhere else have I seen a government effectively sanction an entire town vanishing for Carnival with no apology offered. If you want festivals that connect you to centuries of specific ancestry, Ghana delivers something genuinely rare. If you want a public holiday calendar that doubles as a map of regional grievances nobody's fully resolved, Belgium is an education in itself.

## Conclusion

The sting, if there is one, is this: Ghana's festivals ask you to remember where you came from, and Belgium's holidays ask you to remember that "where you came from" is genuinely, administratively complicated even within one small European country. Both calendars reward the same thing in the end — paying attention to who's actually celebrating what, and why, before you assume your neighbour's day off is also yours. Get that wrong in Ghana and you'll miss a profound ceremony. Get it wrong in Belgium and you'll just show up to a locked office, slightly embarrassed, while a Fleming fifty kilometres away is still very much at their desk.
