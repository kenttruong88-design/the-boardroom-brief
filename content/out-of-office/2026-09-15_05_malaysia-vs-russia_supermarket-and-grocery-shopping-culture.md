# One Country Argues With You at the Fish Counter. The Other Doesn't Speak to You at All, and Both Are Being Perfectly Reasonable.

🇲🇾 **Malaysia** · 🇷🇺 **Russia**

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country reveals its true character at the till, and I say this as someone who has queued for cabbage in minus fifteen degrees and haggled over a durian in forty-degree humidity within the same calendar year. Malaysia treats grocery shopping as a multilingual, multi-sensory negotiation that happens across three or four separate venues before breakfast is even a certainty. Russia treats it as a solved problem — efficient, cold, occasionally beautiful, entirely without small talk — and executes it with the grim competence of a country that spent seventy years perfecting the queue as an art form and has since perfected the opposite of the queue, which is somehow worse.

I have now lived long enough in both to have opinions nobody asked for, which is, incidentally, my entire professional function. What follows is not a travel brochure. It is a field report from two grocery ecosystems that could not be more different in temperament, and both of which will make you a better, more patient human being, provided you survive the adjustment period without weeping in a car park.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇾 Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go to the wet market (pasar basah) before 9am for the best fish and produce | Touch fruit aggressively before buying — a light squeeze is tolerated, a full inspection tour is not |
| Learn to bargain gently at the pasar malam (night market) — a polite counter-offer is expected | Expect halal and non-halal sections to be casually intermingled — supermarkets separate them with real seriousness |
| Master the three-tier system: wet market for fresh, mini-mart for daily top-ups, hypermarket for the monthly stock-up | Assume every supermarket keeps the same hours — Ramadan brings drastically extended evening trading for the Bazaar Ramadan rush |
| Bring your own bags — most supermarkets now charge for plastic | Try to rush the checkout auntie mid-conversation with the cashier — the queue moves at the speed of relationships, not efficiency |

#### 🇷🇺 Russia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the weight-and-price sticker system before queuing for deli items | Make eye contact and smile at cashiers expecting a matching response — service is functional, not performative |
| Explore a rynok (traditional market) at least once for produce, honey, and pickled everything | Dawdle at the till bagging your own shopping — Russian checkout lines move fast and reward preparation |
| Get comfortable with 24-hour hypermarkets like Perekrestok, Magnit, or Lenta | Expect fresh produce variety in winter to match summer — the seasonal swing in quality and price is dramatic |
| Carry small cash or a working card at all times — self-checkout is widespread and unforgiving of indecision | Assume all stores are alike — a dour Soviet-holdover gastronom and a gleaming Azbuka Vkusa are a century apart |

## Malaysia: A Grocery Ecosystem With No Single Correct Address

Nobody in Malaysia does their grocery shopping in one place, and anyone who tells you otherwise has not been paying attention to their own life. The Malaysian household operates a rotating, overlapping system: the pasar basah for fish and vegetables that were, in a meaningful sense, alive a few hours earlier; the mini-mart on the corner for the milk you forgot; the hypermarket on a Saturday for rice sacks, cooking oil, and the sort of bulk-buy mission that requires a trolley the size of a small vehicle. This is not disorganisation. It is a rational response to a country where fresh produce, prepared food, and packaged goods each have a venue that does that one thing better than anywhere else could.

The wet market is the emotional centre of this system and the place where Malaysia is most unambiguously itself. Arrive at 7am at a market in Petaling Jaya or Penang and you'll find a controlled chaos of Chinese fishmongers shouting prices, Malay vegetable sellers arranging chillies into architectural pyramids, and Indian spice stalls that smell like the inside of a very good curry. Bargaining happens, but gently — this is not the aggressive theatre of a Middle Eastern souk, it's a soft, almost apologetic back-and-forth that ends with everyone satisfied and nobody feeling cheated. The multi-ethnic character of Malaysian society is nowhere more visible than at the wet market, where three completely different culinary traditions shop side by side and occasionally borrow each other's ingredients without ceremony.

The supermarket tier tells a different, more corporate story. AEON and Lotus's (the rebranded Tesco, a fact that still confuses long-term expats) are enormous, air-conditioned, and organised with real logic — the halal and non-halal sections are meaningfully separated, not just labelled, reflecting a seriousness about dietary law that a visiting European might initially mistake for excessive bureaucracy. It is not. It is a country that has figured out how to serve multiple religious dietary requirements at retail scale without anyone having to think about it twice.

Then there's Ramadan, which reorganises the entire retail calendar. The Bazaar Ramadan pop-up markets that spring up every evening during the fasting month are grocery shopping reinvented as street festival — vendors selling kuih, grilled meats, and fresh juice to a crowd that has been waiting all day and shows it. If you only ever shop at the supermarket, you have not seen Malaysian food culture; you have seen its filing cabinet.

## Russia: Retail as a Solved, Slightly Joyless Equation

Russian grocery shopping runs on a logic so different from Malaysia's that it barely counts as the same activity. Where Malaysia distributes the task emotionally across venues and relationships, Russia has engineered it into a system: efficient, hierarchical, and almost entirely free of the social performance that makes shopping elsewhere feel like a cultural event. This is not a criticism, or not only one. It is simply what happens when a country spends decades solving the logistics of feeding a vast population across eleven time zones and lands on cold efficiency as the answer.

The modern Russian supermarket — Perekrestok, Magnit, Lenta, or the noticeably upmarket Azbuka Vkusa — is a genuinely impressive piece of retail infrastructure. Shelves are deep, hours are generous (many run 24 hours in major cities, a fact that continues to astonish visitors from countries where Sunday trading is treated as a moral hazard), and the self-checkout technology is often ahead of what you'd find in Western Europe. What it lacks is warmth, and this is not an oversight. The cashier is not there to have a moment with you. The cashier is there to move product, and the entire system — from the beeping scanner to the expectation that you bag your own shopping at speed — is built around that single, unsentimental premise.

The deli and produce counters introduce a peculiarly Russian bureaucratic wrinkle that catches every newcomer at least once: you weigh your own cheese or sausage on a scale near the counter, which prints a barcoded sticker, which you then present at the till. Skip this step and you will be sent back, without sympathy, to do it properly. It is a small ritual, but it tells you something — Russian retail systems, even in their most modern form, still carry a faint procedural rigidity, an inherited instinct that a task done correctly means a task done in the correct order.

The rynok, Russia's answer to the wet market, is where the system relaxes slightly. Farmers sell honey by the season, pickled vegetables by the jar, and produce that actually tastes of something, at prices that reward the shopper willing to compare five stalls before committing. Winter changes everything: fresh produce variety collapses, prices for anything not grown within a thousand kilometres spike, and the household reverts to pickled, preserved, and frozen staples that would have been familiar to a Soviet grandmother. Russia does not apologise for its seasons. It just adjusts.

## The Verdict

If you want grocery shopping to be an experience — a small daily theatre with characters, negotiation, and genuine warmth — Malaysia wins comfortably and without much of a fight. The wet market alone justifies the trip; the multi-venue rhythm of Malaysian shopping turns a chore into something closer to a social circuit, and you will, against your own expectations, come to look forward to it.

But if you want grocery shopping to simply work — to be fast, logical, well-stocked, and completely indifferent to your feelings — Russia's retail system is the more honest machine. It does not pretend to care about you, and there's a strange relief in that. Nobody is performing friendliness they don't feel. The scale ticket system is annoying exactly once; after that, it's just how things are done, and you adapt because the alternative is standing there looking confused while a queue forms behind you with visible impatience.

My honest allegiance is with Malaysia, because I would rather be gently overcharged by someone who likes me than efficiently processed by someone who doesn't care either way. But I say that as a person who has never had to grocery shop through a Moscow winter, and I suspect that changes the calculation considerably.

## Conclusion

Grocery shopping is the most honest cultural indicator there is, because nobody performs it for an audience — it's just what people do when they think no one important is watching. Malaysia shops the way it lives: relationally, across multiple small worlds that overlap without merging, with warmth built into even the most mundane transaction. Russia shops the way its history taught it to: efficiently, without illusions, prepared for winter because winter is always eventually coming. Neither approach is wrong. But only one of them will make you feel like a human being was pleased to see you, and after enough years of relocating, you start to notice which one you're quietly grateful for. Choose your grocery bag accordingly, and for heaven's sake, learn the scale-ticket system before you embarrass yourself in Moscow.
