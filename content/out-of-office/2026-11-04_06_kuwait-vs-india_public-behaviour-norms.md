# Kuwait Will Fine You for Hanging Laundry on Your Own Balcony. India Will Just Honk at You Until You Move.

🇰🇼 Kuwait vs 🇮🇳 India
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Kuwait regulates public behaviour with the precision of a country that has thought very hard about exactly where the line is and then written it into law with a specific fine attached — 100 to 500 dinars for hanging laundry where the street can see it, 2,000 to 5,000 for barbecuing on a sidewalk, 100 for eating in public during Ramadan daylight hours whether you're Muslim or not. India regulates public behaviour the way a leaf blower regulates a conversation: loudly, constantly, and with a fine on the books — ₹1,000 for excessive honking — that absolutely nobody is cashing in.

One country has criminalized spontaneity down to the barbecue. The other has simply decided that 125 decibels is an acceptable background hum for a Tuesday. In fourteen relocations, I have never had to recalibrate "normal" this hard, this fast, twice in the same piece.

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇰🇼 Kuwait

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your dress code right before you even land — one reported case saw a work visa revoked over "inappropriate" embassy attire | Eat, drink or smoke in public during Ramadan daylight hours — the law applies to everyone, Muslim or not, and carries jail time |
| Socialize at home — private parties are the default nightlife, since bars and clubs effectively don't exist | Expect to simply walk into a hotel pool as a single adult — some venues refuse unmarried guests by rule, not preference |
| Budget self-censorship around the opposite gender as a genuine daily adjustment, not a figure of speech | Hang laundry facing the street or barbecue on a sidewalk — a draft cleanliness law puts real fines on both |

## Do's & Don'ts — 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to be honked at constantly — it's functional communication (a sales pitch, a warning, a request to be overtaken), not pure aggression | Expect the ₹1,000–2,000 honking fine, or the "silence zones" near hospitals, to actually change anything on the street |
| Accept that queue-crowding is genuinely debated by Indians themselves — it's not a settled cultural rule you're missing | Assume littering or spitting is consequence-free everywhere — enforcement and fines vary wildly by city, from ₹250 to ₹5,000 |
| Expect unsolicited personal questions in public — age, marital status — as fairly normal social contact | Expect uniform PDA norms — there's no Kuwait-style law, but strong informal social policing exists anyway |

## Kuwait: The Line Is Precisely Drawn and Enforced With a Fine Schedule

Kuwait's approach to public behaviour reads less like culture and more like a tax code — remarkably specific, remarkably enforced. A draft 2022 cleanliness law puts a 100–500 dinar fine on hanging laundry visibly from a balcony facing the street, and a startling 2,000–5,000 dinar fine on barbecuing on a sidewalk, in a park, or along a public waterfront. The Ramadan rules carry real teeth regardless of your faith: eating, drinking or smoking in public during daylight hours during the holy month is illegal under a law dating to 1968, punishable by up to 100 dinars and a month in jail, with businesses that allow it facing closures of up to two months. This isn't selectively enforced folklore — Gulf News covers it as straightforward news, the way another country might cover a parking-fine update.

The social dimension runs on the same precision, just without the formal fine schedule. InterNations' own 2022 Expat Insider survey found 57% of expats in Kuwait feel they cannot openly express their opinions — against an 18% global average — ranking Kuwait dead last of 52 surveyed destinations on that measure, worse even than China. On the ground, the texture of that constraint shows up constantly: one expat.com poster describes the real day-to-day adjustment as navigating an informal "moral police" governing interactions with the opposite gender, not a figure of speech but a literal description of near-constant self-monitoring. Public affection is frowned on even for married couples — holding hands is tolerated, hugging or kissing is not — and some hotel pools and beach day-passes refuse entry to single men or unmarried couples outright under a Ministry of Interior-linked rule, with one hotel running a women-only day instead. Dress policing can escalate further than a disapproving look: a December 2024 case saw a Kuwaiti embassy abroad deny a woman's work-travel visa over her clothing at the embassy itself, after which Kuwait's Interior Ministry revoked her work permit and put her on a non-entry list. The upshot of all this precision is, oddly, a social life that retreats almost entirely indoors — without bars or clubs to speak of, private home parties become the default venue, which at least one poster found "more liberal than Saudi or Iran" once you're inside the door.

## India: No Fine Schedule, Just an Overwhelming Volume of Unwritten Rules

India's public space runs on an entirely different mechanism — not codified restriction but sheer sensory and social volume that everyone has simply agreed to live inside. Urban honking reportedly averages around 100 decibels, comparable to a leaf blower, spiking to roughly 125 in some measurements — louder than a rock concert — and the reasons are structural, not just impatient drivers: rickshaw and taxi drivers honk at pedestrians as an actual sales pitch, trucks lacking mirrors paint "Horn Please" on their backs explicitly inviting other drivers to honk when overtaking, and motorbikes honk continuously through dense pedestrian areas as a functional warning system in the absence of reliable pavements. There is, genuinely, a legal framework meant to rein this in — a 2019 Motor Vehicles Act amendment sets a ₹1,000 fine (₹2,000 for repeat offenders) and mandates 100-metre "silence zones" around hospitals and schools — but one traffic officer quoted in a Bengaluru civic outlet put the gap between law and reality bluntly: "the horn has lost its meaning and is now just a tool to vent out frustration," with no real mechanism to trace or ticket offenders at scale.

The queueing question turns out to be genuinely contested among Indians themselves, which surprised me more than any fixed rule would have. A Reddit thread asking why people stand and touch so closely in lines draws answers ranging from "lack of civic sense" to population density comparisons with similarly sized Western cities, to a more practical theory: leave a gap, and someone else takes your spot, so nobody risks leaving one. It's not a rule you're failing to learn — it's a live debate you're simply joining. Littering and spitting carry real fines in places, wildly inconsistent by city: Varanasi's rules set 250 rupees for spitting and up to 5,000 for creating unsanitary standing water, enforcement levels that vary as much as the honking does. And where Kuwait legislates public affection directly, India relies instead on dense informal social policing — unsolicited questions about your age or marital status mid-conversation, strangers staring at a jogger, a public landscape governed less by statute than by sheer accumulated social pressure.

## The Verdict

Kuwait at least tells you exactly where the line is, fine amount included, which is its own strange mercy — you can memorize the rules and follow them. India gives you no rulebook at all, just an overwhelming, constant negotiation with everyone else's horn, elbow and opinion about your relationship status. I'd rather navigate India's chaos, honestly — at least nobody's revoking my visa over a blouse.

## Conclusion

The honest lesson from both countries is that "public behaviour norms" can mean two completely different things: a precise legal code you can study and follow, or an ambient social pressure you can only absorb by living inside it long enough. Kuwait rewards compliance with a fine schedule you can actually read in advance. India rewards patience with a volume level you eventually stop noticing, mostly. Pack a liquor permit mentality for one. Pack earplugs, metaphorically, for the other.
