# Mongolia Will Freeze You Solid. Spain Will Just Melt You Slowly and Make You Nap About It.

🇲🇳 Mongolia vs 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Weather shapes daily rhythm more than almost any other cultural force, and Mongolia and Spain represent two of the most extreme, and extremely different, adaptations on the planet. Ulaanbaatar is the coldest capital city on Earth, routinely dropping below minus thirty in January, and Mongolians have built an entire civilisation's worth of practical coping mechanisms around not dying of exposure. Spain, meanwhile, faces summer heat that regularly pushes past forty degrees in the interior, and has responded not with technical innovation but with a cultural institution so relaxed it's practically a philosophy: the siesta, plus air conditioning deployed with the discipline of a military operation.

I have worn every piece of clothing I owned, simultaneously, in Ulaanbaatar in February and still felt my eyelashes freeze together, and I have stood on a Madrid street in August at 3pm watching an entire city simply switch off, shutters down, streets empty, everyone apparently agreeing that productivity is a summer luxury nobody can currently afford. Both responses are, in their own way, entirely rational.

## Do's and Don'ts: Mongolia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Layer properly — wool and down, not cotton, which traps moisture and makes cold worse | Underestimate how fast frostbite sets in; exposed skin in a Ulaanbaatar winter is a real emergency risk |
| Invest in proper winter boots rated for extreme cold, not just "warm-looking" ones | Assume city infrastructure means city-level warmth; heating quality varies hugely by building |
| Respect the ger (yurt) heating etiquette if visiting — don't block the stove or step on the threshold | Travel to rural areas in winter without serious preparation and local guidance |
| Check air quality reports in winter — Ulaanbaatar's coal-heating smog is a serious health issue | Assume summer means mild weather everywhere; Mongolia's continental climate swings to extreme heat too |

## Do's and Don'ts: Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Adjust your schedule to the heat — do errands early morning or after 8pm, rest through peak hours | Schedule strenuous activity or exercise during the 2–5pm heat window in summer |
| Embrace the siesta shutdown — many shops and services genuinely close midday in summer, especially in smaller towns | Expect city-wide business hours to match Northern European norms in July and August |
| Hydrate constantly and seek shade — Spanish public spaces are designed around it (awnings, plazas, fountains) | Assume air conditioning is universal; many older buildings and homes rely on shutters and cross-ventilation instead |
| Learn to enjoy a late dinner — the entire eating schedule shifts later specifically because of the heat | Get frustrated by "closed for August" signs — much of the country pauses collectively during peak heat |

## Mongolia: Survival as a Daily Practical Skill

Ulaanbaatar's winters aren't merely cold, they're a genuine annual test of infrastructure and endurance — the coldest capital city in the world, with temperatures regularly dropping to minus thirty or below for weeks at a stretch, made worse by a coal-heating smog problem so severe it periodically shuts down schools and prompts public health warnings. Mongolians treat this not as an inconvenience but as a fundamental design constraint around which the entire year is organised, and the practical competence this produces is honestly humbling to witness as an outsider.

Layering is a genuine skill here, refined over generations — wool, felt, and down are prioritised over cotton, which traps sweat and turns into a liability the second temperatures drop. Traditional dels (the long, wrapped Mongolian coat) are still worn widely, not as costume but because they're simply better engineered for the climate than most modern Western winter wear. Ger (yurt) culture, still central to a significant portion of Mongolian life even in and around Ulaanbaatar, comes with its own detailed heating etiquette — you don't step on the threshold, you don't block the stove's heat from the honoured seating area, and the central stove is treated with a genuine reverence that reflects just how central staying warm is to staying alive.

What surprises visitors most is the flip side: Mongolia's continental climate means summers can swing to genuine heat, and the country's herding communities have equally sophisticated adaptations for that extreme too — moving livestock seasonally, adjusting the ger's felt layers for ventilation rather than insulation. The dzud — a specific Mongolian term for a severe winter following a dry summer, devastating to livestock and rural communities — shows just how seriously weather is tracked and prepared for at a national level, not as background chat but as a matter of genuine economic and survival planning. Visiting Mongolia teaches you fast that "weather small talk" elsewhere in the world is a hobby; here, it's closer to a professional discipline.

## Spain: Heat Managed Through Collective Surrender

Spain's approach to extreme heat is, refreshingly, almost the opposite of Mongolia's technical precision — it's cultural, communal, and built around the radical idea that fighting the heat directly is less effective than simply restructuring the entire day around it. The siesta, much mythologised and much misunderstood by outsiders as pure laziness, is in fact a genuinely sensible response to summer afternoons that regularly exceed forty degrees in interior cities like Madrid, Seville, or Córdoba — working, shopping, or exercising through that window isn't virtuous, it's simply unpleasant and mildly dangerous, so Spain collectively agreed decades ago to just... not.

Daily rhythm shifts accordingly: shops in smaller towns still close for several hours at midday, reopening in the early evening; lunch is the day's biggest meal, eaten around 2 or 3pm; dinner doesn't start until 9 or 10pm, once the worst heat has broken. Foreigners who arrive expecting Northern European business hours in July find themselves confused and hungry, standing in front of shuttered shops at 3pm wondering where an entire city has gone, not realising everyone is simply inside, curtains drawn, doing the sensible thing.

Architecturally, Spain leans on centuries-old passive cooling wisdom rather than universal air conditioning — thick stone walls, small windows, interior courtyards, and the ubiquitous persiana (rolling shutters) that get lowered through the hottest hours to keep interiors bearable without mechanical cooling. Public space is similarly designed around heat management — shaded plazas, awnings, fountains, and siesta-adjusted opening hours for parks and pools. Come August, entire cities like Madrid noticeably empty out as residents decamp to the coast or the mountains, and "cerrado por vacaciones" (closed for holidays) signs become so common they're barely worth commenting on. It's not inefficiency; it's a national agreement that surviving August well matters more than performing productivity through it.

## The Verdict

Mongolia wins on sheer technical mastery — surviving a Ulaanbaatar winter requires genuine, specific, hard-won competence, and watching locals navigate it with total unbothered confidence is genuinely impressive. Spain wins on quality of life during its extreme — nobody in Madrid is suffering through the heat quite the way Ulaanbaatar residents grind through winter; they're mostly just having a long lunch and a nap. If I had to choose which extreme to live through annually, Spain's heat-surrender lifestyle wins easily — there's something appealing about a country that responds to hardship not with heroics but with a shrug, a shutter, and a very late dinner.

## Conclusion

Mongolia and Spain sit at opposite ends of the weather-survival spectrum, and both have made peace with their extremes in ways that reveal something true about each culture — Mongolia through hard-earned technical competence, Spain through collective, unapologetic surrender to the schedule the heat demands. Neither country will apologise for its climate, and neither should have to. Pack the wool for Ulaanbaatar. Pack the patience, and an appetite for very late dinners, for Madrid. Only one of these will actually try to kill you if you get it wrong.
