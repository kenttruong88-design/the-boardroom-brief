🇵🇭 Philippines 🇧🇳 Brunei
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The Philippines will court you with the enthusiasm of a telenovela and the patience of a saint — serenades, go-betweens, months of "we're just talking," and a courtship choreography so elaborate it makes Western dating apps look like vending machines. Brunei, by contrast, will remind you — gently, then not so gently, via the Syariah Penal Code — that public displays of romantic intent between unmarried people are a matter the state has opinions about. One country turns dating into performance art for an audience of aunties. The other turns it into something you handle the way you'd handle contraband: quietly, discreetly, and never where anyone official might be looking.

I've watched both systems operate up close, and the thing nobody tells you is that both are, in their own way, about protecting something precious — just radically different somethings. The Philippines protects the family's investment in who you end up with. Brunei protects public order and religious propriety. Neither is interested in your personal autonomy as the organising principle, and if that offends your dating-app sensibilities, you are going to have an interesting year.

## Do's & Don'ts: Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect "the chase" — a prolonged, sincere courtship phase before anything is official | Try to fast-track intimacy; it reads as disrespect, not confidence |
| Meet and charm the family early; approval matters more than you'd think | Skip the family entirely and assume the relationship exists in a bubble of two |
| Use a go-between (a "tita" or mutual friend) to test interest before a direct approach | Declare feelings cold, without any social groundwork — it can spook rather than flatter |
| Show public affection modestly — hand-holding is fine, enthusiasm is fine, overt PDA draws stares | Assume Western-style open PDA is neutral; it often isn't, especially outside big cities |

## Do's & Don'ts: Brunei

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep any unmarried romantic relationship strictly private and off public display | Hold hands, hug, or show affection in public — khalwat laws govern "close proximity" between unmarried couples |
| Socialise in mixed group settings rather than visible one-on-one outings early on | Check into a hotel room or private space with a partner you're not married to — this is a real legal risk, not folklore |
| Respect that Friday prayer times affect venue hours and social scheduling broadly | Schedule anything that assumes Brunei runs on a secular weekend rhythm — it doesn't |
| Default to conservative dress and conduct in any mixed social setting | Treat Brunei's quiet nightlife as an oversight to work around; it's a deliberate policy, enforced |

## The Philippines: courtship as a full-contact sport

Filipino dating culture runs on *pakikisama* — group harmony — which means a courtship rarely stays between two people for long. The "pa-plano" stage, where a suitor orchestrates elaborate, low-stakes group hangouts specifically engineered to let two people spend time together without the terrifying commitment of calling it a date, can run for weeks. Then comes "the chase": flowers possibly delivered to a workplace, late-night texts that are sincere rather than performative, and a willingness to be visibly, publicly earnest that would read as desperate in most Western dating scenes and reads here as simply doing it properly.

Meeting the family isn't a milestone reserved for serious relationships — it often happens embarrassingly early, because the family's assessment matters, and a *tita* (auntie) with reservations about your intentions can quietly sink a courtship before it's even been named as one. Expats who treat this as optional, or try to keep a relationship compartmentalised away from a partner's family, frequently find themselves mysteriously, permanently stalled at "we're close friends."

Public affection sits in an interesting middle zone: hand-holding and arm-in-arm walking are completely normal and visible everywhere; anything more enthusiastic draws the kind of attention that isn't hostile so much as collectively, theatrically nosy. The Philippines doesn't ban public romance. It just makes it everyone's business, which, depending on your temperament, is either charming or unbearable.

## Brunei: romance under surveillance, by design

Brunei operates under a dual legal system where Syariah law sits alongside civil law, and the khalwat provisions — prohibiting "close proximity" between an unmarried man and woman who aren't closely related — are enforced, not decorative. This isn't a cultural quirk you can charm your way around; religious enforcement officers have, on record, conducted checks on couples in parked cars, hotel rooms, and secluded public spots. For an expat used to dating as a visible, social, documented activity (hello, Instagram-official), the adjustment is less "be a bit more discreet" and more "operate as though under genuine legal risk, because you are."

What this produces, socially, is a dating culture conducted almost entirely through group settings and private, trusted networks — mixed-gender friend groups where romantic interest can develop without ever becoming a visible "couple" in public space. Nightlife is minimal; alcohol is effectively banned nationwide; and the rhythm of the week bends around Friday prayers in a way that reorganises what "going out" even means. None of this means no one dates. It means dating happens in a lower-visibility register than almost anywhere else expats are likely to have lived, and expats who try to import a more public, demonstrative dating style — PDA in a mall, an overnight stay flagged by hotel staff — are not engaging in a grey area. They are taking an actual legal risk, and more than one expat relocation guide exists specifically to say so in plain language.

Breaking up reveals the same cultural divide in reverse. In the Philippines, ending a relationship is rarely a private, two-person affair either — families who were consulted during the courtship often weigh in on its ending too, and a public breakup can carry genuine social consequences, including gossip that follows both parties for months in smaller communities. Social media amplifies this further: a relationship status change, or its conspicuous absence, gets noticed and discussed within an extended social circle almost immediately. In Brunei, the low-visibility dating culture extends naturally to its endings as well — because so few relationships were ever publicly declared in the first place, there's rarely a dramatic public unravelling to witness; things simply, quietly stop, often known only to the two people involved and perhaps one or two trusted friends. Expats used to the modern Western ritual of a public "it's complicated" or an announced single status find both systems disorienting in opposite ways: the Philippines because everyone already knows your business before you've processed it yourself, and Brunei because nobody will ever find out at all, which can feel oddly lonely in its own right.

## The Verdict

The Philippines makes you perform romance for an audience; Brunei makes you erase romance from any audience at all. If you want your love life to be a shared community event, complete with unsolicited commentary from strangers, Manila delivers that in spades, and there's something genuinely lovely about being courted by an entire extended family rather than just one nervous person. If you want to date quietly and keep your private life exactly that — private — Brunei forces a discretion that, once you adjust to it, some expats actually come to prefer. But "forces" is the operative word. One culture makes privacy socially difficult. The other makes visibility legally inadvisable. Choose your inconvenience.

## Conclusion

Here's the sting, delivered with love: the Philippines will teach you that romance is a community sport, and Brunei will teach you that romance can survive — even thrive — without an audience at all. Most expats arrive assuming their home country's dating rules are the neutral baseline and everything else is a variation. They aren't. They're both fully formed systems with their own internal logic, their own protections, and their own prices. Pay the price Manila asks — patience, family approval, public theatre — and you get a courtship that feels like being adored by a village. Pay the price Brunei asks — discretion, legal caution, lower visibility — and you get a love life that answers to no one but the two people actually in it. Just don't confuse either one for the version you grew up on. Neither of them is that.
