# One Country Shops in Silence, the Other Shops by Shouting Prices Across a Stall

🇸🇰 Slovakia vs 🇻🇳 Vietnam

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Bratislava, grocery shopping is a transaction so devoid of small talk that I once had an entire checkout interaction — scan, beep, pay, nod — without a single word spoken aloud. In Hanoi, buying a kilo of dragon fruit involves a negotiation, a joke about my terrible Vietnamese, and an update on the vendor's grandson's exam results, all before money changes hands. Fourteen moves in, I've learned that how a country buys its vegetables tells you more about its soul than its constitution does.

Slovakia has optimised the weekly shop into an efficient, low-interaction errand. Vietnam has turned it into a daily social ritual that happens to also produce dinner. Both feed you. Only one of them makes you feel like part of a community by the time you leave.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇸🇰 Slovakia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your own bags — plastic ones cost extra | Expect small talk at the checkout |
| Weigh your own produce before the till | Block the aisle chatting with friends |
| Shop at discount chains for the best prices | Assume Sunday opening hours are guaranteed |
| Have coins ready for a shopping cart deposit | Dawdle — queues move fast and expect you to keep up |

### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go early morning for the freshest produce at wet markets | Expect fixed prices — bargaining is standard |
| Bring small notes — vendors rarely have change for big bills | Squeeze fruit aggressively without buying |
| Build a relationship with a regular vendor for better prices | Assume supermarkets have replaced markets — they haven't |
| Haggle with good humour, not aggression | Be surprised by live seafood or poultry on display |

## Slovakia: The Weekly Shop as a Silent, Efficient Ritual

Slovak supermarkets run on the unspoken agreement that shopping is a task to be completed, not an experience to be savoured. Discount chains dominate the landscape, and Slovaks approach them with the brisk efficiency of people who have somewhere better to be. You weigh your own peppers, bag your own groceries at speed, and pay without any expectation of conversation beyond the bare transactional minimum. Dawdle at the till and you will feel the collective impatience of the queue behind you like a physical weight.

What foreigners find jarring is the near-total absence of the cashier chit-chat that softens grocery runs elsewhere. This isn't rudeness — Slovaks are perfectly warm once you know them — it's simply that the supermarket isn't considered a social space. It's a logistics exercise, executed with minimal friction and maximum speed.

Farmers' markets do exist and offer a warmer, more personal alternative, particularly for seasonal produce, but they've never fully displaced the discount supermarket's dominance the way markets have in other parts of Europe. Sunday closures, a legacy of labour protections, catch newcomers out constantly — plan your week's shop accordingly or face a very quiet Sunday evening with an empty fridge.

The upside of all this brisk impersonality is genuine efficiency. You're rarely in a Slovak supermarket for more than twenty minutes, prices are transparent and fixed, and there's a certain honesty to a system that doesn't pretend to be more than what it is: a place to get food, quickly, and leave.

## Vietnam: The Market Is Where the Neighbourhood Actually Lives

Vietnamese grocery culture, by contrast, treats the market as a daily social institution as much as a commercial one. Wet markets open before dawn, and the best produce is gone by mid-morning, which means serious shoppers arrive early and stay attentive. Bargaining isn't a loophole in the system, it's the system — prices are opening offers, not final figures, and a good-natured haggle is expected, even enjoyed, by both sides.

Building a relationship with a regular vendor pays dividends fast. Return often enough and you'll get better prices, first pick of the freshest stock, and eventually genuine friendship — the woman who sells me herbs in Hanoi has strong opinions about my cooking and isn't shy about sharing them. This is grocery shopping as ongoing relationship rather than one-off transaction, and it's genuinely one of the loveliest things about daily life here.

Supermarkets and convenience chains have expanded rapidly in urban centres, offering the fixed-price, air-conditioned alternative for those who want it, particularly imported goods. But for fresh produce, meat, and fish, the wet market remains dominant, live poultry and seafood included — a detail that unsettles newcomers before it simply becomes normal.

The sensory intensity takes adjustment: the noise, the smell, the sheer density of stalls competing for attention. But once you find your rhythm, and your vendors, grocery shopping stops being a chore and starts being the most human part of your day.

## The Verdict

Slovakia treats grocery shopping as a problem to be solved efficiently. Vietnam treats it as a relationship to be nurtured daily. If you want your errands invisible and frictionless, Bratislava's supermarkets will never once annoy you, and they'll never once surprise you either.

If you want your errands to actually connect you to the place you live, Hanoi's markets will slow you down, haggle with you, and hand you a free bunch of herbs "for luck" more often than makes any business sense. Suki's verdict: Vietnam wins for soul, Slovakia wins for anyone who resents grocery shopping as a concept and wants it over with fastest — and honestly, some days I want both versions of myself.

## Conclusion

Slovakia's supermarkets get you in and out with your dignity and your schedule intact. Vietnam's markets pull you into a daily social contract you didn't know you were signing, and somehow you end up grateful for it. Neither system is trying to be the other, and neither should. Learn Slovakia's rhythm and you'll shop like a local in a week. Learn Vietnam's rhythm and you'll be adopted by a produce vendor within a month — and honestly, that's the better deal.
