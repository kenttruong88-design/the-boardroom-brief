# Russians Will Shove You. Czechs Will Just Silently Judge You Instead.

🇷🇺 Russia vs 🇨🇿 Czech Republic

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a very particular kind of culture shock that arrives not from the big things — the language, the food, the weather — but from the small, physical choreography of standing near other humans. Russia and the Czech Republic, two post-Soviet neighbours who like to pretend they have nothing in common anymore, both run entirely different operating systems for exactly this. One treats personal space as a Western affectation. The other treats it as a sacred, unspoken contract you will be silently judged for breaking.

I have been elbowed into a Moscow metro car by a grandmother half my size who did not break eye contact once, and I have been quietly, devastatingly disapproved of by a Prague tram rider for putting my backpack on the seat beside me rather than the floor where it apparently belongs. Neither country raised its voice. Only one of them needed to.

**Do's & Don'ts: Russia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask "кто последний?" (who's last?) when joining any queue with no visible line | Expect queues to be orderly single files — they often split into parallel informal clusters |
| Accept being physically jostled on transport as completely normal, not rude | Apologise excessively for bumping into someone — it reads as oddly formal, even suspicious |
| Hold your place in line by staying near it, not standing rigidly in it | Expect strangers to apologise for shopping-cart collisions or crowd contact |
| Budget real time for any government-office queue — the national average is the longest in Europe | Assume a short visible line means a short wait; multiple lines often feed one window |

**Do's & Don'ts: Czech Republic**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Put your backpack on the floor on trams and metros, not on a seat | Shout, laugh loudly, or speak at volume on public transport |
| Hold strict unspoken queue order, even with no visible line marked | Snap your fingers or wave down a server — it's considered rude |
| Give up your seat in the understood order (elderly, pregnant, disabled) | Cross your arms while clinking a toast — seven years' bad luck, by local superstition |
| Hold eye contact for the full toast when someone raises a glass | Break queue order to "just ask a quick question" — you'll be corrected, politely but firmly |

## Russia: Queueing as a Contact Sport

Russians, by their own national data, wait longer in line than almost anyone else in Europe. An 18-country Nextep survey cited by the Moscow Times put the Russian average queue wait at 27 minutes — more than double Italy's 14 minutes and over ten times Sweden's famously brisk 2. That statistic alone tells you something: this isn't a culture with a queueing problem so much as a culture that has made peace with queueing as a permanent condition of public life, the way other nations make peace with rain.

What makes a Russian queue genuinely disorienting to outsiders isn't the wait — it's the shape. A Moscow Times opinion column from 2013, "How to Occupy a Queue in Russian," laid out the actual mechanics: you don't necessarily stand in a tidy line. You ask who's last ("кто последний?"), you note your position, and then you're free to drift, chat, step away, and reassemble when your turn approaches — a system that looks like chaos to a newcomer and functions, to a practiced eye, like a loosely-held reservation system with no app. Government offices frequently run several of these informal clusters simultaneously, all feeding into the same window, which explains why a line that "looks short" from the street can swallow forty minutes whole.

Personal space follows the same logic. The Cultural Atlas notes that Russians generally tolerate closer physical proximity and more incidental contact in public than Western European norms would consider comfortable — being leaned on in a near-empty subway car, nudged without apology in a shop aisle, or boarded onto a bus before you've quite finished stepping off it. One expat blogger, writing as "Russia Simplified," described this bluntly: personal space as a concept barely registers in daily Russian public life, and nobody apologises for a shopping-cart collision because nobody considers it an offence worth apologising for. It's not rudeness. It's a completely different threshold for what counts as an intrusion in the first place.

## Czech Republic: Silence Is the Loudest Rule

The Czech Republic runs on something closer to the opposite philosophy: minimal contact, maximum unspoken order, and a social contract so precise it doesn't need to be enforced out loud because everyone already knows the rules. Expats.cz has built an entire recurring feature around the behaviours that give Czechs away as quietly furious — "17 Expat Behaviors that Czechs Find Rude" reads like a catalogue of tiny physical etiquette failures: speaking too loudly on transport, keeping a backpack on a seat instead of the floor, failing to give up a seat to someone who's earned it by age or circumstance, snapping fingers at waitstaff.

None of these rules get explained to you on arrival. You learn them by committing them and absorbing the silence that follows — a very specific, very Czech form of disapproval that requires no words at all, just a look, or occasionally just a lack of a look, which is somehow worse. Queue order in Czech public life is treated with an almost sacred seriousness, even with no physical line marked on the ground: people simply know, by some social instinct outsiders take months to acquire, who arrived when.

Even the toast carries real weight. Tasting Table and other food-culture outlets have documented the Czech beer-toast superstition in detail: you must hold eye contact throughout the clink, and crossing your arms while doing so is believed to summon seven years of bad luck — a rule nobody states out loud at the table, because by the time you're at the table, you're expected to already know it. Noise, touch, and queue-jumping are all treated as minor social crimes. The punishment for each is identical: you simply won't be told, and you'll simply be judged.

## The Verdict

If you want a public culture that tells you exactly how it feels, loudly, immediately, with its elbows if necessary, Russia wins — there's something almost refreshing about a society that's stopped pretending personal space is a universal right rather than a cultural preference. You always know where you stand, mostly because someone is standing on you.

If you want a public culture that will never once raise its voice at you while making you feel, with devastating precision, exactly how wrong you were, the Czech Republic wins. Russia's chaos is honest. The Czech silence is surgical. I know which one leaves a longer bruise, and it isn't the one with the shoving.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Russia Simplified (Substack) — paraphrased: nobody warns you that "personal space" simply isn't a shared concept here. You will be leaned on in an empty train car. No one will apologise. You will, eventually, stop expecting them to.*

> *r/expats (paraphrased composite) — the Moscow queue system makes no sense until someone explains it: ask who's last, remember your spot, wander off, come back. Try to stand rigidly in line like a normal person and you'll just be confused when everyone else seems to be somewhere else.*

> *Quora (paraphrased composite) — I put my bag on an empty tram seat in Prague for one stop. Nobody said anything. The look I got lasted the rest of the ride. I have never done it since.*

## Conclusion

Here's the sting worth sitting with: both of these cultures have built entire unspoken constitutions around how close you're allowed to stand to another human being, and both will let you violate theirs without a single word of warning. Russia will simply absorb you into the crowd and expect you to keep up. The Czech Republic will let you break the rule once, record it silently, and never quite look at you the same way again. Pick your discomfort. One bruises your shoulder. The other bruises something you can't quite name, and it lasts considerably longer.
