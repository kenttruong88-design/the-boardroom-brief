# One Country Loses The Sun For Two Months. The Other Just Loses The Power Grid For A Weekend.

🇩🇴 Dominican Republic + 🇫🇮 Finland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Weather is destiny, and nowhere proves it quite like these two. The Dominican Republic spends half the year bracing for hurricanes with a shrug that borders on spiritual acceptance, while Finland spends a third of the year in a darkness so total the sun barely bothers clocking in. Both nations have built entire coping cultures around the specific ways their climate tries to ruin their lives, and both would find the other's problem faintly ridiculous.

I've sat through a tropical storm in Santo Domingo where the neighbours treated the blackout as an excuse for an impromptu street party, and I've spent a January in Helsinki watching grown adults sit in front of light-therapy lamps like they were charging phones. One country handles catastrophe with a shrug and a domino table. The other handles the absence of catastrophe — just relentless, grinding darkness — with vitamin D supplements and an alarming amount of quiet stoicism.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇩🇴 Dominican Republic**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stock water, batteries, and cash before hurricane season (June–November) | Panic when the power goes out — it's routine, not an emergency |
| Follow local Civil Defense alerts, not just international news | Assume a hurricane warning cancels plans; locals often wait it out |
| Keep your phone charged in advance of any named storm | Expect infrastructure to bounce back overnight — patience is required |
| Befriend a neighbour with a generator. Seriously | Drive during or right after heavy storms — flooding is fast and real |

**🇫🇮 Finland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a light therapy lamp before your first winter, not during it | Skip outdoor time in winter — locals walk daily regardless of dark |
| Take vitamin D supplements from October onward | Expect socialising to slow down; sauna culture keeps it very much alive |
| Invest in proper winter boots with real grip — ice is everywhere | Underestimate kaamos; the psychological toll is real, not dramatics |
| Embrace the sauna as therapy, not just tradition | Assume July's endless daylight means you'll sleep normally — you won't |

## Dominican Republic: Storms As A Way Of Life

Hurricane season here runs June through November, which means roughly half the calendar year is spent under some level of meteorological side-eye. What's fascinating, and genuinely admirable, is how little this disrupts daily rhythm. Dominicans have internalised storm prep the way other cultures internalise umbrella-grabbing — bottled water, batteries, and a functioning generator are just standard kit, not panic purchases. When a storm actually lands, the response is less "evacuate in terror" and more "move the plastic chairs inside, maybe cancel the beach trip, definitely don't cancel the domino game."

Power outages are so routine that entire neighbourhoods have informal systems worked out: whoever has a generator running becomes the unofficial hub for charging phones and keeping the beer cold, and this is treated as basic community infrastructure rather than a favour. There's a resilience here that's easy to romanticise from a distance and genuinely impressive up close — nobody's pretending the storms aren't dangerous, they've just refused to let unpredictability become paralysis. It helps that most of the country has decided that dancing through a blackout is preferable to sitting in the dark being miserable about it, a philosophy I fully endorse.

Where it gets genuinely serious is in poorer, flood-prone areas, where the same storms that are a minor inconvenience in a resort zone can be catastrophic. Don't mistake the resort-belt cheerfulness for the full national experience — hurricane damage in the DR has, historically, been devastating for infrastructure and livelihoods outside the tourist bubble, and treating storm season as a quirky vibe rather than a real risk is the kind of tourist naivety that gets people hurt.

## Finland: Darkness As A National Personality Trait

Finland's climate problem isn't dramatic weather events, it's the sun simply not showing up. In the far north, kaamos — the polar night — means weeks without a proper sunrise at all, and even in Helsinki, December daylight can shrink to around six hours of grey half-light. This isn't a minor seasonal dip; it's a documented mental health event, and Finland treats it as such with a level of institutional seriousness that puts most countries' approach to seasonal depression to shame.

Light therapy lamps are sold in ordinary shops, not specialty wellness boutiques, and using one is about as remarkable as carrying an umbrella. Vitamin D supplementation is basically assumed from October to March. And the sauna — genuinely, not as a tourist cliché — functions as a real psychological pressure valve, a warm, dark, social ritual that Finns use to counteract the cold, dark, isolating one happening outside. Skipping sauna culture here isn't just missing a tradition, it's missing the coping mechanism the entire society is quietly built around.

What nobody prepares you for is the flip side: summer, when the sun barely sets and Finns become almost manically social, cramming a year's worth of outdoor living, festivals, and lake-swimming into about ten frantic weeks of endless daylight. The exhaustion of permanent twilight in June is its own adjustment — you will not sleep properly, and everyone will insist this is fine.

## The Verdict

The Dominican Republic faces down real, occasionally destructive weather with music and improvisation. Finland faces down the mere absence of sunlight with clinical-grade preparation and an entire retail category built around correcting it. I'd argue Finland's problem is scarier precisely because it's slower and quieter — a hurricane announces itself, kaamos just erodes you gradually while everyone insists they're used to it. Still, I'd rather buy a lamp than evacuate a coastline, so on raw survivability, Finland edges it. On vibe, the DR wins by a landslide, storm damage notwithstanding.

## Conclusion

Weather doesn't just shape a country's infrastructure, it shapes its entire emotional operating system. The Dominican Republic has turned meteorological chaos into a communal coping ritual involving music, generosity, and an admirable refusal to panic. Finland has turned the sun's seasonal disappearing act into a matter of clinical preparation and hard-won stoicism. Visit either expecting the weather to behave, and you'll be humbled fast. Visit either having actually done the prep — batteries or a lamp, depending on hemisphere logic — and you'll cope roughly as well as the locals, which is really the only compliment either country is after.
