# One Country Assigns You a Government-Appointed Neighbourhood Parent. The Other Just Lets the Coffee Shop Handle It

🇲🇰 North Macedonia vs 🇹🇼 Taiwan — Neighbourhood and Community Life
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Community, it turns out, can be either bureaucratised or improvised, and both approaches produce results so effective they make Western "just say hi to your neighbours" advice look like a cruel joke. Taiwan formalises neighbourhood life through an actual civil-service position — the lizhang, an elected community chief who knows your business whether you like it or not. North Macedonia does the same job entirely through the kafana, the neighbourhood café-bar that functions as unofficial parliament, gossip exchange, and social safety net, with zero institutional backing whatsoever.

I have been personally reprimanded by a Taipei lizhang for not properly sorting my recycling, a genuinely mortifying experience delivered with the calm authority of someone who has done this a thousand times. I have also been adopted, without being consulted, by an entire kafana's regular clientele in a small Macedonian town, who decided within one visit that I needed feeding, opinions, and a nickname. Both are forms of community. Only one of them came with a laminated ID card.

## Do's & Don'ts: North Macedonia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Find your local kafana and become a regular — this is how you actually integrate | Expect fast service or a quick coffee; sitting for two hours is the entire point |
| Accept unsolicited advice, food, and opinions from neighbours as genuine care | Take offence at nosiness; knowing your business is how the community looks out for you |
| Learn a few Macedonian phrases — effort here is noticed and rewarded generously | Assume city and village community dynamics are the same; villages are far more tightly knit |

## Do's & Don'ts: Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn who your li's lizhang (neighbourhood chief) is — they handle everything from recycling rules to community notices | Ignore recycling and trash sorting rules; enforcement is real, social and sometimes official |
| Attend a neighbourhood temple festival or community event at least once | Assume anonymity in your building or block; the community office likely already knows you moved in |
| Appreciate how efficiently local disputes and needs get handled through the li system | Bypass the lizhang for local admin issues — they're often faster than calling a government hotline directly |

## North Macedonia: Community Built at a Kafana Table, No Appointment Necessary

North Macedonia's neighbourhood life runs on an institution that has no formal name, no elected official, and no paperwork, yet functions with startling reliability: the kafana. Part café, part bar, part town square, the kafana is where actual community business gets conducted — who needs help moving, whose grandmother is unwell, which local politician is currently being roundly mocked. Sitting for two or three hours over a single coffee or rakija isn't slowness, it's the mechanism itself; the lingering is the community-building.

What strikes most expats, particularly those from more privacy-guarding cultures, is how quickly and thoroughly Macedonians will insert themselves into your life once you're recognised as a regular. Neighbours will comment on your grocery bags, your visitors, your general wellbeing, not as intrusion but as a genuine, if occasionally overwhelming, form of care. In smaller towns and villages this intensifies further — everyone knows everyone's business, multiple generations deep, and a newcomer is simultaneously an object of intense curiosity and, within weeks, an adopted member of the collective, fed, teased, and looked after with a warmth that has nothing to do with any formal community structure and everything to do with sheer, stubborn Balkan hospitality.

This system has no institutional backstop — there's no official you can call if the informal network fails you, no equivalent of a neighbourhood office. It runs entirely on relationship, reputation, and reciprocity, which makes it either the warmest community experience of your life or a slightly claustrophobic one, depending entirely on how much you enjoy being known.

## Taiwan: Community as Civic Infrastructure, Down to the Recycling

Taiwan takes the opposite, formalised approach, embedding neighbourhood life into an actual layer of local government: the li, Taiwan's smallest administrative unit, each headed by an elected lizhang, a neighbourhood chief who is simultaneously community organiser, local government liaison, and unofficial keeper of the peace. Lizhang handle an astonishing range of hyper-local business — distributing government notices, mediating disputes, organising community events, and yes, enforcing the famously precise Taiwanese trash and recycling sorting system, which many first-time expats discover the hard way involves specific bag types, specific collection truck timings (announced by musical jingles that residents chase down the street with bags in hand), and genuine social consequence for getting it wrong.

This formalised structure produces a version of community that is efficient, low-friction, and occasionally unsettling in how much a government-adjacent figure knows about your household. Lizhang offices genuinely resolve neighbour disputes faster than any hotline, organise temple festivals and community events with real institutional backing, and function as the actual first point of contact for practical problems in a way that Western "call the council" bureaucracy rarely achieves with any warmth attached.

The tradeoff is that Taiwanese community life, precisely because it's formalised, can feel less like organic relationship and more like civic participation — you're not building friendship over three-hour coffees, you're being folded into an administrative system that happens to also care about you. It works remarkably well, and Taiwan's famously low crime rate and strong social cohesion owe something real to this structure, but it is community by institution rather than community by choice, and expats used to the latter need a mental adjustment to appreciate the former.

## The Verdict

I'm giving this one to North Macedonia, for reasons that have nothing to do with efficiency, which Taiwan wins outright. Community that emerges because people simply refuse to let each other be strangers, sustained by nothing but coffee, gossip, and stubborn warmth, moves me in a way that even the most beautifully run lizhang system cannot. Taiwan will sort your recycling and settle your disputes with genuine competence. North Macedonia will feed you, embarrass you, and claim you as one of its own within a fortnight. Only one of those is actually a love story.

## Conclusion

Taiwan proves that community can be engineered, institutionalised, and still feel genuinely caring, right down to a recycling jingle. North Macedonia proves the opposite: that community needs no institution at all, just a table, a coffee, and a culture that refuses to let a stranger stay a stranger for long. Move to Taiwan if you want your neighbourhood to function like a well-run small government. Move to North Macedonia if you want your neighbourhood to function like an overbearing, wonderful extended family you never signed up for.
