# Everyone's Money Is Stuck Somewhere, and That Somewhere Has a Queue Number

🇳🇬 NIGERIA vs GABON 🇬🇦

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a particular silence that falls over a room when you tell a Lagos banker you'd like to open an account "quickly." It's the silence of a man watching a tourist try to pet a wild hyena. He is not unkind. He is simply recalibrating how much of your week he is about to take. Nigeria has built, in a single generation, one of the most feverishly inventive fintech cultures on the planet — and somehow, simultaneously, has not shortened a single queue inside an actual banking hall. Both things are true. Nigerians have made peace with this the way you make peace with weather.

Gabon, four time zones and one civilizational temperament away, has the opposite problem dressed in the opposite outfit. Where Lagos is loud, improvisational, and powered by apps built by twenty-six-year-olds who've never seen a typewriter, Libreville is quiet, procedural, and still faintly perfumed by the French colonial civil service that built it — a system where the correct stamp matters more than the correct outcome, and where "come back Thursday" is not an insult, it is administrative doctrine. One country banks like it's racing you. The other banks like it's annoyed you're even here. Let's get into it.

**Do's & Don'ts: Nigeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your BVN sorted before you need it urgently | Assume any single bank app will work when the network dies on a Monday |
| Embrace mobile transfers — NIBSS instant payments are genuinely excellent | Walk into a banking hall expecting to be out in under 90 minutes |
| Keep a secondary fintech wallet (Moniepoint, OPay, Kuda) as backup | Trust POS "cash out" agents with your full balance without checking the rate |
| Smile at the security guard — he decides which queue you're in | Call customer service and expect anyone to answer with urgency |

**Do's & Don'ts: Gabon**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring photocopies of everything, twice, notarized | Expect English — French is the price of entry, literally |
| Build a personal relationship with one teller and keep it | Assume a BEAC/CEMAC regional rule applies the same way at every branch |
| Budget two to three visits for anything involving a stamp | Try to rush a Gabonese civil servant near lunch (12–3pm is sacred) |
| Keep hard currency for the "informal fee" nobody will call a fee | Confuse a oil-boom economy for an easy one — liquidity is not access |

## Nigeria: The Fastest Slow Country on Earth

Here is the genuinely staggering part: Nigerian banks moved roughly ₦284.99 trillion in electronic payments in the first quarter of 2025 alone — a 17.7% jump year-on-year, according to NIBSS data reported by ThisDay Live. That is not a typo and it is not a small, cute fintech story. That is a country that has built, out of sheer necessity and a chronically underbanked population, one of the most sophisticated instant-transfer rails anywhere, used by people who distrust the institutions running it completely and correctly.

The engine behind all of it is the Bank Verification Number, the BVN — a biometric identity layer the Central Bank forced onto every account in the 2010s to fight fraud and multiple-identity theft. It worked almost too well: over 66 million BVN-linked accounts now exist, according to tracking from mobileidworld.com, meaning your fingerprint is now functionally your signature, your ID, and your excuse when the system "is down," which it periodically, theatrically, is.

What nobody tells you before you land is that the digital leap didn't retire the bureaucracy — it just moved it sideways. The app is instant. The human verification behind it is not. You will still, at some point, need to walk into a physical branch, sit under a fluorescent light, and present a utility bill that is somehow never the right kind of utility bill. Nigeria has for years ranked among the hardest places on earth to do business, per the old World Bank index cited by business press — a fact that sits in genuinely comic tension with a population that invented four separate unicorn fintech companies to get around it. Nigerians didn't wait for the bureaucracy to improve. They built a parallel economy next to it and let the old one keep stamping forms to no one in particular.

## Gabon: Oil-Rich, Paper-Richer

Gabon has oil, a tiny population, one of the higher per-capita GDPs in sub-Saharan Africa, and — somehow — a banking experience that moves at the speed of a 1962 Parisian prefecture. The World Bank's 2022 Country Economic Memorandum put it diplomatically: Gabon's growth has been "driven by oil" and constrained by weak diversification and institutional friction that has nothing to do with resources and everything to do with process. Translation: the money is there. The road to it has four tollbooths, all unmarked.

Banking sits inside the CEMAC zone — six Central African countries sharing a currency, the CFA franc, and a regional central bank, the BEAC, headquartered not in Gabon but in Cameroon. This matters more than it sounds: a rule can change regionally and arrive in a Libreville branch weeks later, interpreted slightly differently by whichever teller got the memo. Oxford Business Group has tracked genuine reform momentum — new entrepreneur-focused lenders have started extending real credit to small businesses, and Gabon reportedly captures one of the largest single shares of CEMAC-wide bank credit of any member state. The money is moving. It is simply moving at the pace of a system built by and for a colonial administration that valued the correct stamp over the correct outcome, and nobody has told it the empire ended.

The result is a banking culture of relationships, not rails. You don't beat Gabonese bureaucracy with an app. You beat it by knowing someone's cousin, bringing an extra photocopy nobody asked for, and accepting that "Thursday" is a provisional concept that may, in fact, mean "the Thursday after next."

## The Verdict

If you want speed, Nigeria wins by a landslide — its mobile rails are faster than most of what I've used in London or Tokyo, full stop. If you want certainty — the quiet, grinding, eventually-it-works certainty of a system that has not been disrupted because nobody's bothered — Gabon wins on paper, which is fitting, because paper is the entire product.

But here's the sting: Nigeria built its brilliance by deciding the state could not be trusted to deliver, and Gabon's bureaucracy survives precisely because nobody has yet decided it's worth the fight to replace. One is a triumph born of necessity. The other is a stasis born of comfort — oil money cushions the friction enough that nobody in power feels the burn the way ordinary Nigerians feel theirs. I'd rather fight a system that's visibly trying to outrun itself than tolerate one perfectly content to stand still. Nigeria wins. Reluctantly. Loudly. Correctly.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Zikoko (zikoko.com/money), paraphrased — one contributor described waiting three separate visits just to get a replacement debit card reissued, after the first two attempts failed because the branch's system was "down for maintenance," a phrase she came to regard as a synonym for "we don't feel like it today."*

> *TalesMag Real Post Reports, Libreville, Gabon, paraphrased — a diplomatic spouse's posting report noted that opening even a basic local account required a letter from the employer, a letter from the embassy, and a notarized copy of a lease, and that the actual account activation still took "the better part of a month" after every document was accepted.*

> *Expat.com Nigeria forum, paraphrased composite — a long-term expat wrote that he'd stopped using his bank's hotline entirely and instead drove to the branch for anything urgent, because the call center existed, in his words, "mostly as a concept."*

## Conclusion

Neither country will apologise, and that's rather the point. Nigeria will keep building astonishing fintech on top of institutions that still make you queue for a passport photo nobody will look at twice. Gabon will keep running a quietly competent oil economy through an administrative culture that thinks urgency is vulgar. If you're the sort of person who needs systems to be *consistent* before they're fast, go to Gabon and bring patience, photocopies, and a decent French dictionary. If you'd rather your money move at the speed of thought while the institution underneath groans like a dying fax machine, Lagos is calling. Either way, pack snacks for the queue — because there is always, always a queue, and it does not care how exotic you find any of this. Nothing about needing a stamp is exotic. It's just Tuesday, somewhere, for someone.
