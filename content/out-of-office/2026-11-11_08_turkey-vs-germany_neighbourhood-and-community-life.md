# Turkey Knows Your Neighbour's Business. Germany Knows Your Neighbour's Quiet Hours

🇹🇷 Turkey vs 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Turkey, your neighbours know your name, your job, your mother's health and what you cooked on Tuesday. In Germany, your neighbours know your name from the doorbell and your washing-machine habits from the Hausordnung. One country treats the building as a family. The other treats it as a contract. Both are sincere, and both can drive you mad within a month.

The delicious complication is that Germany contains a great deal of Turkey. An estimated one hundred and eighty thousand German Turks live in Berlin, and two and a half to three million people of Turkish origin live in Germany, according to a food feature. That makes the real story less a comparison of two countries than a study of what happens when a neighbourhood culture travels and puts down roots. A source note: my retrievable pages are about Turkish communities in Berlin, not about life in Turkey, so my Turkish description is general knowledge, flagged where it matters.

## Turkey

I cannot cite a source for neighbourhood life inside Turkey, so this section is general knowledge. The Turkish neighbourhood, or mahalle, is a social unit before it is a postal one. The grocer, the baker, the tea house and the neighbour on the first floor form a web that knows who has visited, who is ill and who has done something worth discussing. Doors are open to a degree that startles northern Europeans.

Hospitality is the organising principle. Tea is offered, and then offered again, and a refusal is treated as an opening position. Children play in the street, elders sit outside, and a stranger who lingers is gently questioned. This is not nosiness in the sinister sense. It is a form of social insurance: everyone is known, and therefore everyone is looked after.

The cost is privacy. A newcomer who values quiet will find the attention intense, and the line between caring and monitoring is thin. The pleasure is the reverse: you are rarely alone, never lost, and always fed.

The sting for the returning expat is the realisation that the warmth is an obligation as much as a gift. You are expected to turn up, to join in and to reciprocate. A neighbourhood that looks after you also expects to be looked after.

A note on rhythm. The Turkish neighbourhood runs on an evening clock: shops open late, tea houses fill after work and the street is busiest when others have gone home. That is the pattern in the community's settled districts in Germany too, where the markets, restaurants and cafés extend the working day into something more sociable. A foreigner coming from a quieter culture should expect to be invited, offered food and asked questions, often on first meeting. The advice, which seems consistent across the traditions, is to accept the first offer, bring something small in return and let the relationship develop at its own warm, unhurried speed.

## Germany

German neighbourhood life, in the stricter sense, is a system of boundaries politely observed. But the Turkish neighbourhoods of Berlin show that the German city is more porous than its reputation. The food feature records a Türkenmarkt on Maybachufer in Neukölln running on Tuesdays and Fridays, more than three hundred community centres in Berlin, Turkish schools and several hamams. The women's hamam in Kreuzberg opened in 1988 in a former chocolate factory, which is the most Berlin sentence imaginable.

A profile of Yasin Müjdeci, born in Ankara, describes the café Luzia on Oranienstraße, opened in 2007, and the concept store Voo, opened in 2010, near Kottbusser Tor. Together they employ fifty people of twenty-two nationalities, with English as the working language. He told the interviewer, in effect, that his business belongs to no nation, and that Kreuzberg's mix is why he stays. Ismail Firat of Doyum in Kreuzberg put the other side: he will always feel more Turkish than German, but Berlin is home.

A blogger writing from Kottbusser Tor in 2011 described Café Kotti, built into an immigrant housing estate, and noted that secular Istanbul Turks often look down on Turks from Germany. He had wanted to send narrow-minded people to Istanbul, then concluded they could learn the same at Kottbusser Tor. Neighbourhood life here is a negotiation between traditions, a community that is German without being only German.

The sting is that the German default remains reserve. Outside these hubs, the stairwell nod is the standard and the quiet-hours rule is the constitution. The warmth exists, but you must find the right street, and the right street may be a ten-minute U-Bahn ride from the wrong one.

Markets deserve a paragraph of their own, because they are where the two cultures meet most openly. The market at Maybachufer, on Tuesdays and Fridays, is a place where German shoppers, Turkish families and curious newcomers share the same stalls, and where the habit of conversation across the counter survives against the German default of efficient silence. It is the one place where the formal, rule-bound city behaves like a bazaar, and it is a fine place to learn which of the two temperaments you actually prefer. Bring a bag, bring some cash and expect your questions about the cheese to be answered at length.

## The Verdict

Turkey wins on warmth and Germany on freedom, and the smart move is to understand which you actually crave. If you want to be known, fed and fussed over, a Turkish neighbourhood is a gift. If you want to be left alone to read in peace, a German one is a luxury.

The most interesting finding is that you can have both in Berlin, if you choose carefully. The Turkish districts offer a hybrid: markets, hamams, tea and community life inside a framework of German public services. That hybrid is arguably the best neighbourhood model in Europe, though its residents would resent my saying so.

For a corporate transferee, the question is how much sociability you can sustain. Turkey demands engagement. Germany permits withdrawal. I would pick Turkey for a year, to be reminded that strangers are made of the same stuff as friends, and Germany for the decade, to recover.

One last practical note for the transferee: choose your district before you choose your flat. A Berlin street near the markets and tea houses will give you a neighbourhood life that feels closer to the mahalle than to the Hausordnung, while a quiet residential block will give you the classic German arrangement of a polite nod and a printed rulebook. Visit at different times of day, including a weekend evening, and talk to the people in the nearest bakery. The street you rent on shapes your life more than the country you move to.

## Conclusion

A neighbourhood is a bargain between privacy and belonging. Turkey leans towards belonging and charges you in attention. Germany leans towards privacy and charges you in loneliness.

The lucky ones live in the overlap, with a tea glass in one hand and a house rulebook in the other. Find your street, learn your neighbours and decide how much of yourself you are willing to lend.
