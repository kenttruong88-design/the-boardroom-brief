# Switzerland Closes the Shops on Sunday and Calls It Civilisation. Czechia Keeps the Tills Open and Calls It Tuesday

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Shopping culture is a country's confession. It tells you what a society thinks time is for, whether rest is a right or a commercial opportunity, and how much it trusts you to plan ahead. Switzerland trusts you completely and expects you to have bought the milk on Saturday. Czechia assumes you will remember at nine on Sunday night, and that someone, somewhere, will still be on shift.

I moved between these two systems in a single year, and the transition was a lesson in humility. One of them is a clock you obey. The other is a convenience you take for granted, until you move to the first and discover what it means to stand in front of a locked supermarket on a Sunday afternoon.

## Czechia

I will be frank that I could not find a Czech source I trusted for this piece, so this section is opinion built on general knowledge, and you should check the details before you rely on them. Even so, the broad shape is not in dispute. Czech retail is flexible. Supermarkets, discounters and corner shops keep long hours, many open on weekends, and the culture assumes that a shop is a service, not a ceremony.

There are exceptions. As far as I understand, large shops are obliged to shut on a handful of public holidays, which means that a Czech Christmas Eve afternoon can catch a visitor out. Check the calendar if you are planning anything around a national holiday. But the default is openness, and the default expectation of a Czech shopper is that a basic need can be met at most times of day.

What Czechia does well is the everyday experience. Supermarkets are accessible, prices are reasonable, and neighbourhood shops are plentiful. A newcomer can fall into a routine within a week. The weak spot is service. Czech retail has a reputation for efficient indifference: you will be served, accurately and without ceremony, but not welcomed. Smiles, in my experience, are rationed and given for good reasons.

The other signature of Czech shopping is cross-border pull. Neighbours from richer countries come to buy cheaper goods and services, which has sometimes lifted prices in tourist-heavy areas. If you shop where the tour groups shop, you pay for the privilege. Walk three streets away and the price drops, which is the oldest rule in travel and works here as everywhere.

What I will say is that Czechia lets you live like an adult. You can buy bread late, forget the milk without penalty, and run an erratic schedule without needing a laminated plan. That freedom is easy to underrate until you lose it.

## Switzerland

Now the other extreme. According to IamExpat, Swiss shops not attached to airports or public transport close on Sundays, with exceptions for a few city areas, ski resorts and some Sundays before Christmas. Expatica's shopper's guide agrees: supermarkets are typically closed on Sundays apart from the occasional Sunday in the run-up to Christmas, with weekday hours of roughly eight or nine to six or seven.

The logic is not laziness, it is politics. IamExpat reports that Sunday work is strictly regulated and richly rewarded through overtime, which makes opening on Sundays expensive for rural shops. And the voters are on the side of the closed door. The union spokesperson Eva Geel of Unia was quoted saying: "In 90 percent of votes the people have said no to deregulation of night and Sunday work." That is not an industry quirk, it is a democratic preference, repeatedly confirmed.

SwitzerLanding adds the practical texture: neighbourhood Migros, Coop and Denner are shut, but big train stations and airports have supermarkets open every day of the year, and Zurich has a few Sunday-shopping dates, which in 2026 fall on 29 November, 6 December and 20 December. Expatica notes another detail new arrivals trip over: Migros sells no alcohol or cigarettes, while Coop sells both.

I find this system both maddening and magnificent. Maddening because I have stood in a station shop paying a premium for a pack of pasta I should have bought yesterday. Magnificent because a whole country has agreed to pause. Sunday is a real day off, and shop staff are not the sacrifice for your convenience. When the shutters come down, you are forced to walk, to cook, to see people.

The cost is planning. The Swiss shopper owns a mental timetable and a full cupboard, and visitors from looser cultures flounder. If you are disorganised, Switzerland will punish you, politely, every Sunday.

There is a deeper point about time here. A closed shop on Sunday is also a closed workplace for the person at the till, and Swiss voters have chosen that trade-off repeatedly. The union quote above is not nostalgia, it is a statement that the working week should end somewhere. Czech openness has a different logic, which values the customer's freedom, and the worker's Sunday depends on the employer. I will not pretend this is a clean moral story. A country that opens late serves its shoppers well and its shop staff less well, while one that closes serves its staff and puts the burden on its shoppers. You are, quite literally, choosing whose convenience counts.

Practicalities follow from the principle. A Swiss household shops in a rhythm: a big trip midweek or on Saturday morning, small top-ups on the way home, and an emergency reserve of pasta, tins and long-life milk for the day the shutters stay down. A Czech household shops in response to need, which is flexible and occasionally wasteful. Newcomers tend to import their old habit and suffer for it. Move to Switzerland with a Czech habit and you will go hungry on Sundays. Move to Czechia with a Swiss habit and you will carry too much food for no reason at all.

## The Verdict

Switzerland wins on principle, Czechia wins on convenience, and I am annoyed to admit that I prefer the principle. A country that tells its retailers to stop and its customers to wait is making a statement about what matters. I would rather have a full rest day than a half-empty shelf of options at ten at night.

But I also know myself, and I know that on a Sunday evening with an empty fridge, Czechia feels like grace. The ability to correct a mistake is a luxury. Switzerland sells you the discipline and gives you the quiet in exchange.

My ruling: Switzerland for people who plan, Czechia for people who live. If you have children and a routine, take Switzerland. If your schedule is chaos, take Czechia, and stop pretending you will change.

## Conclusion

Closing the shops is a choice, and so is opening them. Switzerland has chosen rest and planning. Czechia has chosen availability and improvisation. Neither is wrong, but each demands that you adapt your character to match.

Learn the timetable, keep a tin of something in the cupboard, and make peace with the fact that some Sundays you will eat toast. It is a small price for living in a country that has decided the till can wait.
