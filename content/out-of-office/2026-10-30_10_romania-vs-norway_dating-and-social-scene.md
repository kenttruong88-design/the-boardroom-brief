# Romanians Will Court You Like It's A Sport, Norwegians Will Wait For You To Make The First Move For Two Years

🇷🇴 Romania 🇳🇴 Norway
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Romania approaches romance and friendship with an enthusiasm bordering on the theatrical — compliments offered freely, interest declared early and often, a courtship pace that can feel, to the unprepared foreigner, like being swept along by a current rather than gently invited in. Norway approaches the exact same territory with a caution so profound that an entire local proverb exists about it: friendship, Norwegians will tell you earnestly, takes two winters to properly form, and romantic interest is rarely declared outright so much as implied through a slow accumulation of shared silences that eventually, maybe, resolve into something.

I have been complimented on my shoes, my laugh, and apparently my "aura" within ten minutes of meeting a Romanian stranger at a Bucharest dinner party, and I have sat beside the same Norwegian colleague for four entire ski trips before he finally, unprompted, told me his dog's name — and both encounters were, in their own cultures, completely unremarkable.

## Do's & Don'ts: Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect direct, early expressions of romantic or social interest — it's sincere, not pushy | Mistake fast-moving courtship for a lack of seriousness; Romanians often move quickly toward real commitment |
| Accept compliments graciously; deflecting them too hard can read as cold or suspicious | Assume a first or second date means casual; many Romanians date with marriage as a genuine horizon |
| Engage warmly with a partner's extended family early — it matters enormously, fast | Be surprised by jealousy or possessiveness norms that run stronger than in many Western contexts |

## Do's & Don'ts: Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Be patient — genuine friendship and romance both build slowly, over repeated shared activity | Expect anyone to approach you first in a bar or social setting; the initiating culture barely exists |
| Use shared hobbies — hiking, skiing, cabin trips — as the real entry point into social life | Read initial reserve as disinterest; it is simply the default setting, not a signal about you |
| Appreciate dating apps as a genuinely normalised, low-pressure entry point here | Push for rapid emotional disclosure; Norwegians open up gradually, often only after real trust is built |

## Romania: Courtship As An Art Form

Romanian social and romantic culture runs on open, confident expression, shaped by a Latin-adjacent warmth layered over Eastern European directness. Compliments flow early and often — about your appearance, your intelligence, your taste — and they're generally meant sincerely rather than as a tactic, though the sheer volume can feel disorienting to anyone used to more withheld Northern European norms. Interest, whether romantic or platonic, tends to be stated rather than merely implied, and a Romanian suitor who likes you will typically let you know directly, fairly quickly, rather than leaving you to guess through weeks of ambiguous signals.

This directness extends into the pace of relationships themselves. It isn't unusual for Romanian courtships to move toward meeting family, discussing long-term plans, or even marriage, considerably faster than many Western expats are culturally conditioned to expect — not because the relationship is being rushed carelessly, but because once interest is declared, it's often declared with genuine seriousness of intent behind it. Family involvement arrives early too: a partner's parents, grandparents, and extended relatives become part of the picture almost immediately, and how warmly you engage with them is read as a direct signal of how seriously you're taking the relationship itself.

There's an intensity to Romanian social and romantic culture that can also carry a stronger undercurrent of jealousy and possessiveness than many Western expats are used to navigating, often framed locally as evidence of genuine care rather than a red flag. Understanding this cultural framing — without necessarily adopting every norm wholesale — helps enormously in reading what's actually being communicated versus what might otherwise seem alarming through an outside lens.

## Norway: Friendship As A Two-Winter Project

Norway's social culture could not present a starker contrast. The oft-repeated local saying that "friendship takes two winters" isn't poetic exaggeration, it's a genuinely practical description of how long meaningful social bonds tend to take to form here, built through repeated, unglamorous proximity — the same ski trip, the same cabin weekend, the same office lunch table — rather than through any single dramatic gesture of openness. Norwegians are not cold exactly, but they are profoundly reserved with strangers, and the burden of initiating contact in a bar, a party, or really any unstructured social setting falls overwhelmingly on the newcomer, because the local cultural instinct to approach an unfamiliar person first is, by most accounts, nearly absent.

Dating apps have filled an obvious gap here, becoming a widely normalised, almost structurally necessary entry point precisely because they remove the awkwardness of unsolicited approach from an in-person setting that simply doesn't support it well. Once a connection does form, though, Norwegian social and romantic culture rewards patience generously: relationships tend to build on a foundation of shared activity and gradually accumulated trust rather than early verbal declarations, and disclosures of real feeling, when they finally arrive, tend to land with genuine weight precisely because they've been so rare up to that point.

The practical entry point for any newcomer hoping to build a social life here is almost always activity-based — joining a hiking group, a ski club, a climbing gym, a cabin-trip circle — because shared doing, not shared talking, is how Norwegians actually build the comfort required to eventually talk. Arrive expecting the open, verbally expressive warmth of a Romanian dinner party, and Norway's social scene will feel, for the first year at least, like trying to warm your hands on a closed door. Adjust your expectations and your tactics, and the door does eventually open — just considerably more slowly, and considerably more quietly, than you're probably used to.

## The Verdict

Romania offers warmth, speed, and sincerity worn openly on the sleeve — you will know exactly where you stand, usually within the first conversation, and the relationship, if it takes, will move fast and mean it. Norway offers the opposite: a slow, activity-anchored trust-building process that rewards patience and punishes any attempt to accelerate it, but delivers a depth of connection, once earned, that feels unusually solid precisely because it took two winters to build. If you want to know immediately whether someone likes you, Romania will tell you, loudly, within the hour. If you want a friendship that will quietly outlast almost anything, Norway will eventually hand you one — just don't expect a timeline, or a single word of warning before it finally clicks into place.

## Conclusion

Dating and friendship, in the end, are simply another language each country speaks with its own grammar, and fluency requires actually learning the rules rather than assuming your home culture's rules travel intact. Romania speaks loudly, warmly, and fast, rewarding directness with directness in return. Norway speaks slowly, quietly, and through shared action rather than shared words, rewarding patience with a depth that rushed cultures rarely manage to build at all. Show up to Oslo expecting Bucharest's pace and you'll spend a lonely first winter wondering what you did wrong. Show up to Bucharest expecting Oslo's caution and you may miss something wonderful simply because you were still waiting for a second, more "careful" signal that was never coming.
