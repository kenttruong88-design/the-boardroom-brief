# One Country's Healthcare Runs on Private Insurance and Prayer, the Other's Runs Like Clockwork and Costs Almost Nothing

🇧🇼 Botswana 🇪🇸 Spain
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Gaborone, the honest advice every expat receives within their first week is identical: get comprehensive private medical insurance, know exactly which private clinic to call, and treat the public system as a genuine last resort. It's not said to be alarmist, it's said because it's simply accurate, and the expats who skip this advice tend to learn its importance at the worst possible moment.

Spain operates from an almost embarrassingly different starting position: a public healthcare system so consistently ranked among the world's best that expats spend their first few months waiting for the catch, the hidden cost, the bureaucratic nightmare, and mostly just... don't find one. Both countries will, eventually, take excellent care of you. Only one of them will make you fill out considerably more paperwork to get there, and only one requires you to price-check first.

## Do's & Don'ts: Botswana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrange comprehensive private medical insurance before you arrive, not after | Rely on the public health system for anything beyond basic or emergency stabilisation |
| Identify your nearest private clinic and hospital immediately upon relocating | Assume rural or smaller-town facilities match Gaborone's private-clinic standard |
| Keep cash or card readily available for private consultations; upfront payment is common | Delay treatment hoping symptoms resolve; distances to quality care can be significant |
| Ask locals and other expats for trusted doctor recommendations early | Assume your home country's insurance automatically covers treatment; verify explicitly |

## Do's & Don'ts: Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for your "tarjeta sanitaria" (health card) as soon as your residency allows | Expect immediate specialist appointments through the public system; waits can run weeks |
| Use the public system (SNS) confidently for most needs; quality is genuinely excellent | Assume private insurance is necessary for good care — it's a speed convenience, not a necessity |
| Consider affordable private insurance if you want to skip specialist queues | Show up at a public hospital expecting American-style billing; it barely exists |
| Learn basic medical Spanish; regional public hospitals may have limited English | Panic about costs; even private consultations are dramatically cheaper than in the US |

## Botswana: A Two-Tier System Where the Gap Genuinely Matters

Botswana's public healthcare system deserves real credit within the regional context: it's more developed and better funded than in many neighbouring countries, with a genuine national commitment to expanding access, and for common illnesses and basic care, many Batswana rely on it successfully every day. But for expats, particularly those coming from countries with robust private or universal systems, the honest reality is that the public system faces real strain: understaffing, resource limitations, and significant capacity gaps outside major centres like Gaborone and Francistown.

This has produced a firmly established, almost universally followed expat norm: comprehensive private medical insurance isn't optional, it's the baseline. Private clinics and hospitals, concentrated mainly in Gaborone, offer a standard of care comparable to much of the developed world, with shorter waits, better-resourced facilities, and English-speaking staff, but they operate largely outside the public system's cost structure, meaning uninsured visits can become expensive quickly.

Distance compounds the calculation. Botswana is vast and sparsely populated outside its handful of urban centres, and for anyone living or working beyond Gaborone, the nearest quality private facility might be a genuinely significant drive away, a fact that shapes everything from where expats choose to live to how seriously they take preventive care and early treatment rather than waiting to see if something resolves on its own.

Serious or specialist conditions often mean medical evacuation, typically to South Africa, is factored into insurance planning from the outset, not as an unlikely worst case but as a realistic, budgeted possibility. None of this makes Botswana's healthcare landscape frightening exactly, it makes it something that rewards genuine preparation: the expats who arrive with strong insurance, a known clinic, and realistic expectations do just fine. The ones who assume it'll be "basically like home" learn otherwise, usually at an inconvenient moment.

## Spain: Public Healthcare That Actually, Consistently Delivers

Spain's Sistema Nacional de Salud is one of the genuine, unglamorous triumphs of European public policy: universal, largely free at the point of use for residents, and consistently ranked among the highest-performing healthcare systems in the world by international bodies. For expats who've spent time navigating the American system's billing labyrinth or the rationed realities of other public systems, Spain's version tends to produce a specific, recurring reaction: quiet, slightly stunned relief.

Registration is straightforward once residency status is sorted: the tarjeta sanitaria grants access to public doctors, hospitals, and pharmacies at minimal or no direct cost, funded through the tax and social security system rather than billed per visit. Emergency care is excellent and genuinely fast; it's non-urgent specialist care where waits can stretch to weeks, occasionally longer in certain regions, which is the one real friction point expats encounter.

The private system exists as a genuinely reasonable middle option rather than a necessity, offering faster specialist access at costs that would look almost absurdly low to anyone coming from the US: private insurance premiums and out-of-pocket consultation fees run a fraction of equivalent American pricing, making it an easy, low-stakes upgrade rather than an essential survival purchase the way it is in Botswana.

Regional variation matters more than most guides admit: healthcare is administered at the regional level, meaning quality, wait times, and English-language availability shift noticeably between, say, Madrid or Barcelona and smaller towns in Extremadura or rural Galicia. Learning at least functional medical Spanish smooths the experience considerably outside the major cosmopolitan centres, where English fluency among staff can't be assumed the way expats sometimes expect.

## The Verdict

Spain wins, decisively and without much argument, on system design: universal coverage, genuinely excellent public care, and private options priced for convenience rather than survival. Botswana's system asks considerably more of expats, real preparation, real insurance, real contingency planning, but rewards that preparation with private care that, once accessed, is genuinely excellent. If healthcare peace of mind matters to you above all else, Spain is close to unbeatable. If you're headed to Botswana, do the prep work; the system won't hold your hand, but a well-insured, well-informed expat does just fine.

## Conclusion

Botswana and Spain sit at genuinely different points on the healthcare-preparedness spectrum, and both punish complacency in their own particular way. Botswana rewards the expat who arrives with insurance sorted and a known clinic on speed dial. Spain rewards the expat who simply registers promptly and otherwise gets out of the system's way. Assume either will function exactly like home and you're setting yourself up for a bad week. Do the homework in advance, in both cases, and you'll likely receive better, calmer care than you were getting back home in the first place.
