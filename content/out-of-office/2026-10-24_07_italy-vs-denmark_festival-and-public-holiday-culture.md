# Italy Closes Its Shutters. Denmark Opens the Schnapps.

🇮🇹🇩🇰

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Ask an Italian what day it is and, depending on the month, the honest answer is often "closed." Italy does not merely observe its holidays — it evacuates for them. By the second week of August the nation's shutters roll down in near-perfect unison, as though a bell only native speakers can hear has gone off, and the entire peninsula decants itself onto a beach, up a mountain, or at bare minimum into a grandmother's kitchen. Call it inefficiency if you like. Italians call it civilisation, and have been calling it that since Emperor Augustus invented the holiday to give everyone a rest after the harvest, which tells you precisely how long this argument has been settled.

Denmark takes the opposite approach: it keeps the office open and simply gets everyone quietly, thoroughly drunk inside it. The Danes have spent two decades exporting a brand of cosy minimalism — candles, oat-coloured knitwear, bicycles with wicker baskets — while running, underneath it, one of Europe's most ruthless workplace drinking rituals disguised as a "Christmas lunch." Hygge is the marketing. Julefrokost is the invoice. Both countries have perfected the public holiday as an art form. Only one of them tends to leave its citizens with their dignity, and their marriages, fully intact by January.

## Do's & Don'ts: Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the phrase "chiuso per ferie" before August 1st | Don't expect your dentist, your bank, or your will to live to be available in August |
| Book your Ferragosto beach umbrella in June | Don't assume you can "just pop into town" for anything after August 10th |
| Respect your town's patron saint day, even if you'd never heard of the saint until last Tuesday | Don't schedule a delivery, an inspection, or a breakup during a local *festa* |
| Pack a genuine appetite for the extended, three-hour, multi-course festival lunch | Don't rush an Italian festival; nobody else involved is going anywhere fast |

## Do's & Don'ts: Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| RSVP to the julefrokost invitation, even the frightening all-office one | Don't skip it thinking attendance is optional; socially, it rarely is |
| Pace yourself through the snaps toasts, of which there will be many | Don't leave before the rice pudding; an early exit is noticed and remembered |
| Learn the rules of pakkeleg, the gift-stealing dice game, before you're handed a badly wrapped mystery object | Don't bring a genuinely good present; it will be stolen within minutes and resented forever |
| Bring, or at minimum admire, a candle | Don't mistake a colleague's stone-faced silence on the following Monday for a grudge; it is merely Tuesday-level composure reasserting itself |

## Italy: The Nation That Closes For Business

Italy's relationship with the calendar is less a schedule than a philosophy, and the philosophy is this: the work will still be there when you get back, and the work has never once thrown a festival. Ferragosto, on August 15th, is the fulcrum of the whole system — a holiday Emperor Augustus formalised in 18 BC as the *Feriae Augusti*, a stretch of rest after the agricultural grind, which the Catholic Church later annexed for the Feast of the Assumption without anyone seriously objecting, because by then the nation had already decided August belonged to the sea. The modern result, confirmed by ANSA's own reporting on Coldretti's seasonal surveys, is that roughly six in ten Italians leave home for Ferragosto itself, and a great many more have already left weeks earlier.

What that means logistically is where the fun starts. Nurseries close for the entire month, which blindsides working parents, especially the imported kind, every single year. Doctors' offices, run by a single practitioner more often than not, post handwritten signs and vanish until September, routed through the regional ASL in emergencies only. Hairdressers, florists, and the butcher who actually likes you all tape up a "chiuso per ferie" notice and disappear toward Puglia. The big supermarket chains stay open, because capitalism has its limits on patience but none on opening hours, yet the soul of the high street simply switches itself off. Add the *esodo* — the exodus — clogging every autostrada the last weekend of July, and its mirror image, the *rientro*, doing the same in reverse come September, and you have a country that treats seasonal migration as a civic duty.

Then there are the patron saints, which matter more locally than any national decree. Milan stops for Sant'Ambrogio, Naples for San Gennaro, Florence for San Giovanni, and none of these appear on anyone else's calendar, which is rather the point. A festival that only your own town understands is not a bug in Italian culture. It is the entire operating system, and it has never once apologised for the inconvenience.

## Denmark: The Country That Hygges Its Way Through A Hazing

Denmark's holidays look, from a cautious distance, like the gentlest in Europe: candlelight, cinnamon, a bicycle parked against a Scandinavian-blue door. Get closer and you find a culture that treats festival season as a controlled release valve for a famously reserved population, administered once a year in heavy doses of schnapps. The julefrokost — the Christmas lunch, held anywhere from the office to the bowling club — is less a party than a sanctioned pressure test, and the data backs up the folklore: a widely cited Danish survey found roughly one in ten Danes admits to infidelity committed specifically at a julefrokost, and the country's unions field a steady stream of regretful calls every December from employees who said something unforgivable to their boss while both parties were several schnapps deep.

Then there's Sankt Hans, on June 23rd, when Denmark burns effigies of witches on bonfires along every coastline while singing a 19th-century folk song, a ritual so cheerfully pagan it makes the country's Lutheran public holidays look almost like an afterthought — which, culturally, several of them are. Denmark is a deeply secular country that nonetheless keeps its calendar dotted with Christian feast days purely out of habit, a contradiction so pointed that in 2023 the government simply scrapped one of them, Store Bededag (Great Prayer Day), to help fund a NATO defence-spending target. Danes protested in the streets wearing white, the day's traditional colour, and lost the fight anyway, which tells you precisely how much romantic nostalgia survives contact with a budget line.

What outsiders rarely grasp until it happens to them is the whiplash: a colleague who danced on a table with you in December will nod politely and say almost nothing on the Monday after, as if the lunch were classified. Hygge, it turns out, is not the absence of intensity. It is intensity with the volume turned down to a candle's flicker, ready to spike without warning once a year.

## The Verdict

If you want a culture that treats its holidays as sacred and its calendar as non-negotiable, Italy wins without needing to try. It shuts a continent-sized economy down for a month, offends nobody by doing so, and reopens exactly when it said it would. There is an honesty in that kind of closure that Denmark, for all its cosiness, cannot quite match.

But if you want a single night that tells you more about a country's psychology than a year of small talk ever could, Denmark takes it. The julefrokost is a culture revealing, once annually and under the cover of aquavit, everything it normally keeps under excellent Scandinavian wraps.

So: Italy for the holiday that respects you enough to disappear completely. Denmark for the holiday that respects you enough to let you make a fool of yourself in front of your boss and call it team-building. Neither country is lying about who it is. One is simply louder about it after the third toast.

Winner, on pure festival architecture: Italy, by the width of a shuttered pharmacy.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Fodor's Travel Talk Forums, "Italy in August" thread — paraphrased: a traveller recalls being in Rome in August when "it was swelteringly hot and most things were closed," while another poster in the same thread insists the closures barely register outside Milan — proof that even Italians' guests can't agree on how shut "shut" really is.*

> *Brian King, American expat, quoted in The Copenhagen Post — paraphrased: after his first julefrokost he began to wonder if he was having a stroke, since he could barely see out of one eye by the end of the night, and concluded that only the strong survive the experience.*

> *Tiny Maerschalk, columnist, The Copenhagen Post, "Living In An Expat World: High season for hygge" — paraphrased: she and her Danish husband developed a secret signal, a small nod, to warn her she was about to break an unwritten Christmas custom in front of his family.*

## Conclusion

Here is the part nobody puts on the tourist board: both of these holiday cultures are quietly hostile to outsiders who assume "festival" means "fun for everyone, equally, all the time." Italy's hostility is architectural — the whole country simply isn't there. Denmark's is social — the whole country is there, bonded by aquavit, and you are the only one who didn't get the unwritten manual. Neither will apologise, and neither should. You moved to someone else's country; you do not get to edit its calendar.

So go on, relocate, and be charmed. Just don't book a dentist appointment for August 16th, and don't skip the julefrokost thinking you'll catch the next one. There isn't always a next one. There is only ever the one where you found out, the hard way, what the locals already knew.
