# One Country Will Watch You Struggle. The Other Will Just Switch Languages and Move On.

🇧🇷 Brazil · 🇲🇹 Malta
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Brazil is a country of 215 million people who, broadly speaking, have decided that English is your problem, not theirs, and I say this with genuine admiration — there is something bracing about a nation this large that simply doesn't need you to be understood on your own terms. Malta, by contrast, is a country of half a million people who will greet you in Maltese, pivot to English mid-sentence, throw in an Italian phrase for flavour, and never once break stride, because trilingualism here isn't an achievement, it's Tuesday. One of these countries will let you flounder charmingly. The other will simply meet you wherever you are without comment.

I have tried to order a coffee in halting Portuguese in São Paulo and been met with polite bewilderment. I have tried to order a coffee in English in Valletta and had the barista switch to it before I'd finished the sentence, then switch back to Maltese to argue with a colleague about something unrelated. Only one of these felt like an actual barrier.

## Do's & Don'ts: Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Portuguese greetings and numbers before you arrive — even minimal effort is warmly rewarded | Assume Spanish will get you by; it helps with reading signs but often lands oddly when spoken aloud |
| Use a translation app freely in restaurants and shops — it's completely normalized, not embarrassing | Expect English fluency outside upscale hotels, major airports, and tourist-facing agencies |
| Lean on gestures and enthusiasm; Brazilians respond warmly to visible effort over perfect grammar | Get frustrated in service interactions — raised voices read as aggressive, not as clarifying |

## Do's & Don'ts: Malta

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to English confidently — it's a co-official language and near-universal, even in villages | Assume everyone finds code-switching mid-sentence unusual; to Maltese ears, it's just how people talk |
| Pick up a few Maltese phrases anyway — it signals respect even though you'll rarely need them | Be surprised when a Maltese-Italian TV channel or radio station plays in the background of daily life |
| Ask if you're unsure which language a sign or menu defaults to — Malta genuinely runs on all three | Expect Maltese itself to resemble any Romance language you already know; it's Semitic-rooted and looks nothing like it sounds |

## Brazil: A Country That Doesn't Need You to Understand It

Brazil's relationship with English is refreshingly indifferent, and I mean that as a compliment to Brazilian self-assurance rather than a knock on hospitality. This is a country of continental scale with an enormous, self-sufficient internal economy and culture — Portuguese is spoken by over 200 million people within its own borders, which means the practical, day-to-day incentive to learn a second language for most Brazilians simply isn't there in the way it is for someone living in, say, a small trading nation wedged between larger economies. English proficiency skews heavily toward the urban, educated, tourism-facing classes: hotel staff in Copacabana, corporate professionals in São Paulo's financial district, guides in Rio's more visited neighbourhoods. Step outside those specific contexts — into a neighbourhood padaria, a pharmacy, a bus terminal — and you are, functionally, on your own.

What makes this manageable rather than isolating is the warmth with which Brazilians receive effort. Fumble through a sentence in bad Portuguese with genuine enthusiasm and you'll usually get patience, amusement, and real help rather than the faint impatience some cultures reserve for language learners. Translation apps have also become so normalized that pulling out your phone mid-conversation to type a sentence is barely remarked upon anymore, even in fairly traditional settings — it's simply understood as how a modern conversation between a foreigner and a local proceeds.

The genuine barrier isn't hostility, it's scale and accent. Brazilian Portuguese is phonetically dense and fast, full of nasal vowels and contractions that don't map cleanly onto anything you'd learn from a phrasebook built around European Portuguese, and Spanish speakers arriving overconfident because "it's basically the same language" tend to get a ruder awakening than genuine beginners, since their fluent-sounding attempts get responded to at native speed. The honest advice, which almost nobody wants to hear, is that there is no shortcut here — you either commit to real Portuguese study or you accept you'll be navigating large parts of the country through gesture, patience, and goodwill for as long as you're there.

## Malta: A Nation That Speaks Three Languages Before Breakfast

Malta operates on a linguistic setup so unusual it takes visitors a genuine adjustment period to stop being disoriented by it. Maltese, the national language — a genuinely unique Semitic language written in Latin script, the only one of its kind in the EU — coexists as co-official with English, a legacy of British colonial rule that ended in 1964 but left behind an education system, legal framework, and everyday fluency that never really faded. Add in the historical and geographic proximity to Italy, whose television, radio, and commerce bled into Maltese life for decades, and you get a population that moves between three languages with a fluidity that looks, to an outsider, almost showy, even though nobody here experiences it that way at all.

For an English speaker, this makes Malta close to frictionless in a way few non-Anglophone countries are. Government offices, medical care, restaurant menus, road signs — all comfortably operate in English alongside Maltese, and you will rarely if ever hit a genuine communication wall. What surprises people more is the code-switching itself: a conversation between two Maltese friends might start in Maltese, drop into English for a technical or professional term, borrow an Italian phrase for something that just sounds better in Italian, and return to Maltese, all within a single sentence, without anyone treating this as remarkable.

The learning curve, if you attempt Maltese itself, is steeper than its EU membership might suggest — its Arabic-derived grammar and vocabulary, filtered through centuries of Sicilian, Italian, and English borrowing, means none of your intuitions from other European languages transfer cleanly. Most expats don't bother beyond pleasantries, and locals genuinely don't expect it, since English does everything practical Maltese would. What effort you do put in gets received less as impressive competence and more as charming curiosity — a village grandmother might light up that you tried "bonġu" over "good morning," then immediately switch back to English because it's simply faster.

## The Verdict

Brazil demands more of you and rewards the attempt generously; Malta demands almost nothing of you and rewards you with the same warmth regardless. If ease of daily life is your metric, Malta wins outright — there is no serious language barrier here for an English speaker, full stop. But Brazil offers something Malta structurally cannot: the genuine, hard-won satisfaction of actually cracking a language barrier rather than never encountering one in the first place.

I'll call it a draw, weighted by mood. Some trips you want friction and the story that comes with overcoming it. Some trips you just want the coffee order to go smoothly the first time. Pick your poison.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a respondent to "can you get by in Rio without speaking Portuguese" warned that Copacabana and Ipanema create a false sense of security that evaporates the moment you take a bus fifteen minutes inland.*

> *expat.com Malta forum — a British retiree wrote that after two years she still hadn't needed a single word of Maltese for anything practical, and felt slightly guilty about how little effort the country actually demanded of her.*

> *Internations Malta — one member described being mid-conversation with a Maltese colleague who switched to English specifically to make a joke funnier, then switched straight back, calling it "the most efficient use of trilingualism I've ever witnessed."*

## Conclusion

Brazil will let you struggle, warmly, for as long as it takes you to earn fluency or accept you never will. Malta will simply never let a language barrier form in the first place, which is either the more civilized outcome or a missed opportunity for character growth, depending on how insufferable you want to be about your travel experiences afterward. Either way, bring a translation app to Brazil and bring nothing but confidence to Malta — you genuinely won't need more than that.
