# Finland Will Let You Stand Alone at a Bus Stop Forever. Morocco Will Not Let You Be Alone, Ever.

🇫🇮 Finland vs 🇲🇦 Morocco — Public Behaviour Norms

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Finland has built an entire national identity around the sanctity of personal space — a bus stop with six people waiting will somehow arrange itself into six separate postcodes, each person maintaining a two-metre force field of dignified silence. Morocco operates on the opposite principle entirely: the souk is loud, close, and constantly negotiating, and personal space is a concept most Moroccans would find mildly baffling, if not slightly rude, given that connection is the whole point of being in public at all.

I've stood at a Helsinki tram stop in silence so complete I could hear my own doubts, and I've been physically steered by the elbow into a Marrakech spice stall by a vendor who'd decided we were already friends. Both are valid ways to exist in public. Neither will prepare you for the other.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇫🇮 Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Leave at least an arm's length of space at any queue, bus stop, or sauna bench | Make small talk with strangers unprompted; it will be received as mildly alarming |
| Speak quietly on public transport, even on the phone | Sit next to someone on an empty bus or tram if other seats are available — this is borderline hostile |
| Respect silence as a valid, comfortable social state, not an awkward gap to fill | Comment on someone's personal life uninvited, even kindly; it will not land as kindness |

### 🇲🇦 Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to be greeted, engaged, and possibly gently pulled toward a stall while walking through a souk | Take vendor banter or a firm sales pitch personally; it's a performance both sides are expected to enjoy |
| Dress modestly in public, especially away from tourist zones, out of basic respect | Photograph people, especially women, without asking first | 
| Accept mint tea if offered during a negotiation; declining outright can read as rejecting the relationship, not just the tea | Rush a greeting; a full "how are you, how is your family" exchange is expected before any actual business |

## Finland: The World Capital of Respectful Distance

Finnish public behaviour operates on a single, deeply held principle: your business is your business, and mine is mine, and the greatest kindness one stranger can offer another is to leave them completely, blissfully alone. This manifests everywhere — bus stops where six strangers instinctively space themselves as far apart as the geometry allows, elevators ridden in total silence even between people who technically know each other, saunas where profound personal disclosures are apparently more socially acceptable than a stray "so, hot today, isn't it?"

Small talk, that global lubricant of public life, is treated here with genuine suspicion. Striking up conversation with a stranger in a queue doesn't read as friendly — it reads as slightly unhinged, an unprompted intrusion into a social contract that everyone else is quietly, contentedly honouring. Finns aren't cold, exactly; they're just operating on the theory that comfortable silence is a form of respect, not a failure of manners, and once you adjust to this you start to notice how exhausting constant small talk actually is elsewhere.

What nobody warns you about is how this scales to genuine crisis. A Finn will not perform concern in public the way other cultures might, but ask for real help — directions when you're properly lost, assistance when something's actually gone wrong — and the same reserved stranger will drop the distance instantly and solve your problem with quiet, thorough competence. The silence was never indifference. It was just very, very good boundaries.

## Morocco: Public Life as a Continuous Conversation

Morocco's public behaviour operates on the opposite axiom: to be around other people is to be in relationship with them, at least briefly, and silence between strangers is the anomaly that needs explaining, not the default state. Walk through any medina and you will be greeted, engaged, invited, and occasionally gently physically redirected toward a rug shop by a vendor who has already decided, correctly, that ignoring him takes more energy than a two-minute chat over mint tea.

The volume and density take real adjustment. A souk at midday is a wall of sound — vendors calling out prices, motorbikes weaving through impossibly narrow lanes, someone's radio, someone's argument, someone's laughter, all layered on top of each other with no apparent master volume control. This isn't chaos exactly; it's a public life that has simply never adopted the Northern European assumption that shared space requires shared quiet.

Negotiation itself is public performance, not private discomfort. Haggling over a carpet or a tagine is expected to be theatrical — mock outrage, dramatic walk-aways, a final price that both parties knew roughly where it would land twenty minutes earlier, but the twenty minutes were the point, not an obstacle to it. What nobody prepares you for is how genuinely warm this can feel once you stop resisting it; the constant engagement, once you stop reading it as intrusive, reveals itself as a culture that has simply decided isolation in public is a problem to be actively solved.

## The Verdict

Finland wins on peace — a society that has perfected the art of coexisting without demanding anything of each other, which sounds cold until you've had a truly restful commute for the first time in your life. Morocco wins on warmth — nowhere else does simply walking down a street feel this consistently, insistently alive. If you crave silence as a form of respect, Finland will feel like a held breath finally released. If you crave constant, textured human contact, Morocco will feel like coming up for air. Just don't bring Finnish reserve to a Marrakech souk — you'll be gently, relentlessly pulled out of it anyway.

## Conclusion

Finland teaches you that silence between strangers can be its own quiet gift, freely given and freely received. Morocco teaches you that a street full of noise and negotiation can be its own form of hospitality, insisted upon whether you asked for it or not. I've been happily ignored in Helsinki and happily ambushed with mint tea in Marrakech, and both, somehow, felt like being welcomed. Bring earplugs to one and an open schedule to the other. You'll need them for entirely different reasons.
