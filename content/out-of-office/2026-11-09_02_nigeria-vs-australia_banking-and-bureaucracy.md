# One Country Will Lose Your Passport for Ten Months, the Other Will Just Make You Wait in a Very Orderly Line

🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Bureaucracy is the purest test of a country's character, because nobody is performing for you. There's no tourist board gloss on a bank queue or a passport office. You get the system exactly as its own citizens get it, friction and all, and in fourteen relocations I have learned more about a nation's soul from its Byzantine paperwork than from any museum it's built to flatter itself.

Nigeria and Australia sit at opposite ends of a very long, very tedious spectrum. One runs on cash, hustle and a healthy mutual suspicion between customer and counter staff. The other runs on forms, numbers and the unshakeable national belief that if you just fill in the right one, the problem resolves itself. Both will test your patience. Only one will occasionally test your resolve to stay in the country at all.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Nigeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Do get your Bank Verification Number (BVN) sorted early — it's compulsory, tied to your biometric data, and nothing moves without it | Don't assume a Nigerian bank account alone will serve you day to day; keep a foreign account running, since internet banking here is widely considered unreliable |
| Do bring every document the first time: passport, work/residence permit, references if asked — and consider trying a second branch if one is unhelpful | Don't expect loan access or credit facilities as a foreign resident without a strong local track record — assume "no" until proven otherwise |
| Do keep paper and digital records of every transaction and check statements often | Don't be shocked by card or ATM fraud; it's common enough that vigilance is simply part of having a local account |
| Do treat a same-week appointment or "come back in 8 working days" with good humour, because fury changes nothing | Don't pay a facilitator or "expeditor" for government paperwork without knowing exactly what you're buying — and knowing it may still take ten months anyway |

**Australia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Do open your account online from overseas — most major banks let you start up to 12 months before you arrive | Don't forget to get a Tax File Number (TFN) from the Australian Taxation Office; without one, you're taxed at a punitive rate on savings and interest |
| Do bring ID — passport and driver's licence are the standard combination — and expect an in-person verification step once you land | Don't expect an easy credit card as a temporary visa holder; many applications require an Australian address, an existing local account and employment history you probably don't have yet |
| Do budget time for branch visits; closures are reshaping the system and queues are getting longer as everything shifts online | Don't assume online banking replaces every function — big-ticket matters like credit and loans still want a human and a stack of paperwork |
| Do notify any home-country bank before you leave, especially if you're coming from a country whose transactions get flagged for fraud review | Don't walk into Centrelink or Home Affairs processes expecting speed; visa and benefits processing delays are a well-documented national pastime of their own |

**Nigeria: a system that runs beautifully, right up until it doesn't**

Nigeria's banking sector is, on paper, genuinely sophisticated — close to a hundred banking and financial institutions under the Central Bank of Nigeria, slick mobile apps, agile fintech upstarts nipping at the heels of giants like GTBank, Zenith, Access and First Bank. Then you try to actually use it, and the gap between the architecture and the experience opens up like a sinkhole. One expat on an Expat.com forum described the GT Bank process as painless, "less than a week," while another, banking with Zenith, called it "difficult, lots of forms and fees to pay" — which tells you the real variable isn't the system, it's which branch you happened to walk into that day.

The compulsory Bank Verification Number, a biometric identifier the Central Bank rolled out and now requires even from diaspora customers opening accounts from abroad, is the gatekeeping mechanism for everything, and skipping the groundwork on it stalls you before you've started. Ordinary Nigerians fare no better once they're inside the system: readers interviewed by Zikoko described being left stranded without weekend access to their own money after a branch's "insensitive" customer service team failed to resolve a simple issue, and calling customer care "literally every day" to chase a failed transfer. That's not an edge case. That's Tuesday.

And then there's the wider bureaucratic backdrop that makes banking frustrations feel almost quaint. Nigeria's passport system, as reported by Al Jazeera, runs on an informal economy of expediting fees and favoritism so entrenched that one applicant who'd paid for faster service still asked, reasonably, "Why should I beg for something I have paid for?" — only to watch others from different states collect same-day passports after paying considerably more under the table. A national survey found fewer than one in ten Nigerians even hold an international passport, making it the least-held government ID in the country, which puts the whole system's friction into sobering context: this isn't an expat inconvenience, it's a daily national reality.

What actually keeps the country moving is the parallel system nobody officially sanctioned: mobile money. Opay, Moniepoint and Kuda built fintech apps precisely because the traditional banks couldn't be trusted to move money reliably, and now a huge share of daily commerce — market stalls, taxi fares, school fees — runs through a QR code and a phone rather than a counter queue. It's the clearest evidence that Nigerians didn't wait for the bureaucracy to fix itself; they built a faster one next to it and let the old one keep muddling along for whoever still needs a stamp.

**Australia: bureaucracy with excellent manners and a very long queue**

Australia's system is the anti-Nigeria in almost every respect: transparent, numbered, procedurally polite, and almost entirely indifferent to your personal charm. You can open most bank accounts online up to twelve months before you even land, which sounds marvellously efficient until you discover that withdrawals often stay frozen until you show up in person to verify your identity — a bureaucratic trust-but-verify move that feels distinctly Australian in its blend of friendliness and suspicion.

The Tax File Number, issued by the Australian Taxation Office, is the single document the entire system quietly organises around. Skip it and you're not denied service, you're simply taxed as though you're trying to hide something — a gentler but equally effective version of Nigeria's BVN gatekeeping. One long-time forum contributor on Expat.com called the overall account-opening process not "hectic," estimating "only 1-2 hours" with a passport and a proper street address (a PO box, notably, won't cut it at most banks), though a debit card can still take a week to arrive by post.

Where Australia's bureaucracy shows its teeth is in the slower-moving, higher-stakes stuff: credit, visas, benefits. Temporary visa holders routinely get refused credit cards outright. Centrelink processing delays and Home Affairs visa backlogs are such a stable national irritant that advocacy groups and even politicians regularly campaign on fixing them, with little to show for it. Branch closures are quietly pushing everyone online whether they like it or not, and the trade-off for all that order is time: everything works, eventually, assuming you're willing to wait in a queue that is, at the very least, impeccably organised.

The other quietly defining feature is just how few banks there actually are to choose between. The "Big Four" — Commonwealth, Westpac, ANZ and NAB — dominate the market so thoroughly that switching between them feels less like comparison shopping and more like picking which sibling annoys you least. Bills get paid through BPAY, a universal reference-number system so embedded in daily life that every invoice, utility bill and council rate notice assumes you already know how to use it, with zero explanation offered to the newly arrived.

**The Verdict**

Nigeria's bureaucracy will cost you money you didn't budget for and patience you didn't know you had; Australia's will cost you time you'll never get back and a faint, nagging sense that a form somewhere is quietly judging you. If I'm picking a winner on pure functionality, Australia takes it — nobody there has ever had to ask "why should I beg for something I've paid for?" about their own passport. But if I'm picking the system with more soul, more genuine warmth from the humans stuck operating it, and more entertaining stories to tell at dinner afterwards, Nigeria wins on personality alone. Efficiency is not the same as charm, and bureaucracy, as it turns out, is the one place where charm genuinely doesn't help you at all.

**The Parting Shot**

Everyone arrives in a new country bracing for the big cultural clashes — the food, the language, the holidays — and gets blindsided instead by the bank queue. Nigeria will teach you that charm, patience and a well-chosen second branch solve more problems than any amount of righteous anger. Australia will teach you that a form filed correctly the first time is worth more than any amount of charm you were planning to deploy. Neither lesson is comfortable. Both are, infuriatingly, correct. Pack accordingly, and bring a TFN application either way — Nigeria won't ask for one, but it'll feel, by the end, like it should have.
