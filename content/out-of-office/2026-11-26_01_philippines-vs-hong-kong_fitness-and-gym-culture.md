# Hong Kong Rents You a Treadmill and Manila Gives You a Park

🇵🇭 Philippines vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Hong Kong treats exercise like a transaction. You sign a contract, you pay a monthly fee that would embarrass a small yacht, and you attend at 7.40am or 1pm, because those are the only hours when a person with a job can possibly move. The Philippines treats exercise like a party that somebody forgot to cancel. Somebody drags a speaker into a plaza, forty people appear from nowhere, and nobody has asked for your credit card.

I have lived in cities where the gym is a cathedral and cities where it is a rumour, and I can tell you that the interesting thing is not the equipment. It is who is allowed to use the space, who is exhausted before they arrive, and who finds joy in a car park at dawn. Between these two places runs one of the largest migrant labour corridors in Asia, which makes the comparison less cute than it first looks and a good deal more interesting.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇭 Philippines ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the plaza or park group exercise session and just copy the person in front | Stand at the edge with your arms folded and judge the choreography |
| Exercise early morning or after sunset when the heat drops | Plan a noon run and act surprised when you melt |
| Bring a towel, water and a sense of humour | Expect air-conditioned changing rooms everywhere |
| Say yes when a stranger invites you into a game of basketball | Treat the court as private property |

| 🇭🇰 Hong Kong ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the gym contract in full before signing | Assume a cheap trial means a cheap exit |
| Book classes ahead, because the good ones fill fast | Turn up at 6.30pm and expect a free bench |
| Use the harbour promenades and country trails on weekends | Assume Hong Kong has no green outdoors at all |
| Wipe down your equipment and keep your phone calls short | Treat the gym floor as your private office |

## 🇵🇭 The Philippines

Exercise in the Philippines starts from a different premise: space is scarce, so you share it, and effort is more fun in company. Basketball is practically a civic religion. A hoop is bolted to a wall in almost every barangay, and a game starts the moment two people and a ball occupy the same corner. Early mornings and evenings belong to joggers, walkers and anyone in the neighbourhood with a Bluetooth speaker and a grudge against silence. Group aerobics and Zumba in public plazas are not niche. They are what ordinary people do after work, and the instructor is often a volunteer.

The commercial gym exists, of course, and the glossy ones in the big business districts have everything you would expect. But the heat decides most things. At midday the tropics tell you to sit down, so exercise gets pushed to the edges of the day, when it is cooler and the whole neighbourhood is awake and watching. That public quality is the point. You are not hiding in a basement with headphones. You are being seen, and being cheered, and being quietly told your form is terrible by an auntie who has done this for thirty years.

The downside is infrastructure. Pavements are patchy, air quality varies by road, and a park run can mean dodging a tricycle. The upside is a culture where nobody thinks fitness is a lifestyle brand. It is just what you do with other humans when work is over, which is why so many Filipinos take it with them when they leave.

There is also a social grammar to Filipino exercise that no gym brochure will teach you. Age is not a barrier: grandmothers and teenagers share a dance floor without anyone blinking. Instructors are addressed warmly, advice is given freely and a newcomer is adopted by the second song. It is a culture in which being bad at something in public is not embarrassing, merely a stage on the way to being less bad, and that single attitude does more for national fitness than any marketing campaign could.

## 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

Hong Kong goes the other way. Space is the most expensive commodity on earth, so a gym is a rented slice of it, sold by the hour in a tower you reach by lift. A Localiiz tour of the city's gym archetypes describes the Workaholic, the person who crams an intense session into lunch or the end of the working day, usually in the central business district. That is not a joke so much as a time-use report. The same piece cites a Hong Kong Confederation of Trade Unions study finding that one in five employees works an average of 55 hours a week. Of course the gym is a sprint.

The contracts are the other half of the culture. Memberships get sold hard, cancelled harder, and the fine print has ruined more than one expat's January. Equipment is excellent, classes are slick, and the whole experience is optimised for efficiency over fun. If you want to run outdoors, though, Hong Kong has a secret: the hills. Country trails begin within a short bus ride of the densest streets on the planet, and anyone who says the city has no nature has never seen it from the Dragon's Back at dawn.

Then there is Sunday, and this is where the corridor between the two places shows up. Foreign domestic helpers, a population that reached 340,380 by the end of 2016 according to a Global Voices account of Hong Kong's Filipino workers, get one day off a week. A good portion of them spend it in public parks and plazas, organising themselves into dance societies and practice teams. One of those workers, Michelle Saluta, told the writer her team practises together every Sunday, and said they should enjoy the day off rather than sit around sad. That is not a workout trend. That is people building a gym out of nothing, in the only hours the city grants them.

The Standard's April 2026 account adds the detail that makes this concrete. The G-Class sessions mix high-energy cardio, yoga and dance, are sometimes led by volunteer trainers and take sign-ups through a Facebook group, which is about as far from a corporate membership funnel as you can get. Nobody checks your credit score at the gate. The mood, by the paper's description, is a weekly reset, and a Hong Kong that treats Sunday as the only day you are allowed to be fully human has accidentally built one of its best fitness products.

## The Verdict

The Philippines wins, and it is not close. Hong Kong has better equipment, better air conditioning and better changing rooms, and I will happily use all of them. But a gym is not what makes a culture of fitness. What matters is whether ordinary people move because it is enjoyable, and on that point Manila has this pinned.

The fairest summary is that Hong Kong gives you a product and the Philippines gives you a habit. The product is good. It is also priced, scheduled and policed, and it asks you to arrive pre-exhausted from a 55-hour week. The habit is free, loud and a little chaotic, and it works because nobody has taught it to be exclusive.

What Hong Kong deserves credit for is the trails, and for the quiet fact that its Sunday parks now carry some of the best free group fitness anywhere in the region. The G-Class, a free session at Tamar Park started in September 2022 by coach Walter Vaz for domestic helpers, began with fewer than a dozen people and has grown into a network of thousands, with no cap on attendance. Vaz started it because he noticed helpers exercising alone near where he trained paying clients. That is the best thing in the whole comparison, and it did not come from a gym chain.

## Conclusion

Both places will tell you they are busy, and both are right. The difference is what they do with a spare hour. Hong Kong rents it to you. The Philippines hands it back and puts on music.

If you are moving to either, pack trainers and a flexible attitude. In Hong Kong, read the contract, learn the trails and find the Sunday crowd if you can. In the Philippines, go outside before the sun does and join whatever is happening in the plaza. The worst thing you can do in either is stay home and call it rest. The best thing you can do is accept that the person next to you has been doing this longer, and is better at it, and does not care that you are sweating through your shirt.
