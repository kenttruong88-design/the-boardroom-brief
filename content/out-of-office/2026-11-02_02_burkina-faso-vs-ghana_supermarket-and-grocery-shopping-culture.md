# One Nation Shops Like The Market Owes It Money. The Other Shops Like An Auditor.

🇧🇫🇬🇭 Burkina Faso & Ghana
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Nobody tells you, before you move to West Africa, that "going to the shops" will become a daily negotiation, a social event, a transport logistics exercise, and occasionally a minor diplomatic standoff over the price of onions, all at once. In Ouagadougou, the market is the supermarket, the town square, and the news cycle rolled into one dusty, glorious institution, and you will haggle over everything because not haggling is its own kind of insult. In Accra, you can do that too, if you want the authentic chaos — but you can also drive to a mall with air conditioning, a loyalty card, and a till receipt, and increasingly, that is exactly what the growing Ghanaian middle class chooses to do.

This is, fundamentally, a story about two countries at different points on the same road, and anyone who tells you one way is "more authentic" than the other has clearly never had to carry four kilos of yams uphill in 38-degree heat because the formal retail option simply didn't exist yet.

## Do's & Don'ts: Burkina Faso

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Haggle on everything that isn't a fixed-price imported good — it is expected, and skipping it reads as rude, not polite | Pay the first price quoted to a foreigner; it is a starting bid, not a final offer |
| Shop early morning for produce, before the heat and the crowds peak | Expect refrigeration for anything; plan your cooking around what's fresh that same day |
| Bring small denominations of CFA francs — vendors rarely have change for large notes | Assume card payment is an option outside the handful of formal supermarkets in Ouagadougou |
| Build a relationship with one or two regular vendors — loyalty gets you better prices and first pick of stock | Treat the market as a one-off errand; repeat custom is the entire pricing system |

## Do's & Don'ts: Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the big supermarkets (Marina Market, Palace, MaxMart) for imported and packaged goods, and the markets for produce and fish | Expect one shop to cover everything — the smart move is splitting your list across formal and informal retail |
| Haggle at Makola or Kaneshie markets exactly as hard as you would anywhere else in the region | Assume a shiny supermarket exterior means supermarket prices throughout; imported items carry serious import-duty markups |
| Keep cash and mobile money (MoMo) both ready — plenty of smaller vendors prefer MoMo transfers now | Rely solely on card payment; plenty of the best food shopping still runs on cash and mobile transfer |
| Ask Ghanaian colleagues which supermarket chain is "fresh that day" — it varies by branch and delivery schedule | Buy a week's worth of fresh produce in one supermarket trip and expect it to hold up; the climate has opinions |

## Burkina Faso: The Market Is The Whole Economy, Loudly

Burkina Faso has formal supermarkets — a handful of them, clustered in Ouagadougou and Bobo-Dioulasso, selling imported pasta and tinned tomatoes to NGO staff and a small professional class — but they are a footnote to the real system, which is the open-air market, and specifically the relationship economy that runs it. The national statistics institute's own household consumption data shows the overwhelming majority of food spending still flows through informal and semi-formal channels, not registered retail, and that is not a gap waiting to be filled by Western-style convenience; it is the system, functioning exactly as designed for a population where the market vendor is also your neighbour, your credit line in a lean week, and your source of gossip.

What this means practically, for anyone arriving expecting a Carrefour, is a recalibration of what "shopping" even means. You don't make a list and clear it in one stop. You go to the grain seller, then the vegetable stall, then the fish or meat vendor, each a separate relationship with its own pricing dynamics, its own expectations of small talk, its own tolerance for how hard you're allowed to haggle before it becomes rude rather than respectful. Landlocked, with import costs that bite hard on anything shipped in, Burkina Faso makes imported goods a genuine luxury — tinned European biscuits are not a snack, they're a small statement.

## Ghana: Shopping For Two Different Countries At Once

Ghana has leapt further down the formal-retail road than most of its neighbours, and Accra in particular now has a real supermarket culture — Ghanaian-owned chains, the occasional South African import, air-conditioned aisles stocked with everything from local plantain chips to suspiciously expensive imported cereal. The Ghana Statistical Service's living standards data tracks a visible shift in urban consumption patterns over the past decade, and anyone who's spent time in both Accra and a smaller regional town will tell you it is genuinely two different shopping cultures operating under one flag.

But step outside the mall car park and Makola Market is still doing most of the actual feeding of the city, and it is magnificent, overwhelming, and entirely unbothered by your schedule. The smart Accra resident, expat or otherwise, treats these as complementary systems rather than competing ones: supermarket for the packaged and the imported, market for the fresh and the cheap, mobile money bridging both because nobody wants to carry that much cash through a crowded market at midday. Ghana's retail sector is also simply more plugged into regional supply chains than Burkina Faso's, landlocked and import-cost-burdened as it is, which shows up in your grocery bill whether you notice the geopolitics or not.

## The Verdict

If efficiency is your religion, Ghana wins, no contest — you can genuinely do a weekly shop in under an hour if you pick your supermarket well. But if you want to understand how a food economy actually works at ground level, Burkina Faso's market system will teach you more in one haggling session than a year of Accra mall trips ever could. I'd rather eat the inefficiency and learn the vendor's name.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations Accra — paraphrased: a relocated logistics manager admitted it took three months to realise the "cheap" supermarket near their flat was marking up imported goods nearly double what the market stalls fifteen minutes away charged for local equivalents.*

> *Reddit r/Ghana — paraphrased: a thread full of residents arguing over which supermarket chain's produce actually turns over fast enough to be trusted, with no consensus reached after forty comments.*

> *expat.com Ouagadougou — paraphrased: a longtime NGO worker noted that after two years, they still hadn't found anywhere selling fresh milk reliably, and had simply made peace with powdered.*

## Conclusion

Both of these systems work. That's the part people miss when they parachute in with Western assumptions about what "developed" grocery shopping should look like. Burkina Faso's market economy runs on memory, trust, and daily renegotiation. Ghana's hybrid model runs on choosing your battlefield — fridge or stall — meal by meal. Neither is backward. Neither is quaint. Both will outlast whatever retail trend currently has Western investors excited, because both were built by people who actually need to eat, every single day, regardless of what the market research says.
