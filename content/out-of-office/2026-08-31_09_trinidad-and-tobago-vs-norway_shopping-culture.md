# One Nation Haggles For Sport, The Other Just Pays The Bill And Weeps Quietly

🇹🇹 Trinidad and Tobago 🇳🇴 Norway

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Shopping reveals more about national character than any tourist board would like. Trinidad and Tobago treats buying a phone charger as a contact sport — opening offers, counter-offers, the faint possibility of a new best friend by the till. Norway treats shopping as something to be endured quietly, like a dentist appointment, except the dentist charges 25 percent VAT on the way out and nobody makes eye contact.

I have watched grown adults in Port of Spain negotiate a phone case with the theatrical commitment of a courtroom drama, and watched grown adults in Oslo tap a card, say nothing, and leave looking vaguely bereaved. Both are valid responses to capitalism. Only one is fun to watch.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Trinidad and Tobago**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Open with an offer 10–20% below asking price at street stalls and markets | Haggle inside an actual retail shop or mall — it reads as bizarre, not savvy |
| Budget extra for anything imported — dairy, electronics, name-brand anything | Assume mall prices and market prices for the "same" item are comparable |
| Head to Chaguanas if you actually want a deal, not a souvenir | Expect Charlotte Street vendors to take cards — bring cash |
| Treat the negotiation as a social ritual, not a hostile transaction | Get insulted when a vendor counters hard — it's the game, not an attack |

**Norway**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comfortable paying by card or Vipps for literally everything, including a bun | Carry much cash — some places will look at it like it's a foreign artefact |
| Order from abroad if you're patient and factor in the 25% import VAT | Expect a "harrytur" to Sweden to save you what it used to — the gap has narrowed |
| Expect fewer, calmer, more minimalist shops — Scandinavian retail is edited hard | Expect haggling to work anywhere, on anything, ever |
| Compare prices before a big purchase — the sticker price is rarely negotiable but it does vary | Assume a higher price automatically means higher quality — sometimes it's just Norway |

## Trinidad and Tobago: The Art of the Deal, Literally

Trinidad's retail landscape runs on two operating systems sharing a currency. There's the mall system — Trincity Mall, Long Circular Mall, The Falls at West Mall, Grand Bazaar in Chaguanas, SouthPark, Gulf City — fixed prices, air conditioning, the same brands you'd find anywhere with a food court. And there's the market system, centred on Charlotte, Henry and Frederick Streets in Port of Spain, where the price tag is an opening bid, not a commandment.

According to Rough Guides, haggling has no place in formal retail but is entirely normal with street vendors and beach traders, who build negotiation room into their asking price from the start. The accepted choreography is to open ten to twenty percent under asking and let the vendor counter — a respectful offer gets a respectful compromise, and everyone walks away feeling like they won something. Chaguanas has built a local reputation as the place to find the sharpest deals on virtually anything, which tells you where Trinidadians themselves shop when nobody's watching.

The sting is in the imports. Expat Focus notes imported goods and familiar Western products carry premiums worth budgeting for before you relocate — dairy, brand-name cereal, foreign wine, electronics, all taxed and freighted into a wince-inducing price. Local produce, tropical fruit and poultry stay sensible; anything with a foreign logo gets treated like contraband. It's a two-speed economy on one small twin island — cheap if you buy local and bargain hard, punishing if you insist on comforts from elsewhere. MovieTowne and the bigger malls sell the cinema experience rather than compete on price, the correct call given what they're up against on Charlotte Street.

## Norway: Pay The Price, Say Nothing, Order From Sweden

Norway's shopping culture is built on a foundational trait: paying the asking price without complaint while privately seething about it. There is no haggling infrastructure here — retail is minimalist, curated, priced like it resents you. The Local Norway's coverage of "harryhandel" — Norwegians driving into Sweden for a day of bargain groceries, alcohol and tobacco — traces the term to a 2003 dig from a Norwegian agriculture minister calling such trips tacky, which the public gleefully reclaimed as a badge of honour. Norway's EEA arrangement with the EU means food, drink and tobacco attract import duties and agricultural protections EU-resident Sweden doesn't carry — a gap once wide enough to justify a tank of petrol and a border queue.

It's narrowing, though. Reporting on the harrytur phenomenon notes Swedish prices have climbed too, trimming the appeal — though alcohol, tobacco and select groceries still make the trip worthwhile. What's replaced the day trip, increasingly, is the inbox. PostNord's Nordic e-commerce research puts cross-border shopping at roughly a quarter of all Norwegian online retail activity, with well over half of online shoppers having ordered from abroad — China, the US and the UK lead, Sweden a strong second. The catch, per expat.com's Norway forum threads, is that anything ordered from outside Norway gets hit with 25 percent VAT on arrival, so the saving has to be real, not cosmetic. Underpinning it all is a cashless culture so thorough that guides warn you could do an entire holiday without touching a banknote — card and Vipps cover everything from a coffee to a car.

## The Verdict

Trinidad and Tobago wins this one outright. Shopping there is a genuine cultural activity — social, theatrical, occasionally competitive — and it rewards you for showing up with a bit of nerve. You leave Charlotte Street with a story and a fair price. Norway's retail experience, by contrast, is a masterclass in quiet, joyless efficiency: tap your card, accept the number on the screen, drive to Sweden twice a year to feel something. Norwegians will tell you this is "sensible" and "transparent," and they're not wrong — it's just that sensible and transparent describe a filing cabinet, not a shopping trip. Trinidad's two-speed economy, punishing as it is on imports, at least lets you fight back. Norway hands you the bill and calls the lack of negotiation "fairness." I call it surrender with excellent lighting.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — a recurring theme in answers about Trinidad's cost of living: imported goods and "foreign" brands carry such a markup that long-term residents relearn what counts as a treat versus an everyday purchase.*

> *expat.com Norway forum — posters discussing overseas orders note the 25 percent import VAT on arrival routinely erases whatever discount looked tempting on the original website, and it catches newcomers every time.*

> *expat.com Norway forum — contributors discussing rising living costs flag that grocery budgets comfortable a couple of years ago now fall noticeably short, with specific staples cited as having jumped well over 100 percent.*

## Conclusion

Neither country will apologise for how it shops, and neither should. Trinidad and Tobago has built a retail culture where the price is a conversation, and the imported stuff is the cost of wanting a taste of elsewhere. Norway has built one where the price is final, the card machine is god, and the real bargain-hunting happens across a border or behind a customs form. Want theatre? Go to Charlotte Street. Want to stand in a beautifully lit shop and pay exactly what's on the tag while feeling a deep, specific Scandinavian resignation? Oslo will oblige instantly. I know which one I'd rather be doing on a Saturday, and it involves raising my voice slightly and meaning it.
