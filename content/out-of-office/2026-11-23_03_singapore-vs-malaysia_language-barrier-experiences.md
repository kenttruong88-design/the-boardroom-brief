# Singapore and Malaysia Share One Word and Disagree About Everything After It

🇸🇬 Singapore vs 🇲🇾 Malaysia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every expat arrives in Singapore believing they speak English. By the end of the first week in the office they are not so sure. "Can" is a complete sentence. "Already" is a tense. "Lah" is a verdict. Someone says "this one cannot be, lah" and a decision has been taken, a deadline has moved and a colleague has quietly been offended, all in four words you could have sworn you understood.

Cross the Causeway and a rather similar thing happens, except that the English has a different accent and the same word means something fractionally different. I have had a taxi driver in Kuala Lumpur and one in Singapore both tell me, in perfect good faith, that a thing was "no problem, lah", and I have never been more certain that two people were describing two different universes. The surprise is not that the languages differ. It is that two such neighbours can be so confident that the other is the one speaking oddly.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇸🇬 Singapore ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Listen to the particles, because they carry the tone | Treat lah and lor as meaningless filler |
| Keep sentences short and direct | Pad every request with polite English preamble |
| Use "can" and "cannot" as complete answers | Wait for a full explanation after a "cannot" |
| Let the speaker finish before judging the grammar | Correct someone's English in a meeting |

| 🇲🇾 Malaysia ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a mix of English, Malay and Chinese dialects in one sentence | Assume English alone will cover a market stall |
| Learn that sial is a swear word | Borrow Singlish words without checking |
| Use "kena" for something unfortunate that happened to you | Confuse it with a Singapore usage without context |
| Ask, politely, when you have not understood | Nod along and sign something |

## 🇸🇬 Singapore

Singlish is the most efficient piece of language engineering I have ever encountered. According to Culture Trip, it uses around eleven particles, many borrowed from Hokkien and Cantonese, and "lah" is the star: a pragmatic particle that does the work tone of voice does in other languages. It can affirm, dismiss, soothe, exasperate or exclaim, depending on pitch and context, and the same two letters can mean "fine" or "don't be ridiculous". The article also warns newcomers off overusing it, which is excellent advice, because an Englishman saying "lah" sounds like an Englishman doing an impression, and nobody has ever enjoyed that.

Coconuts Singapore sets out the differences with its Malaysian cousin in word pairs. Singlish says "sia", an emphatic tag for envy or surprise; Malaysian English says "sial", which the piece describes as a swear word. Singapore says "genna" or "kena" for receiving something negative, such as a fine or a scolding; Malaysia says "kena", too. Singlish uses "gajiao" for disturbing or annoying someone, where Manglish has "kacau". Singapore has "scarly" for what if, and Malaysia has "sekali". The Independent repeated the same list, which tells you the list is where the internet stopped reading, but the point stands: the vocabulary overlaps and the shades diverge.

In Singapore the language also behaves like a national signature. The official register is polished, Commonwealth English, and the street register is Singlish, and the Singaporean switches between them without visible effort. The foreigner, who has learned only the first, will be understood perfectly and will also be a little bit outside the room. Singlish is intimacy: you are being told, gently, that you are among people who do not need to perform.

There is a historical reason the two feel so close and yet so mismatched. Culture Trip reminds us that Singapore was briefly part of Malaysia before separating in 1965, which means both inherited British Malaya's habits of English and a shared stock of Malay, Hokkien and Cantonese words. After the split, the two went their own ways: Singapore leaned on English as the working language of a deliberately multiracial city-state, while Malaysia kept Malay as its national language and let English sit beside it. The same old ingredients were simply cooked for fifty years in different kitchens, and the results are cousins who finish each other's sentences and then disagree about what was said.

What does this mean for a corporate expat? It means your colleagues are more fluent than you, and that what looks like terseness is economy. A flat "can" is agreement. A prolonged silence followed by "can, can" is a polite no. Ask a follow-up question in plain terms, and you will be given a real answer. Do not interpret speed as rudeness, and do not interpret warmth as consent.

## 🇲🇾 Malaysia

Malaysia's version of the story is messier and, I think, more interesting. The country is multilingual in the open, with Malay as the national language, English as the business lingua franca and Chinese dialects and Tamil in everyday circulation. Culture Trip notes the historical background, with British Malaya, Singapore's brief membership of the federation until 1965 and a 2017 figure of about 75 percent Chinese in Singapore against about 24 percent in Malaysia. That demographic gap is part of why the two Englishes feel different: they are blended from different ingredients.

Manglish therefore leans harder on Malay. A phrase like "sekali" does the job of "in case" or "what if", a Malay word embedded in an English sentence without ceremony, and "tembak" is used for doing something without preparation, where Singapore's version is "tikam". The word "sial", which Coconuts flags as a swear word in Manglish, is a reminder that borrowed words arrive with their original weight. Say the thing a Singaporean says as a friendly emphasis and you may find that the Malaysian listener has heard an insult.

The experience on the ground is warm and chaotic in the best way. A Malaysian colleague will code-switch mid-sentence, a market trader will address you in whichever language she suspects you have, and the whole performance is delivered with a relaxed good humour that makes mistakes feel cheap. Malaysia is more forgiving of the half-learned phrase, I find, partly because everyone is half-learning someone else's.

It helps to remember that the expat is not the only person improvising. A Malaysian speaking to a Singaporean will often slow down and standardise, and a Singaporean speaking to a Malaysian will do the same, each quietly translating the other's slang into something neutral. You are watching two groups of native speakers perform a courtesy for each other that they will never mention. Once you notice it, you can join in: speak a little slower, avoid idiom you cannot explain and ask people what they meant when it matters.

The danger is false confidence. Because so much sounds familiar, the expat assumes understanding. It is the same trap as Singapore, but wearing a sarong. When a Malaysian says "maybe" in a meeting, the answer may be no. When a Malaysian says "no problem", it may mean "I heard you", not "I will do it". Ask for details, confirm in writing and never rely on tone alone.

## The Verdict

Singapore wins for clarity and Malaysia wins for warmth, and I would rather be lost in Malaysia. In Singapore the system is efficient and the language, once decoded, is wonderfully direct. A Singapore office rarely wastes your time, and the code is learnable in a month.

Malaysia is harder to decode and kinder to those who try. The multilingual blend means everyone is making accommodations all the time, and an expat's errors vanish into the general generosity. If you have to negotiate a contract, take Singapore. If you have to survive a week with no itinerary, take Malaysia. And in either country, keep "lah" in your ear rather than your mouth until somebody tells you that you have earned it.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Coconuts Singapore — paraphrased: the Singlish word sia and the Manglish word sial look almost identical, but sial is treated as a swear word in Malaysia, so borrowing it carries a risk.*

> *Culture Trip — paraphrased: lah is a pragmatic particle used for affirmation, dismissal, exasperation and exclamation in both countries, and newcomers are warned against overusing it.*

> *The Independent Singapore, crediting Coconuts — paraphrased: kena means receiving something unpleasant such as a scolding or a fine, and gajiao and kacau both describe disturbing or annoying someone.*

## Conclusion

Two countries, one overworked syllable and a thousand ways to misread it. Singapore will say "cannot" and mean it. Malaysia will say "maybe" and might mean it. Learn the vocabulary, listen for the particles, ask the second question and never use a word you cannot trace to its owner. The English you arrived with is a perfectly good tool, but it will not get you through a lunch order, let alone a negotiation.
