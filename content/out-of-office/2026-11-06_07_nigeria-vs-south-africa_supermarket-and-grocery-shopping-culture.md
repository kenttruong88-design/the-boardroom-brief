🇳🇬 Nigeria vs 🇿🇦 South Africa

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

South Africa went to the trouble of establishing a supermarket in Nigeria, growing it to 25 stores across eight states, employing over 2,000 Nigerians directly — and then watched it collapse so thoroughly that by late 2025 its remaining branches had empty shelves and staff openly admitting "there is no supply." Shoprite's own corporate history is, in miniature, the entire story of why these two grocery cultures don't actually translate to each other, no matter how many flags get printed on the awning.

Meanwhile, South Africans back home are busy conducting their own internal supermarket civil war, arguing on Reddit about whether Checkers has finally dethroned Pick n Pay as the nation's best-value chain. Two countries, two completely different grocery anxieties. Neither one involves a calm trip to buy milk.

## Do's & Don'ts: Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shop little and often — perishables demand it, and even one Lagos-based expat admits weekly produce quality swings wildly, "great one week, mush the next" | Expect a reliable monthly supermarket run; most forum veterans describe buying only cheapest acceptable items a week at a time |
| Learn specific local markets by name and reputation — Balogun for general goods, Mile 12 for wholesale produce, Tejuosho for a cleaner, renovated experience | Assume fixed prices anywhere outside a formal supermarket; haggling is structurally built into Nigerian market shopping |
| Budget real money for imported staples — a kilo of apples in an expat-area Lagos shop runs close to ₦4,600 | Count on a dying chain to carry what it used to; Shoprite's own Nigerian footprint shrank from 25+ stores to a shuttered handful inside six years |
| Check out Ebeano or Hubmart, the local chains that outlasted the foreign entrant | Treat a Chinese- or foreign-owned supermarket as risk-free; one Abuja store restricted customer entry to one at a time after an armed robbery cost it over ₦2.5 million |

## Do's & Don'ts: South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Track the PMBEJD monthly food basket if you want a genuinely official read on prices — Johannesburg consistently runs the most expensive of the three major metros | Assume Pick n Pay is still the budget default; Reddit's own shoppers now rate Checkers as better value and describe PnP and Spar as "last resort" options |
| Give Woolworths a chance on fresh produce specifically — it's increasingly rated as price-competitive on fruit and vegetables, not just "gourmet" | Dismiss Woolworths as purely a premium brand; one decade-old but still-cited forum review calls it "pricey but gourmet... worth every penny" |
| Try Food Lover's Market for specific good-value produce lines | Assume frozen fish at any chain is fresh; at least one longtime Johannesburg resident remains openly skeptical: "I sometimes think the fish have been frozen and then thawed" |
| Treat Sandton City or Mall of Africa's weekend markets — Neighbourgoods, Rosebank Sunday Market — as genuine grocery-adjacent social outings | Expect full-service bagging; self-bagging at the till is standard across Checkers, Woolworths, and Pick n Pay alike |

## Nigeria: a market culture that survived the supermarket's collapse

The Shoprite story is, on its own, close to the whole article. The South African chain entered Nigeria in 2005, grew to more than 25 stores across eight states including the Federal Capital Territory, and employed over 2,000 Nigerians directly at its peak — a genuine retail success story, right up until it wasn't. In September 2019, xenophobic attacks on Nigerians in South Africa triggered boycotts and the vandalism and looting of Lagos stores; police had to physically seal and guard locations. Sales fell 8.1% on a constant-currency basis that year alone. By 2020, Shoprite had sold off its Nigerian operations entirely, shifting to a franchise model. The Kano store closed in January 2024 over unsustainable rents. By September 2025, the Ibadan and Ilorin stores had shut completely, while the Lagos and Abuja locations were reporting bare shelves — one staff member telling a reporter plainly, "there is no supply," blaming supplier price negotiations and recurring power outages. A rival South African chain, Mr Price, closed four of its five remaining Nigerian stores in the same window. This is not a cautionary tale about one bad year. It's a six-year documented collapse.

What actually filled the gap tells you more about Nigerian grocery culture than the supermarket ever did. Local chains like Ebeano and Hubmart outlasted the foreign entrant, but the real backbone remains the open-air market — and these markets have genuine, specific identities, not interchangeable stalls. Balogun Market on Lagos Island handles general goods, clothing, and groceries with haggling built into every transaction; Mile 12 is the city's wholesale produce hub; Tejuosho in Yaba has been renovated into something genuinely more orderly, with over 4,000 shops and its own tailors; Oyingbo specialises in fresh produce, meat, and spices. One Expat.com forum veteran, ordering UK shelf-stable goods monthly through an online retailer because local options for decent cheese, bacon, or bran flakes require "hunting all over Lagos," captures the core trade-off precisely: fresh local markets are vivid and functional, but anything resembling a reliable, imported-goods supermarket run simply doesn't exist the way it does elsewhere. A Talesmag respondent puts a number on the result — roughly $150 a week on groceries for two people, buying only what's needed a few days at a time, because supply is "unstable" and imported products often arrive damaged or overpriced. And a documented flashpoint worth noting honestly: a Chinese-owned supermarket in Abuja began restricting customers to entering one at a time, or with a police escort for groups, after a 2016 armed robbery cost the store over ₦2.5 million and injured its owners — a reminder that the friction here cuts in directions a tidy culture-clash narrative doesn't always expect.

## South Africa: a formal supermarket culture, fighting itself in public

South Africa's grocery landscape is the Nigerian scenario's mirror image: a mature, formal, tiered supermarket hierarchy that locals argue about constantly, in public, on social media, with real data to back up their grievances. The PMBEJD's monthly household food basket — a genuinely official, government-adjacent tracking index covering 44 items — put Johannesburg at R5,559.46 in April 2025, the most expensive of the country's three major metros and R139.16 above the national average; Cape Town came in cheapest at R5,316.56, with Durban in between.

The real drama, though, is playing out in the chains themselves. A widely discussed Reddit thread on r/southafrica, documenting what it calls "the rapid decline of Pick n Pay," has South African shoppers actively relitigating their own brand loyalty in real time: the original poster notes finding Woolworths now cheaper than Pick n Pay on most fresh produce except avocados, describing Pick n Pay — once the reliable family default — as having lost that status entirely; on a Sunday morning at their local Cape Town mall, the crowds were "upstairs at Woolworths" rather than downstairs where Pick n Pay sits. A commenter in the same thread ranks Checkers as "probably the best value right now" and calls Pick n Pay and Spar both "last resort options," with Food Lover's Market earning a nod for specific good-value produce lines. Talesmag's own long-running Johannesburg archive adds historical texture and a few genuine surprises: one 2022 respondent found groceries both cheaper and higher quality than back home in the US; a 2019 contributor stayed openly suspicious of supermarket fish, wondering aloud if it had been "frozen and then thawed"; and a 2010 reviewer called Woolworths "pricey but gourmet... worth every penny," a verdict apparently still holding up well over a decade later. Self-bagging at the till is the universal norm across all three major chains — a small but genuine etiquette trip-wire for anyone arriving expecting full service.

## The Verdict

Nigeria's grocery culture survived the literal collapse of its own biggest formal supermarket chain and barely noticed, because the market system underneath it was never dependent on that chain to begin with. South Africa's grocery culture, by contrast, is so formalised that its citizens have turned brand loyalty into a genuine, data-backed public debate — essentially a national hobby. If resilience is the metric, Nigeria wins without much argument: a system built on open-air markets and constant price negotiation doesn't notice when one corporate player quietly dies. If convenience and consistency are the metric, South Africa wins just as clearly — you can at least trust that Woolworths will still be there on Monday, supply chain intact, whatever Reddit currently thinks of its prices.

## Conclusion

The honest takeaway isn't that one country shops "better" than the other — it's that Nigeria's grocery culture was built to survive without institutional reliability, and South Africa's was built around assuming it. One of those assumptions just got tested by six years of real corporate history, and only one country came out the other side without noticing much had changed at all. Bring cash and a willingness to haggle to Lagos. Bring an opinion about Checkers to Johannesburg — you'll need one within the week.
