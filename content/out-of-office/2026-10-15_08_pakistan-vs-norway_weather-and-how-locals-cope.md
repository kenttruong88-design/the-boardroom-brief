# One Country's Weather Tries To Cook You. The Other's Tries To Make You Disappear.

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇳🇴 Norway

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have sweated through a linen shirt in Lahore at a temperature that made the pavement genuinely dangerous to touch, and I have sat in a Tromsø living room at 2pm in December under a lamp designed specifically to convince my brain that the sun still exists. Both experiences involved real, documented physical danger to my sense of time. Only one involved visible heat shimmer off a parked car.

Weather doesn't just inconvenience a place; it architects the entire culture around coping with it. Pakistan and Norway sit at opposite poles of that problem, quite literally in Norway's case, and the coping mechanisms could not be more different.

## 🇵🇰 Pakistan: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Restructure your entire day around the heat — early starts, long indoor middays, late evenings | Plan outdoor errands for midday in peak summer; locals have long since stopped doing this |
| Keep a backup power plan; load-shedding during heatwaves is common and genuinely disruptive | Assume air conditioning will always be available or affordable; many households ration its use |
| Hydrate relentlessly and respect heatstroke warnings; they are issued for real reasons | Underestimate how dangerous 45-50°C heat actually is, even if you "handle heat well" at home |
| Adopt the shaded-courtyard, rooftop-evening lifestyle locals have refined over generations | Wear anything heavy or dark-coloured outdoors; light, loose fabric is a survival strategy, not fashion |

## 🇳🇴 Norway: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in a daylight lamp before your first real polar night, not during it | Expect normal energy levels through December and January; even locals plan around the dip |
| Learn "friluftsliv" — outdoor life as near-mandatory antidote to winter darkness | Hibernate indoors entirely; Norwegians famously insist you go outside anyway, bundled up |
| Track sunrise/sunset times seriously; they shift dramatically and locals plan around them | Assume the far north and the south experience winter the same way; Svalbard and Oslo are worlds apart |
| Stock up on candles ("koselig" cosiness culture) — it's a genuine coping ritual, not just aesthetic | Schedule anything requiring daylight-dependent energy for January without a backup plan |

## Pakistan: Heat As Daily Architecture

Pakistan's summer heat isn't weather so much as a structural force that reorganises everything around it. Temperatures regularly climb into the mid-40s Celsius across much of the country, with documented extremes well past 50°C in the hottest regions — numbers that don't just mean "uncomfortable," they mean genuinely dangerous, heatstroke-and-hospitalisation dangerous, and Pakistan's own meteorological authorities issue heatwave advisories with real urgency rather than polite suggestion. Locals have responded by restructuring the entire rhythm of daily life: early starts before the sun commits to its worst, a long indoor retreat through the brutal middle hours, and a second wind in the evening once the heat finally, grudgingly, loosens its grip.

What complicates this further, and what catches expats genuinely off guard, is load-shedding — scheduled or unscheduled power cuts that hit hardest exactly when demand for air conditioning peaks during a heatwave, meaning the one tool that makes extreme heat survivable can simply vanish for hours at the worst possible moment. Households adapt with backup generators, battery inverters, and a learned patience that would break most Western visitors within a week. The shaded courtyard, the rooftop evening gathering once the concrete finally stops radiating the day's heat back at you, the loose, light, pale clothing worn not as a fashion choice but as thermal engineering — all of it reflects generations of practical knowledge about surviving a climate that genuinely wants to hurt you.

The thing I respect most, having sweated through a fraction of what locals handle every summer without complaint, is the total absence of drama about it. Nobody's performing endurance. It's simply Tuesday, again, at 47 degrees, and life proceeds anyway, just slower, shadier, and considerably later into the night.

## Norway: Darkness As Communal Project

Norway's winter weather problem isn't temperature so much as light itself vanishing entirely, in some regions, for weeks. The polar night — when the sun doesn't clear the horizon at all — hits different parts of the country with wildly different severity: Svalbard goes dark from mid-November to late January, Tromsø loses the sun for roughly seven weeks, and Lofoten gets a shorter but still disorienting stretch starting in December. Further south, Oslo never experiences true polar night, but even there, winter daylight shrinks to a thin, grudging window that makes a 3pm sunset feel like an afterthought rather than an event.

What Norway has built, impressively, is an entire cultural infrastructure around surviving this without simply going feral indoors. "Friluftsliv" — outdoor life, treated as near-compulsory rather than optional — insists you go outside anyway, bundled against the cold, because fresh air and movement are considered the actual antidote to the psychological weight of sustained darkness, not a luxury for good weather days. Daylight lamps, designed to mimic sunlight and nudge the brain's circadian rhythm back into line, are common enough to be unremarkable, sold and recommended with the casualness of a vitamin supplement. And "koselig" — the candlelit, blanket-heavy cosiness culture Norway shares a cousin concept with in Danish "hygge" — turns the darkness itself into an aesthetic and social occasion rather than something purely to be endured.

The genuinely striking thing, for an outsider, is how little panic accompanies any of this. Norwegians plan for the dip in energy and mood the polar night brings the way other cultures plan for a rainy season — not happily, exactly, but without the sense that something has gone wrong. The darkness is scheduled. The coping is scheduled right alongside it.

## The Verdict

Pakistan's heat is immediate and physically threatening in a way that demands constant, practical vigilance — hydration, shade, timing, backup power. Norway's darkness is slower and psychologically threatening in a way that demands deliberate, almost ritualised counter-measures — lamps, outdoor discipline, candlelight. One climate tries to cook you in real time. The other tries to quietly erode your sense of day and night until you've forgotten what either felt like.

I'd rather face Norway's darkness, if forced to choose, purely because you can engineer your way through it with a lamp and a jacket. Pakistan's heat offers no such workaround when the power cuts out at 2pm and the mercury's past 48. Respect to both populations for making either survivable at all, let alone with this much apparent calm about it.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Pakistan forum — paraphrased: a longtime resident described load-shedding during a heatwave as the single hardest adjustment, explaining that losing air conditioning for hours at 46°C is a different category of discomfort than anything a mild-climate newcomer has prepared for.*

> *r/Norway — paraphrased: several users agreed that the hardest part of the polar night isn't the cold, it's the disorientation of losing track of time entirely without a visible sunrise or sunset to anchor the day.*

> *Internations Oslo — paraphrased: an expat recommended buying a daylight lamp in October, before the darkness actually arrives, rather than waiting until the mood dip hits and trying to source one in a panic.*

## Conclusion

Pakistan's summer will test your body. Norway's winter will test your sense of time. Both will humble anyone who's ever claimed to "handle extreme weather fine" based on a difficult fortnight somewhere mild. I've been defeated by both, in very different ways, and I'll say this without diplomacy: respect the locals in either place, because they've built entire cultures, generations deep, around surviving conditions that would have the rest of us booking the next flight home by day three.
