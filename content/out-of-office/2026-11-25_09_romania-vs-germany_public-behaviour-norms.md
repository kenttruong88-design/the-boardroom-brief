# Romania Treats a Rule as an Opening Bid and Germany Treats It as Scripture

🇷🇴 Romania vs 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Hundreds of thousands of Romanians live in Germany, and each one has a story that begins with a note on the door. It is a short, handwritten note, polite in tone and murderous in intent, concerning the recycling, the noise, the stairwell or the question of whether anyone should be hoovering on a Sunday. It is the German national love letter, and it arrives exactly once, before the formal warning.

Romania and Germany sit on opposite sides of Europe's great cultural fault line between improvisation and order. In one, rules are a negotiation and a coffee can settle most things. In the other, rules are a sacred text and the neighbour is the priest. I have been a guest in both, and I have come to believe that public behaviour is where a country keeps its real constitution.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇷🇴 Romania ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept hospitality with enthusiasm and bring something for the host | Refuse a second helping, which is a small insult |
| Treat a rule as a starting point and ask who can help | Assume the written regulation is the final word |
| Expect conversation, noise and warmth in public places | Read loud as hostile |
| Greet people properly before you ask for anything | Launch straight into your request |

| 🇩🇪 Germany ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Observe quiet hours in the evening and all day Sunday | Drill, vacuum or play music at ten past ten |
| Arrive five minutes early | Show up late and expect forgiveness |
| Use Sie with officials, shopkeepers and older neighbours | Switch to du without an invitation |
| Sort your rubbish properly and stand on the right of the escalator | Throw it all in one bin and block the left |

## 🇷🇴 Romania

Romania's public life is loud, social and gloriously unbothered. People talk. They talk in cafes, in queues, on trams and across the street, and the volume carries no malice. Public space is a living room that happens to have traffic in it. Hospitality is a point of honour, and I have been fed so much in Romanian homes that I have considered applying for residency out of gratitude and cholesterol.

Rules exist, naturally, and there are many of them, but the relationship to them is interpretive. The lovely Romanian expression about things being all right in the end is the national philosophy: queues bend, parking is optimistic and a problem is solved by knowing a person who knows a person. This is infuriating if you are a foreigner in a hurry and delightful if you are a foreigner in need. A good conversation will often do more than a form, and the formal channel is a last resort.

I will not pretend this is purely charming. It can mean noise is tolerated beyond reason, rules are applied selectively and a stranger can feel isolated by the very informality that makes everything else bearable. But the social glue is thick. People help each other. They notice you. The neighbour who might be a little loud on a Saturday night is also the one who will climb a ladder to fix your gutter without being asked. It is a society built on human relationships rather than on signs.

Romania also has a distinctive sociability around public transport and the street. People give directions at length, strangers will comment on your shopping, and the line at the pharmacy is a negotiation conducted with great theatre. I have seen grown adults argue passionately about the order of a queue and then share a cigarette, and the whole thing felt like ordinary civic life. Informality is not chaos here. It is a style of trust. The pitfall is that outsiders read it as disorder, and Romanians moving abroad often find themselves scolded for the very warmth that made them popular at home. Learn to lower the volume, not the kindness.

## 🇩🇪 Germany

Germany has signs. Germany has so many signs that the signs have their own sub-signs. The expat guides are unanimous: quiet hours, called Ruhezeit, are generally ten at night to six or seven in the morning, plus all day Sunday, and loud music, drilling or vigorous hoovering can bring a complaint to the building management. One writer who moved to Freiburg in 2014 received a handwritten note about badly sorted recycling, and she is not an exception. She is the rule.

The detail is dazzling. Stand on the right of the escalator. Do not walk in the bike lane. Arrive five minutes early, because at an authority office lateness can mean being turned away and waiting weeks for a new slot. Use Sie, the formal you, with officials, shopkeepers and older neighbours, and do not switch to du until you are invited. Carry cash, because roughly half of point-of-sale payments still use it. A Romanian press piece lists even more: dogs barking beyond a limited number of minutes a day, a ban on washing your car in the street with a fine said to reach 500 euros, hedges that may only be cut between October and March. I cannot verify the numbers, and the article does not cite them, but the spirit is accurate.

What the foreigner misses is that this is not hostility. It is a social contract so thoroughly internalised that people feel genuinely wounded when it is broken. The German friendship is slow to build and durable once built, and being overly chatty can read as needy. You are not being punished. You are being asked to be a predictable member of a very large, very quiet organisation. Do it well and you will be rewarded with the most reliable neighbours in Europe.

The German rules can look absurd until you see what they protect, which is sleep, space and the shared infrastructure of a dense country. An expat guide says the Ruhezeit is typically ten at night to six in the morning plus Sundays, and that a neighbour may complain, or building management may issue a formal warning, if you drill or play music during it. The same guide cites a central bank study finding cash used in around half of point-of-sale payments and advises carrying 20 to 50 euros. It sounds fussy. It is also why a German stairwell is silent at midnight and a Romanian one is not.

## The Verdict

I will say what I feel. Germany is a better country for sleeping and Romania is a better country for living, and anyone who tries to tell you those are the same thing has not had a baby or a hangover. The German system is wonderfully liveable if you like silence, order and a recycling bin with a manifesto. It is oppressive if you like spontaneity.

Romania wins on warmth and on the sheer human quality of public life. A society that treats rules as flexible is a society that assumes people are reasonable, and I find that more flattering than a stairwell full of laminated instructions. It has costs, of course. Trust is slower to build in institutions and faster to build between people.

My verdict is Romania for the dinner party, Germany for the Monday morning. If you want to be left alone to work, move to Germany and buy earplugs for the neighbour who is not yours. If you want to be fed, argued with and hugged, move to Romania and learn to say no gently. You will fail. That is part of the charm.

## Conclusion

Romania and Germany are not two opposite moral systems. They are two ways of keeping a crowded continent bearable. One does it with relationships and forgiveness. The other does it with rules and a hard look.

The wise expat learns to carry both. Be warm in Bucharest and quiet in Berlin. Bring cake to the first neighbour you meet in either. And if you receive a handwritten note on your door in Germany, do not take it personally. Fix the recycling, apologise in writing and wait for the next one. There will be a next one. That is not hostility. That is simply what a rule looks like when it has found its home.
