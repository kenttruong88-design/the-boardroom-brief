# Pakistan Charges You Rent for Abs; Britain Sells You a Membership to Feel Guilty

🇵🇰 Pakistan vs 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Fitness, like everything else, has a class system. In Pakistan it has lately acquired the full apparatus of an aspirational lifestyle: the packed gym, the personal trainer, the protein tub, the yoga session, the padel court, all paid for in rupees that seem to have a rapid-onset allergy to staying in your wallet. The Express Tribune's recent piece, "The cost of fitness", reads like a household budget written by someone who has discovered kettlebells.

Britain has the opposite problem. The gym is cheap, ubiquitous and mostly empty, a monument to January optimism that the nation continues to fund through direct debit. The UK is also, rather interestingly, the setting for a quieter story about who feels welcome at the gym and who does not. Between the two, there is a revealing contrast about what exercise is for: a status project in one country, a nagging obligation in the other, and for a large number of women in both, a question of whether the room is safe to walk into.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇵🇰 Pakistan ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget for the full cost: membership, food and supplements | Assume the gym fee is the whole bill |
| Try bodyweight training at home or outdoors | Believe social media about what fitness requires |
| Find a trainer with real credentials | Pay triple for a trainer because it looks serious |
| Pick your hours carefully, since many gyms are now packed all day | Turn up at six in the evening expecting space |

| 🇬🇧 United Kingdom ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Look for women-only gyms and sessions if that suits you | Assume the nearest chain is the only option |
| Ask about prayer rooms, Ramadan hours and modest-wear policies | Assume they will be offered unprompted |
| Compare memberships and cancel what you do not use | Pay for a year of January resolutions |
| Check for modest activewear from specialist brands | Put up with an oversized hoodie |

## 🇵🇰 Pakistan

The Express Tribune article, published in August, is a wonderfully honest document. It profiles Ashas Rahim, who spends two to two and a half hours at the gym, 45 minutes on yoga and a couple of hours on padel some days, six days a week, aims for at least three litres of water and 8,000 to 10,000 steps and spends around Rs18,000 a month on the gym, Rs15,000 on healthy food and Rs5,000 on supplements. With fuel and gear, her total is somewhere between Rs68,000 and Rs76,000 a month. That is not a membership, that is a mortgage on a physique.

The other voices are more relatable. Kabir Hussain, about 20 kilograms above his ideal weight, says household and work responsibilities leave him three to four hours after work, and notes that colleagues are reportedly spending Rs40,000 to 50,000 a month on fitness, while a personal trainer can roughly triple the membership cost. Shehzar Muhammad, a first-class cricketer and trainer who runs a studio, is the voice of reason: social media makes fitness look more expensive than it is, gyms are packed all day, unlike five years ago, food and training come first, supplements are an add-on, and bodyweight work at home or outdoors costs nothing.

That last point deserves repeating. The commercial gym is not the only route to a fitter life, and a country with a park, a rooftop and a decent pair of trainers has all it needs. But the gym boom is real, and it reflects a genuine shift in the way an urban, middle-class Pakistani thinks about health. For women the picture is more complicated. I could not fetch the Daily Times piece on women reshaping gym culture, so I will not claim to know the details, but I would say that women-only spaces and mixed-gender comfort levels are a significant part of how Pakistani gym culture evolves.

For an expat, the practical advice is to treat a Pakistani gym as a social space as much as a training one. Prices vary widely, and the best deals are often through word of mouth. Do your own research on what you need, and be sceptical about the add-ons. The fundamentals are free, and that is a rather good sentence to keep in your head.

## 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

The British gym is a more standardised creature. You can join a budget chain for the price of a few coffees and a nagging sense that you ought to go. What is more interesting is the growth of gyms designed for people the chains forgot. Hyphen's piece, by Yousra Samir Imran, describes Health Club for Her in Batley, West Yorkshire, a women-only gym opened in 2016 by Wajjad and Waqas Hussain, with a prayer room, membership freezes during Ramadan and class times adjusted for the month. It added an extra floor for weights in 2024 after member requests. Other centres named include WOH Gym in Newport and Swann Fitness in Oldham.

The voices are persuasive. Ishrat Mehmood, a teacher who has been a member since 2017, praises the Ramadan accommodation and says modest options used to mean an oversized hoodie. Aishah Siddique, a Cardiff personal trainer of seven years who works only with Muslim women, says non-Muslim women at women-only gyms have also told her they are tired of being watched. The article cites a 2024 study in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology finding that women who strength train two to three times a week have a lower risk of dying from heart disease, which is the sort of fact that justifies a prayer room and a squat rack in the same building.

Stylist, writing in 2021, adds background. It cites a Mud Orange study in which 86 percent of British Muslim women said they would prefer women-only gyms, classes or pools, and 55 percent said home workouts and free time during lockdown helped them exercise sustainably. It notes that halal-friendly supplements are harder to find because of gelatine, and that modest sportswear was, at that point, offered by only a minority of brands. Khadija Mamsa, a finance analyst, was quoted saying that existing hijab sportswear was not suited to HIIT or yoga. The figures come from a single organisation and the article is five years old, so I would treat them as indicative.

What the British story shows is a market that has finally started to listen. The standard gym still serves the standard customer. But the niche is flourishing, the accommodations are specific and the cost of a membership is a fraction of what Pakistan's aspirational fitness economy demands.

## The Verdict

Britain wins for accessibility and Pakistan wins for energy, and I would take the Pakistani gym for atmosphere and the British one for price. The UK offers a cheap, regulated, varied market and a small but growing number of places designed for specific communities, which is a decent measure of a mature market. If you are an expat, you will find a gym within a mile of your door and a place that fits within a month.

Pakistan's gym culture is newer, more social and more expensive relative to income. It is also genuinely growing, and the packed gyms say something good about a society that is finally taking its own health seriously. If I had to choose, I would take London's price and Karachi's crowd. And if a Pakistani trainer tells you the secret is a free walk and a bodyweight routine, believe him.

## Conclusion

Pakistan will charge you for the privilege of getting fit, and Britain will charge you for the privilege of not turning up. Between them is a perfectly good plan: find a space where you feel comfortable, train with intent and ignore anyone who says the equipment must cost a fortune. The best gym is the one you actually walk into. Everything else is marketing.
