# Everyone Speaks English Until the Exact Moment You Need Them To

🇿🇲 Zambia 🇲🇾 Malaysia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Somewhere between "official language: English" and "actually understanding a single word being said to you," there is a canyon, and both Zambia and Malaysia have built entirely different bridges across it. One nation gave you English and then quietly layered 70-odd local languages underneath it like a trap door. The other invented an entire dialect just to mess with you and called it charming.

I've had genuinely fluent conversations in both countries that ended with me nodding, smiling, and understanding absolutely nothing. This is that story, twice.

## Do's & Don'ts: Zambia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Nyanja or Bemba greetings — it lands enormously | Assume English fluency means shared vocabulary or idiom |
| Expect code-switching mid-sentence, sometimes mid-word | Get flustered when a conversation shifts languages around you |
| Use simple, direct English in official settings | Rely on British idioms — they land flat or confusingly literal |
| Ask which of the 70+ languages someone actually grew up speaking | Assume "Zambian English" and "British English" are interchangeable |

## Do's & Don'ts: Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to love "lah," "lor," and "meh" — they carry real meaning | Correct someone's Manglish grammar; it's a dialect, not an error |
| Expect Malay, Mandarin, Tamil, and English in one conversation | Assume Manglish speakers "don't really" speak English |
| Use short, direct requests — elaborate phrasing gets lost | Get impatient when a shopkeeper switches languages mid-sale |
| Pick up basic Bahasa Malaysia — it's genuinely easy and appreciated | Assume everyone under 40 is equally comfortable in English |

## Zambia: The Bemba-English-Nyanja Relay Race

Zambia's language situation is deceptively described on paper: English is the official language, full stop, government business and schooling all run through it. In practice, English is the formal outer layer of a country running on more than 70 living languages underneath — Bemba, Nyanja, Tonga, Lozi, and dozens more, depending entirely on which province you've wandered into. Expat Arrivals' own relocation guide is blunt about it: English gets you through paperwork and polite conversation, but it does not get you into the room where the actual social life is happening.

What catches newcomers off guard isn't a lack of English — it's the code-switching. A conversation can start in English, drift into Nyanja for a joke that clearly doesn't translate, snap back to English for your benefit, and you'll spend half the exchange trying to figure out if you missed something important or just missed the punchline. A Tales Mag "real post report" from an expat in Lusaka put it plainly: local-language classes are available and genuinely worth the money, not because you'll become fluent, but because even a handful of Bemba phrases visibly changes how people treat you — it signals effort in a country where showing up and expecting English to carry you all the way through is, quietly, noted.

There's also a formality gap that trips up Brits and Americans alike: Zambian English carries a British colonial-era structure — precise, somewhat formal, heavy on titles and indirect requests in official settings — that doesn't match the more relaxed English either group expects to hear "in Africa," whatever that phrase is supposed to mean (it means nothing; stop using it). Meanwhile, everyday marketplace English is a different register entirely: fast, transactional, warm, and completely unbothered by the version you learned in school.

## Malaysia: Four Languages, One Sentence

Malaysia doesn't have a language barrier so much as a language blender, and the button is stuck on "combine." Malay is the national language, English is widely spoken and taught from childhood, Mandarin and various Chinese dialects dominate large parts of the ethnic Chinese community, and Tamil serves much of the ethnic Indian population — and in any given conversation in Kuala Lumpur, you may hear all four take turns within a single sentence. This isn't chaos; it's an entire functioning social contract, and it works beautifully once you stop trying to pin it down as one language happening at a time.

Then there's Manglish, which deserves its own passport stamp. It is not broken English, whatever a first-time visitor might assume — 360info's own coverage on the subject is emphatic that Manglish is a legitimate, rule-governed dialect, not an error pattern, complete with its own grammar (dropped articles, particle words like "lah," "lor," and "meh" doing genuine emotional and grammatical work) and its own internal logic that took linguists decades to properly document instead of dismiss. Correcting it, as more than one mortified tourist has learned the hard way on an expat.com thread, reads as condescending, not helpful.

The actual barrier isn't comprehension — it's register-switching speed. Malaysians routinely downshift into more standard English for foreigners without missing a beat, which creates the illusion that "everyone speaks perfect English here," right up until you're in a wet market, a kopitiam, or a government office where the transaction reverts instantly to whatever combination of Malay, Mandarin, and Manglish is fastest for everyone except you. An Expat Forum thread on "Speaking English in Malaysia" sums up the collective foreigner experience in one sentence: fluent enough to get by everywhere, fluent enough to actually understand almost nowhere.

## The Verdict

Malaysia wins this one, and it's not close, because Malaysia's language chaos is at least a *system* — four languages doing genuine, structured, mutually intelligible work, with Manglish acting as the charming connective tissue between all of them. Zambia's barrier is quieter and, frankly, sneakier: English lulls you into false confidence right up until a room full of Bemba or Nyanja reminds you that the official language was never the whole story. Both will humble you. Only one will do it while calling you "lah" affectionately on the way out.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Tales Mag "Real Post Report" — a Lusaka-based diplomatic spouse said local-language tutors were "cheap, easy to find, and the single best money I spent all year" for basic Bemba phrases alone.*

> *expat.com Malaysia forum — a new arrival asked which language to prioritize before moving and was told, bluntly, "Bahasa gets you respect, English gets you by, Manglish gets you understood — learn all three attitudes, not just the words."*

> *Quora — a longtime KL resident answering "Do Malaysians actually speak English fluently?" wrote that fluency "isn't the question — the question is which version of English you're going to get, and it changes by postcode."*

## Conclusion

If you want the comforting illusion of a shared language that quietly isn't, go to Zambia and let English lull you into a false sense of security. If you want to be honestly, upfront confused by four languages doing backflips around each other in real time, go to Malaysia and learn to love it. I'd take the honest chaos over the polite illusion every time — at least in Kuala Lumpur, nobody's pretending the English you learned in school is the whole story.
