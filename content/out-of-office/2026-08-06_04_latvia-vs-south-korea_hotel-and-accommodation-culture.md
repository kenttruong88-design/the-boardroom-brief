# Latvia Gives You a Key and Leaves You Alone; South Korea Gives You Forty Amenities and a Curfew Notice

🇱🇻 Latvia vs 🇰🇷 South Korea — Hotel and Accommodation Culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Latvia and South Korea have arrived at completely different answers to the same question: what does a traveller actually need from a place to sleep? Latvia's answer is minimalist to the point of Nordic austerity — clean, quiet, unobtrusive, hosts who will greet you once and then respect your privacy with almost suspicious thoroughness. South Korea's answer is maximalist and slightly manic — accommodation as an entire ecosystem, from twenty-four-hour jjimjilbangs to officetels with better gyms than most Western hotels, a country that has turned "somewhere to sleep" into a genuine competitive industry.

I have stayed in a Riga guesthouse where the host handed me a key, pointed at a coffee machine, and did not speak to me again for four days out of what I can only describe as principled hospitality. I have also stayed in a Seoul jjimjilbang at 2am, surrounded by sleeping strangers on heated floor mats after a night out, paying less than a coffee back home for a bed, a sauna, and a genuine cultural experience I did not fully understand until the following morning. Both are, in their own countries, completely unremarkable. Neither will make sense on arrival.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇱🇻 Riga & Latvia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book guesthouses in the Art Nouveau district for character over corporate chains | Expect round-the-clock front desk service at smaller properties — many are minimally staffed |
| Respect quiet hours seriously — Latvians take noise complaints in shared buildings very seriously | Assume tipping housekeeping is expected; it's appreciated but not culturally mandatory |
| Ask hosts for genuine recommendations — Latvians tend to be understated but reliably honest | Expect small talk from staff; reserved doesn't mean unfriendly, it's just not performative |

**🇰🇷 South Korea**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Try a jjimjilbang or goshiwon at least once for the cultural experience, even briefly | Assume all budget stays are hostels; officetels and love motels are distinct categories worth knowing |
| Expect extraordinary amenity density even at mid-range hotels — saunas, PC rooms, laundry | Wear shoes past the entrance of most accommodation types — footwear etiquette is strict |
| Book ahead during festival seasons; domestic tourism moves in intense, coordinated waves | Be surprised by love motels' upscale, discreet design — they're a mainstream, normalised category |

## Latvia: Hospitality by Omission

Riga's accommodation scene runs on a philosophy that would read, in a more performative hospitality culture, as almost rude: you are given what you need, told where things are, and then left entirely to your own devices. This isn't coldness. Latvians, having spent much of their modern history under occupations that made privacy a precious and hard-won thing, tend to treat a guest's autonomy as the highest form of respect they can offer. A host who doesn't hover isn't failing at hospitality — they're succeeding at it, by their own cultural measure.

The city's Art Nouveau architecture, some of the finest and most concentrated in Europe, has produced a genuinely lovely tier of small guesthouses and boutique stays carved into century-old buildings, all ornate façades and understated interiors, run by people who will happily talk your ear off about Riga's history if you ask directly but will never volunteer it unprompted. This is a recurring Latvian pattern worth learning fast: warmth exists, generously, but it has to be requested. Passive travellers who wait to be entertained will find Latvia standoffish. Travellers who ask direct questions will find themselves adopted, in a quiet, undemonstrative way, for the length of their stay.

Quiet hours are enforced with a seriousness that surprises people from noisier hospitality cultures. Shared apartment buildings and even some guesthouses take noise complaints genuinely seriously, not as a formality but as an expression of a broader cultural value around not imposing on others. Loud phone calls in hallways, music after 10pm, doors slammed at odd hours — these will generate a level of quiet, pointed disapproval that a Latvian neighbour is fully capable of delivering with a single look and zero words.

Staff, similarly, are not going to perform enthusiasm at you. This is a genuine cultural gap, not a service failure — Baltic reserve isn't unfriendliness, it's a default communication style that treats excessive cheerfulness as slightly suspect. Ask a direct question and you'll get a direct, honest, often genuinely helpful answer. Expect small talk and a big smile at check-in and you'll be disappointed by a country that simply doesn't operate that way.

## South Korea: Accommodation as Competitive Sport

South Korea has, with characteristic national intensity, turned the entire concept of "a place to sleep" into a wildly diverse and fiercely competitive ecosystem. Beyond conventional hotels, there's the jjimjilbang — a twenty-four-hour public bathhouse and sauna complex where, for a small fee, you can also simply sleep on a heated floor mat in a communal room, a genuinely popular option for late-night stragglers, budget travellers, and locals who've simply missed the last train home. There's the goshiwon, tiny, cell-like rooms originally built for exam-cramming students, now a real (if grim) budget housing option in expensive cities. There's the officetel, part apartment, part hotel, increasingly popular with longer-staying expats who want a kitchen without a full lease commitment. And there's the love motel, a distinct and thoroughly mainstream category offering surprisingly stylish, private, hourly-or-nightly rooms that carry none of the seediness the name implies to outsiders — Koreans of all ages use them for privacy, budget travel, and yes, romance, without particular stigma.

The amenity density even at ordinary mid-range hotels routinely outpaces far more expensive Western equivalents — in-house saunas, PC rooms, laundry facilities, elaborate breakfast spreads, all bundled in without the premium pricing those features would command elsewhere. This isn't showing off. It reflects a genuinely competitive domestic market where hotels differentiate on amenities because Korean travellers, who move around their own country constantly and enthusiastically, expect it.

Footwear etiquette carries real weight here, more strictly than in most other accommodation cultures. Shoes off well before the main living space in nearly every category of Korean accommodation, jjimjilbangs included, and getting this wrong reads as a genuine, if minor, breach rather than a quirky foreigner mistake to be waved off.

Domestic tourism, meanwhile, moves in intense, coordinated seasonal waves — Chuseok, Lunar New Year, cherry blossom season — during which accommodation books out with a speed that catches foreign travellers used to more evenly distributed demand completely off guard. Plan around the Korean calendar, not your own.

## The Verdict

Latvia offers restraint as luxury — space, quiet, and the dignity of being left alone to enjoy a genuinely beautiful, under-visited European capital. South Korea offers abundance as culture — a bewildering, brilliant range of accommodation types that turn even a budget trip into an exploration of the country's social fabric. If you want to disappear quietly into a city and be trusted to manage yourself, Riga wins outright. If you want your accommodation itself to be part of the adventure, complete with saunas, heated floors, and the occasional 2am stranger, South Korea will deliver an experience no amount of Nordic minimalism could match.

## Conclusion

Latvia and South Korea are not competing for the same traveller, and that's precisely the point — one has perfected the art of getting out of your way, the other has perfected the art of surrounding you with options you didn't know you needed. Show up in Riga expecting Seoul's amenity blitz and you'll find yourself unimpressed by a country that simply isn't playing that game. Show up in Seoul expecting Latvian quiet and you'll be kept awake, cheerfully, by a nation that has never once considered restraint a virtue worth pursuing. Pack accordingly. Adjust your expectations more than your suitcase.
