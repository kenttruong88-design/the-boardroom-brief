# Qatar Has Weaponised Air Conditioning Against An Entire Season. Serbia Just Puts On a Second Coat and Keeps Arguing at the Kafana.

🇶🇦 Qatar 🇷🇸 Serbia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There's a moment every August in Doha when you step outside at 7pm, expecting the mercy of evening, and instead get hit by a wall of heat so total it feels almost personal, like the desert has been saving it up all day specifically for you. There's an equivalent moment every January in Belgrade when the wind off the Sava river finds the one gap in your coat you thought you'd sealed, and you understand, suddenly and completely, why every Balkan grandmother owns four scarves. Qatar has responded to its climate by essentially building an indoor civilisation with occasional outdoor decoration. Serbia has responded to its climate by simply deciding the cold isn't going to stop anyone from doing anything, ever, including sitting outside a kafana in November because the ashtray's out there and so, therefore, is everyone else.

I've melted in one and gone slightly hypothermic in the other, and I maintain that Doha's approach — total climate control, engineered indoor everything — is the more honest response to genuinely hostile weather, even if it does mean the city sometimes feels like one very beautiful, very expensive mall connected by tunnels. Serbia's approach is more romantic and considerably worse for your circulation.

## Qatar: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Schedule anything outdoors for early morning or after 8pm from May through September | Underestimate the AC bill — budget for it like a second rent payment |
| Use the Msheireb underground walkways and metro's climate-controlled links whenever possible | Attempt a "quick walk" between buildings at midday in July; it is never quick and never fine |
| Carry a light layer indoors — public AC is set aggressively cold, almost as a competitive sport | Assume outdoor labour laws (midday work bans in summer) don't apply to you if you're supervising a project |

## Serbia: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Layer properly — locals treat a good coat as a genuine investment, not an afterthought | Expect central heating to be optional; radiators run on a fixed municipal schedule, not your preference |
| Embrace the kafana terrace year-round; heat lamps and blankets appear the moment temperatures drop | Assume a grey Belgrade winter means the social calendar slows down — if anything it gets busier indoors |
| Stock up on rakija and comfort food traditions — winter eating here is a genuine cultural pillar | Try to power through summer humidity without respecting the afternoon slowdown; locals pace themselves |

## Qatar: A Country That Built Its Way Out of Its Own Climate

Doha's summer isn't weather so much as a structural constraint that shapes literally everything — building design, work schedules, urban planning, and the entire rhythm of daily life. Average daytime highs regularly clear 40°C for months, humidity off the Gulf makes it feel considerably worse, and the government doesn't treat this as background trivia: outdoor labour is legally restricted during the hottest midday hours in summer specifically because heat-related deaths among outdoor workers became an undeniable, documented crisis. That's not a quirky local custom, it's a labour protection written directly in response to real harm, and it tells you everything about how seriously the heat is taken here.

The response, architecturally and socially, has been total climate engineering. Malls aren't just shopping destinations, they're the de facto public square for eight months of the year — air-conditioned, walkable, endlessly extended. The Msheireb downtown district was built with underground and covered walkways specifically so pedestrians never have to cross open heat. Even outdoor leisure gets rescheduled wholesale: the entire cultural calendar of festivals, markets, and outdoor dining shifts to November through March, when Doha's winter is, genuinely, spectacular — mid-20s, dry, blue-skied, a complete inversion of the summer misery. Locals and long-term expats alike talk about the year in two seasons only, "inside season" and "outside season," and plan literally everything — weddings, sports, construction schedules — around which one you're currently in. It's not charming exactly. It's disciplined, expensive, and it works.

## Serbia: Continental Extremes, Met With Stubborn Communal Warmth

Belgrade sits in a climate that refuses to pick a lane — properly hot, sticky continental summers giving way to genuinely cold, grey, wind-whipped winters, with the Košava wind funnelling down from the northeast making a already-cold day feel actively hostile. Unlike Doha, there's no engineering your way fully out of it; central heating in most buildings runs on a municipal schedule rather than individual preference, which means a cold snap in early October before the official heating season starts can leave you genuinely shivering in your own kitchen, learning to appreciate a good space heater the way Qataris appreciate a good AC unit.

What Serbia does instead is refuse to let the weather shrink social life. The kafana culture — small, often smoky, deeply convivial cafes and bars — doesn't pause for winter; it adapts, with outdoor terraces sprouting heat lamps, blankets, and regulars who will absolutely still be sitting outside in December because that's where the conversation, the smoking, and the rakija are. Serbian winter food culture leans hard into the practical and the fortifying — thick soups, slow-cooked meats, pickled everything from the autumn harvest — and there's a real cultural pride in enduring the cold together rather than retreating from it individually. Summer, meanwhile, brings its own extremity: Belgrade heat is humid and relentless, and the local coping mechanism is largely the same one Mediterranean cultures use — a slower afternoon pace, more time by the rivers, and an unofficial but widely observed slowdown that outsiders mistake for laziness and locals correctly understand as self-preservation.

## The Verdict

Qatar's answer to hostile weather is control: build indoors, schedule around the heat, engineer comfort at enormous cost. Serbia's answer is endurance: layer up, keep the kafana terrace open, let communal warmth do what the radiator schedule won't. I respect Doha's total commitment to the bit — there is something almost admirable about a nation that simply refused to let 45°C dictate a lesser life and built an entire underground civilisation instead. But my body, and frankly my soul, responds better to Serbia's version: cold hands, hot rakija, a terrace full of people who refuse to go inside just because it's November. Qatar wins on comfort. Serbia wins on character.

## Conclusion

Somewhere between a Doha mall at 2pm in August and a Belgrade terrace at 11pm in January, you'll realise both cultures have simply made peace with a climate that was never going to compromise for them. Qatar built its way indoors. Serbia layered up and stayed outside anyway, out of sheer communal stubbornness. Pack for both extremes, respect the local rhythm rather than fighting it, and whatever you do, don't schedule an outdoor Doha lunch in July or expect a Belgrade radiator to care about your personal preferences before the municipality says so.
