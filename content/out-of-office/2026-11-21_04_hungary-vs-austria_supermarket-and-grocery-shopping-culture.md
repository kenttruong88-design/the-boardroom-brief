# Austria Sells You Groceries Politely; Hungary Sells Them to You in a Negotiation

🇭🇺 Hungary vs 🇦🇹 Austria

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Two countries share a border, a former empire and an unreasonable fondness for pork, and yet go shopping in entirely different moods. In Austria, you put a coin in a trolley, collect a flawless tomato, pay at a till staffed by someone who clearly respects the process, and leave knowing exactly what the receipt says. In Hungary, the same errand can involve a market stallholder with two prices for an orange, a loyalty card that wants your life story, and a promotional shelf label that has gently drifted from the product it once described.

I have done my weekly shop in both capitals, and what fascinates me is not which is cheaper, though that is the question every expat asks first, but which one trusts you. Austria's supermarkets assume that you are an adult. Hungary's assume you are an opponent. Both approaches have charm, and both have a tendency to end with you reading a receipt in the car park.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇭🇺 Hungary ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check every price tag against the till receipt | Leave the shop without reading the slip |
| Compare the market hall with the discounter before buying | Assume the tourist stall has a local price |
| Shop on Saturday morning before the weekend closures bite | Plan a Sunday food run without checking opening hours |
| Learn the word for "receipt" and use it | Argue about change in English at length |

| 🇦🇹 Austria ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry a one or two euro coin for the trolley | Leave the shopping cart in the car park for someone else |
| Do the big shop before Saturday evening | Expect a supermarket to open on Sunday |
| Bag your own groceries, quickly and without ceremony | Take your time at the till when a queue has formed |
| Buy your painkillers at the pharmacy | Look for medicines on the supermarket shelf |

## 🇭🇺 Hungary

Hungarian grocery shopping rewards the vigilant. A US-born poster on the Expat.com food-shopping thread described stallholders at Budapest's Great Market Hall quoting her father 300 forints for an orange and, minutes later, 650 to someone else. Another said Hungarian law requires vendors to display the price of each product, while a third complained that shops sometimes swap a promoted item for a pricier one. One poster claims to have watched a Spar clerk overcharge, then tear up the receipt on being challenged. These are anecdotes from an old thread, not a survey, and I would not describe the national retail sector as criminal. But the pattern is not exactly reassuring.

The numbers are the other half of the story. A comparison published by Daily News Hungary in 2021, drawing on G7 research on a basket of twenty-nine products, found the Hofer/Aldi basket cost roughly 9,588 forints in Hungary and 12,404 in Vienna, a difference of about 29 per cent, with Lidl showing a gap of around 25 per cent. That sounds like a win for Hungary until you read the next line: using median wages, the basket took about 4.6 per cent of a Hungarian wage and about 1.5 per cent of an Austrian one. Cheap and affordable are two different words, and this is why the Hungarian shop-floor has the air of a quiet contest.

Closing hours add to the sport. An expat on the Budapest Sunday thread compared the Saturday-afternoon-to-Monday shutdown unfavourably with the Soviet era, while others noted that Saturdays turn into a stampede and that small sole-proprietor shops keep different hours. The practical lesson is simple. In Hungary, you do not shop casually. You shop with intent, a bag, a calculator and a very keen eye on the till display. And you may well find a Spar that is a pleasure, and a market stall that is a bargain. You just have to be looking.

The social meaning of the supermarket is worth a mention. In Hungary, the market hall remains a place where people go to see, be seen and gossip over paprika, and the neighbourhood grocer still knows your name. That is a real pleasure, and it survives in part because the big chains have never fully won. The Great Market Hall is a tourist trap upstairs and a genuine shop downstairs, and the difference between the two is the difference between an expat who is overcharged and one who is greeted by name. It takes a few weeks to learn which is which, and the education is worth the price of a few oranges.

## 🇦🇹 Austria

Austria, by comparison, shops like a country with nothing to prove. Expatica counts more than 1,500 Spar stores, over 1,100 Billa shops, about 450 Hofer discount outlets, more than 400 Nah & Frisch neighbourhood shops and another couple of hundred each of Lidl and Penny. You cannot walk four blocks in Vienna without tripping over a competent supermarket. The shelves are full, the produce is clean, the staff are quick, and the bread aisle is a small religion.

It is also, famously, regulated into submission. Supermarkets mostly close on Sundays, they do not stay open all night, and most open from about eight in the morning to eight in the evening, with a few city stores pushing on to ten or eleven. Trolleys need a coin deposit. Medicines are not sold on the shelves. If you grew up with a 24-hour mega-store, this feels like a monastic order. You learn to buy on Saturday or starve on Sunday, and in a strange way it produces a calmer city, one where an entire population is out for a walk instead of a lasagne.

What the system gives you is predictability. The price on the shelf is the price at the till. The receipt is correct. The queue moves at a Germanic pace of controlled efficiency. It is also considerably more expensive, particularly for an expat on a local salary, and the discounters become a quiet act of rebellion. But for most foreigners, the trade is perfectly fair: you pay more, and nobody tries to rob you. I used to find it dull. I now find it restful, which is what age and several Hungarian market halls will do to you.

In Austria, by contrast, the supermarket is almost a civic institution. The staff are trained, the stock is dependable and the whole experience is quietly tuned to reward regular customers. Discounters such as Hofer, which Expatica describes as Austria's largest discount grocer, have a loyal following even among people who could easily afford the more expensive chains, because a bargain is a point of national pride. You will notice Viennese pensioners comparing leaflets with the focus of a trader. It is not stinginess, it is a form of sport, and it is conducted with impeccable manners.

## The Verdict

Austria wins the shopping trip, and Hungary wins the wallet, and I hope you appreciate how annoying it is to have to say both. If you earn a Western salary and live in Budapest, you will enjoy some of the best-value groceries in the region, and the thrill of the hunt will keep you alert. If you earn a local salary, the Austrian basket is a luxury item. There is no honest comparison between the two that does not involve admitting that cheap food and honest food are not always on the same shelf.

For pure shopping culture, though, I give it to Austria. A supermarket should be dull. It should be a place where you leave your mind in the car and collect your cheese. Hungary's version asks for a degree of attention that I would rather spend on the novel in my bag. And yet I would take the Great Market Hall on a Saturday morning over any Viennese chain on earth, so my vote is not entirely rational, nor, I suspect, is yours.

## Conclusion

Do your weekly shop with the wrong expectations, and both countries will punish you. Go to Austria expecting a bargain and you will be wounded at the till. Go to Hungary expecting calm and you will end up arguing about an orange. The best strategy is to go to each country with the other country's habits: be vigilant in Budapest, relax in Vienna, buy your bread early, and always, always read the receipt. Nobody ever regretted reading the receipt. Many have regretted the Sunday without milk.
