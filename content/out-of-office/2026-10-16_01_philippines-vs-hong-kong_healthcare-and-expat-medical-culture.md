# Nobody Moves To Asia For The Healthcare, And That Is Exactly The Problem

🇵🇭 🇭🇰 Philippines & Hong Kong
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every expat has a healthcare origin story, and it is never "and then I calmly researched the local system before I needed it." It is always a 3am fever, a WhatsApp group chat, and someone's cousin who "knows a guy at the hospital." The Philippines and Hong Kong have built two wildly different monuments to this chaos. One runs on relationships, receipts, and resourcefulness. The other runs on queues, triage categories, and a government spreadsheet that will tell you, with devastating Cantonese precision, that your cataract surgery is scheduled for some time after your retirement party.

Neither system was built with you in mind, which is the first thing every relocation consultant forgets to mention. The Philippines built a safety net with PhilHealth and expects the gaps to be filled by family, cash, and a frankly heroic private hospital sector. Hong Kong built a world-class public system so good that everyone wants it and so oversubscribed that wanting it is the easy part. Getting it, if you are not dying quickly enough to count as urgent, is another matter entirely. Welcome to the two faces of Asian healthcare: one that will bankrupt you slowly with kindness, and one that will heal you eventually, assuming "eventually" still works for your calendar.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇵🇭 Philippines
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a proper international or HMO health plan before you land, not after your first hospital bill | Assume PhilHealth alone will cover you as a foreigner without a sizeable out-of-pocket top-up |
| Keep cash or a credit card ready for same-day deposits at private hospitals, even with insurance in hand | Expect to walk out of a private hospital until the cashier's office says you can |
| Ask locals which private hospital in your specific city is actually good, because "private" is not a synonym for "excellent" | Wait until a crisis to learn your nearest hospital doesn't have a working ICU |
| Use PhilHealth's accreditation list to understand what it actually covers before you rely on it | Confuse a cheap barangay health centre visit with the standard of care you'll get at a city hospital |

### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get Voluntary Health Insurance Scheme (VHIS) cover or an international plan the day your visa is approved | Rely on the public system for anything that isn't urgent and expect it to move fast |
| Use a private GP for routine complaints — it's expensive but it's quick | Show up to public A&E for a sprained ankle and expect sympathy for the five-hour wait |
| Learn your "Eligible Person" status and what it actually discounts | Assume holding a visa means you get subsidised public rates like an HKID holder |
| Build a relationship with one private clinic you trust, for continuity | Bounce between walk-in clinics hoping for a different, faster answer |

## Philippines

Here is the first thing you need to understand about Philippine healthcare: it is bifurcated with the brutal clarity of a telenovela plot twist. Public hospitals are staffed by doctors who trained hard, often abroad-bound the moment they're qualified, working in buildings that range from "fine" to "this ceiling fan predates martial law." Private hospitals, by contrast, are where the actual expat healthcare experience lives — gleaming lobbies, air conditioning that functions, specialists who studied in places with stricter malpractice law, and bills that arrive with the speed and confidence of a loan shark.

PhilHealth, the national insurance scheme, is not nothing. It is mandatory for most working foreigners, and it does genuinely subsidise accredited treatment. But "subsidise" is the operative word nobody translates clearly enough for newcomers: PhilHealth covers a slice, you cover the rest, and that rest can be substantial. Expats who try to get by on PhilHealth alone discover its limits exactly once, usually mid-crisis, which is the worst possible moment for a learning experience.

Then there's the deposit culture. The Philippines passed the so-called Anti-Hospital Deposit Law specifically because hospitals were routinely demanding upfront payment before treating emergencies — a practice brazen enough to require its own legislation. The law bans deposits for emergency and serious cases. What it does not ban, and what every expat.com forum thread on this subject eventually circles back to, is hospitals declining to discharge you until the bill is settled. You can be treated without paying first. You absolutely will not be leaving without paying after. Patients have been functionally held hostage by invoices, which is a sentence that should alarm you more than it alarms most long-term residents, who shrug and say "yes, bring cash."

The upshot: routine care here is a genuine bargain — consultations and basic diagnostics cost a fraction of Western prices, and that is precisely why medical tourism to Manila and Cebu is a real industry. But anything serious means private insurance, a financial cushion, and the humility to accept that "excellent private hospital" is a per-city, per-building judgment, not a national guarantee.

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong's healthcare system has the architecture of a city that genuinely believes in universal care and the waiting list of a city that cannot possibly deliver it to everyone who wants in. The public system, run by the Hospital Authority, is extraordinary value if you qualify for subsidised rates and extraordinarily slow if your condition isn't actively trying to kill you today. The Hospital Authority's own published figures for new, "stable" category bookings tell the story better than any expat ever could: median waits of well over a year for ophthalmology and internal medicine referrals, with the slowest 10 percent of patients in some clusters waiting close to two years for a first appointment. Urgent cases get seen within a week. Everyone else gets a lesson in patience dressed up as a booking slip.

Public A&E is its own particular theatre. On bad flu-season days, Hong Kong's own press has reported waits stretching past eighteen hours at some hospitals, with wards running above full occupancy. This is not a niche complaint; it's a recurring front-page story every winter, and the expat subreddits treat it as a seasonal certainty rather than breaking news.

So the real expat healthcare culture in Hong Kong isn't really about the public system at all — it's about opting out of it as fast as your bank balance allows. Private GPs are everywhere, efficient, and priced like the luxury good they are. A straightforward consultation can run you several hundred Hong Kong dollars before you've touched a prescription. Private hospital care for anything significant is, by multiple insurer comparisons, among the most expensive in the world outside the United States — a delivery at a private hospital can run past HK$50,000. The Voluntary Health Insurance Scheme exists to make private cover slightly less punishing and slightly more standardised, and any expat planning to stay more than a holiday should have either VHIS-compliant cover or an international plan sorted before, not after, they need it.

The quiet unfairness underneath all this: your visa does not make you an "Eligible Person" for subsidised public rates the way a Hong Kong ID holder is. Without that status, public care costs rise sharply too — so the fabled cheap public safety net is, for many expats, neither cheap nor fast.

## The Verdict

If you want cheap, warm, slightly chaotic care with a side of hope that your hospital has the right equipment: Philippines. If you want clinical excellence available instantly, as long as "instantly" is backed by a platinum insurance plan and a willingness to never see the inside of a public waiting room: Hong Kong. Neither city hands you both at once, and that is by design, not oversight.

Hong Kong wins on competence. The public system, when you can access it at subsidised rates and aren't in a hurry, is genuinely world-class — just ask anyone who's had a routine procedure handled flawlessly for pocket change. The Philippines wins on humanity. Private care there often moves faster for cash-paying foreigners than Hong Kong's public queue ever will, and the price tag for routine treatment won't make you reconsider your life choices.

But if I'm handing out the trophy for "system most likely to reward you for simply having money," it goes to Hong Kong, by a mile. Manila will nickel-and-dime you with deposits and discharge holds. Hong Kong will simply let you queue behind 150,000 other eye patients while your private-insurance-holding neighbour gets seen next week. Pick your poison. Preferably with travel insurance already active.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com Philippines forum — a recurring theme on private comprehensive insurance is that comprehensive plans for foreigners run roughly 100,000 pesos a year and often still exclude major conditions, leading posters to conclude self-insuring is more practical*

> *r/HongKong — a recurring complaint about public hospital specialist referrals is that a "stable" new case can sit on the waiting list for well over a year, sometimes close to two, before the first appointment*

> *r/Philippines and r/expats — a recurring warning is that private hospitals will treat emergencies without an upfront deposit under the law, but will not discharge a patient until the final bill is paid in full*

## Conclusion

Here's the uncomfortable truth both cities would rather you didn't notice until you've already signed the lease: healthcare quality, in either place, correlates almost perfectly with how much you're willing or able to spend to skip the system entirely. The Philippines solves this with private hospitals and a cash economy of goodwill. Hong Kong solves it with insurance premiums steep enough to make a Zurich banker wince. Neither solves it for the person who assumed "universal" or "subsidised" meant what it sounds like.

So do the unglamorous thing. Read the PhilHealth exclusions. Check your VHIS eligibility. Buy the insurance before you need it, not during the ambulance ride. Out of Office has relocated fourteen times and eaten dinner in sixty countries, and if there's one universal law of expat life it's this: the hospital bill always arrives at the worst possible moment, in a currency you weren't expecting, for a procedure nobody mentioned during orientation week.
