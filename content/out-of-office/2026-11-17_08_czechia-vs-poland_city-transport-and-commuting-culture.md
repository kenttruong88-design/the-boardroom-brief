# Prague Rides the Tram Like a Religion and Warsaw Builds the Metro Like a Grudge

🇨🇿 Czechia vs 🇵🇱 Poland

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Prague has the most photogenic commute in Europe, and its residents have been so thoroughly spoiled by it that they treat a tram ride past a medieval castle as the ordinary business of getting to a dentist. The trams rattle, the metro is clean and the system is cheap enough that owning a car in the centre feels like a hobby for masochists.

Warsaw rebuilt itself from rubble, and its transport carries the same spirit: a stubborn, practical, no-nonsense network that expands steadily and does not apologise for the concrete. Two Central European capitals, a few hundred kilometres apart, and two very different ideas about what a commute should feel like. One is a postcard. The other is a plan.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Czechia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy and validate the right ticket or pass | Ride without validating and trust your luck with inspectors |
| Learn the tram numbers and the night routes | Assume the metro runs all night |
| Offer your seat to older passengers | Sprawl across the bench with a rucksack |
| Keep an eye on pickpockets near the tourist hubs | Treat a crowded tram near the castle as a safe zone |

**Poland**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the city transport app and a loaded travel card | Rely on cash and a vending machine at midnight |
| Offer your seat without being asked | Stay glued to the phone while an older person stands |
| Expect clear signage and plan for roadworks | Assume the same route works as it did last month |
| Say "dzień dobry" to the driver where it is customary | Board in silence like a ghost |

## Czechia

Start with the headline. Expats.cz, the English-language news site for foreign residents, reported that Prague ranks among the top European cities for public transport use, and has written about the city's public transport being among the cheapest in Europe, even with a fare rise on the way. The system is dense and integrated: metro lines, trams that go almost everywhere, buses to cover the gaps and commuter trains linking the suburbs. For a foreigner used to a single dreary bus, it is a revelation.

The tram is the heart of it. Prague's trams are part of daily life, from the old red-and-cream carriages that carry tourists to the modern low-floor models that carry everyone else. They run frequently, they stop where you want to go and, in the old town, they glide through streets that make you forget you are late. The metro is fast, deep and efficient, with escalators that appear to descend toward the centre of the earth. The night trams take over once the metro closes.

Ticketing is the one place where a foreigner can get hurt. Guides such as the ExpatExchange page on Prague transport and the Flatio commuter guide explain that you must buy a ticket and validate it or use a pass that is active, because inspectors patrol, and they are not interested in your cheerful ignorance. A modest fine for a basic mistake is a rite of passage for many newcomers. Contactless payment and apps have made it easier, but it remains a system that rewards the person who reads the instructions.

Culturally, the Czech commute is quiet and orderly. People read, sleep and stare, rarely speak to strangers and almost never make eye contact. They will, however, offer a seat to an older person without being asked, and a person who does not is judged silently and permanently. It is a system that runs on shared habit, and the habit is excellent.

## Poland

Poland's cities do transport with a different temperament. Warsaw's network, which comparison pages such as GetWhereNext's Warsaw versus Prague profile treat as a major selling point for expats, combines metro, tram, bus and rail with an apparently endless programme of construction. The metro is newer than Prague's and growing, the trams are plentiful, and the whole thing is run with a sense of purpose that reflects a country investing heavily in its infrastructure.

The experience is modern and practical. Stations are clean, signs are clear, ticketing is increasingly app-based and the buses run on a dense grid that is easy to follow once you have an app. The weakness is that the city is large and spread out, so a commute from a suburb can be longer than Prague's, and roadworks have a way of reshaping your route on a Monday with no warning. Winter is brutal, and a bus stop at minus ten is a test of character.

Polish commuters have a recognisable manner. They are polite in an understated way, quick to offer a seat to an older person or a pregnant woman and quietly irritated by people who block the door. Greetings to the driver are common in some places and optional in others. The silence is similar to Prague's, though the pace is faster, and the sense of a city in motion is stronger.

Beyond Warsaw, Kraków, Wrocław and Gdańsk have their own systems, usually trams and buses, and the quality varies. A newcomer in a smaller city will find that the car is more important than in the capital, while a newcomer in Warsaw may realise within a month that they do not need one. That is the sign of a good system.

A final observation, because it matters more than the timetables. Public transport is where a city shows its attitude to strangers. In Prague and in Warsaw you will see people give up seats, help with prams on the steps and quietly point a lost tourist to the right platform, all without a word of fuss. It is a reassuring sign of a society that works at the small scale. Compare it with the big-city commutes that run on elbows and earphones, and you realise how rare it is for a rush hour to remain civilised. Both countries could improve their trams, their apps and their winter shelters. Neither needs lessons in basic decency, and that, for a woman who has commuted in a great many places, is the most underrated infrastructure of all.

## The Verdict

Czechia wins on charm, and Poland wins on ambition. If I am choosing a commute to enjoy, I choose Prague, with its trams, its cheap fares and its habit of making a Tuesday morning look like a film. If I am choosing a system to rely on for the next decade, I pay close attention to Warsaw, because the investment is real and the direction is upward.

For an expat the real difference is the character of the system. Prague is mature, beautiful and slightly complacent. Warsaw is younger, bigger, more disruptive and more modern. Both are cheap by Western European standards, both are safe, and both will let you live without a car if you choose your neighbourhood sensibly.

The one real piece of advice: buy the right ticket. In both countries the inspector has seen every excuse, and none of them is original.

## Conclusion

A commute is a country's daily argument with itself. Czechia answers with trams, tickets and a quiet confidence that the old way is the best way. Poland answers with cranes, new lines and a determined belief that tomorrow will be better than yesterday. Both are persuasive.

If you are moving from Czechia to Poland, enjoy the newer metro and brace for the roadworks. If you are going the other way, learn to validate, get a pass and surrender to the tram. Either way, take a seat, keep an eye on the window and remember: the best commute is the one that lets you read.
