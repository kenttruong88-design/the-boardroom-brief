# One Country Flirts Across a Dance Floor, the Other Flirts Across a Phone Screen in Total Silence

🇨🇴 Colombia × 🇳🇴 Norway
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

I have been asked to dance in a Medellín salsa bar by a stranger who took my hesitation as a personal challenge rather than a no, and within four minutes I was laughing too hard to remember I'd claimed I couldn't dance. I have also spent an entire Oslo dinner party watching two people who were, I later learned, definitely dating, communicate almost exclusively through a shared phone screen, silently scrolling a meme thread between them like it was foreplay.

Dating culture tells you how a country feels about pursuit, patience, and public affection. Colombia treats flirtation as a loud, physical, immediate art form conducted in full view of a room. Norway treats it as a quiet, deliberate process best conducted at a careful distance, often mediated by an app, until both parties are genuinely certain.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇨🇴 Colombia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept a dance invitation graciously, even if you can't dance well | Refuse outright; it's considered a missed social moment |
| Expect compliments (piropos) as a normal part of daily interaction | Take every flirtatious comment as a serious proposition |
| Be prepared for warmth and physical closeness early on | Assume affectionate touch always signals a serious relationship |
| Engage family early if things get serious | Keep a partner hidden from family for too long |

**🇳🇴 Norway**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect dating apps to be the primary starting point, even locally | Assume approaching someone cold in public is normal or welcome |
| Give people real space before assuming mutual interest | Push for quick physical closeness early on |
| Let silence sit comfortably during a date | Fill every pause with nervous chatter — it can read as odd |
| Respect a slower pace toward defining the relationship | Expect public displays of affection to be common |

## Colombia: Flirtation as Loud, Immediate Performance

Dating in Colombia, particularly in cities like Medellín and Cali, doesn't wait for the right moment — it assumes every moment is the right one. Salsa and reggaetón aren't just background music at social events, they're the actual mechanism through which flirtation happens; being asked to dance by a stranger is a completely normal opening move, not a bold gesture, and turning it down is treated less as a boundary and more as a small tragedy that the whole room quietly mourns for a second before moving on.

Piropos — the flirtatious compliments strangers pay each other on the street, ranging from genuinely charming to eye-rollingly bold — remain a fixture of daily life, and navigating them is something locals do instinctively: acknowledge, smile if it lands well, keep walking if it doesn't, no real offence taken either direction unless it crosses an obvious line. This comfort with open, verbal, physical expression extends into actual dating too. Compliments arrive fast and often, physical affection appears early, and the emotional temperature of a new relationship tends to run hot from the very first date, in a way that visitors from more reserved cultures sometimes mistake for a seriousness that isn't necessarily there yet.

What tempers all this heat is family. Once a relationship does become serious, Colombian family involvement kicks in with real intensity — meeting the family isn't a distant future milestone, it's an expected, fairly early step, and parental and grandparental opinion carries genuine weight in how a relationship is perceived to be going. The performance-forward flirting culture, in other words, eventually resolves into something quite traditional and family-centred, once both people have decided the dancing wasn't just dancing.

## Norway: Courtship as Careful, App-Mediated Distance

Norway offers something close to the opposite experience, and the paradox is almost funny once you notice it: a country famous for close-knit small towns and a strong sense of community somehow produces one of the most app-dependent, cautiously-paced dating cultures around. Approaching a stranger cold in a bar or on the street, the way it happens constantly in Colombia, is rare and can genuinely unsettle the person on the receiving end — not because Norwegians are unfriendly, but because the social contract here assumes strangers deserve their space unless a clear, safe, mutually-understood channel (an app, a shared friend, a structured event) has already established interest first.

Once a match or connection is made, the pace tends to be unhurried and low-pressure. Silence during a date isn't something to panic-fill with conversation — comfortable quiet is read as a good sign, evidence that two people don't need constant performance to enjoy each other's company, rather than evidence that something's wrong. Physical affection escalates slowly and deliberately; public displays of it remain modest even well into a relationship, and rushing this timeline is more likely to read as overeager than romantic.

This same caution extends to defining the relationship. Norwegians are famously comfortable letting a "seeing each other" phase run for a long stretch without formal labels, not out of ambiguity for its own sake but because the culture generally distrusts anything that moves faster than genuine certainty allows. It produces relationships that, by the time they are actually named, tend to be built on real, tested compatibility rather than early-stage chemistry — slower to start, but arguably sturdier once they do.

## The Verdict

Colombia makes courtship a loud, physical, immediate performance, one that assumes boldness is charming and hesitation is the real risk. Norway makes courtship a slow, careful negotiation of space and certainty, one that assumes patience is respect and rushing is the real risk. Both produce genuine, lasting relationships — they've just built completely different tolerances for how fast interest is allowed to show itself in public.

Given my own temperament, I'd rather be pulled onto a dance floor by a confident stranger than spend a full dinner silently trading memes and calling it romance — but I understand, grudgingly, why the Norwegian approach produces relationships that seem to actually know what they're getting into by the time they name themselves. Colombia sells you the spark. Norway makes you earn it.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *r/Colombia — paraphrased: a commenter said a visiting friend was baffled to be complimented by three separate strangers within a single block, and even more baffled that nobody involved treated it as anything unusual.*

> *Internations Oslo — paraphrased: an expat described matching with someone on an app who lived two streets away and had, it turned out, been a neighbour for over a year without either of them ever having spoken in person first.*

> *Quora, "Why is dating in Norway so app-dependent despite low population density?" — paraphrased: a respondent explained that Norwegian social norms discourage approaching strangers directly, so apps function as the culturally acceptable substitute for that opening move.*

## Conclusion

Colombia and Norway have built two entirely different machines for the same basic human pursuit, and neither would recognise the other's version as recognisably "dating." Colombia says: show your interest loudly, immediately, and let the room see it. Norway says: show almost nothing until you're sure, and let an app carry the risk of that first move instead of your nerve. Which one works better probably depends on whether you'd rather be swept off your feet on a dance floor or quietly, patiently, correctly chosen. I know which one makes for a better story afterward.
