# One Country Flirts Over Wine at Midnight, the Other Asks Your Intentions by Dinner Two

🇵🇹 Portugal vs 🇮🇩 Indonesia

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

In Lisbon, a first date can start at 9pm and drift, unplanned and unhurried, into a second bar, a third, and eventually a rooftop conversation about nothing in particular until 2am, with no one checking a watch or an agenda. In Jakarta, I once had a second date interrupted by a phone call from the woman's mother, who wanted to know, politely but directly, what my intentions were. Both moments taught me something true about how these countries approach romance: one treats it as a leisurely art form, the other treats it as a family project with a timeline.

Portugal courts you slowly and informally, with wine and long nights doing most of the work. Indonesia, particularly outside the most cosmopolitan pockets of Jakarta, moves faster toward seriousness, and family is rarely far from the conversation, even on date two.

## Do's & Don'ts

### 🇵🇹 Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect dates to start late and run long | Rush toward defining the relationship early |
| Enjoy the slow build of bars, dinner, and conversation | Be surprised by casual affection in public |
| Let the evening unfold without a rigid plan | Assume punctuality is a priority — it rarely is |
| Be genuinely present in conversation — small talk is an art | Take offense at playful, flirtatious teasing |

### 🇮🇩 Indonesia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect family to become relevant fairly early | Assume Western-style casual dating norms apply everywhere |
| Be respectful and a little formal in early stages | Show excessive public affection, especially outside big cities |
| Understand religion and tradition may shape the pace | Rush physical intimacy — it can read as disrespectful |
| Take introductions to family seriously when offered | Dismiss the seriousness with which dating is approached |

## Portugal: Romance as a Long, Unhurried Evening

Portuguese dating culture rewards patience and punishes anyone in a hurry. A first date rarely has a defined endpoint — dinner slides into drinks, drinks slide into a walk along the river, and somewhere in the fourth hour you realise nobody's checked their phone once. This isn't laziness, it's a genuine cultural belief that romance can't be rushed into a neat sixty-minute coffee slot the way it might be elsewhere.

Flirtation here is playful, verbal, and constant — the Portuguese have turned teasing into an art form, and being on the receiving end of it is generally a compliment, not an insult, however it might land on unprepared ears. Public affection is normal and largely unremarkable; holding hands, a kiss on a park bench, none of it draws a second glance in Lisbon the way it might elsewhere.

What surprises newcomers is how relaxed the pacing is even once things get serious. There's no rush to define the relationship, introduce partners to family, or hit any particular milestone by a particular date. Portuguese relationships tend to develop organically, over months of long dinners and later nights, rather than through the anxious box-ticking exercise dating can become in more schedule-obsessed cultures.

The dating app scene exists, of course, layered awkwardly over this more traditional slow-burn approach, and the mismatch between "let's grab a quick coffee" app culture and the Portuguese instinct toward long, unstructured evenings produces its own particular brand of confusion for newcomers trying to read the signals correctly.

## Indonesia: Family Enters the Chat Almost Immediately

Indonesian dating culture, particularly outside Jakarta's most Westernised social circles, treats romance as something inherently connected to family and, often, faith. This isn't a formality tacked on later — it's frequently present from the earliest stages, with questions about intentions, background, and long-term seriousness arriving far sooner than many foreigners expect or feel prepared for.

Public affection is far more restrained here than in Portugal, particularly outside cosmopolitan Jakarta and Bali's more international enclaves — holding hands might draw little attention, but anything more is generally kept private, out of respect for religious and social norms that remain influential across much of the country. This isn't repression, it's a different cultural logic: intimacy is treated as something earned through commitment, not displayed as a performance for public consumption.

Family involvement, once it begins, tends to escalate quickly and sincerely. Meeting parents isn't a milestone reserved for a serious, long-term relationship the way it might be in Portugal — it can happen surprisingly early, and it's taken with total seriousness on both sides. Being asked your intentions by a partner's mother on the second date isn't overbearing by local standards, it's simply due diligence, and treating it dismissively is one of the fastest ways to end things.

What foreigners often misjudge is the pace mismatch: casual, low-stakes dating as understood in much of the West simply doesn't map cleanly onto Indonesian social expectations. Approach it with genuine respect and patience for how seriously it's taken, and you'll find a warmth and sincerity that Portugal's playful pace rarely matches.

## The Verdict

Portugal offers romance as leisure — unhurried, playful, built on long evenings that ask nothing of you beyond presence. Indonesia offers romance as commitment — sincere, family-anchored, built on questions that demand real answers sooner than you'd like.

If you want to date without pressure, without milestones, without anyone's mother on the phone by the second outing, Lisbon will let you drift pleasantly for as long as you want. If you want dating that means something immediately, that folds you into a family structure with real weight behind it, Jakarta will not let you coast. Suki's verdict: Portugal wins for the pure pleasure of the evening, Indonesia wins for anyone who wants romance to actually mean something from the start — and both have, at various points, made a fool of me in entirely different ways.

## Conclusion

Portugal will teach you that romance doesn't need a destination to be worthwhile. Indonesia will teach you that romance, taken seriously from the start, can be its own kind of gift, even when it arrives with a family interview attached. Move to Lisbon and let the evenings run long without asking where they're headed. Move to Jakarta and be ready to answer, sincerely, when someone finally asks. Just don't confuse the two approaches — nothing ages a foreigner faster than misreading which country they're in.
