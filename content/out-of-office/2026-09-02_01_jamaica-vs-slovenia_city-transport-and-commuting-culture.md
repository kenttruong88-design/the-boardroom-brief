# A Bus That Leaves When It's Full Beats a Train That Leaves When It Feels Like It

🇯🇲 Jamaica vs 🇸🇮 Slovenia — city transport and commuting culture

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Kingston will get you there eventually, on its own schedule, negotiated in real time between the driver, the traffic, and whichever deity is responsible for potholes. Ljubljana will get you there in eleven minutes, on a bicycle, without breaking a sweat, because someone in city hall decided in 2007 that cars were an embarrassing personal failing and built an entire capital around proving it. Both systems work. Only one of them will make you late for your own wedding.

I've commuted on six continents and I stand by this: nothing humbles you faster than a Jamaican route taxi deciding your stop wasn't really a stop, or a Slovenian cyclist gliding past you at 8am looking so smug about their carbon footprint you want to key their BicikeLJ dock. Neither country will apologise. Why would they — they've both built commuting cultures that work precisely because they refuse to be anything other than exactly what they are.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇯🇲 Jamaica**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry small bills — route taxis don't do change diplomacy | Expect a fixed timetable from a coaster bus; it leaves when it's full, not when the sign says |
| Learn to flag a taxi like you mean it, arm out, no hesitation | Sit in the front seat uninvited — that's often reserved, informally, ruthlessly |
| Accept that "just now" means anywhere from five minutes to never | Assume JUTC and route taxis are interchangeable — locals have very strong, very loud opinions on which is which |

**🇸🇮 Slovenia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a BicikeLJ subscription within your first week — it's basically a Ljubljana citizenship test | Cycle the wrong way down a one-way street without checking — some are contraflow-legal, most aren't |
| Validate your Urbana card properly; inspectors are unbothered by your confusion | Block Slovenska cesta's shared space gawking at how pleasant it is — locals are trying to get to work |
| Walk or cycle Ljubljana's compact centre rather than bother with a car | Bring a car into the old town expecting anywhere convenient to park it |

## Jamaica: Organised Chaos, Beautifully Executed

Here is what nobody tells you before you land in Kingston: there is no such thing as "the bus system." There are several overlapping systems, all pretending to be one system, and your job as a commuter is to divine which one currently applies to your specific journey. The Jamaica Urban Transit Company runs upwards of 70 routes through Kingston, Portmore, and Spanish Town, radiating out from the Half Way Tree Transport Centre like spokes on a wheel that occasionally catches fire. That's your official option — cheap, fixed-route, prone to overcrowding at peak hours because everyone knows it's the cheapest option and acts accordingly.

Then there's the route taxi, Jamaica's actual backbone, and the thing that separates people who've merely visited Jamaica from people who've lived there. Route taxis are private cars running fixed but informal routes, red PPV or white P plates, picking up and dropping off multiple strangers who did not consent to carpool but are now deeply, silently bonded by the experience. You don't wait at a marked stop so much as perform a specific, culturally coded flag-down gesture and hope the driver interprets your trajectory correctly. Coaster buses — the minibuses that reach every village the JUTC doesn't bother with — take this principle further: they leave when full, not when scheduled, which sounds maddening until you realise it means you're never waiting for an empty bus to justify its own existence.

This July, JUTC rolled out new routes 14 and 14B linking Greater Portmore to Portmore Mall at half the route-taxi fare, and the taxi drivers are, understandably, livid — their entire livelihood undercut by a state bus with the audacity to be affordable. It's a genuinely interesting fight, and it tells you everything about how seriously Jamaicans take their transport turf wars: this is not a system anyone treats casually, least of all the people whose income depends on you choosing them over the alternative.

The unglamorous truth is that Kingston traffic is genuinely brutal, the infrastructure genuinely underfunded, and the whole apparatus runs on something closer to collective improvisation than planning. And yet it moves an entire capital every single day without a fraction of the budget Ljubljana spent on its bike lanes. Respect where it's due.

## Slovenia: A City That Actually Listened to Its Own Traffic Engineers

Ljubljana, meanwhile, is what happens when a small European capital decides to be smug about infrastructure and then actually earns it. In 2007 the mayor pedestrianised the historic centre and the local taxi lobby reacted the way lobbies always do — badly, loudly, wrongly. Nearly two decades later, Slovenska cesta, a street that didn't even have a bike lane before 2013, is now dubbed "Ljubljana's living room," carbon emissions in the area are down 70%, noise is down six decibels, and 95% of residents polled think the changes were a good idea. When was the last time 95% of any population agreed on anything involving urban planning?

The mechanics are almost boringly sound: over 450 kilometres of cycling routes, more than 80 BicikeLJ bike-share stations plus dedicated e-bike docks, contraflow cycling permitted on 123 one-way streets, and a bike-share membership rate — 17% of residents hold an annual subscription — that would make most Western capitals weep with envy. The trains and buses actually connect to the cycling network rather than pretending it doesn't exist, so you get the flexibility of a bike for the first and last kilometre stitched to something faster for the middle stretch. It's not romantic. It's just extremely, almost irritatingly, well designed.

What Ljubljana lacks is Kingston's texture — the negotiation, the personality, the sense that the system is alive and occasionally feral. Slovenian commuting is efficient to the point of sterility. You will not bond with a stranger on a BicikeLJ. You will not have an opinion about your cyclist the way Jamaicans have opinions about their taxi driver. You will simply arrive, on time, mildly smug, having interacted with no one.

## The Verdict

If you want a commute that respects your calendar, take Ljubljana — it will deposit you at your desk on time, guilt-free, carbon-light, and utterly without incident. If you want a commute that makes you feel something, take Kingston, where a stranger's Toyota Corolla becomes a temporary community and lateness is a shared, forgivable condition rather than a personal failing. Slovenia wins on competence. Jamaica wins on soul. I'd rather be ten minutes late with a good story than on time with nothing to say about it — but I concede that's an easy position to hold when I'm not the one running the meeting I've just missed.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Quora — "What is it like commuting in Kingston, Jamaica without a car?" — paraphrased: a longtime resident describes route taxis as "faster than any app-based rideshare could ever be, because the driver already knows every shortcut and every pothole by heart."*

> *Expat.com Kingston forum — paraphrased: a new arrival admits it took three weeks before they could flag a taxi without visibly hesitating, and that the hesitation itself marked them as a tourist faster than their accent did.*

> *Digital Nomad Exchange, Spanish Town guide — paraphrased: a remote worker notes that Ljubljana's bike infrastructure spoiled them so thoroughly that returning to any car-centric city now feels like a personal insult.*

## Conclusion

There's a version of this comparison where I tell you one system is objectively better, hand out a gold star, and move on. I won't, because that would be dishonest — and because both cities have, in their own completely opposite ways, solved the same problem: how do you move a lot of people who don't want to be moved. Ljubljana solved it with engineering. Kingston solved it with relationships. If you're the sort of expat who needs a fixed timetable to feel safe, go to Slovenia and thank the mayor. If you're the sort who thrives on a bit of chaos and doesn't mind negotiating your own commute in real time, Jamaica will teach you more about patience — and about yourself — than any bike lane ever could. Either way, stop complaining about your Tube delay. You have no idea how good you have it.
