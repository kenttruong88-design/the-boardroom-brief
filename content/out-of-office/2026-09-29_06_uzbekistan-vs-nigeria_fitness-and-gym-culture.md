# Tashkent Trains Like It's 1978 and Wins Olympic Medals. Lagos Trains Like It's a Party and Posts About It.

🇺🇿 Uzbekistan vs 🇳🇬 Nigeria

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Modern fitness culture has been largely colonised by a single aesthetic: the matte-black boutique studio with a DJ, a juice bar, a membership fee pegged to a car lease and a changing room with better lighting than most restaurants. It has spread from Los Angeles to London to Singapore with the relentless efficiency of a fast-food franchise. Uzbekistan and Nigeria have both been visited by this aesthetic. Neither has fully surrendered to it, and both are more interesting for it.

Uzbekistan's fitness culture is Soviet-era iron, courtyard pull-up bars, and the ancient discipline of kurash wrestling — a country quietly producing world-class wrestlers and boxers out of gyms that look as though they haven't been redecorated since Brezhnev. Nigeria's is loud, communal and fiercely social: Saturday morning jogging clubs of a hundred people chanting in the streets of Lagos, aerobics on the beach, and gyms where the playlist is Afrobeats at a volume that makes the treadmill vibrate. One country treats fitness as discipline. The other treats it as a gathering. I know which I'd rather attend, and which would make me stronger.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇺🇿 Uzbekistan**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the outdoor pull-up bars and parallel bars in mahalla courtyards and parks | Expect boutique-style amenities in neighbourhood gyms — towels and air-con may be optional |
| Ask about women-only gym hours or facilities if you want them; they're common | Wear very revealing gym clothing outside modern city-centre clubs |
| Watch a kurash or wrestling session — respect for the sport is enormous | Underestimate the quiet older man on the bench press. He was probably a champion |
| Train early in summer; Tashkent heat can top 40°C | Skip the post-workout tea. Hospitality applies even at the gym |

**🇳🇬 Nigeria**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a Saturday morning jogging or "keep-fit" club — it's the social event of the week | Plan to drive to a gym across Lagos at rush hour. Traffic will eat your workout |
| Greet everyone properly, especially elders, even in the gym | Expect a quiet workout. The music is part of the experience |
| Try the Lekki–Ikoyi Link Bridge for early morning runs and walks | Train outdoors in midday heat and humidity without water |
| Check generator and power arrangements before signing up for a gym | Assume the gym is just for exercise — it's also for networking and flirting |

## Uzbekistan: Iron, Discipline and the Ghost of the Soviet Sports Machine

Uzbekistan inherited one of the most serious sports infrastructures in the world — the Soviet one — and it shows. Walk into a neighbourhood gym in Tashkent or Samarkand and you'll find heavy iron, worn rubber floors, and men of all ages training with a quiet, unfussy seriousness. There are no motivational slogans painted on the walls. The motivation is implied: you are here to get stronger, and if you're not, why are you here?

Combat sports are the national pulse. Kurash, an ancient Central Asian form of upright wrestling, is both a sport and a source of identity, and Uzbekistan has become a formidable force in boxing, wrestling and judo on the international stage. Boys are sent to wrestling clubs the way British boys are sent to football. The coaches are stern, the gyms spartan, the results — at Olympic level — impressive.

Outside, the mahalla (neighbourhood community) tends to have pull-up bars, parallel bars and simple outdoor exercise equipment, and in the cooler mornings and evenings you'll see men doing sets of pull-ups before breakfast, teenagers practising handstands, and the occasional grandfather doing rigorous calisthenics with a cigarette tucked behind his ear. Fitness is a public, unglamorous, everyday thing.

For women, the picture is more mixed. Modern chains in central Tashkent now offer group classes, yoga and women-only spaces, and fitness for women is growing fast, particularly among younger urban professionals. But more conservative norms still shape the gym floor outside big city centres, and many women prefer segregated hours. The expat who expects a Californian studio will find a few. The expat who wants the real thing will find a room full of iron and a country quietly producing champions in it.

## Nigeria: Fitness as a Festival

In Lagos, working out is less of an individual pursuit than a communal celebration. On Saturday mornings, jogging clubs take to the streets in matching T-shirts, sometimes a hundred strong, singing call-and-response chants, stopping for group stretches and ending with a communal breakfast that undoes a meaningful portion of the effort. It's part fitness, part church, part networking event, and fully joyful.

The fitness scene has boomed among Nigeria's young urban middle class, especially in Lagos and Abuja. Upmarket gyms in Victoria Island, Ikoyi and Lekki offer modern equipment, personal trainers and memberships priced for people who also own generators. Beach bootcamps and dance-fitness classes draw crowds. Early morning, the Lekki–Ikoyi Link Bridge fills with runners, walkers and small groups doing burpees against the skyline. And Instagram, of course, is watching.

Obstacles are real. Lagos traffic is legendary, and a gym forty minutes away on paper can be two hours away in practice, which is why many people choose facilities within walking distance of home or office. Power outages mean gyms rely on generators, and air-conditioning can be a luxury. Heat and humidity are relentless. None of this has dented the enthusiasm; it has simply shaped it — early morning sessions, neighbourhood clubs, outdoor workouts in the cooler hours.

What stands out most is the social energy. Nigerian gym culture is loud, friendly and extroverted. Strangers will encourage you mid-set. Trainers will shout, laugh and roast you. Music is non-negotiable. It feels less like a sterile health transaction and more like a party at which, somewhere between the Afrobeats and the gossip, you also happen to deadlift.

## The Verdict

Nigeria wins, narrowly, and only because I've come to believe the best exercise is the one you'll keep turning up for. The Saturday jogging club, the singing, the stretching on a closed road with a hundred strangers who become friends — that's how you build a habit that lasts a lifetime rather than a January.

But I'll be honest: if you actually want results, go to Uzbekistan. There is something bracing about a country where fitness hasn't been turned into a lifestyle brand — where the gym is a room full of weights and the only accessory is effort. Nobody's posting their split squats. Nobody's selling you a protein smoothie with activated charcoal. They're simply training, the way their parents did, and producing world champions while the rest of us argue about which app to download.

Lagos will make you love exercise. Tashkent will make you good at it.

## Conclusion

Fitness culture is one of those things the globalised world has tried very hard to homogenise, and it hasn't quite managed. Uzbekistan still trains like strength is a virtue in itself, without much interest in how it looks on camera. Nigeria trains like the point of being fit is to be together, loudly, with a playlist and a post-run breakfast.

Both would baffle the average London boutique-studio customer, paying a small fortune to pedal in the dark while an instructor tells them they're "on a journey". Neither Tashkent's wrestlers nor Lagos's jogging clubs are on a journey. They're just turning up, week after week, because it's part of life.

That's the lesson. Stop buying fitness. Go and do some. Ideally in a courtyard with pull-up bars, or on a bridge at dawn surrounded by singing strangers.
