# In Abidjan, Your Banker Is Basically Family. In Toronto, Your Banker Is a PDF.

🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

There is a very specific kind of despair that sets in when you realise your Canadian bank considers a two-week wait for a "simple" account verification to be excellent service, and an equally specific kind of despair that sets in when your Ivorian bank clerk tells you the loan you're after will require your uncle's cousin's signature, obtained in person, preferably over lunch. Both systems are bureaucratic. Only one of them pretends it isn't.

Canada has built banking into something so sanitised, so thoroughly digitised and call-centre-optimised, that the actual humans involved have been engineered almost entirely out of the process — you deal with an app, a hold policy, and occasionally a chatbot that insists it understands your problem. Côte d'Ivoire has done the opposite: banking remains stubbornly, unapologetically social, running on networks of tontines, personal relationships, and a document trail that somehow always requires one more stamp than the list suggested. One system is frictionless and lonely. The other is frustrating and warm. Pick your poison.

## Côte d'Ivoire: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a personal relationship with your bank branch staff — it genuinely speeds up every future request | Don't expect a loan or major account change to be processed without an in-person visit, often more than one |
| Join or at least understand the local tontine (rotating savings group) system; it underwrites a huge amount of real economic life | Don't assume a digital-only relationship with your bank will cover you for anything beyond basic transactions |
| Carry certified copies of every document twice — the original request list is rarely the final one | Don't be shocked when "the system is down" becomes a valid, repeated excuse for a multi-day delay |
| Budget extra time, always, for anything involving a government stamp or an administrative signature | Don't expect weekend or evening banking hours; plan errands for mid-week, mid-morning |

## Canada: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Set up online and mobile banking immediately — almost nothing meaningful happens at a physical branch anymore | Don't expect a human to pick up the phone quickly; hold times for "support" can eat your whole lunch break |
| Get a credit history started early, even with a secured card — it quietly controls your access to almost everything later | Don't assume cash deposits or cheques clear instantly; holds of several business days are standard, not a glitch |
| Keep every reference number from every call; you will need it again when the first representative's notes mysteriously vanish | Don't ignore a "we need additional verification" email; it can freeze an account with almost no warning |
| Use direct deposit and pre-authorised payments wherever possible — the whole system is quietly built around automation, not conversation | Don't expect any flexibility or discretion from a bank employee; policy is policy, however small the exception requested |

### Côte d'Ivoire: Bureaucracy as a Social Network

Banking in Abidjan is not something you outsource to an app and forget about; it is something you actively maintain, the way you'd maintain a friendship that occasionally needs money. The relationship with your branch matters enormously — walk in regularly, know the staff by name, and a surprising number of "impossible" requests become merely slow instead of outright refused. Walk in as a stranger demanding efficiency, and you will discover exactly how creative a bureaucracy can get when it wants to make a point about respect.

Running alongside, and often more powerful than, the formal banking system are tontines — informal rotating savings associations where a group contributes a fixed amount regularly and takes turns receiving the pooled sum. For a huge swath of Ivorian economic life, this isn't a quaint cultural footnote, it's the actual financing mechanism for school fees, small businesses, and emergencies, built on trust and social accountability rather than credit scores and interest rates. Expats who dismiss it as informal, or beneath a "real" financial strategy, miss that it often works faster and more reliably than the formal alternative, precisely because the social cost of defaulting is higher than any late fee a bank could charge.

Documentation, meanwhile, follows its own logic: the list of required papers for any administrative request is less a checklist than an opening negotiation, and it is entirely normal to be told, after submitting everything originally requested, that one more certified copy or one more signature is now required. "The system is down" is a sentence you will hear with a frequency that eventually stops sounding like an excuse and starts sounding like weather — an environmental condition you plan around rather than argue with.

### Canada: Bureaucracy as an Automated Service You're Not Allowed to Question

Canadian banking has engineered itself into something almost aggressively impersonal, in a way that reads as either refreshingly efficient or coldly alienating depending entirely on whether your problem happens to fit inside the system's pre-approved categories. Everything meaningful routes through an app or a web portal; the physical branch, once the centre of the relationship, now exists mostly for the handful of tasks — notarising a document, a large cash transaction — that the system hasn't yet figured out how to digitise away.

The catch is that when something doesn't fit the standard flow, you discover just how little human discretion is left in the machine. A "hold" on a cheque deposit, an automated fraud flag on a legitimate transaction, a request for "additional verification" that arrives by email and can freeze access with no phone call first — these aren't personal decisions anyone made about you specifically, they're rules executed by a system that doesn't particularly care whether you understand them. Call support about any of it and you'll be met with hold music, a representative reading from a script, and occasionally the surreal experience of being told your case has "no notes" despite the three previous calls you made about exactly this.

Credit, too, is a quiet gatekeeper behind nearly everything — an apartment lease, a phone plan, a car loan all discreetly check a credit history that most newcomers don't yet have, meaning the sensible move is to start building one immediately, often with a low-limit secured card, specifically so some invisible algorithm decides you're trustworthy enough for things that have nothing to do with banking at all.

## The Verdict

Côte d'Ivoire's system is slower on paper and faster in practice once you're actually inside the relationship; Canada's system is faster on paper and maddening the moment you fall outside its predicted categories. One rewards patience and personal investment. The other rewards never needing help in the first place.

Honestly, I'd rather negotiate with a human who remembers my name, even if it costs me an extra stamp and a long lunch, than spend forty-five minutes on hold with a Canadian call centre explaining, for the fourth time, why a legitimate transaction got flagged as suspicious. Côte d'Ivoire's bureaucracy is exhausting, but it's exhausting in a way that acknowledges you exist. Canada's is exhausting in a way that occasionally forgets you do.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *expat.com forums, Ivory Coast — paraphrased: multiple posters describe being told, after submitting a full document list for a bank loan, that one additional certified copy was now required — a pattern frequent enough that several warn newcomers to expect at least one extra round of paperwork as standard.*

> *expat.com forums (French), Côte d'Ivoire — paraphrased: several threads on bank loans note that having a personal contact inside the branch dramatically changed how quickly a request moved, with posters explicitly recommending relationship-building over purely transactional visits.*

> *Internations Toronto community notes — paraphrased: newcomers commonly report frustration at how long call-centre hold times run for even simple account issues, with a few describing having to visit a physical branch in person specifically because phone support felt like a dead end.*

## Conclusion

What both systems reveal, in their own stubborn way, is what each country has decided bureaucracy is actually for. Côte d'Ivoire's version assumes institutions run on relationships because, historically, the institutions themselves have not always been reliable enough to trust blindly — so people built redundancy into each other instead. Canada's version assumes institutions can be trusted precisely because they're standardised, automated, and theoretically applied the same way to everyone, which is reassuring right up until your specific problem doesn't match the template.

I've made peace with needing both skill sets now: the patience to charm an Abidjan bank clerk over tea, and the stubbornness to stay on hold with a Toronto call centre until a human, any human, finally answers. Only one of those skills has ever gotten me a better interest rate. It wasn't the hold music.
