# One Nation Closes for the Season, the Other Argues About a Public Holiday for Decades First

🇫🇮 Finland vs 🇳🇿 New Zealand
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Finland doesn't do a festival calendar so much as a single, catastrophic exhale every June, after which the entire country disappears to a lake for two weeks and stops answering emails. New Zealand, by contrast, spent the better part of fifty years relitigating whether it was allowed to have a public holiday for its own indigenous new year. Both approaches produce a day off. Only one produces a nation that genuinely believes it has earned it.

I've spent Midsummer in a sauna in Savonlinna being swatted with birch branches by a stranger named Pekka, and I've spent Waitangi Day in Paihia watching a debate about colonial history unfold with more civility than most parliaments manage on a Tuesday. Neither country treats its holidays as decoration. Both treat them as identity, which is precisely why getting them wrong as an outsider is so easy, and so mortifying.

## Do's & Don'ts: Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept a Juhannus (Midsummer) cottage invite without hesitation — it's a genuine honour | Schedule a meeting, delivery, or anything requiring a human being, for the last week of June |
| Bring your own alcohol to any summer gathering, unasked | Assume Independence Day (Dec 6) is a party — it's candle-lit and solemn |
| Learn the difference between Juhannus and Vappu before you confuse them out loud | Expect shops, doctors, or your landlord to function during Midsummer week |

## Do's & Don'ts: New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn what Matariki actually commemorates before you show up to an event | Call Waitangi Day "just the founding day thing" — it's contested and it matters |
| Expect "Mondayisation" — a holiday landing on a weekend gets moved, so check the actual date | Assume Boxing Day sales culture translates to reverence for the day itself |
| Show up to a Waitangi Day event and actually listen | Treat Anniversary Day (regional!) as a national holiday — it isn't, and locals will correct you |

## Finland: The Nation That Simply Leaves

Here is what nobody tells you about Finnish public holidays: there aren't that many of them, officially, but the ones that matter shut the country down more completely than any statute could achieve on paper. Vappu (May Day) is the loud one — students in boiler suits, sparkling wine at breakfast, a genuinely deranged national commitment to day-drinking in a park. But Juhannus, Midsummer, is the one that actually reveals the Finnish soul, and the Finnish soul, it turns out, wants to be alone at a lake.

Helsinki empties. Not metaphorically — actually empties. Restaurants close. Pharmacies post handwritten signs. An entire population of 5.6 million people relocates to mökki (summer cottages) that have been in the family since before Finland was a country, and proceeds to have the most private, most unphotographed, most deeply felt celebration of the year: a bonfire, a sauna, herring, and a silence so complete you can hear the lake breathing. If you're invited, understand you've been let into something. If you're not, understand that's not personal — this is a holiday designed around the exact people you already know, and expanding the guest list defeats the purpose.

The tension for outsiders is that Finland's festival calendar looks sparse on paper and is actually maximalist in practice — it just maximalizes in the direction of absence rather than spectacle. Try to book anything commercial in the last week of June and you'll learn this the hard way, standing outside a shuttered pharmacy while a country of famously reserved people has, somewhere out of sight, the best night of its year.

## New Zealand: The Country Still Arguing About What the Holiday Means

New Zealand's public holidays are a masterclass in a country actively working out its own identity in real time, and doing it, mostly, out loud. Waitangi Day commemorates the 1840 treaty between the British Crown and Māori chiefs, and unlike most countries' national days, it isn't a fireworks-and-forget affair — it's a day where genuine, ongoing disagreement about sovereignty, land, and history gets aired at Waitangi itself, on television, in workplaces. Politicians get heckled. That's not a bug. Treating Waitangi Day as a beach day, which plenty of tourists do, is the single fastest way to mark yourself as someone who hasn't done five minutes of homework.

Then there's Matariki — the Māori new year, marked by the rising of the Pleiades star cluster, made a permanent public holiday only in 2022 after decades of advocacy. It is, refreshingly, a joyful one: remembrance of the dead, gratitude for the present, hope for the year ahead, celebrated with kai (food), storytelling, and early mornings watching the stars. New Zealand got there slower than it should have, but it got there with more sincerity than most nations manage for holidays they've had for centuries.

The practical trap is "Mondayisation" — when a statutory holiday falls on a weekend, it shifts to the following Monday, which means the date on the calendar and the date everything actually closes can differ. Combine that with regional Anniversary Days that only apply to specific provinces, and you have a country where "is today a holiday" is a question locals answer with visible hedging.

## The Verdict

Finland wins on sincerity of execution — nobody performs Midsummer for tourists, which is exactly why it's worth witnessing if you're lucky enough to be asked. New Zealand wins on moral seriousness — a country willing to keep renegotiating what its holidays mean, in public, is doing something braver than a country that simply closes and goes quiet.

If you want a holiday that asks nothing of you except to disappear gracefully, Finland has you covered. If you want a holiday that asks you to actually think, New Zealand will oblige, whether you're ready or not. I'd rather be handed a herring in silence than a microphone at Waitangi, but that's a comment on my constitution, not on either country's calendar.

## Conclusion

Finland's holidays are a closed door with a very good party behind it; New Zealand's are an open forum with genuine stakes. Neither wants your performative enthusiasm — Finland wants your absence, New Zealand wants your attention. Get the register wrong in either direction and you'll be the outsider who either barged into a family lake house or turned a solemn commemoration into a photo op.

The through-line, if you're paying attention, is that both nations have quietly rejected the idea that public holidays exist to be convenient. Finland's are inconvenient by design — that's the whole point. New Zealand's are, increasingly, uncomfortable by design, because comfort was never really the goal. Go to the lake. Go to Waitangi. Just don't confuse the two, and don't, whatever you do, ask a Finn to explain Vappu while they're still recovering from it.
