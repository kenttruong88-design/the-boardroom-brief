# One Country Makes You Wait for an Appointment. The Other Makes You Wait for a Stamp on the Appointment.

🇪🇸 Spain · 🇩🇿 Algeria
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Spain's bureaucracy has achieved a very specific kind of infamy: not incompetence exactly, just an institutional commitment to appointment systems so backlogged that securing a slot to get your foreigner ID number can take longer than most people's entire visa validity period. Algeria's bureaucracy operates on an older, more analogue logic entirely — multiple physical stamps, multiple offices, multiple photocopies of documents you already submitted once, and an entire cottage industry of intermediaries who exist purely to help you survive the process. One country has digitized its bureaucracy just enough to make the waiting technically trackable. The other never pretended digitization would save you.

I have refreshed a Spanish appointment booking website at 8am sharp for three consecutive weeks like it was concert tickets, and I have watched an Algiers bank clerk stamp the same document four separate times across two different desks. Only one of these felt like it was, on some level, designed to test my will to live.

## Do's & Don'ts: Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book your NIE (foreigner ID number) appointment the moment you know you're moving — slots vanish fast | Assume any Spanish bank branch operates the same way; policies vary noticeably even within the same chain |
| Use a gestor (paid bureaucracy specialist) if your situation is remotely complex — it's normal and worth it | Expect English-language service outside major international bank branches in big cities |
| Keep physical photocopies of every document; digital copies are frequently not accepted at the counter | Show up without an appointment expecting a walk-in slot; most offices simply will not see you |

## Do's & Don'ts: Algeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring more photocopies of every document than you think you'll need — offices rarely photocopy for you | Expect a single-visit resolution to anything administrative; budget multiple trips as the default |
| Build a relationship with a knowledgeable local fixer or colleague for anything involving multiple agencies | Assume online banking replaces in-person visits; most meaningful transactions still require a physical stamp |
| Dress and present formally at any government or bank office — it visibly affects how quickly you're processed | Lose your patience audibly; frustration directed at a clerk rarely speeds things up and often slows them further |

## Spain: Bureaucracy by Appointment, and the Appointment Doesn't Exist

Spain's administrative system for foreigners has a reputation that precedes it across every expat forum on the internet, and the reputation is earned. The NIE — Número de Identidad de Extranjero, the foreigner identification number required for essentially everything from opening a bank account to signing a lease to getting paid — requires an appointment (*cita previa*) booked through a notoriously unreliable government website that, in high-demand cities like Madrid and Barcelona, can have new slots released at seemingly random times and snapped up within minutes. Entire communities of expats have formed around sharing tips on when slots tend to appear, refreshing scripts, and, for those with the money, paying gestores — professional bureaucracy navigators — to handle the entire appointment-hunting process on their behalf.

Once you actually have your paperwork, Spanish banking itself is relatively modern and functional — major banks offer solid digital apps, and opening an account is straightforward once you clear the NIE hurdle. But the sequencing is the trap: you typically need proof of address to get the NIE, and sometimes a bank account to prove financial stability for residency, creating a bureaucratic chicken-and-egg problem that seasoned expats navigate through informal workarounds and patient explanation, rather than any officially documented process. It's not that any single step is unreasonable; it's that the steps assume each other in an order that doesn't logically exist.

What softens the frustration, somewhat, is that Spaniards themselves navigate the exact same system and complain about it with equal, weary familiarity — this isn't bureaucracy calibrated to be harder for foreigners, it's simply bureaucracy that's difficult for everyone, applied evenly. There's a strange camaraderie in a Spanish friend commiserating over your shared NIE appointment trauma, even though their version involved a different form for a different reason entirely.

## Algeria: Analogue, Thorough, and Not in a Hurry

Algerian bureaucracy runs on a fundamentally different logic than Spain's appointment-scarcity model — the challenge here isn't booking a slot, it's the sheer accumulated weight of physical process: multiple offices, multiple required stamps, and a documentation culture that treats the photocopy as a nearly sacred unit of proof. Opening a bank account, registering residency, or handling nearly any official transaction typically involves visiting more than one office in person, often more than once each, with each visit requiring its own fresh set of photocopied documents that the previous office, despite having seen the originals, will not simply pass along.

This is not, most people who've lived through it will tell you, actually about inefficiency for its own sake — it reflects a deeply established administrative culture where the physical stamp and the in-person verification remain the trusted unit of legitimacy, in a way that digital verification hasn't fully displaced, even as mobile banking and online services have started to expand around the edges. The system rewards patience, formality, and relationships far more than it rewards speed; showing up well-dressed, calm, and respectful to a government clerk genuinely and visibly affects how your case gets handled, in a way that's more culturally embedded than in most Western bureaucracies, where presentation matters less than paperwork completeness.

An entire informal ecosystem of fixers, well-connected relatives, and knowledgeable colleagues exists specifically to help people — locals and foreigners alike — navigate which office needs which stamp in which order, and leaning on this local knowledge isn't considered a shortcut or a workaround; it's simply understood as the normal, expected way anyone competent handles Algerian administrative life. Trying to go it alone as a foreigner with no local guidance, expecting a clean linear process, is the single most common source of expat frustration reported across nearly every forum thread on the subject.

## The Verdict

Spain's bureaucracy is frustrating because of artificial scarcity — a genuinely modern system throttled by an appointment bottleneck that seemingly nobody in government has prioritized fixing despite years of well-documented public complaint. Algeria's bureaucracy is frustrating because of inherent thoroughness — an older, physically-rooted system that simply requires more steps, more stamps, and more patience by design, not by broken technology.

Neither wins on speed. But Algeria's system, for all its friction, at least behaves predictably once you understand the logic — do the steps in the right order with the right fixer, and it works. Spain's system remains maddeningly stochastic, at the mercy of a government website's server capacity on any given Tuesday morning. Predictable frustration beats random frustration. Algeria takes it.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Internations Madrid — a member described spending six weeks trying to book an NIE appointment before finally paying a gestor, calling it "the best €150 I've spent since moving here."*

> *expat.com Algiers forum — a French expat wrote that his biggest early mistake was assuming a single visit to any government office would resolve anything, and that budgeting for a minimum of three visits per task saved him significant frustration once he adjusted his expectations.*

> *Quora — a respondent answering "is Algerian bureaucracy as difficult as people say" argued the process is entirely navigable, just not alone — "bring someone who's done it before, every time, no exceptions."*

## Conclusion

Spain will keep releasing NIE appointments into the void at unpredictable hours, and an entire generation of expats will keep refreshing browser tabs at dawn hoping to catch one. Algeria will keep requiring its stamps, its photocopies, and its patient, formal, in-person visits, exactly as it always has. Bring a gestor to Madrid and bring a well-connected friend to Algiers — neither bureaucracy is going to meet you halfway, and both know it.
