# Monrovia Cleans Its Graves. Accra Buries the Living in Glitter. Pick Your Holiday.

🇱🇷 Liberia vs 🇬🇭 Ghana

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country has a theory of what a public holiday is for. Most Western ones have quietly decided it's for mattress sales. Liberia and Ghana did not get that memo, and thank god, because both have produced holiday calendars so specific, so unrepentantly local, that trying to export either one to a European bank-holiday Monday would cause a diplomatic incident.

Liberia spends one entire national holiday scrubbing and painting its ancestors' graves. Ghana spends several holidays a year competing over whose funeral had the better brass band. Between the two, I have never once been bored on a weekday in West Africa, and I have been bored on a staggering number of weekdays in Zurich.

## Do's and Don'ts: Liberia 🇱🇷

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit a cemetery on Decoration Day — it's a genuine community event, not a solemn one | Expect fixed notice on public holidays; the president can proclaim one with a day or two's warning |
| Ask about Poro and Sande society customs respectfully, as cultural context, not spectacle | Wander into a vault-painter's patch at Duport Road Cemetery without small change ready |
| Buy a lappa (wax-print fabric) quilt as a souvenir that actually means something locally | Assume July 26th is a quiet date; Independence Day gets a full presidential address at the Capitol |

## Do's and Don'ts: Ghana 🇬🇭

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend a Saturday funeral if invited — it is the social event of the Ghanaian calendar | Skip Homowo season in Ga areas assuming it's "just a harvest thing"; it's a month-long ritual cycle |
| Travel for a named regional festival — Aboakyer, Odwira, Fetu Afahye — rather than a generic "cultural tour" | Try to drum during the Koninfemo noise ban; the silence is enforced for weeks, not a suggestion |
| Wear white to a funeral for someone who lived past eighty; it signals celebration, correctly | Underestimate December; Afrochella-era homecoming season turns Accra into a monthlong festival |

Liberia's holiday calendar is modest on paper — eleven official national holidays, set under Labour Practice Laws §18 — and genuinely unpredictable in practice, because the president can and does proclaim extra one-off holidays with as little as a day or two's notice, a bureaucratic quirk that has derailed more than one visiting consultant's meeting schedule. The standout is Decoration Day, observed in March, when the entire country turns cemetery maintenance into a communal event: families converge to clean and repaint ancestral graves, vendors set up shop between the headstones, and at Monrovia's Duport Road Cemetery the burial vaults are packed so tightly that people sit and walk across them, occasionally causing actual traffic jams of mourners. A grave-painter named Anthony Wogbeh, documented doing exactly this work, charges roughly a dollar sixty per vault — a price point that somehow makes the whole ritual feel more human, not less. Independence Day, July 26th, is the other anchor: a full presidential address at the Capitol marking the anniversary, this year the 179th, of a nation founded by freed American slaves and still negotiating what that inheritance means in public ceremony. Beneath the official calendar runs something older and less photographed: the Poro and Sande secret societies, which govern initiation rites tied to the spirit world for men and women respectively, and which one diplomatic spouse's account flatly described Liberia as "a VERY religious country" because of it. Drumming marks weddings, naming ceremonies, and holidays alike, less as entertainment than as infrastructure.

Ghana, by contrast, treats the calendar as a battlefield of competing spectacles, and wins most of them. The Ga people's Homowo festival — the name literally translates to "hooting at hunger," commemorating an ancient famine survived — is the clearest example: it opens with a millet-sowing rite performed by seven priests, proceeds through a mandatory month-long noise ban called Koninfemo during which drumming is forbidden, and climaxes with kpokpoi, a steamed millet and palm-oil dish sprinkled on the ground for ancestors before anyone else eats. Elsewhere on the calendar: Aboakyer in Winneba, a literal live-deer-catching contest each May; Odwira in Akuapim, marking both the yam harvest and an 1826 military victory; Fetu Afahye in Cape Coast, billed as a carnival of new clothes with a lagoon boat race attached. And then there's the funeral, which is not technically a holiday but functions as one more reliably than most actual holidays do — custom coffins shaped like the deceased's profession, hired criers, DJs, bodies kept in cold storage for weeks or months awaiting the right send-off date, families openly competing to out-spend each other, white clothing worn without irony when the departed made it past eighty, because that's a life worth celebrating rather than grieving. December folds all of this into diaspora homecoming season, when Accra effectively becomes one extended festival built on the back of Afrobeats concerts and returning relatives determined to prove the trip was worth it.

Ghana's regional festival roster goes deeper than the headline few, too, and the variety is genuinely the point. Damba, celebrated across Dagbon and the northern regions, centres on horseback processions rather than anything coastal visitors expect from "Ghanaian festival" as a category. Hogbetsotso, held by the Anlo-Ewe in the Volta Region each November, commemorates a historical exodus from Togo. Feok, in Sandema among the Builsa people each December, involves headgear topped with actual antlers and marks a historical victory over a slave-raider named Babatu — a genuinely heavy piece of history wrapped in a genuinely festive public event, which is a combination Ghana seems to manage better than most countries attempt.

The honest comparison: Liberia's calendar is sparse but weighted entirely toward ancestry, memory, and a single enormous act of national maintenance. Ghana's calendar is dense, regionally fractured, performative in the best sense, and increasingly powered by its own diaspora's homesickness. One nation quietly tends its graves once a year. The other turns death itself into the best-dressed event on the social calendar, repeatedly, on purpose.

One last point on Liberia's calendar before we get to the verdict: the Poro and Sande societies aren't a historical footnote confined to textbooks — initiation rites tied to them still shape how weddings and naming ceremonies get conducted in many communities today, which means the "secret society" framing outsiders reach for undersells just how mainstream the practice actually remains in everyday Liberian family life, holiday or not.

## The Verdict

If you want a holiday culture that treats memory as sacred and modest, Liberia's Decoration Day is a genuinely moving thing to witness once, assuming you can forgive the government for announcing your day off with less notice than your dentist gives for a cancellation. If you want a holiday culture engineered for maximum spectacle, repeat attendance, and the occasional live-deer chase, Ghana wins by a landslide. I'm giving this one to Ghana, mostly because any culture that has turned funerals into must-attend social theatre understood something about public holidays that the rest of the world is still catching up on.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Monrovia, paraphrased: Liberia is "a VERY religious country," and expats are drawn into buying lappa fabric quilts and taking local drumming or dance classes almost by social gravity rather than deliberate choice.*

> *Expat.com, Ghana regional forum, paraphrased: if offered a drink of alcohol, pour a small amount on the ground first as a libation for the ancestors before drinking — a small gesture that's noticed instantly if skipped.*

> *GoOverseas volunteer review, Accra, paraphrased: arriving on Christmas Eve, the city was described as in total "frenzy" — an overwhelming, joyful, slightly chaotic sensory event rather than a quiet holiday evening.*

## Conclusion

Neither country is going to hand you a quiet long weekend, and if that's what you came for, you have badly misjudged the region. Liberia will ask you to sit with its dead for an afternoon and call it a holiday, and it will be oddly lovely. Ghana will ask you to dress better for a funeral than you dressed for your own wedding, and then it will ask you to do it again next Saturday. Bring good shoes to both. You'll need them for very different reasons, and neither reason involves a mattress sale.
