# Germans Join a Club to Exercise. Danes Join a Club to Exercise, Then Join Another Club for the Beer

🇩🇪 Germany vs 🇩🇰 Denmark

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

If you are planning to land in either country and pump iron alone at five in the morning in a sparkly chain gym, I applaud your individualism and I expect you will be quietly disappointed. Northern Europe does not believe in fitness as a solo performance. It believes in fitness as a civic duty, administered through the humble association. Germany has the Verein. Denmark has the forening. Both nations will politely ask you to fill in a form, pay a modest fee, and show up on Tuesdays.

The differences are subtler than the similarities, which is exactly why they irritate. Germany treats sport as a structured, rules-based pastime with a membership number and a committee. Denmark treats it as one slice of a national obsession with joining things, to the point that more than six in ten of the country's voluntary associations are sports clubs. In both places the gym chain is the awkward cousin at the wedding. This is a guide to which country will make you sweat in good company, and which will make you sweat while someone fills out the minutes.

**Do's & Don'ts**

**Germany**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a Verein, since about one in three Germans plays sport in a club | Assume the gym is the default; most Germans do not belong to one |
| Budget for modest dues, a Turnverein typically charges about EUR 100 a year | Expect a club to hand you a locker and a smoothie bar |
| Try a flatrate like Urban Sports Club if you want variety across cities | Show up on a Sunday expecting a packed class; it is the quietest fitness day |
| Learn the rules and the routine before you turn up late | Treat the committee as a joke; they are not |

**Denmark**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join your local club; Denmark has more than 16,500 voluntary clubs and associations | Assume you can stay anonymous in a society where two thirds belong to something |
| Expect volunteers to run things, and offer to help | Treat the fee as a service charge; the cheapness is made possible by unpaid volunteers |
| Try football, which has almost 350,000 club members | Be surprised that children are in clubs from a very young age, over 90% of under-13s |
| Be social after the session, because the club is also the community | Disappear the moment the exercise ends |

## Germany: Order, Dues and a Very Large Forest

The German relationship with exercise is conducted through the Verein, and the Verein is a machine for turning enthusiasm into paperwork. According to the German Sports Federation, as relayed by the expat guide How To Germany, one in three Germans plays sport in a club. Most Germans, interestingly, do not belong to a gym at all. A Turnverein, the traditional gymnastics club, typically charges around EUR 100 per year, which is roughly the price of one decent dinner and a bad decision. The sport is typically weekly or biweekly, not daily, and the point is regularity rather than punishment.

The country has also embraced the flatrate culture. Urban Sports Club, which says it covers 26 German cities in the guide and offers access to over 50 sports on its own blog, has built a business on the idea that Germans want variety without commitment, a surprisingly un-German combination. Its own data, which is company data and therefore a bit of a flex, shows Nuremberg as its most active city, Sunday as the quietest day, Wednesday as the busiest in most places, and January predictably packed while December sags. That is the whole national psyche in a spreadsheet: resolve in January, collapse by Christmas.

Outside the studio, Germany leans on its landscape. The How To Germany guide describes marked trails everywhere and organised Volkslauf walks of five to twenty kilometres along forest paths, with stamp cards and a beer-garden finish. This is the German genius: take exercise, add a stamp, add beer, and nobody complains. For an expat, the lesson is to find a Verein for the social structure and a flatrate for the flexibility. Do not expect either to be run with American enthusiasm. They will be run with excellent paperwork.

Consider what this does to the average expat's expectations. A newcomer from a gym-chain culture arrives believing fitness is a product you purchase, like a mattress, and discovers that in northern Europe it is closer to a membership in a small, slightly demanding family. The Verein does not care about your personal best. It cares that you showed up, that you paid your dues, and that you know where the spare balls are kept. This is mildly maddening for the first month and quietly moving by the third, which is roughly how all the best cultural lessons work.

## Denmark: A Country That Has Turned Joining Into a National Sport

Denmark has a number that should alarm any hermit: more than 16,500 voluntary clubs and associations, with about two thirds of Danes belonging to one or more. More than 60% of those associations are sports clubs. Sixty-one national federations sit under the Sports Confederation of Denmark, and the DIF and DGI together represent more than three million members in a country of under six million. Among children under thirteen, over 90% belong to a sports club; for teenagers the figure is 60%. Football leads with almost 350,000 members.

The part that interests an executive is the economics. According to the Region Nordjylland document on Danish associations, club membership is cheap because most of the work is done by unpaid volunteers and municipalities cover part of the cost. That is the Danish bargain: low fees, high civic participation, an expectation that you will bring a cake or stack a chair. You do not pay for convenience; you pay with your Saturday morning. If you are an expat who wants to be left alone, this will feel like an invasion. If you want to actually meet Danes, a club is the only door that opens.

Gyms exist, naturally, and large chains operate across the country, but the culture around them is different. In Denmark fitness is woven into the same fabric as everything else social: you exercise, then you stay, then you chat, and the whole thing happens with the unhurried calm of a nation that has solved most of its problems and is now waiting for the rest of us. The Danish forening is a place where you are noticed, welcomed, and, if you are lucky, given a job on the committee by the end of the season.

I will add one heretical observation: the reason these systems work is that they remove the heroics. Nobody is asking you to transform yourself in January. They are asking you to be at the hall at seven on Thursday. A regular, modest, communal habit beats a heroic, expensive, solitary one almost every time, which is why both countries are healthier and more irritating than the rest of us. If you want to be left alone to suffer, buy headphones. If you want to actually become fitter, find the hall.

## The Verdict

Denmark wins on community, Germany on variety. If I had to choose where to sweat, I would choose Denmark, because the Danish system builds belonging into the sport and the German system treats belonging as an optional extra. Germany has an immense network of clubs, trails and flatrate options, and it is wonderful, but the sources describe a culture where most people are not in a gym and the one in three who join a club do so for structure rather than friendship.

Denmark makes you part of something. That is both its great virtue and its great demand. If you want to disappear into an anonymous gym with headphones on, Germany will let you, with a mild sniff. If you want to be woven into a community of people who will definitely notice if you stop turning up, move to Denmark and bring a cake.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Urban Sports Club community team, company blog — German members check in least on Sundays and in December, most on Wednesdays and in January, which is to say resolve peaks at the start of the year and fades by Christmas. (Institutional finding, not a first-person account.)*

> *Region Nordjylland guide to Danish clubs — membership is cheap because most work is unpaid and volunteer-driven, and municipalities help cover costs, so participation is expected in return. (Institutional finding, not a first-person account.)*

> *How To Germany expat guide — most Germans do not belong to a gym, and sport is typically club-based and weekly or biweekly rather than daily. (Institutional finding, not a first-person account.)*

## Conclusion

A fitness culture tells you what a country thinks a body is for. In Germany, apparently, it is for getting through the week with some structure and a beer-garden finish. In Denmark, it is a way of belonging to the neighbours. Neither is an unreasonable philosophy, and both beat the lonely cult of the treadmill at five in the morning.

My parting advice: whichever country you choose, do the paperwork, go to the first session even if you are tired, and do not skip the part afterwards. The exercise is a pretext. The club is the point.
