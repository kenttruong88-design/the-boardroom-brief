# Canada Makes You Wait and Hong Kong Makes You Pay

🇨🇦 Canada vs 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Every country thinks it has solved healthcare. Canada thinks it has solved it by making it universal and treating the wait as a moral good. Hong Kong thinks it has solved it by running two systems side by side and letting you pick your poison: a heavily subsidised public network for residents, and a private sector for people who value their afternoons.

I have been unwell in a surprising number of countries, and I can say with confidence that the quality of a health system is revealed at three in the morning with a fever and a document you cannot find. Canada and Hong Kong will both treat you well. They will simply do it on very different terms.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇨🇦 Canada ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for your provincial health card as soon as you are eligible | Assume you are covered the day you land |
| Find a family doctor or clinic early and join the waiting list | Wait until you are sick to start looking |
| Use walk-in clinics for minor problems | Go to the emergency room for a sore throat |
| Ask your employer about extended benefits for drugs and dental | Forget that prescriptions and dental are often not covered |

| 🇭🇰 Hong Kong ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrange private medical insurance before you arrive | Rely on the public system if you are not an eligible resident |
| Ask about English-speaking doctors and clinic hours | Assume every specialist will explain things in English |
| Keep a copy of your medical history and prescriptions | Turn up at a new clinic with no records |
| Check what your policy covers for pregnancy, dental and pre-existing conditions | Read the exclusions after the claim |

## 🇨🇦 Canada

Canadian healthcare is built on a simple promise: you will be treated based on need, not your bank balance. Provincial plans cover doctor visits, hospital stays and a long list of essential services. Nobody hands you an invoice at the front desk, and nobody asks about your credit limit. That is a genuinely civilised thing, and anyone who has watched a family argue with an insurance form will understand why Canadians hold on to it.

The catch is access. Because the system is free at the point of use, the bottleneck becomes time. Finding a family doctor can take months or years in some provinces, and many people rely on walk-in clinics instead. Emergency rooms can mean long hours in a waiting room, and specialist referrals add another queue behind the first. A newcomer typically has a waiting period before provincial coverage starts, which means private cover for the gap.

What the system does not cover will surprise you. Prescriptions outside hospital, dental, vision and physiotherapy usually sit with your employer's benefits or your own pocket. Canadians accept this the way they accept winter, as a fact of life that is best discussed over coffee.

Culturally, Canadians are polite patients. They wait, they complain quietly, and they tell their friends about it later. The result is a system with good clinical care, a patient population that rarely makes a fuss, and a nationwide hobby of complaining about wait times with the same affection used for the weather.

There is a cultural note that matters too. Canadians tend to trust their doctors and obey their instructions, and the relationship is formal and unhurried. Appointments are for conversation as well as examination, and a good family doctor knows your history across years. When you finally find one, the continuity is a real benefit that no private clinic can match. It is also the reason that losing a doctor to retirement can feel like a bereavement, and why newcomers are advised to ask around, join lists and try more than one clinic.

## 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

Hong Kong has a famously strong public hospital network run by the Hospital Authority, with charges that are very low for eligible residents. Quality is high, doctors are well trained, and the city has one of the longest life expectancies in the world. The problem is that public services are designed for locals, and the demand is immense. Waiting times for non-urgent care can be long, and the experience is brisk, busy and not built for hand-holding.

For most expats, private care is the answer. Private hospitals and clinics offer quick appointments, English-speaking doctors, comfortable rooms and a level of service that feels more like a hotel than a ward. You can often see a specialist within days. That speed comes with a price tag that has little in common with the Canadian model, which is why almost every international employer provides private insurance as part of the package.

Medical culture is practical and fast. Appointments are short. Doctors prescribe, test and move on. Dispensing medication directly from the clinic is common, and so is traditional Chinese medicine alongside the Western kind, with herbal shops standing on corners of the same streets as modern hospitals.

The risk for newcomers is the gap between systems. If your insurance excludes a condition or your employer changes policy, you may suddenly find yourself in a very expensive waiting room. Read the policy, then read it again, and then ask your broker what the footnotes actually mean.

A word on pharmacies and traditional medicine, because they are a surprisingly large part of daily life. In Hong Kong, a trip to a pharmacy or a herbal shop is a normal first step for a cold or a sprain. Pharmacists offer advice, and a practitioner of Chinese medicine may be the first stop for a chronic complaint. The two traditions sit alongside each other without drama. Expats can make use of both, as long as they tell their doctor what they are taking.

## The Verdict

If you are healthy, young and wealthy, Hong Kong's private system will serve you beautifully. If you are older, ill or poor, Canada's principle of treatment first and paperwork never is superior, and nobody should pretend otherwise. A health system is not a luxury brand. It is the thing you want when everything else has failed.

But I will not romanticise Canada. A promise of care that arrives eight months later is a promise with a delivery problem. Hong Kong's model solves the delivery and ignores the fairness, while Canada's solves the fairness and fumbles the delivery. Pick your flaw. For expats on a corporate package, Hong Kong is simply easier. For anyone building a life, Canada is the safer bet, provided you get your health card, find a doctor early, and bring a book to the waiting room.

The financial gap is the point I would stress. In Canada, the worst thing about the system is the delay. In Hong Kong, the worst thing is the bill if your cover fails. Both can be managed with planning. Neither can be managed with optimism.

One last point for families. Children's care and vaccinations are straightforward in both places, though the paperwork differs. Canadian clinics follow provincial schedules and keep records for years. Hong Kong clinics are quick and thorough, and you pay at the door. Keep a folder, scan it, and carry a copy, because medical records have a habit of disappearing at precisely the wrong moment.

## Conclusion

Healthcare is the one subject where national pride and personal terror meet. Canada believes in the principle and tolerates the queue. Hong Kong believes in the speed and tolerates the invoice. Neither is wrong, and both will keep you alive.

The sensible expat does the paperwork before the symptoms. Get the card, get the insurance, find the doctor, keep the records. And if anyone tells you their country has the best healthcare in the world, ask how long it took them to get an appointment. Their face will answer for them.

And if you have a chronic condition, or you are planning a family, speak to an adviser before you accept the posting. It is a dull task that prevents a very bad week.
