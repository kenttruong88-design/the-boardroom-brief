# One Country's Government Has an Official List of 12 Usable Hospitals. The Other Has Two Vets, Total.

🇸🇳🇲🇱 Senegal and Mali, reviewed by someone who has been treated, with varying degrees of competence, on five continents

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The UK government's entire vetted list of usable medical facilities in Mali — for the whole country, not one region — runs to twelve entries, and all twelve are in Bamako. If you're sick anywhere else in a country roughly twice the size of France, the official position is: get to the capital, or get on a plane. Senegal, by comparison, looks almost luxurious — 70% of the country's doctors and 80% of its dentists cluster in Dakar, which sounds equally alarming until you remember that's still infinitely more than twelve addresses for an entire nation. Both countries will cheerfully let you believe a headache is just a headache, right up until it very much isn't.

## Senegal: A Two-Tier System You Learn to Navigate Fast

Senegal runs a clean, if occasionally brutal, two-tier healthcare model. Public hospitals — Hôpital Principal de Dakar, Hôpital Aristide Le Dantec, Hôpital Général de Grand Yoff in the capital, plus smaller regional hospitals scattered through Pikine, Thiès, Kaolack and Saint-Louis — are technically free or cheap for citizens, but are consistently described, even in official-adjacent guidance, as overcrowded and resource-constrained, with the standing advice to avoid them for anything beyond routine needs. Private clinics — Clinique du Cap, Bellevue Clinic, Clinique Casahous, Medic'Kane, the PISAM maternity network — are where expats actually go, and they typically demand a cash deposit on admission unless you're covered by an insurer with a direct-billing arrangement.

The mandatory formal-sector insurance scheme, the IPM, deducts 6% of salary, split between employer and employee, capped at a 250,000 CFA salary base, and covers only 50 to 80% of basic costs — explicitly excluding medical evacuation, which means even insured residents are one serious diagnosis away from needing a second plan entirely. The price anchors are genuinely useful if you're budgeting: a public GP visit runs $5 to $16, a private one $16 to $33, and a full day in a private clinic costs roughly $80 before any actual treatment. Three specific apps structure daily medical logistics in a way I found almost charming in its practicality: AfriDoctor for finding and booking a doctor, "Allo Docteur" for locating the pharmacy currently on 24-hour rotation and checking drug availability and prices, and SOS Médecin, a private service that sends an actual doctor to your home rather than making you come to them. Pharmacies mark themselves with a green cross and rotate night-duty status, a system that works precisely because everyone already knows which one is on tonight.

What nobody tells you upfront, and what one long-posted Dakar expat account flags with real specificity, is that Dakar's dust, sand storms and humidity are genuinely rough on anyone with allergies — the respondent's own childhood asthma, dormant for decades, reportedly returned after relocating — and that dehumidifiers aren't sold locally, so bring one. That same account rates routine care as locally trustworthy, having had significant dental work done without flying out, while still treating serious conditions as an automatic evacuation trigger. And the city is bluntly described as "extremely difficult" for anyone with a physical disability, since almost nothing is built to be accessible. One forum poster, by contrast, reports good experiences with both public and private care, including an MRI done in the public sector outside Dakar, and says the price gap versus US healthcare is wide enough that they've simply never bothered buying insurance at all — a genuinely different risk calculation than the evacuation-first mindset dominating Mali's material below.

## Mali: The Plan Is Evacuation, and Everyone Knows It

Mali's healthcare reality is best understood through one blunt institutional fact: the UK government's entire officially vetted list of usable medical facilities for the whole country is twelve entries, every single one in Bamako. Named public teaching hospitals — Hôpital Gabriel Touré, Hôpital du Point G, the latter perched on a hill overlooking the capital and among the country's oldest — sit alongside a small cluster of private clinics, including Bamako International Polyclinic, Clinique ALDI, and Hôpital American Golden Life, that expats and diplomats gravitate toward by default. Beyond Bamako, on paper, there's essentially nothing officially sanctioned at all.

A 2025 Afrobarometer-sourced survey puts hard numbers on what that gap feels like from the inside: only 14% of Malians have any health coverage whatsoever; 82% worry about affording care, 58% of them "a lot"; 9% of people who visited a public facility in the past year admit paying a bribe to get seen; 47% went without needed medicine or treatment at least once in the past year, 22% describing it as happening "many times." And yet 83% still want government-guaranteed universal care even if taxes rise, with 68% expressing trust in the Ministry of Health — a genuinely striking combination of present frustration and continued institutional faith.

What this translates to practically, repeated across multiple posted diplomatic accounts, is that the default plan for anything serious isn't "find the good private clinic," it's evacuation, full stop — local care is flatly rated "poor," and malaria dominates the daily health conversation with a sharper edge than in Senegal, including reports of people becoming seriously ill specifically after skipping their anti-malarial regimen. Even taxi-avoidance gets partly rationalised through the health system, with one account noting that avoiding local taxis is partly about not wanting to be in an accident given "the poor medical care available in Bamako." For a smaller, specific, almost absurdly vivid detail: Bamako reportedly has exactly two known vets for its entire expat pet population, no kennel anywhere in the city, and one of those two vets makes house calls personally. English-language expat forums for Mali, meanwhile, are strikingly thin — threads exist, but mostly sit unanswered, which I'd normally read as bad research luck except it's consistent enough across sources to be its own honest data point about how small and quiet the current community there actually is.

## The Verdict

Senegal wins, clearly, on infrastructure you can actually plan around — apps, named clinics, price anchors, a functioning (if imperfect) insurance scheme. But I want to resist making this sound like Mali simply loses, because that's not quite the right frame. Mali's healthcare reality isn't a worse version of Senegal's; it's a fundamentally different proposition, one where the honest, standing advice from the people who've actually lived it is simply: don't get seriously sick here, and if you do, leave. That's not a lower score. That's a different game entirely, and pretending otherwise would do a disservice to anyone actually making the decision to go.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Bamako — paraphrased: the standing plan for anyone who gets seriously sick or injured is evacuation out of the country entirely; there's no real expectation of local treatment for anything major, and malaria is the dominant daily worry through the rainy season.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Dakar — paraphrased: "good healthcare is available, at least for routine needs," including significant dental work done locally rather than flying out — but serious conditions still mean evacuation; Dakar's dust and humidity are flagged as genuinely rough on allergy and asthma sufferers.*

> *Expat.com, Senegal forum — paraphrased: one poster reports good experiences with both public and private care, including a public-sector MRI outside Dakar, and says prices run so far below US rates that they've simply never bought health insurance at all.*

## Conclusion

Senegal will let you build a reasonably functional medical life, apps and all, provided you accept that "insured" doesn't mean "evacuation-covered." Mali will teach you something blunter and more useful: that sometimes the most honest healthcare advice a country can give you is simply "don't," and that the dignity is in planning around that truth rather than pretending it isn't there. Pack your own chronic medication either way. Pack a dehumidifier for Dakar. And if you're bringing a pet to Bamako, befriend Dr. Niare early — he is, by all accounts, one of exactly two options you have.
