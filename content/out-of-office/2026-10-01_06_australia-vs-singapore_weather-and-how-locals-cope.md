# One Country Fights the Weather With a Hat. The Other Fights It With an Entire Indoor Civilisation.

🇦🇺🇸🇬 Australia & Singapore
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Climate shapes culture far more than most people admit, and nowhere is that clearer than comparing how Australians and Singaporeans have each made peace with weather that would flatten most other nations. Australia's response to brutal, dry, UV-soaked heat is a kind of defiant outdoor normalcy — slap on sunscreen, wear a hat, carry on with the barbecue regardless. Singapore's response to relentless, wet, inescapable humidity has been to simply build an entire parallel civilisation indoors, connected by air-conditioned walkways, so the actual weather becomes almost optional information.

I have sunburned in both countries within the same calendar year, which tells you less about the weather and more about my own poor planning, but it did give me a front-row seat to two completely different national coping strategies.

## Do's & Don'ts: Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear sunscreen daily, even on overcast days — the UV index here is no joke, ever | Don't schedule outdoor exercise for midday in summer unless you enjoy minor medical risk |
| Carry water everywhere, always, as a baseline habit rather than an afterthought | Don't assume shade means safety — UV reflects off pavement and water with real intensity |
| Respect total fire ban days and bushfire warnings without exception | Don't mock the "slip, slop, slap" sun-safety messaging — it's genuinely saved an enormous number of lives |
| Check the forecast before a beach day — conditions can shift from pleasant to dangerous within hours | Don't underestimate how fast dehydration sets in during a dry heatwave, even if you don't feel especially thirsty |

## Do's & Don'ts: Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry a light jacket for indoor spaces — malls and offices are kept aggressively, almost punishingly cold | Don't plan an outdoor itinerary without a midday indoor buffer — the heat and humidity peak hard from noon to 3pm |
| Use the covered walkways and underground malls to move between destinations without ever stepping into the sun | Don't be surprised by sudden, intense afternoon thunderstorms — they arrive fast and leave just as quickly |
| Check the haze index (PSI) during the dry season — some days genuinely call for staying indoors | Don't assume humidity "gets easier" with time — most long-term expats say it never fully does |
| Hydrate constantly, even though you're mostly indoors — humidity hides how much you're actually sweating | Don't wear heavy fabrics outdoors; breathable, light clothing isn't a style choice here, it's survival gear |

## Australia: Defiance as a National Sport

Australians have developed a relationship with extreme heat that's part genuine adaptation, part cultural bravado, and the line between the two is not always obvious to outsiders. The "slip, slop, slap" sun-safety campaign, running in various forms since the 1980s, has measurably changed national behaviour — sunscreen use is high, hats are normal daywear, and skin cancer awareness is baked into school curricula from an early age. This is a country that takes its UV index as seriously as other nations take their stock market.

And yet, alongside all that genuine caution, there's a stubborn insistence on continuing to live outdoors regardless of what the thermometer says. Barbecues happen in 40-degree heat. Beach cricket happens in 40-degree heat. The response to brutal weather isn't retreat; it's better preparation followed by exactly the same outdoor behaviour you'd have anyway, just with more sunscreen and a wider hat brim. Total fire ban days bring a seriousness that cuts through the defiance — this is the one weather-related rule nobody jokes about, because the consequences of ignoring it are measured in lost homes and lives, not sunburn.

What foreigners consistently misjudge is how quickly Australian heat turns genuinely dangerous versus merely uncomfortable. The dryness means you don't necessarily feel as drenched or miserable as you would in a humid climate, which creates a false sense of manageability right up until dehydration or heat exhaustion arrives, often with little warning. Locals read the signs — dry mouth, mild dizziness, that specific quality of light at 2pm in January — with an instinct built from childhood. Newcomers have to learn it the hard way, usually once, memorably.

## Singapore: Building Your Way Out of the Problem

Singapore's approach to its climate is less about defiance and more about comprehensive infrastructural surrender, executed with extraordinary competence. Faced with heat and humidity that never meaningfully let up across the calendar, the country has built an interconnected network of air-conditioned malls, underground walkways, and covered linkways extensive enough that you can genuinely cross large parts of the city without stepping directly into the weather at all. This isn't an accident; it's deliberate urban planning, decades in the making, treating climate control as public infrastructure rather than private luxury.

The result is a curious psychological effect: Singaporeans experience their own tropical climate as something slightly external, almost optional, managed rather than endured. The famous aggressive over-air-conditioning of malls and offices — cold enough that locals carry cardigans in a country that's permanently 30 degrees outside — is less eccentricity and more a kind of overcorrection, a flex of the fact that the technology to escape the heat entirely now exists and is used liberally.

What newcomers find hardest to adjust to isn't the heat itself but the whiplash of moving constantly between extremes — stepping out of a freezing office into a wall of 90 percent humidity, then into another freezing taxi, then into another freezing mall. The haze season, when agricultural burning elsewhere in the region drifts across the island, adds a further layer: an officially published PSI index that locals check as routinely as a weather forecast, because some days genuinely do call for staying inside the climate-controlled bubble entirely.

## The Verdict

Australia has made peace with its weather by building resilience into its people. Singapore has made peace with its weather by building an entire shadow city that lets you mostly ignore it. Both are legitimate, high-effort national projects; one is biological and cultural, the other is architectural and technological. If you want to feel hardened by your environment, Australia will oblige, sunburn and all. If you want to feel protected from it almost entirely, Singapore has quietly built you an escape hatch at every corner.

I'm giving this one to Singapore, mostly out of pure self-preservation. Any country that has engineered its way around an entire climate, to the point where carrying a cardigan in the tropics is considered sensible rather than absurd, has earned my begrudging admiration.

## Conclusion

Neither Australians nor Singaporeans experience their weather the way a visitor does in the first week, and that's the entire point — both cultures have spent generations building workarounds so thorough that the climate stops feeling like an obstacle and starts feeling like a fixed condition you simply plan around. Visit either expecting to "just get used to it" and you'll be half right: you will adapt, eventually, but only by adopting an entire inherited system of habits, hats, cardigans, and architectural tricks that took the locals decades to perfect and will take you considerably less time to appreciate, usually right around your first proper sunburn or your first unexpected shiver indoors.
