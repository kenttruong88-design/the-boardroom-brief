# One Country Built Its Parks for Views, the Other Built Them for Barbecues

🇵🇹 Portugal 🇦🇺 Australia
*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

Portugal and Australia have both made green space central to daily life, but they've built it around entirely different instincts. Portugal's parks and miradouros exist primarily so you can sit still, look at something beautiful, and feel vaguely poetic about it, ideally with a coffee or a glass of vinho verde in hand. Australia's parks exist so you can cook a large amount of meat on a free public barbecue, surrounded by people you may or may not know, in weather that would send most of Europe indoors.

Both are, in their own ways, a genuine civic triumph — proof that a government can take green space seriously without making it precious or inaccessible. I have sat in both with the specific contentment of someone who has eaten in sixty countries and finally found two places that understand a park should actually be used, not just admired from a bench with a small sign asking you not to.

## Do's & Don'ts

**🇵🇹 Portugal**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Seek out a miradouro at golden hour, it's the actual national pastime | Rush through a garden, they're built for lingering, not passing through |
| Bring a bottle and a blanket, informal picnicking is widely accepted | Expect manicured, closely-mown lawns everywhere, the style is looser |
| Greet fellow bench-sitters with a nod, quiet shared space is respected | Play loud music in a quiet garden, it breaks an unspoken contract |

**🇦🇺 Australia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the free public BBQs, they exist specifically for this and are genuinely free | Hog a BBQ for hours when others are waiting, there's an unspoken turnover etiquette |
| Bring sun protection seriously, shade structures exist for real reasons | Underestimate how large and well-equipped a standard suburban park is |
| Respect off-leash dog hours and zones, they're taken seriously by regulars | Leave rubbish behind, the "leave no trace" expectation is strong and enforced socially |

## Portugal

Portuguese green space is built around the view as much as the greenery itself, and nowhere is this clearer than in the miradouros scattered across Lisbon's hills — small, often modest viewpoints that have become genuine social institutions, where locals and visitors alike gather at golden hour with a beer from the nearby kiosk and simply watch the city do its evening thing. It is unhurried, unpretentious, and entirely without the self-conscious "wellness" branding that similar spots get elsewhere; people go because it's lovely and free, not because an app told them it was good for their mental health.

The gardens themselves, from Lisbon's Jardim da Estrela to Porto's Jardins do Palácio de Cristal, favour a looser, more romantic style than the clipped formality you'd find further north — benches tucked under mature trees, peacocks wandering with total confidence in at least one notable case, and a general sense that the space exists for contemplation rather than performance. Picnicking is informal and widely accepted; a bottle of wine and a blanket on the grass raises no eyebrows and attracts no officials.

What's distinctly Portuguese is the quiet social contract around these spaces — shared, unhurried, a little bit wistful, the kind of place where a stranger might nod at you from the next bench and that's the entire interaction, and it's enough.

The azulejo-tiled benches and wrought-iron kiosks scattered through many of these gardens add a specific, slightly faded elegance that feels distinctly Portuguese — nothing overly manicured, nothing trying too hard, just old infrastructure quietly doing its job for another generation of bench-sitters. Street cats have an unofficial but widely tolerated residency in many of Lisbon's gardens, fed informally by regulars who've appointed themselves unofficial custodians, and nobody considers this a problem worth fixing. There's also a specific etiquette around the kiosk culture attached to many miradouros — a small quiosque selling coffee, beer, and simple snacks, functioning as the unofficial anchor of the whole space, and locals will have strong, specific opinions about which kiosk pours the better imperial, a debate conducted with real seriousness despite the stakes being, objectively, extremely low.

## Australia

Australian parks operate on a completely different, more actively social logic, built around infrastructure that assumes you're going to do something, not just sit and look at something. The free public barbecue, usually a simple electric hotplate bolted into a shaded structure, is a genuine piece of civic genius — functional, free, maintained, and available in nearly every suburban park, turning a Sunday afternoon into an impromptu gathering point for extended families and groups of mates with zero advance booking required.

The sheer scale of Australian public green space, even in dense suburbs, tends to startle newcomers — proper ovals, well-equipped playgrounds, shaded picnic shelters, and often a dog off-leash zone, all within a short walk of most residential streets. Shade infrastructure in particular is taken seriously, for obvious reasons given the climate; a well-designed Australian park treats sun protection as core engineering, not an afterthought.

The etiquette around shared facilities, especially the BBQs, is understated but real: there's an unspoken expectation you won't monopolise one when others are visibly waiting, and an equally strong expectation that you'll leave the space as clean as you found it, enforced less by signage than by simple social pressure from fellow park-users who take genuine pride in these shared amenities.

Many larger parks also fold in genuinely excellent public infrastructure beyond the barbecue itself — free outdoor gym equipment, water fountains built specifically for refilling bottles and for dogs, and shaded amphitheatre spaces that regularly host free community events without ever charging for entry. The off-leash dog culture deserves particular mention: entire friendships form between owners who've never exchanged names, known only by their dog's name, meeting at the same time most mornings with the easy, low-commitment regularity that defines so much of Australian social life. What ties all this together is a clear underlying belief that green space is a public good worth properly funding, not a luxury amenity — and the sheer density of well-equipped parks across even modest suburbs reflects a civic priority most countries simply haven't matched.

## The Verdict

Portugal wins on atmosphere — nowhere else have I felt quite as content doing absolutely nothing in a public garden with a view. Australia wins on sheer functional generosity — free, well-maintained infrastructure that actively invites you to use the space properly, not just admire it from a bench. If you want a park that makes you feel wistfully content, Portugal has you covered. If you want a park that will actually feed you, lunch included, Australia wins decisively, and frankly, I think that's the harder trick to pull off.

## Conclusion

Portugal and Australia have both decided public green space is worth investing in properly, just toward opposite ends — one for stillness and view, the other for activity and shared food. Spend time in both and you'll start judging every other city's parks by a new, much higher standard: where's the view, and more importantly, where's the free barbecue. Most places, I'm sorry to report, have neither.
