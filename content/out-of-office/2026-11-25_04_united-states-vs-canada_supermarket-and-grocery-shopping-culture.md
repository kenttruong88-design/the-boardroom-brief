# America Gives You Forty Peanut Butters and Canada Gives You a Receipt That Hurts

🇺🇸 United States vs 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Suki Nakamura, Out of Office*

The world's longest undefended border separates two countries that look identical from a motorway and are completely different in the cereal aisle. Every foreigner who lands in Toronto assumes it will be America with better manners. Every American who lands there finds out that the manners come with a 13 per cent tax and a fourteen dollar chicken.

I have shopped for dinner in sixty countries, and nothing humbles a person faster than a supermarket in a place that is almost home. In Cairo you know you are lost. In Calgary you think you are fine, and then you pay for a lime. This is a story about two North American grocery cultures that share a language, a cola and a grudge, and about the specific, petty ways they have diverged.

## Do's & Don'ts

| 🇺🇸 United States ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a car and buy in bulk at the warehouse club | Assume you can walk to a decent supermarket in most suburbs |
| Check the price at the shelf, because the tax is added at the till in many states | Forget that tax varies wildly by state and city |
| Use the store app and digital coupons | Pay full price for anything with a loyalty discount |
| Tip the delivery driver if you order online | Treat grocery delivery as a free service |

| 🇨🇦 Canada ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Shop around, because the same item can vary hugely between chains | Assume the first supermarket you pass is the cheapest |
| Know that basic groceries are often not taxed but prepared foods and snacks are | Be shocked by the tax on a prepared sandwich |
| Check the province, because alcohol rules vary wildly | Expect to buy wine at the supermarket in Alberta |
| Bring your own bags and learn to pack them quickly | Argue with the cashier about how to load a bag |

## 🇺🇸 United States

America is the land of choice, and I mean that as both a compliment and a medical warning. The average big supermarket stocks a cathedral of breakfast cereals, an aisle devoted entirely to chips and a peanut butter section that looks like a municipal planning exercise. One expat who moved to Mexico noted that a local store carries perhaps one brand of plain peanut butter, which made me realise how much of the American supermarket is an argument that every desire deserves its own brand. The sheer variety is spectacular, and I will not pretend I do not love it.

The format is built around the car. The enormous parking lot, the trolley the size of a hatchback, the warehouse clubs where a family buys toilet roll in quantities that suggest a siege. Costco's cheap hot dog is a cultural monument, and one Houston-to-Toronto mover swears the Canadian version at a dollar-fifty still matches the American one. Prices are often lower than across the border, there are budget chains that squeeze the whole market, and the discount culture is aggressive and fantastic.

The downside is the things you pay for in other ways. Fresh food quality is wildly uneven, the 'healthy' aisle is a marketing exercise, and the health system may well make you pay for your cheap groceries later. The sales tax is also a mess, varying by state and often by county, and the sticker price is never the full story. Still, if you want to buy anything at three in the morning in a neon-lit cavern, America is your continent.

The American supermarket also understands the pleasure of a small bargain. The hot dog at the warehouse club is a cult object because it stays cheap while everything else rises, and the loyalty app, the weekly circular and the bulk pack are all designed to make you feel clever for spending. That cleverness is the point. It trains a whole population to hunt a deal as a form of recreation, to compare unit prices on the shelf and to treat the weekly shop as a campaign. It is an exhausting way to buy dinner and I admire it enormously. The American shopper is a trained tactician, and the supermarket is a battlefield with excellent lighting.

## 🇨🇦 Canada

Canada looks at all that choice and replies with a polite, expensive shrug. The supermarket experience is more compact, more regional and, to many immigrants, more expensive. A UK-born Reddit user in Toronto went viral for paying 99 cents per lime and nearly four dollars for a head of broccoli at Metro, then converting the Tesco price of the same items at roughly a third of the cost. The commenters said what everyone says: Canada lacks the deep-discount chains like Aldi and Lidl that keep prices honest elsewhere, and a handful of large grocers dominate the market so thoroughly that the government has launched a grocery code of conduct.

The details are lovely and slightly maddening. A London-born writer in Calgary noted that cashiers will often pack the bags for you, which sounds charming until you realise their system is not yours. In Alberta, the supermarket does not sell alcohol at all, so you must visit a separate liquor shop, which she envied British Columbia for escaping. Packaging uses more plastic than in the UK, and a chicken pack that costs about five pounds back home was fourteen Canadian dollars at the checkout. Tax is added at the till, and in Ontario that is 13 per cent on most things, with only a few grocery items exempt.

Yet there is a decency to it. The stores are friendlier, the staff talk to you, and the whole thing is less of a gladiatorial sport than the American version. Canadian grocery shopping is a smaller, calmer experience, and the produce is often better than you were led to expect. You pay for the privilege, mind you. You pay in a currency that is very much not Canadian maple syrup.

The Canadian complaint is not that the food is bad but that the market feels closed. Commentators say a few large grocers dominate, with no real discount rival, and the government has responded with a Grocery Task Force, a code of conduct and planned changes to competition law. One mover from Texas thinks the food in Canadian shops and restaurants is less impressive than in American cities and suspects the supply chain. Another quietly notes the price of alcohol, which he calls a tax on the poor. None of this is fatal. It is simply the sound of newcomers doing arithmetic at the till and finding the answer rude.

## The Verdict

America wins on price and choice. It is not close. If your only criterion is how much you can feed a family for, how many versions of a thing you can buy and how quickly you can get it, the United States laps Canada twice and then goes for a hot dog.

But I will not pretend that is the whole story. Canada's grocery culture is quieter and more civilised, and in a decent neighbourhood you can walk to a shop and be greeted by a human being who is not trying to sell you a membership. The things that sting about Canadian groceries are structural, a market dominated by a few players, and structural problems can be fixed. A hostile aisle cannot.

So here is my ruling. America for the weekly stock-up, Canada for the daily experience. If you are an expat on a budget, move to America. If you are an expat who wants to enjoy the shopping, move to Canada and take out a second mortgage for the limes.

## What Nobody Warned You About

> *Many Flags newsletter, anonymous Houston-to-Toronto mover — Ontario sales tax is 13 per cent and almost everything except a few grocery items is taxed; deli-style American cheese costs too much to bother with, though the Costco hot dog at a dollar-fifty plus tax still tastes the same.*

> *Narcity, London-born writer in Calgary — Cashiers sometimes pack the bags themselves and never the way I would; alcohol is not sold in Alberta grocery stores, and a pack of chicken breasts that cost about five pounds at home was around fourteen dollars at Walmart.*

> *Daily Hive, reporting a UK-born Reddit user in Toronto — Two limes at 99 cents each and a head of broccoli at 3.99 came to 5.97 at Metro, and commenters said Canada has no deep-discount grocers like Aldi or Lidl to keep prices in check.*

## Conclusion

The cruelest thing about the American and Canadian supermarkets is how familiar they look. You walk in assuming you know the rules, and by the till you are paying a stranger's idea of tax for a lime. The border is invisible and the receipt is not.

Take a calculator, take your own bags and take the advice of anyone who has crossed the line in either direction. And if you ever find yourself in Canada asking why a head of broccoli costs more than a decent lunch, remember that the Americans have a warehouse for that. Then drive there. Take a very large car.
