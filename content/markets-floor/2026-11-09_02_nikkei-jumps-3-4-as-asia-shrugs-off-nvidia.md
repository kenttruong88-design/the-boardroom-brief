# Nikkei Jumps 3.4% as Asia Shrugs Off Nvidia Scandal

Japan's Nikkei 225 gained 3.4% on Monday as Asian equities rallied, with TSMC up 5.6%, according to figures carried by investingLive from the Associated Press. The summary on the same page described a market where memory chipmakers had rebounded and traders were "shrugging off the Nvidia scandal". The notable exception was SoftBank, which fell 2.9% amid what the AP called robotics deal woes.

## What a bounce looks like

Some context is in order. On Thursday, the AP reported, South Korea's Kospi fell 5.3% and Japan's Nikkei 225 fell 1.8% as a global rout in AI-linked stocks reached Asia. A 1.8% fall is comfortably covered by a 3.4% rise, whatever happened in Friday's session, which these sources do not describe. The Kospi is the harder case: a 5.3% decline needs a gain of about 5.6% to be undone, which is arithmetic rather than sentiment, and the arithmetic is rarely kind to people who sell at the bottom.

Thursday's damage elsewhere gives the bounce its proportions. The AP reported that Wall Street's S&P 500 fell 4.4% to 7,353.73, its worst day since the April 2025 "Liberation Day" tariffs, and the Nasdaq composite dropped 5.5%. Nvidia fell 9.3%, AMD 17.3% and CoreWeave 9.8%, while AppLovin rose 31%, making it the day's lone conspicuous winner. The FTSE 100 slipped a modest 0.4%, which in context counted as composure. A Monday rally in Asia is a response to that, not a rewriting of it.

None of this is a verdict on the sector. A single strong session after a very weak one is what a market does when sellers run out of urgency before buyers run out of caution. The test is Tuesday.

## The rest of the Monday tape

Asia's session carried other news that markets were obliged to digest. Per Reuters headlines on the investingLive page, China is suspending its 24% tariff on US goods for two more months while keeping a 10% levy, and extending a port-fee waiver to January 2027. The page also noted that China's producer prices recorded their biggest rise since 2022. Bitcoin slipped, with a headline putting it under $74,600.

Oil swung back to flat on mixed signals about Iran. The investingLive report said crude fell after President Trump said he was "highly unlikely" to order fresh strikes on Iran while talks continue, and that Secretary of State Marco Rubio had described the talks as not productive. A suspected Iranian drone strike was reported to have halted output at Iraq's Khor Mor gas field, and a linked headline reported Houthi attacks on targets in Riyadh, including the Aramco Tower. Both Washington and Tehran reported shipping disruptions near the Strait of Hormuz. For reference, the AP had Brent crude at $98.29 after a 3.9% jump on Thursday.

Gold, meanwhile, edged higher in Asian trade, extending a weekly gain of roughly 5%. A linked headline on the same page said Goldman Sachs had cut its gold forecasts to $4,150 for 2026 and $4,800 for 2027 as a hawkish Fed bites, which is a rare case of a bank trimming a forecast and raising a rate-hike probability in a single sentence. The metal's gains are being capped by the Fed. Per investingLive, citing CME FedWatch, December hike odds sit near 80%, up from about 70% before Friday's payrolls report. US consumer prices are due on Tuesday, and the page called them the next test of that pricing.

## The scoreboard

Rebounds are the unglamorous half of every selloff story, and this one has the usual structure. Thursday's losses were global; Monday's gains are regional. Chips are up, but so is the probability of a Fed hike, which is available to anyone prepared to look at it. The oil market has a dozen reasons to move and no consensus on which to believe, so it chose to go sideways, which is what a market does when everyone is right.

The sensible reading of Monday's session is that Asia declined to extend Thursday's panic, and that it received a number of reasons to do so: cheaper-looking chip stocks, a tariff pause, and a rate story that has yet to become a rate decision. The less sensible reading is that the scandal has been priced and filed. Investors have been wrong about that sort of thing before, usually on a Tuesday.
