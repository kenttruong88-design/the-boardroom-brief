# S&P 500 Sits 8.14% Below Its Peak While Healthcare Quietly Wins

The S&P 500 closed Wednesday at 7,182.78, which is 8.14 percent below its October 6 peak of 7,818.93, according to JournalArta. A correction is conventionally defined as a fall of 10 percent from the high, so the index remains, by 1.86 percentage points, entirely fine. The Nasdaq Composite is less fortunate: it sits 14.54 percent below its October 6 high, after an 11.16 percent slide over the past month. Nobody is calling it a correction. The Nasdaq has, in the technical sense, moved past it.

## Arithmetic as comfort

Start with the headline distance. From 7,818.93 to 7,182.78 is a fall of 636.15 points. To reach the 10 percent line the S&P 500 would need to close at about 7,037, which is some 146 points below Wednesday's close, or roughly 2 percent from here. A market can cover 2 percent in an afternoon, as the Dow demonstrated on Wednesday when it recovered from an intraday drop of more than 1,100 points, as Dimsum Daily reported. The point is that the current distance between "fine" and "correction" is smaller than a single bad Tuesday.

The one-month figure is less forgiving. JournalArta puts the S&P 500's loss over the past month at 7.25 percent. That is a faster slide than the peak-to-close number implies, which suggests the index spent early autumn near its highs and has been giving ground with unusual enthusiasm since. Wednesday was its second consecutive decline, with a fall of 18.31 points, or 0.25 percent.

The practical consequence is that every desk in the building now has a favourite number. Bulls cite 8.14 percent and note that corrections are a matter of definition. Bears cite 7.25 percent in a month and note that definitions have never stopped anything from falling. Both are quoting JournalArta correctly, which says something about how much room a single statistic can offer a determined reader. Rex notes only that the S&P 500 has now closed lower in two sessions running, and that neither of the two possible interpretations required a single new fact.

## The sector nobody invited to the party

While technology has been under pressure, healthcare has been collecting. JournalArta reports the S&P 500 healthcare sector is up 15 percent this year, against 5.52 percent for the broad index. That is nearly three times the market's return from the sector investors usually own so that they can say they own something sensible.

The reason is rotation. According to JournalArta, investors moved out of technology during the AI selloff and into healthcare. Dimsum Daily describes the same pressure from the other side: semiconductor and AI shares were the focus of selling as investors kept scrutinising capital expenditure and profitability across the sector. Nvidia fell 3.91 percent to $149.30 on the day, per Dimsum Daily.

There is a tidy morality tale available here, in which the unglamorous sector is rewarded for its patience. Rex declines to write it. Sectors do not hold values. They hold earnings, and people sell what has gone up to buy what has gone down until the two meet somewhere inconvenient.

## What the VIX is trying to say

The VIX rose 7.32 percent, or 1.93 points, to 28.29 on Wednesday, according to JournalArta. Its one-year peak was 35.89 on November 20, so the gauge is 21.18 percent below that high. A VIX at 28 does not describe a calm market. It describes a market that has stopped shouting and started speaking firmly.

Volume offered some perspective. About 3.47 billion shares changed hands on Wednesday, against a 20-day average of 5.54 billion, per JournalArta. Holiday-shortened sessions tend to be quiet, which is also why they are poor guides to anything. A decline on light volume proves little. A rally on light volume proves less.

Dimsum Daily added two items for the rest of the week's agenda: October core PCE inflation of 3.3 percent year on year against a 3.2 percent consensus, and futures pricing a 98 percent probability of a quarter-point Fed rate hike in December. Neither is unexpected, which is why neither is comforting. US markets are closed on Thursday and trade a half-day on Friday, ending at 1:00 pm Eastern.

So the position as of the holiday: the broad index is a couple of points shy of a correction, the growth corner of it is well beyond one, and the sector that has dominated the year is the one nobody mentions at dinner parties. Rex's closing observation is that a market can be "not in a correction" for exactly as long as it takes to be in one. The measurement, like most things on Wall Street, is tidy only until it is needed.
