# CoreWeave Jumps 24.7% After Vowing Not to Borrow at Current Yields

Shares of CoreWeave rose 24.7% on Wednesday to close at $60.77, a gain of $12.04 from Tuesday's close of $48.73, after the AI infrastructure company reported third-quarter results and said it would not fund itself in the unsecured debt market at present yields. The stock traded between $53.81 and $62.03 during the session, according to market data cited by The Motley Fool.

The headline numbers were good. Revenue came in at $3.66 billion, up 168% from a year earlier and ahead of the roughly $3.55 billion analysts had expected. The backlog of contracted work reached about $132.5 billion, up from roughly $104 billion at the end of June. CoreWeave raised its 2026 revenue guidance to a range of $13.0 billion to $13.3 billion, against a consensus of $12.86 billion.

The less cheerful numbers were also good, in the sense that they were disclosed. The company recorded a GAAP net loss of $709 million for the quarter and an adjusted loss of $0.95 a share, against the $1.16 loss analysts had forecast. Interest expense for the quarter was $846 million.

## A dividend of interest

Set the last figure beside the first. Interest of $846 million against revenue of $3.66 billion means that roughly 23 cents of every dollar CoreWeave billed last quarter went to the people who lent it the money to build the thing it was billing for. That is the business model, and it is not a secret. CoreWeave rents out Nvidia-powered computing capacity to AI developers, financing the chips and the buildings largely with debt, and then collects from customers under long contracts. The backlog is the collateral for the story; the interest bill is the price of it.

For several weeks that bill has been the subject of unease. A CoreWeave bond maturing in 2031 was yielding 15.76% at one point, according to Boerse-Express in early November, a rate at which a borrower is being asked to pay for its reputation. Markets sold AI-linked shares sharply on November 5, a move that Jane Gibbons of Jefferies described as "reactionary, not a fundamental reset," per TIKR.

## The announcement

Against that backdrop, Chief Executive Michael Intrator delivered the sentence the market wanted to hear. The company, he said, would not use the unsecured debt market to fund its business at current yields, "nor do we need to," according to Newscord. He called it "a breakout quarter," with revenue rising by more than $1 billion from the previous quarter.

The substance behind the sentence was specific. CoreWeave expects roughly $9 billion of customer prepayments and deposits over the next three months, it said it needs less than $5 billion of new parent-level capital through 2027, and it closed a $2.6 billion delayed-draw term loan. The message is that the company has found other people's money to spend before it has to ask the bond market for more.

It should be said that this is a good outcome. A company that can fund growth with customer deposits rather than 15% paper is a healthier company than one that cannot. The shares rose 24.7% because the likelihood of a refinancing crisis fell, not because the underlying loss shrank.

## Reading the tape

The oddity is the logic of the rally. The fear that had pushed CoreWeave and its peers lower was that expensive debt would swallow the AI build-out. The reassurance that lifted the stock was a promise not to take on expensive debt. In other words, the market was relieved to hear that a business which depends on borrowing would try not to borrow, which is the sort of relief usually reserved for the weather.

The move also arrived on a day the broader market was feeling generous. The S&P 500 rose 1.3% on Wednesday and the Nasdaq 1.6%, according to AP, so some of the gain belongs to the tide. But a stock that rises 24.7% on a day the Nasdaq gains 1.6% has not merely floated.

There is one more number worth tracking. Backlog of $132.5 billion is a promise from customers, not cash. It converts into revenue only if the data centers are built on schedule, the chips arrive, and the customers keep paying, each of which is a different kind of risk from the bond market. Wall Street has swapped one risk for another and priced it at 24.7%.

The unsecured debt market, for its part, was not available for comment. It has been told it is not needed.
