# Hot CPI Lifts Two-Year Yield 18bp as Stocks Hand Back Monday's Rally

Wall Street's two-session recovery lasted one session. On Tuesday, US consumer prices for October came in hotter than economists had forecast, the two-year Treasury yield posted its biggest one-day rise since April 2025, and the S&P 500 closed down 29.01 points, or 0.38%, at 7,520.93.

*Photo: Alex Luna via Pexels*

The inflation data did the damage. According to TheStreet's market wrap, headline CPI rose 0.4% on the month against an expected 0.3%, after a 0.6% gain in September. Core prices, which strip out food and energy and are therefore harder to blame on anyone, rose 0.3% against a forecast of 0.2%. Year-on-year comparisons were muddied by last year's government shutdown, which left gaps in the 2025 data, so traders were left to argue about the monthly numbers. They did so with some enthusiasm.

The reaction in rates was brisk. Euronext, citing Reuters, reported that the two-year yield rose 18.44 basis points to 4.731%, the largest one-day gain in more than a year. The ten-year gained 12.55 basis points to 4.988%, which is within sight of 5% and, depending on the data vendor, may have touched it: TheStreet put the move at 13.8 basis points to 5.00%. When two reputable sources disagree by a rounding error on the most-watched yield in the world, the correct response is to note both figures and proceed.

## The index damage

Equities took the news with something closer to resignation than panic. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 157.86 points, or 0.30%, to 52,231.89. The Nasdaq Composite lost 187.26 points, or 0.74%, to 25,065.87. Technology was the worst-performing sector, down 1.13%, which is what happens when the market is reminded that long-duration growth stocks are worth less when money costs more.

Context helps. On Monday the S&P 500 had risen 2.35% to 7,549.94, the VIX had collapsed, and the narrative was relief. Tuesday's close surrendered roughly 29 points of that gain, which is modest in index terms and considerably less modest in terms of conviction. A rally that survives a single inflation print is a rally. One that does not was a mood.

Brian Jacobsen of Annex Wealth Management, quoted by Euronext, summed up the problem: "It's not just headline CPI that is too high, but the oil shock has seeped into core." That sentence is the entire Federal Reserve debate in 17 words. Skyler Weinand of Regan Capital told TheStreet the print "sets the stage for another rate hike in December."

## Currencies, and the Fed's shadow

The dollar took the opportunity to flex. The dollar index rose 0.56% to 102.25, according to Cappnotes, while the euro fell 0.53% to $1.1163 and the dollar gained 0.55% on the yen to 158.77. A yen at that level will remain a topic of conversation in Tokyo, where the central bank has its own inflation conversation to manage.

Rate expectations moved with the yields. Euronext reported that the odds of a 50-basis-point increase at the Federal Reserve's December 8-9 meeting rose after the data. Fed Governor Christopher Waller had said before the release that a 0.3% core reading would lead him to consider a half-point move, and core obliged with exactly 0.3%. Cappnotes cited Kalshi prediction-market pricing of 86% for at least a quarter-point hike in December. That is a single secondary source for a market that did not exist in this form a few years ago, so treat it as a mood indicator with decimal places.

The curious part is the oil. Brent has fallen sharply in recent weeks, and Tuesday's CPI was largely a lagged reading of an earlier energy shock rather than a current one. Traders are therefore pricing a Fed that will respond to October's prices with decisions that arrive in December, by which time October will be a historical curiosity. Central banks are, by design, the people who react to the previous quarter's weather.

For equity investors, the practical lesson is that the market is now sensitive to every inflation release between here and December 9. Each print is a small referendum on whether the Fed hikes by 25 or 50 basis points, and the S&P 500, sitting about 0.4% below Monday's high-water mark, has shown how quickly a referendum can flip.

One might hope that the next data point arrives with better manners. One might also hope the ten-year yield finds a number below five percent that it likes. Both are hopes, which is the only asset class not marked down on Tuesday.
