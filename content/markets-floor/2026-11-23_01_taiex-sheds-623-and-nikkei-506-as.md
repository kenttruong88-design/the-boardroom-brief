# Taiex Sheds 6.23% and Nikkei 5.06% as Nvidia Rout Crosses the Pacific

Taiwan's Taiex fell 6.23% to 36,033.29 on Friday, and Japan's Nikkei 225 lost 5.06% to 56,436.65, as the selloff in artificial-intelligence stocks reached the Asian supply chain, according to a market wrap published by BigGo Finance on November 22. The Taiex's decline took its slide for the week to 17.5%.

## The week in numbers

The week's damage was concentrated where the AI trade is physically made. Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing fell NT$100 to NT$2,000, BigGo reported, and SoftBank Group dropped more than 16%. Micron, which is not Asian but is closely related to the story, is down more than a third over the past month and roughly 50% below its June record. AMD has lost about 20% over four weeks.

At the centre of it sits Nvidia. BigGo reports that the stock fell 14% on Friday in the United States, 22.9% over the week, and is now 47.5% below its October 6 high, with a market value of about $3.03 trillion.

Friday's US session did not offer a refuge. The same BigGo wrap puts the S&P 500 down 3.70% at 6,910.39, its seventh consecutive loss and the longest such streak since February 2020. The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 1.93% to 48,147.33 and the Nasdaq Composite lost 5.28%, leaving it more than 12% below its October peak. The VIX closed at 35.89, its highest level in 19 months.

The week's marquee listing was no safer. Anthropic priced its initial public offering at $83 and raised $93.5 billion, according to BigGo. The shares opened on Thursday at $111.50, touched $115, closed that day at $97.17 and ended Friday at $88.91. That is still about 7% above the offer price, a fact that Friday's buyers will find more comforting than Thursday's did. Robert Pavlik of Dakota Wealth observed that the stock had "faded relatively quickly."

## Concentration, rediscovered

Asia's exposure is not a mystery. Taiwan's benchmark leans heavily on the companies that make the chips. When the buyers of those chips are marked down, the makers follow, and the makers' indices follow them. SoftBank's 16% drop is the purest version of the pattern, being a holding company whose valuation depends heavily on what the market thinks of the things it holds.

The mood among strategists is what one would expect. Arun Sundaram of CFRA described the situation to the wire as "a meltdown right now," according to the BigGo account. A Goldman Sachs note dated November 20, as quoted there, said the selloff had "expanded this week from an AI unwind to a broader growth downgrade." That is a significant sentence from a bank, which tend to reserve the word "downgrade" for other people's growth.

There was a dissent. David Kelly of JPMorgan Asset Management said, per the same report, that "this is not a fatal illness." It is the traditional position of a fund manager at the bottom of a drawdown, and it has the merit of being unfalsifiable until Monday.

## What Monday has to answer

Seventeen and a half percent in a week is not a rounding error, even for an index that had enjoyed a long run. The Taiex's slide suggests that the region's investors were either more concentrated, more leveraged, or simply earlier to leave than their American counterparts. The next few sessions will say which, without needing to be asked.

Charles Schwab's market update, published at the open on Friday, had already described the setting. The firm put the PHLX Semiconductor Index almost 30% below its recent peaks and called the week the worst for US stocks since April 2025. Nathan Peterson of the Schwab Center for Financial Research said investors were "selling into any rallies" instead of buying pullbacks, which is a polite description of a market in which the bid has gone for a long lunch.

The macro backdrop is unhelpful. BigGo reports that futures price a December Federal Reserve rate increase at about 95%, so the central bank is expected to tighten into a growth scare. Chips are long-duration assets, and long-duration assets tend not to enjoy that combination.

The closing observation is a modest one. For several years the market treated the concentration of the AI supply chain in a handful of companies as evidence of quality. On Friday it was treated as evidence of concentration. Both descriptions were accurate throughout, which is the awkward thing about accurate descriptions.
