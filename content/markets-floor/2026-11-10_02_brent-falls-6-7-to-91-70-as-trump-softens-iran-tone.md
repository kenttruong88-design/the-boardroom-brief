# Brent Falls 6.7% to $91.70 as Trump Softens Iran Tone

Brent crude fell 6.73% to $91.70 a barrel in Asian trading on Tuesday, a drop of $6.62, after President Donald Trump said US military action against Iran is not necessary "at this point" and that new attacks are highly unlikely while talks continue. US West Texas Intermediate fell 5.45% to $85.88, according to market data compiled by JournalArta, which timestamped its figures at 04:35 GMT.

The price action was a textbook removal of risk premium. Nothing about the physical supply of oil changed overnight. What changed was the vocabulary. Analysts cited by JournalArta said the more conciliatory language took geopolitical insurance out of the price, which is the kind of thing that happens when a barrel of crude is partly a commodity and partly a forecast about a press conference.

## The caveats are doing a lot of work

The market's relief is conspicuously unsupported by the facts on the water. According to the same report, Mr Trump plans to keep a naval blockade in the Strait of Hormuz, ships have been hit near the waterway, and senior US and Gulf officials doubt a deal is imminent. Iran's security council chief, Mohsen Rezaei, dismissed talk of negotiations as "fake news about imaginary talks". Qatar's prime minister urged restraint. A trader reading this list and concluding that the correct response is to sell a further six percent of the world's most politically sensitive commodity is making a bold assumption about tomorrow's tone.

Supply is not helping the bulls either. An OPEC survey showed October output rose, led by Saudi Arabia, Kuwait and Iraq. More barrels, fewer threats: it is a combination that tends to produce lower prices, whatever the hardliners in Tehran say about imaginary talks.

For context, US crude futures were trading around $88 a barrel on Monday morning, and fell about 3% to roughly $87 as stocks rallied, according to StockTitan's live coverage of CNBC reporting. Tuesday's move extends that slide. JournalArta's headline from the previous day had Brent down nearly 5%, which suggests a two-session decline that has taken a sizeable bite out of the war premium.

## Everything else, briefly

Gold behaved in a manner that will console no one. Spot gold slipped 0.48% to $4,114.09 an ounce, down $19.94, as the dollar weakened. The Dollar Index fell 0.6% to 101.682. Lower oil, a weaker dollar and softer gold is a slightly peculiar combination, since the usual script has a weaker dollar helping gold, and the market appears to have decided that peace talks, imaginary or otherwise, reduce the appetite for safety.

Elsewhere in the metals complex the mood was brighter. Silver rose 2.57% to $53.135 an ounce, and copper gained 2.09% to $6.348 a pound. Natural gas jumped 7.31% to $3.217 per million British thermal units, which sat oddly beside the crude slide and served as a reminder that not every energy contract reads the same newspaper.

Equities took the hint. In London, miners led the market, with Antofagasta up 6.17% and Anglo American up 5.21%, a move that fits neatly with copper's gain. The Nikkei 225 rose 2.33% to 60,142.12, and Singapore's Straits Times and Australia's ASX 200 each gained 1.39%. The rally is not an endorsement of the diplomatic process. It is an endorsement of the price of oil, which is a different and more fickle thing. Applied Materials and Cisco report after the US close on November 12, and Nvidia reports on November 17, so traders will soon have corporate news to distract them from the Gulf.

The US ten-year Treasury yield was little changed at 4.864%. That is the figure to hold onto, because the bond market is where the real argument is being had. If cheaper oil gives the Federal Reserve a more benign inflation reading, the case for a December rate rise weakens slightly. If it does not, the energy retreat is a rounding error against everything else.

Today's October consumer price report, due at 8:30 a.m. Eastern, will be the first serious test. Gasoline prices were expected to have eased in October, which would flatter the headline figure. It will not, however, say anything about November, when crude was at the mercy of a single politician's choice of adjective.

The oil market has priced in a calmer Iran. It has not yet been asked to price in a calmer Iran that stays calm for more than a news cycle.
