# Nasdaq Falls 5.3% as S&P 500 Enters Correction on Seventh Straight Loss

Wall Street closed on Friday with the Nasdaq Composite down 5.28% at 22,617.73, the S&P 500 down 3.70% at about 6,910, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average down 1.93%, or 949.73 points, at 48,147.33, according to Reuters. It was the S&P 500's seventh consecutive daily decline, its longest losing streak since February 2020.

The arithmetic of a correction is unglamorous. Reuters reported that both the S&P 500 and the Dow now sit more than 10% below their record closes, the conventional threshold, which Friday crossed with some enthusiasm. Schwab's market commentary added that the Nasdaq is now in the red for 2026. For the week, Reuters said the S&P 500 and the Dow posted their largest weekly percentage losses since April 2025, while the Nasdaq suffered its largest weekly drop since March 2020. Schwab called it Wall Street's worst week since the tariff turmoil of April 2025, a comparison that will be of limited comfort to anyone who owned stocks during the tariff turmoil of April 2025.

## The usual suspect, plus the new ones

Nvidia fell 13.8%, according to Reuters, and Axios reported that the chipmaker has now shed more than $2.5 trillion of market value since its all-time high on October 6. AMD lost 13.3%, Schwab said, and the sector's more leveraged enthusiasts fared worse: Nebius fell 16.1% and CoreWeave about 12%. Schwab put the damage to the technology sector at more than 7% in a single session.

Axios assembled the cast of explanations: doubts about whether AI spending is sustainable, tightening credit, bipartisan scrutiny of chip export controls, midterm voters who backed data-center moratoriums, and the departure of Jensen Huang as Nvidia's chief executive. Schwab noted a late-Friday Reuters report that the Securities and Exchange Commission is examining Nvidia's accounting practices, which suggests the list of reasons is not yet closed to new submissions.

The week's marquee listing was no refuge. Anthropic fell 9.1% in its second day as a public company, Reuters said, though it remained above its $83 offer price after closing Thursday 17.1% above it. A stock that drops nearly a tenth in a day and still leaves its IPO buyers in profit is, by the standards of this week, a defensive holding.

## Breadth, or the lack of it

The selling was not subtle. Volume reached 25.19 billion shares, against a 20-day average of 19.50 billion, according to Reuters. On the New York Stock Exchange, decliners outnumbered advancers 3.77 to 1; on the Nasdaq the ratio was 4.27 to 1. Amazon fell 3.9%, Meta 5.9% and Intuit 12.8%, while Caterpillar, an industrial with no known exposure to anyone's chatbot, lost 5%.

Financials, usually the place investors go when they want to feel like adults, offered no shelter. Schwab said JPMorgan Chase and Bank of America both fell more than 3% and Citigroup 4%. Only consumer staples finished higher, and by less than 1%. The market, in other words, found its safe haven in toothpaste.

## The rock and the hard place

The macro backdrop did not help. Reuters reported that Federal Reserve Governor Lisa Cook said the policy rate is not high enough, and that futures priced a near-certain 25 basis point increase at the December meeting, according to the CME FedWatch tool. Schwab put the odds at 92%. Kevin Gordon, head of macro research at the Schwab Center for Financial Research, described the setting as a "rock-and-a-hard-place backdrop for stocks." The rock is a central bank that wants to tighten into a correction. The hard place is an equity market whose largest constituents have just discovered what happens when growth assumptions meet tighter credit.

Bill Northey, senior investment director at US Bank Wealth Management, told Reuters that "sentiment is clearly winning the day today." Chuck Carlson, chief executive of Horizon Investment Services, said, per Reuters, that Nvidia had "done everything right" in its quarterly report. This is the most uncomfortable detail of the week. Nvidia reported results and raised its outlook, and the shares fell anyway. When good earnings cannot defend a stock, investors are no longer trading the earnings.

## What to watch

The first question is whether the Fed's resolve survives a 10% drawdown. The second is whether the S&P 500's seventh straight loss becomes an eighth, which would take the streak past anything the index has produced since before the pandemic. The third is whether the selling is about valuation, about governance at Nvidia, or about financing, because each answer points to a different kind of bottom.

For now, the index has told us what it thinks. It prices the AI trade as a story that needed everyone to keep believing it, and on Friday a lot of people took a break from believing. Correction territory is, formally, just a number. It is also what a market looks like when it has run out of reasons to be polite.
