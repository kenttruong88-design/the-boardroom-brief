# S&P 500 Needs Only to Avoid a 0.2% Drop to Beat Last Year's Thanksgiving Week

The S&P 500 walks into Friday's half-day session, which ends at 1 p.m. Eastern, with a modest request of the market gods: do not fall more than about 0.2%. Clear that bar and this year's Thanksgiving week will have beaten last year's, according to Yahoo Finance and 24/7 Wall St, which published the arithmetic on Thursday.

The benchmark closed Wednesday at 7,182.78, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average at 50,182.95. For the holiday-shortened week, the S&P is up 3.94%, the Dow 4.23% and the Nasdaq Composite 4.28%. Last year's Thanksgiving week for the S&P was a gain of 3.73%. A Friday close near 7,168 would square the two. Wall Street was shut on Thursday for the holiday.

## The week before the week

A recovery is only as impressive as the hole it climbs out of, and this one is deep. In the week ended November 20, the S&P 500 fell 8.61%, the Dow 8.02% and the Nasdaq Composite 10.02%. The VIX, Wall Street's preferred fear gauge, peaked at 35.89 on November 20.

The rebound has been brisk and, by the standards of a market that has just misplaced a tenth of its Nasdaq, almost insultingly cheerful. On Monday, November 23, the S&P gained 4.73% to 7,237.17. On Tuesday it surrendered 0.50%, closing at 7,201.09. Wednesday took another quarter of a percentage point off, by the arithmetic of the closing prints. The VIX, meanwhile, had eased to 26.36 by Tuesday: still elevated, but no longer the sort of number that gets a risk committee out of bed.

Yahoo's account attributes the recovery to stocks regaining "most of their steep early losses as a pullback in AI stocks eased". The article offers no named strategist to explain why a pullback in AI stocks needed to ease so promptly. This correspondent notes only that the same asset class that caused the rout is now credited with ending it, which is a tidy piece of accountability.

Not everyone's feelings have been repaired by the arithmetic. On Saxo's reading of Wednesday's session, the Nasdaq 100 briefly entered bear-market territory before recovering. A bear market that lasts a lunch break is, technically, still a bear market, in the way that a fire that lasts a lunch break is still a fire.

## How good is good?

The headline claim is relative, so the comparisons matter. The S&P's best Thanksgiving week on record in the Yahoo and 24/7 Wall St comparison was 2008, at 12.03%. That week followed the collapse of Lehman Brothers, which is to say that the market's best recoveries tend to be borrowed against its worst moments. This year's 3.94% is roughly a third of that, and it is measured from a Friday that left the index 8.61% poorer than the Friday before.

Put differently, the S&P is up for the week and, taking the two weeks together, still down. The index sits at 7,182.78, with a year-to-date gain of 4.9%. The Dow is up 4.4% on the year. Anyone who bought in January and held through everything has, in aggregate, been paid a rate of return comparable to a decent savings account, for the added entertainment of a week in which the Nasdaq Composite lost 10.02% and then spent the next one winning a good part of it back.

The thin holiday liquidity deserves a footnote. Half-sessions after Thanksgiving are traditionally low-volume affairs in which a few large orders can move an index more than the news does. Saxo's morning note put US futures up about 0.6% for the S&P and the Nasdaq 100 and 0.5% for the Dow after the holiday. If those hold into the open, the 0.2% cushion looks comfortable. If they do not, the market will have discovered a new and efficient way of failing to beat 2025.

## What to watch

Three things decide whether Friday is a footnote or a headline. The first is whether AI-related shares, the cause and the cure of the past fortnight, behave as a group. The second is the 10-year Treasury yield, which Saxo put at 4.854% and which remains the number against which every equity multiple is eventually judged. The third is whether anyone is at their desk to notice.

The sober reading is that nothing about the past nine trading days has been resolved; it has merely been priced and repriced at a pace faster than the facts. A week that loses 8.61% and a week that gains 3.94% belong to the same market, and the market has not volunteered which of them it meant.

Still, credit where it is due. Few asset classes can lose roughly a tenth of their value in one week, win back nearly half of it in the next three sessions, and be described, with a straight face, as having a good Thanksgiving. Wall Street has once again demonstrated that gratitude is easiest to feel when the alternative has just been rehearsed.
