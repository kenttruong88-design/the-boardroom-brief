# Nvidia Jumps 31.5% as Dow Adds 3.1% and Nasdaq Surges 6.5%

Wall Street staged a rally on Monday, November 23 that gave the Nasdaq its best day in more than a year and a half, after Nvidia said it had reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive. According to Trading Economics, the Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 1,496 points, or 3.11 percent, to close at 49,643. The S&P 500 jumped 4.73 percent. The Nasdaq Composite gained 6.51 percent, which the data provider described as its strongest single-day gain since April 9, 2025.

## One press release, one very large rebound

The catalyst was a press release. In a filing dated November 23, Nvidia said it had reinstated Huang as president, chief executive and board director, effective immediately. Colette Kress, who had been interim chief executive, remains chief financial officer. Board chair Harvey Jones said the directors believe Huang "is the right person to lead NVIDIA." The company said the board had received input from senior levels of the United States government and concluded that the reinstatement poses no national security risk. A separate investigation by independent directors, into reports of foreign intelligence targeting of the chief executive, continues.

The shares did what shares do when a risk premium is removed by press release. Trading Economics reported that Nvidia rose 31.5 percent, the biggest gain on the Dow. Its price table showed the stock at $165.05, up $39.52. A week earlier the same stock had fallen 14.01 percent in a single session, on November 16, when the Dow dropped 739 points and the Nasdaq lost 626, according to Schaeffer's Research. The round trip is worth stating plainly: a leadership question moved the most valuable corner of the market by more than the quarter's earnings had.

Those earnings, for the record, were not the problem. The problem was the uncertainty, which Kiplinger described on November 9 when it reported that Nvidia's board had opened an independent investigation and Huang had written to employees that leading the company was "the privilege of my life." On that day the stock fell 4.3 percent while the Dow rose 2.2 percent. The tape has been telling the same story for two weeks: the semiconductor trade is a bet on one man and a few other people.

## The chip complex follows

Nvidia did not rise alone. Trading Economics listed Micron up 19 percent, Marvell Technology up 18.4 percent, Applied Materials up 14.6 percent and Lam Research up 14.4 percent. Intel gained 16.3 percent. The AI infrastructure names that trade as leveraged opinions on Nvidia behaved as designed, with CoreWeave rising 22.3 percent and Nebius 20.3 percent. Caterpillar, an industrial company with no obvious exposure to the governance of a chipmaker, added 7.17 percent, and Cisco rose 6.80 percent.

Megacaps participated, too. The same tables showed Tesla up 11.46 percent, Meta up 6.55 percent, Alphabet up 6.34 percent and Amazon up 3.47 percent. Apple and Microsoft, the stocks investors own to avoid having opinions, rose 1.90 percent and 1.65 percent respectively. Exxon Mobil, one of the few large names to fall, slipped 1.17 percent in one of the tables.

One large stock moved the other way for its own reasons. CrowdStrike fell 8.9 percent on the day it agreed to buy Zscaler in an all-stock deal valued at about $43.9 billion, according to the companies' filing. Acquirers, as a class, rarely get a rally with the announcement.

## A note on the numbers

Readers who check the figures will find that Trading Economics does not agree with itself. Its news text puts the Dow at 49,643, while its index table lists 49,720.77, up 3.01 percent. Its table shows the VIX at 26.62, down 9.27 percent. No exchange close was available from the sources consulted for this piece, so the figures above are attributed and should be treated as approximate until the official settlements are published. The direction, size and cause of the move are not in dispute. The decimals are.

## The arithmetic of a rebound

A gain of 31.5 percent does not erase a 14 percent one-day loss and the damage around it. It does, however, return the stock to a level at which the market can again argue about valuation rather than about who is in the corner office. That is a better argument to be having, though not a quieter one.

Volatility fell, but not to calm. A VIX reading in the mid-twenties is a market that has stopped panicking and not yet agreed to relax. The Federal Reserve meets on December 8 and 9, and the futures market has been pricing a quarter-point rate rise with near certainty. Monday's rally changed nothing about that arithmetic. It merely reminded everyone that on the right day, one company's personnel decision can outweigh a central bank's.

There is a lesson here for portfolio managers, and it is not a comforting one. The index rose nearly five percent in a day not because the economy improved, or inflation eased, or profits surprised, but because a board changed its mind about a person. The market had priced a succession plan that did not exist, and when the plan was withdrawn it priced the old arrangement back in, with interest. In finance this is called discovery. In every other profession it is called finding out.
