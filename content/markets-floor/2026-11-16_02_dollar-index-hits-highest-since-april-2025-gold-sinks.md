# Dollar Index Hits Highest Since April 2025 as Gold Slips to $3,886

The US dollar index closed Friday at 103.38, its highest level since April 2025, while December gold futures settled at $3,886.30 an ounce after a week in which the metal fell roughly 4% to new 52-week lows, according to a closing recap published by Investrade on November 13. The dollar rose about 1% on the week, its strongest showing in two months.

Gold's Friday gain was $1.00. It is worth recording, if only because it was the first up day after three consecutive losses, and because it is the sort of rally one describes using the word "technically".

## A hedge without a pulse

Gold is held by people who expect trouble. This week there was plenty of trouble to expect: a hotter-than-forecast US inflation report, a Federal Reserve openly discussing rate hikes, a chief executive resigning from the world's most valuable chipmaker, and a share sale of more than 800 million Anthropic shares on the way. Gold fell anyway.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Gold pays nothing, and when the 10-year Treasury yield rises to 5.152% and the dollar strengthens, the opportunity cost of holding it climbs in step. Hawkish remarks from Fed Governor Christopher Waller and Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan last week, as reported by Kitco, did the rest. Investors are now assigning roughly an 88% probability to a quarter-point hike in December, according to the Investrade recap. A non-yielding asset struggles in that environment, regardless of how worried its owners feel.

Silver, the cheaper and more excitable sibling, closed at $49.12 an ounce, down 1.89% on the day. Investrade notes the metal is now about 59% below its January high. That is not a pullback. That is a change of address.

## Everything priced in dollars looks worse

Bitcoin was on course for a sixth straight weekly decline and traded below $70,000 for the first time since late August, the recap said. The cryptocurrency's long-standing pitch as a refuge from conventional monetary policy continues to be tested by conventional monetary policy.

The euro lost about 0.8% on the week. EUR/USD closed near 1.1022, according to Investrade, and CMC Markets noted that the pair had broken through support around 1.115 and was testing 1.105. Both figures describe the same direction, which is down.

The yen is under the most pressure. USD/JPY closed at 159.75, and CMC Markets said the pair is testing the 160 level. Traders have long watched that number for evidence that Tokyo might intervene.

The calendar helps determine whether they will need to: Japan's October inflation figure is due Friday, with core consumer prices expected to rise 2.3% from a year earlier, up from 2.1% in September, according to CMC. A hot print would strengthen the case for a Bank of Japan hike in December, which might ease the pressure on the yen. A cooler one would leave 160 undefended.

## Oil keeps the lights on

Commodities that people actually consume held up better than the ones they merely own. Brent crude ended Friday at $95.84 a barrel, up two cents, and West Texas Intermediate at $88.56, down 18 cents. Energy was the best-performing S&P 500 sector on the week.

The pattern deserves a plain reading. Assets that pay a yield, or are used up in the course of ordinary commerce, have outperformed assets valued mostly because other people may one day also want them. Last week, that group included gold, silver and bitcoin. A rising dollar and a 5% Treasury are not kind to any asset whose case rests on narrative.

Context helps. Silver's fall of about 59% from its January high is far more dramatic than gold's roughly 4% weekly loss, and a good reminder that precious metals can lose a great deal of value without anyone declaring a crisis.

The dollar's strength is not confined to the euro and the yen. The index's climb to 103.38 puts it at the highest since April 2025, Investrade said, and the rise in US yields is the obvious driver. When American government debt pays above 5%, capital has a reason to come home, and currencies that pay less tend to be left waiting at the airport.

None of this means gold has been refuted. Metals have survived harsher repricings than a single bad week. But for now, the world's oldest safe haven is trading in the same direction as the newest, and is losing to a government bond. A haven earns the name by holding up when the harbour is rough. This week the harbour was rough, and the haven checked the yield curve and went home.
