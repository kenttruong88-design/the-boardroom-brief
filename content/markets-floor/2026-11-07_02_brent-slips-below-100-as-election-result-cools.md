# Brent Slips Below $100 as Election Result Cools Iran Fears

Brent crude futures fell below $100 a barrel on Friday, according to Yahoo Finance's market coverage, after a roughly 9% drop on Wednesday that Benzinga attributed to bets that the US midterm result lowers the odds of renewed fighting with Iran. The energy sector, which has had a very good war, had a very bad week: the Energy Select Sector SPDR fund (XLE) fell 11%, Yahoo reported.

The December crude contract was quoted at $90.34 on Friday, down 18 cents or 0.20%, on Yahoo's quote panel. Gold slipped 0.83% to $4,079.50. Neither moved enough to disturb anyone's weekend.

## A war premium, repriced

To understand the size of the move, consider where oil had been. The Bank of England's November Monetary Policy Committee minutes put Brent futures at an average of $108 a barrel over the 15 working days to 27 October, which the Bank said was 38% above the level assumed in its July Report. UK natural gas futures averaged 216 pence per therm, up 81% on the same comparison. The minutes also describe the conflict in the Middle East as being in its ninth month.

A price that has been averaging $108 and then loses nine percent in a session is not responding to supply and demand. It is responding to a change in the probability of something unpleasant. Markets call this "repricing geopolitical risk." Everyone else calls it relief.

The Wednesday move was not a Friday-only story either. Brent had already dropped on the election result before the week's second shock, the technology sell-off, took over the front pages. Oil, in other words, had the week's first tantrum and was upstaged by the second, which is a fate that rarely befalls a commodity that large.

## Who loses when peace is cheaper

The energy sector's 11% weekly loss is a reminder that oil equities were carrying the war premium alongside the commodity. Yahoo's sector figures show technology down about 7% on the week and energy down 11%, a combination that left investors with remarkably few places to be right.

Consumers, by contrast, have reason to cheer. The University of Michigan's preliminary November survey showed year-ahead inflation expectations falling to 4.3% from 4.7%, the lowest since August, according to Benzinga. The survey does not attribute the improvement to oil, and this publication will not either, but nobody has ever been sad to see a fuel price fall.

Central banks are watching the same chart with a different set of feelings. The Bank of England's minutes said direct energy effects contributed around one percentage point of the 3.5% UK inflation reading in September, mostly through fuels. The Bank raised its rate to 4% on Thursday. A lower oil price will not undo that decision, but it does give the policymakers who voted for it something to argue about in December.

The inflation backdrop explains why the Fed and its peers care. Yahoo Finance's Jennifer Schonberger noted that wages "did catch up" last month to the pace of inflation, which grew 3.1%. The 10-year Treasury yield sat at 4.86% after Friday's jobs data, according to Benzinga, so cheaper oil is arriving in a world where borrowing is not cheap. Energy relief is helpful; it is not a rate cut.

## The fine print

There are two caveats. First, one session does not make a trend; the Bank of England's figures show that even in late October Brent was running well above the level its July forecast assumed, and a price that has fallen 9% can rise 9% just as quickly. Second, the move rests on a political judgment about the behaviour of a third country, which is the kind of thing that has historically proven robust for about as long as it takes to read this sentence.

Traders with long memories will note that "below $100" is not a low price. It is a lower price. In a market where the war has been running for nine months and the benchmark was at $108 only a fortnight ago, a three-figure barrel is a bargain only relative to the alternative.

Friday's closing picture, then: the oil price has fallen, the stocks of the companies that sell it have fallen further, and the assets that were meant to hedge the whole arrangement, namely gold, also fell. The one position that paid on Friday was having no position at all, a strategy that is available to every reader at no commission.
