# European Stocks Rally Up to 2.1% While America Is Away Carving Turkey

European equities rose sharply on Thursday, with Germany's DAX up 2.1% to 25,215, on a day when the world's largest stock market was closed for Thanksgiving. The Stoxx Europe 600 added 1.7%, according to a Saxo market note published on November 27. The continent, it appears, can do perfectly well without American supervision, at least for one session.

London's FTSE 100 gained 1.8% to 10,609, and the CAC 40 in Paris rose 1.6% to 8,109. Saxo reported that US futures were pointing higher after the holiday, with S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100 contracts up about 0.6% and Dow contracts up about 0.5%. The rally followed a Wednesday session in which, in Saxo's words, the Nasdaq 100 "briefly entered bear market territory before recovering".

## A holiday in the sun

There is a long tradition of European markets rallying into an American holiday, and an equally long tradition of attributing it to something other than low volume. Fewer participants mean fewer sellers, and a market with fewer sellers tends to look confident. This correspondent offers no view on whether 2.1% in the DAX is conviction or merely the absence of an opposing opinion.

The backdrop was not obviously benign. Saxo noted new US tariffs on China, the European Union and about a dozen other economies, and recorded Japan's protest at a 15% levy. Equities rose anyway. The market's reading of the tariff news appears to be that it is a known risk, and that known risks, like known relatives, are best greeted briefly and from a distance.

Elsewhere, the Treasury market kept its counsel. Saxo put the 10-year yield at 4.854%, a level that sits awkwardly beside equity indices recently hunting for records. In foreign exchange, Saxo had the dollar at roughly 159.25 yen and the euro at about 1.0960 against the dollar. The New Zealand dollar fell by more than 1% in early Asian trade.

## Dr. Copper reports poorly

The more interesting number is not in an equity index. According to Saxo, copper is down about 17% in November, which it described as one of its worst monthly performances in more than a decade. The metal is known to traders as "Dr. Copper" because its price is thought to diagnose the health of the global economy. The diagnosis for November is not encouraging, and it sits oddly alongside equity indices climbing by two percentage points in a day.

There are two ways to reconcile the two signals. Either equity investors know something that copper does not, or copper knows something that equity investors would prefer to defer until January. A 17% fall in a month is too large to be only a technical event, and too ambiguous in its cause, in a market that has also seen a sharp sell-off in artificial-intelligence shares, to be pinned on any single culprit. The doctor has filed a note. Nobody has read it aloud.

## The case of Kioxia

The most colourful corporate story in Saxo's note concerns Kioxia, the Japanese memory-chip maker. Its shares have fallen by more than 40% this month and trade at around 1.7 times forward earnings, a multiple more commonly associated with a company that has been asked to leave the building. They surged, according to Saxo, on a reported tie-up with Nvidia. The word "reported" is carrying a great deal of weight in that sentence, and this column has not seen the report itself.

The same note observed that Hewlett Packard Enterprise fell despite an earnings beat, after warnings about memory and NAND supply. Best Buy rose 3.1% in pre-market trading on Wednesday after a quarter in which it beat and raised. Taken together, the two moves describe an economy in which the winners are the businesses that sell finished goods to consumers, and the worry is about the components inside them.

## What the numbers say

Strip the commentary away and the day offers three facts. Europe's major indices rose between 1.6% and 2.1% without American participation. Copper, a commodity with a reputation for honesty, has had a terrible month. And a Japanese chip company can lose two-fifths of its value in four weeks, rise on a report, and still be priced like a distressed asset.

None of that is a forecast. It is a description of a market that has become comfortable holding two views at the same time. On Friday, Wall Street returns for a half-day session ending at 1 p.m. Eastern, and the Americans will have to decide which of Europe's views to adopt. Given the available time, they may split the difference and go home early.
