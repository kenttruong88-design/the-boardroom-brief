# Fed Minutes: "Almost All" Officials Expect More Hikes; 10-Year Holds 5.08%

The minutes of the Federal Reserve's 27-28 October meeting, published on Wednesday at 2pm Eastern, said that "almost all" policymakers expect further interest-rate increases. The September minutes said "most". Nobody discussed a cut, according to TheStreet's account of the document. The ten-year Treasury yield stood at 5.08 percent in mid-morning trading, against 5.10 percent on Tuesday, and the market did what it does with news it has already priced: it nodded.

## What the minutes said

The fed funds target range currently stands at 3.75 percent to 4 percent. Reversing all three of the 2025 cuts would take it to 4.25 to 4.5 percent. Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack dissented at the October meeting. According to the minutes, "a couple" of participants said a move larger than 25 basis points could be considered in December if inflation data disappoint. Core CPI has risen about 0.3 percent a month for three months running, which is the sort of consistency that makes a data-dependent central bank feel unusually dependent.

Governor Christopher Waller has been the loudest voice for escalation. He backs a 50 basis point hike at the 8-9 December meeting and has said the Fed may need to "shock people". CME FedWatch put the odds of a half-point move at about 10 percent on Wednesday, up from zero before Waller's remarks on 10 November. Bowman and Williams favour a gradual path, TheStreet reported, while Dallas Fed president Lorie Logan expects at least two more quarter-point increases. A committee in which the doves argue for a steady crawl and the hawks for a sprint is, in the strictest sense, still a committee in agreement.

The bond market took the hint last week. The two-year Treasury yield jumped by the most since 2024, a move that suggested investors were repricing the path of policy rather than its destination.

## The tape

Equities took Wednesday's minutes in stride, though the session did not lack for incident. By 10am Eastern, the Associated Press reported, the S&P 500 was up 0.2 percent after a four-day losing streak, the Nasdaq composite was up 0.8 percent and the Dow Jones Industrial Average was down 231 points, or 0.5 percent. In London the FTSE 100 fell 0.7 percent after UK inflation reached its highest in a year, which added pressure on the Bank of England to keep raising rates. South Korea's Kospi rose 2 percent a day after posting its lowest close since February. This is what a global rate scare looks like when it is working properly: everyone is somewhere else.

Retail earnings gave the equity tape a second storyline. The AP showed Target up 5.8 percent and TJX up 9.9 percent at 10am, while Lowe's was down 0.6 percent. The mix suggested that investors still pay for evidence of a consumer who is spending, even when the price of money points the other way.

## The survey

Bank of America's November Global Fund Manager Survey, cited by TheStreet, found that 50 percent of respondents expect a soft landing, 42 percent expect no landing and 3 percent expect a hard landing. Some 47 percent described the outlook as stagflation. Respondents held 3.6 percent of their portfolios in cash. Bank of America's own strategists now expect Fed hikes at each of the next three meetings, and they set 2027 year-end targets for the S&P 500 at 7,926, for gold at $4,341 and for the ten-year yield at 4.9 percent, down from 5.1 percent.

There is a pleasing symmetry in a survey in which half of fund managers expect a soft landing and nearly half describe the same economy as stagflationary. Both camps appear to have been invited to the same meeting and to have left with different agendas.

## The read

The ten-year yield at 5.08 percent is the number that most investors should keep in view. It is the benchmark against which every equity multiple, mortgage and corporate bond is ultimately judged, and it has been hovering around 5.1 percent while the Fed signals that the policy rate has further to climb. If almost all officials are right, the front of the curve has some catching up to do. If the minority arguing for caution is right, the long end is already doing the Fed's work for it.

For now the market has accepted the premise. The Fed has stopped saying "most" and started saying "almost all", and in the vocabulary of central banking that is the closest thing to shouting. Traders, who have heard it, are listening for the next adjective.
