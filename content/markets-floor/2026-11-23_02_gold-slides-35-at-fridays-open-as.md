# Gold Slides 3.5% at Friday's Open as Ten-Year Yield Ends at 4.91%

Gold fell 3.46%, or $127.30, to $3,558 at the start of Friday's US session, while the 10-year Treasury yield fell to 4.96% and the VIX rose 10% to 31.30, according to Charles Schwab's market update published at 9:16 a.m. Eastern time. By the close, BigGo Finance reports, the yield had dropped 14 basis points on the day to 4.91% and the VIX had reached 35.89, its highest in 19 months.

## The refuge that wasn't

The textbook response to an equity rout is a flight to safety, and the textbook lists two destinations: government bonds and gold. On Friday, one of them cooperated. The 10-year yield fell, which means Treasury prices rose, but the fall was a moderate 14 basis points on a day when the S&P 500 lost 3.70% and the Nasdaq Composite 5.28%, per BigGo.

Gold, the other refuge, did not attend. Schwab's snapshot put it down by more than 3% before the stock market had opened. The Schwab note puts the dollar index at 103.63, down 0.25%, so the metal's decline cannot be explained by a rampant dollar. West Texas Intermediate crude was little changed at $82.33, down 0.13%, and bitcoin was at $61,430, down 0.98%. In other words, the commodity that was meant to protect investors fell the most of any asset in the table, which is its own kind of volatility.

The session also deteriorated as it went. At the open, Schwab had the S&P 500 down 2.02% at 7,175.83, the Dow down 2.19% and the Nasdaq Composite down 2.25%. By the close, BigGo puts the S&P 500 down 3.70% at 6,910.39 and the Nasdaq down 5.28%. Anyone who bought the opening dip was, by the afternoon, an unintended long-term investor.

A likely explanation, though not one that the reports confirm, is that investors facing losses elsewhere sold what they could sell. That is a familiar feature of fast markets: when margin calls arrive, assets are liquidated for being liquid, not for being wrong.

## The Fed in the background

Nothing about the rate outlook softened to help. Futures price a December Federal Reserve rate increase at about 95%, per BigGo, and the Kiplinger economic calendar, updated on November 20, put the odds above 97%. Schwab, citing CME FedWatch, said the market expects the Fed's target range to be 50 to 75 basis points higher by next June.

That matters for gold, which pays no interest and therefore competes poorly against a Treasury note yielding close to 5%. It also matters for stocks, because a central bank expected to raise rates into a selloff is a central bank that has no obvious rescue to offer. The yield decline may be less a sign of confidence in bonds than of a bet that growth, rather than inflation, is about to become the problem.

Schwab's Nathan Peterson described the equity mood as one in which investors are "selling into any rallies," not buying the pullbacks. If so, the lower yield is not a vote of confidence but a record of where the money went when it ran out of other places to stand.

## What to watch

The data calendar this week is short but loaded. Kiplinger lists the October PCE price index and third-quarter GDP for Wednesday morning, with US markets closed on Thursday for Thanksgiving. A hot PCE print would add to the hike pricing, and a cool one would test whether anything in the market can still be cheered.

The inputs to that print are not reassuring. September's PCE price index rose 3.6% from a year earlier, with the core measure at 3.1%, according to the Bureau of Economic Analysis figures cited by Kiplinger. The Cleveland Fed's inflation nowcast, as of November 19, pointed to 3.8% for October headline PCE and 3.2% for core. Kevin Nam of BCA Research was quoted by Kiplinger noting that the October retail sales control group rose 0.6% against a 0.3% consensus forecast, which suggests the consumer has not read the equity market's notes.

The VIX at 35.89 is a figure worth keeping in view. It is the highest in 19 months, which means that insurance against falling prices is as expensive as it has been in that time, and that the people selling it are demanding a premium for doing so. Whether they turn out to have been underpaid will be known by Wednesday afternoon, or possibly by Tuesday, because markets do not always wait for the data.
