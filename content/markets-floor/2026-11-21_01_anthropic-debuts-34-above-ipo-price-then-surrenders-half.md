# Anthropic Debuts 34% Above IPO Price, Then Surrenders Half

Anthropic shares changed hands for the first time on Thursday at $111.50, which is 34.3 percent above the $83 IPO price. The stock then spent the afternoon returning roughly half of that premium to the market. Listed on the Nasdaq under the ticker ANT, it closed its first session at $97.17, up 17.1 percent on the offer price, according to StockTitan's day-one tally.

A disclosure is in order. Anthropic makes Claude, the AI model that assists this newsroom. The Alignment Times intends to be as dispassionate about its supplier as the average analyst is about a stock with a fair-value estimate lower than its price.

## The deal

Anthropic priced the offering on Wednesday, 18 November, above the $70 to $78 range it had marketed, at a valuation of about $1.5 trillion, Fortune reported. The company sold 980 million shares, up from the 825 million first indicated, for base proceeds of about $79.4 billion, rising to $91.6 billion if the over-allotment option is exercised in full. Seven strategic partners, including Google and Nvidia, agreed to buy a further $26 billion of non-voting stock at the IPO price. That lifts the potential total to $118 billion.

Fortune notes that a $1.5 trillion valuation would make this the second most valuable IPO on record, behind SpaceX's $1.77 trillion listing in June. The raise itself, at about $79 billion, exceeds SpaceX's $75 billion. It is one of the few categories in which the runner-up takes home the larger cheque.

The financials for the nine months to 30 September show revenue of $33.2 billion, against $2.5 billion in the same period a year earlier, and a net loss of $104 billion. Fortune attributes most of that loss to the accounting treatment of convertible notes. A loss of that size on revenue of that size is, in fairness, exactly the sort of arithmetic that accounting treatment exists to explain.

## The session

The shares were supposed to open at 9:30am. According to StockTitan, the first print arrived at 11:48am Eastern, roughly two hours into the session, via the Nasdaq opening auction. That was $111.50. A minute later the stock touched its high of $114.99, which proved to be the high of the day and, so far, of the company's life as a listed entity.

From there the tape ran in one direction. The low came at 1:18pm at $92.72, 19.4 percent below the high. The close, at $97.17, was 15.5 percent below the high and 12.9 percent below the first trade. About 832 million shares changed hands, equivalent to roughly 85 percent of the shares sold in the offering, which suggests that the early buyers and the early sellers did not struggle to find one another.

After hours the last print was $92.60 at 7:59pm. That is notable because Morningstar analyst Malik Ahmed Khan, quoted by Fortune, put fair value near $92 per share, on the argument that the valuation reflects the significant growth expected from AI adoption. The market therefore spent a full session travelling from 21 percent above that estimate to a rounding error from it. Price discovery is rarely so punctual.

## What the number is paying for

The case for the valuation rests on the growth. Revenue rose from $2.5 billion to $33.2 billion in a year, a thirteen-fold increase that no spreadsheet template anticipates. The case against rests on the same growth being asked to continue at a scale that the valuation already assumes. Chief executive Dario Amodei, in the roadshow video quoted by Fortune, said, "I think if we do this right we can create an age of miracles." Investors have now been invited to price the conditional clause.

The share structure adds a second variable. The strategic partners' $26 billion is in non-voting stock bought at the IPO price, so the largest customers and suppliers are also, in effect, shareholders. Circular arrangements of this kind are a feature of the AI trade generally, and a diligent reader of the prospectus will want to know how much of the $33.2 billion in revenue flows from the same parties that are buying the stock.

PitchBook's live coverage, citing Bloomberg, put Amazon at about 15 percent of the company after the offering and Google at about 14 percent. Two of the largest companies in the world therefore own close to 30 percent of the business between them, which is a useful reminder that the free float is a smaller number than the headline valuation implies.

This column was unable to verify Friday's session from sources it trusts, and it declines to guess. What can be said is that the stock closed Thursday above the IPO price, below the first trade and, for a moment after hours, almost exactly where the one published fair-value estimate said it should be. A trillion-dollar company has, in its first day, achieved something many smaller ones never manage: it disappointed the optimists and the pessimists in the same afternoon.
