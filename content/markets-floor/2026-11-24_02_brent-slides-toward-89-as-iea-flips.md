# Brent Slides Toward $89 as IEA Flips the Oil Market to Surplus

Crude oil has spent two weeks quietly dismantling the premium that the Iran war built into it. Brent traded around $88.84 a barrel on the latest quotes shown by Trading Economics, down about 4 percent, with US crude near $80. A fortnight ago Brent was above $90 and a headline in itself.

## From deficit to surplus

The turning point was the International Energy Agency. On Monday, November 16, December West Texas Intermediate fell 3.2 percent to settle at $85.72 a barrel, after the agency reversed an earlier forecast of a deficit as Middle East exports recovered faster than demand, according to Schaeffer's Research. Reuters, in its report on Treasury yields that day, said the IEA now sees the oil market returning to surplus in the fourth quarter of 2026. Oil fell more than 3 percent on the session.

The reversal matters because of what the earlier forecast had been doing. Reuters attributed the rise in oil prices to the Iran war, which began in February 2026. A market that expects a deficit pays for the privilege of having barrels. A market that expects a surplus asks for a discount instead. The IEA, in other words, did not move the oil price so much as move the argument.

The price path since the beginning of the month has been consistent. On November 10, Dow Jones Newswires reported that Brent for January delivery had fallen more than 2 percent to just above $90, after dipping below $90 for the first time since August. US crude traded around $85 that morning. A day earlier, President Trump had said on Truth Social that there was "positive momentum toward an end to this conflict," and that further strikes against Iran "seem to be quite unlikely." By November 11, December WTI was at $86.63, according to Schaeffer's. By the 16th it was $85.72. The latest quotes put it near $80.

## What a cheaper barrel buys

Energy is one of the few inflation inputs that the Federal Reserve can watch without a seminar. Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller said on November 10 that September's inflation data "were bad" and that he would consider a half-point rate rise in December if core consumer prices stayed hot. Lower oil does not fix core inflation, but it removes one of the pressures on the headline figure.

The bond market noticed. Reuters reported that the 10-year Treasury yield fell 3.2 basis points to 5.121 percent on November 16, while the five-year breakeven inflation rate eased to 2.251 percent from 2.304 percent the previous Friday. The 10-year breakeven stood at 2.207 percent. Traders put the odds of a half-point hike in December at about 5 percent and a quarter-point hike at about 95 percent, according to CME FedWatch. Peter Cardillo, chief market economist at Spartan Capital Securities, told Reuters that the market had "already discounted a hike of 25 basis points, possibly 50 basis points in December." A cheaper barrel makes the larger number a little less likely, and the smaller one no less certain.

## Who loses when the barrel gets cheaper

Producers, naturally. Exxon Mobil was among the few large stocks to fall in Trading Economics' tables on Monday's rally day. The energy sector, a hedge against geopolitical alarm, has been a poor place to hide from calm.

The same logic ran through gold. Schaeffer's reported that December gold futures settled at $3,847.40 an ounce on November 16, having touched a 52-week low earlier in the session, down 0.8 percent. Gold had been above $4,100 on November 10, according to Dow Jones. A commodity that investors buy for fear has been losing ground to fear's falling price. It is an arrangement that works until the next headline.

## A forecast, and its footnote

The IEA's forecast is exactly that. The agency changed its view because exports were recovering, and exports can stop recovering. The war that started this price cycle is, according to the president, winding down, and presidents have been known to revise their statements. The oil market has priced a surplus. It has not yet received one.

For now, the barrel has the look of a trade that was crowded and is no longer. Between November 10 and the latest quotes, Brent has fallen from just above $90 to just under $89 by one measure, and WTI from about $85 to about $80 by another. Those are not identical data series, and they should not be treated as one. They do point in the same direction, which in commodities counts as consensus.
