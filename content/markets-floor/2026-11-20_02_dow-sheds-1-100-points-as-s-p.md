# Dow Sheds 1,100 Points as S&P 500 Notches Sixth Straight Loss

The Dow Jones Industrial Average fell 2.19%, or about 1,100 points, to roughly 49,097 on Thursday, November 19, while the S&P 500 lost 2.00% to about 7,177 and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 2.23% to about 23,886, according to Motley Fool's market report and the AP. It was the S&P 500's sixth consecutive decline, the longest losing streak since 2024.

*Photo: Aedrian Salazar via Pexels*

The AP reported that more than 90% of the stocks in the S&P 500 fell, which is a rare thing for a benchmark that spends most weeks being carried by a handful of large technology names. On Thursday there was no one left to do the carrying. The index now sits 8.2% below the record it set last month, per the AP.

## Not the usual suspect

When the market drops this sharply, the first instinct is to blame interest rates. This time the bond market largely declined the invitation. The 10-year Treasury yield ended at 5.05%, against 5.07% on Wednesday, according to the AP, with Motley Fool's figures agreeing. A two-basis-point move does not explain a 2% decline in stocks, even on a generous reading of both.

December gold futures settled 1.50% lower at $3,685.50, per Motley Fool, so the usual shelter was not especially sheltering either. CNBC, relayed by StockTitan, reported that the VIX volatility index neared 29 after Anthropic began trading, its highest since March. This desk could not confirm the figure from a primary source, and notes that second-hand fear is still fear.

If rates and haven assets do not account for the day, what remains is sentiment, and the AP's list of explanations was long. It cited doubts that AI profits can justify data-center spending, high interest rates, regulatory worries after the Democrats' midterm wins, and the sudden resignation of Nvidia's Jensen Huang. The AP wrote that the technology's breathtaking boom may be over, a sentence that appeared without attribution and with considerable confidence.

The Federal Reserve gave nervous investors some homework the day before. Its minutes from the October meeting, published Wednesday, said staff and most participants flagged rapid growth in AI-related debt arranged through opaque financing, and that credit spreads had widened on high-yield bonds financing data-center construction. The same minutes said almost all participants judged further rate increases likely to be appropriate. None of that is new on Thursday's tape, but it supplies an adult explanation for a market that was looking for one: the boom is expensive, the borrowing is hard to see, and the central bank expects to make money dearer.

## The IPO nobody could hold against the tape

The timing deserves its own paragraph. Anthropic, the artificial-intelligence company, priced the largest IPO on record at $83 and closed up 17.1% on a day when 90% of the index fell. Alphabet, one of its investors, fell about 2.9% by Motley Fool's ticker, a figure that may not be the final close, even as the listing put a fresh value on its stake.

Strategists reading these two facts together tend to reach different conclusions. One camp holds that the IPO proves appetite for AI remains strong. The other holds that the market spent $81 billion on new AI supply on the same afternoon it questioned whether it wanted more. Both camps are correct, and both will publish notes.

## What the six days add up to

Six down days in a row is not a crash. It is, by the numbers, a drawdown of just over 8% from a record, which is the sort of thing that happens in a normal year and feels like the end of the world in a year with a trillion-dollar IPO in it.

What changed is the narrative. A week ago, the story was that yields were the problem. Today yields barely moved and stocks fell anyway, which is the pattern one sees when investors have run out of excuses and begin to examine their positions. The Fed's December meeting, on the 8th and 9th, will bring the rate story back. Until then, the market must find a reason to rise that does not involve being told one.

The 90% figure is the one worth keeping. When nearly every stock falls together, the market is no longer deciding that some companies are worth less. It is deciding that it would like to be somewhere else for a while.
