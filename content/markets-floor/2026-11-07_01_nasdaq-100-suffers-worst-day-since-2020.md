# Nasdaq 100 Suffers Worst Day Since 2020 as Chips Lose 12%

The Nasdaq 100 fell 6.4% on Thursday, its worst session since March 2020, as the S&P 500 lost 4.4% and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped 12%, according to Benzinga's market wrap on TradingView. Roughly $3 trillion of value left the S&P 500 in a single day. By Friday evening, the index that had lost it was up 0.31%, which is the sort of recovery one might describe as "technically a rebound."

*Photo: Alex Luna via Pexels*

The S&P 500's drop was its steepest since the April 2025 tariff crash, Yahoo Finance reported, and the Nasdaq Composite slipped into correction territory. Nvidia fell 9%, its worst day since the DeepSeek scare of January 2025, and lost about $450 billion of market value, per TradingView. The Nasdaq 100 closed Thursday nearly 15% below its October 6 record.

## The catalyst was a midterm election

The trigger was not an earnings miss, a rate decision or a data point. According to both Yahoo Finance and Benzinga, it was politics. Democrats won both chambers of Congress in Tuesday's midterms, and investors concluded that the data-centre build-out holding up the AI trade may now face restrictions. Benzinga noted that Texas Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa campaigned on pausing new data-centre construction.

The market's first reaction to the election was, in fairness, cheerful: the Dow jumped 3% on Wednesday. It took a day for the same traders to read the second paragraph.

The damage was concentrated where the year's gains were. SanDisk fell 22% on Thursday and Micron fell 17%, according to Benzinga, and both remain among the four best performers in the S&P 500 in 2026. Being the best performer of the year and the worst performer of the day are, it turns out, compatible achievements.

## Friday: a rebound with an asterisk

Friday's closing figures, taken from Yahoo Finance's quote panels, were: Dow Jones Industrial Average 51,285.73, down 237.33 points or 0.46%; S&P 500 7,376.86, up 23.13 or 0.31%; Nasdaq Composite 24,579.42, up 264.43 or 1.09%; Russell 2000 2,754.11, up 0.89%. The VIX closed at 25.64, down 9.01% on the day.

The asterisk is the week as a whole. Yahoo reported that the technology sector was down about 7% on the week and the S&P 500 was on pace for its worst week since early June. Benzinga described the Nasdaq 100 as heading for its longest weekly losing streak since March.

The bounce had help from the labour market. October payrolls rose 165,000 against a consensus of 65,000, and the unemployment rate fell to 4.1%. Good news for the economy was, as is traditional, mixed news for stocks: Benzinga said traders now price about an 80% chance of a Federal Reserve rate hike in December, up from roughly 70% before the report. The 10-year Treasury yield rebounded to 4.86% after the jobs data.

Not every corner of the market was in the same mood. General Motors touched a record $93.70 on Wednesday before the sell-off erased its weekly gain, according to Benzinga, while Ford rose about 2% on the week even as it reported October US sales down 0.8% to 174,123 vehicles, with electric-vehicle sales down 71%. When the AI trade is the thing being sold, an old-fashioned car company can look like a safe harbour, which says something about the harbour.

## What the numbers say

The University of Michigan's preliminary November sentiment index came in at 51.0, up from 48.0. Survey director Joanne Hsu said consumer comments "revealed growing anxiety over a possible AI bubble," according to Benzinga. It is worth pausing on the fact that the American consumer, surveyed before most of this week's price action, had arrived at the same worry independently and without a Bloomberg terminal.

Yahoo noted that the Nasdaq Composite slipped into correction territory on Thursday; Friday's 1.09% gain, welcome as it was, did not undo that. The VIX's 9% decline to 25.64 is likewise a move in the right direction from a level that still invites the word "elevated."

The lesson of the week is narrower than the headlines suggest. A $3 trillion drawdown in one session was caused by a policy rumour about concrete and electricity contracts, and the market's recovery of a few tenths of a percent was caused by a jobs report that makes the Fed less friendly. Both are, by the standards of this publication, entirely normal behaviour.
