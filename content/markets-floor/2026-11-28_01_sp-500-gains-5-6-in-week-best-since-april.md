# S&P 500 Gains 5.6% in Week, Best Since April 2025

The S&P 500 rose 114.71 points, or 1.6%, to 7,297.49 on Friday in the shortened session after Thanksgiving, completing a weekly gain of 5.6%, according to an Associated Press market report. That is the index's biggest weekly advance since April 2025, and it leaves the benchmark up 6.6% for the year.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average added 591.99 points, or 1.2%, to close at 50,774.94, taking its week to 5.5%, a gain of 2,627.61 points. TradingKey, which summarised the session, noted that this was the Dow's largest weekly gain since October 2022. The Nasdaq Composite did the heavy lifting on the day, climbing 543.70 points, or 2.3%, to 24,129.50, for a weekly gain of 6.7%. Small caps joined in: the Russell 2000 rose 1.7% to 2,750.30 and finished the week up 6.5%.

The AP described the week as Wall Street's best in more than a year. Those who prefer their superlatives audited will note that "more than a year" is accurate, modest and, conveniently, unfalsifiable by anyone not holding a calendar.

## Technology does the arithmetic

The session belonged to semiconductors. TradingKey reported that the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index gained 4.69% to 9,186.71, with all 30 of its constituents advancing. Memory and storage names led: SanDisk rose 15.96%, Seagate 7.90%, Micron 7.60%, SK Hynix 7.52% and Western Digital 7.22%. Anthropic, the newly listed artificial intelligence developer, rose nearly 11% to $91.88, according to the same summary.

A rally in which every member of an index participates carries its own kind of information. It suggests buyers were not selecting; they were returning. When 30 out of 30 constituents advance, the explanation is rarely that thirty separate managements had a good morning. It is usually that someone, somewhere, stopped selling.

A weekly gain of 5.6% is also a statement about the starting point. Percentage gains are calculated from wherever the week began, and the AP's own framing, its biggest since April 2025, tells you how rarely a week like this comes along. The market's sense of triumph should be calibrated accordingly.

The breadth deserves a note. The Russell 2000, which tracks smaller companies that are more exposed to borrowing costs, outpaced both the S&P 500 and the Dow on the week, up 6.5% against 5.6% and 5.5%. Only the Nasdaq, at 6.7%, did better. When the market's more rate-sensitive corner keeps pace with its technology heavyweights, the rally is harder to dismiss as one sector's good afternoon.

## What the year-to-date column says

The AP's year-to-date figures put the week in proportion. The S&P 500 is up 6.6% for 2026, the Dow 5.6%, the Nasdaq 3.8% and the Russell 2000 10.8%. Eleven months of effort have produced a Nasdaq return of 3.8%, which a single good week of 6.7% has now exceeded by some distance. The small-cap index, meanwhile, leads all four on the year, a result that nobody on a morning panel predicted and several will now claim to have foreseen.

There is a respectable bull case. A broad rally, in which small companies and large ones rise together and every chip stock advances, is healthier than a narrow one carried by two or three heavyweights. There is also a respectable bear case: the session was shortened, and a half-day close after a holiday is not a vote of confidence so much as a vote of whoever turned up.

TradingKey also noted a reported complication for the sector's biggest name. Nvidia reportedly asked its manufacturing partners to pause server shipments to four Southeast Asian countries, a story covered in more detail elsewhere on this site. The semiconductor index rose regardless. Markets are entitled to look past a headline; they are less entitled to look past it on the same day they cheer the sector it concerns.

## The part nobody models

The honest reading is that Friday told investors very little they did not already know, and told them it loudly. Prices rose because buyers outnumbered sellers for a few hours on a quiet day. That is a fact, not a forecast, and the distinction tends to survive precisely until Monday's open, when full volume returns and the market resumes expressing opinions at its usual length.

Traders will return to their desks to a December calendar crowded with central bank decisions. A week this strong has a way of persuading people it was inevitable. The numbers say only that it happened, which, in markets, is already a considerable achievement.
