# Rice at Your Desk vs. an Hour Away From It: Lunch in Jakarta and Vienna

**🇮🇩 Indonesia · 🇦🇹 Austria**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Jakarta, lunch is a rolling, communal event that starts with "sudah makan?" — have you eaten? — and ends with someone else's rice on your plate whether you asked for it or not. In Vienna, lunch is a scheduled, near-sacred hour away from your desk, and asking a colleague to eat while working through it would register as a faintly strange request. Both cultures treat the midday meal as more than fuel. Neither one will forgive you for treating it as an afterthought.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇩 Indonesia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask "Sudah makan?" (have you eaten?) as a genuine rapport-building greeting, not small talk to rush past | Don't assume a business lunch invitation means everyone pays their own way — the inviter typically covers the bill |
| Eat with your right hand when dining informally or communally, as custom expects | Don't eat with your left hand in shared or traditional settings |
| Clear your plate — it's read as a sign of respect for the meal and the host | Don't schedule meetings through the 12:00-13:00 lunch window that most Jakarta offices observe in sync |
| Accommodate Ramadan scheduling proactively — reschedule lunch meetings, eat discreetly around fasting colleagues | Don't assume everyone eats individually — meals are overwhelmingly a shared, family-style affair even at work |
| Say clearly upfront if you expect to split a bill you're inviting someone to — otherwise you're expected to pay | Don't rush a shared meal — pace is part of the social function, not incidental to it |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take the full hour lunch break away from your desk — it's the norm, not an indulgence | Don't eat a rushed sandwich at your computer as a default — it reads as slightly sad, not efficient |
| Expect the workday to run roughly 8am-5/6pm with lunch built firmly into the middle | Don't schedule meetings straight through the midday break without checking first |
| Pick a nearby, quick-service restaurant with locally sourced food if eating out — that's what most Austrians prioritize | Don't expect Friday to run a full day — many offices close at 3pm and lunch culture shifts accordingly |
| Shop for fresh groceries frequently rather than stockpiling — daily or near-daily runs are the norm | Don't assume Austria's relaxed lunch hour means a relaxed attitude toward punctuality returning from it |
| Treat the sit-down lunch as part of how Austrians protect work-life balance, not just a meal preference | Don't push through lunch to "get ahead" — it won't be read as diligence, just as slightly off |

Indonesian food culture at work runs on visible togetherness. Meals are, as one guide to [Indonesian eating traditions](https://www.vinhood.com/en/magazine-en/tradizione-della-cucina-e-del-mangiare-insieme-in-indonesia/) puts it, almost never a solitary act — dishes land in the center of the table for everyone to share, and the office lunch window between noon and 1pm is observed with enough consistency across Jakarta's office towers that scheduling around it is simply expected. The etiquette carries weight beyond taste: using your right hand, finishing what's on your plate, and greeting colleagues with "sudah makan?" all function as small, constant signals of respect within a culture that scores low on Hofstede's individualism index (14) and correspondingly high on collectivist, group-oriented norms. During Ramadan, the calculus shifts again, and expats who proactively reschedule lunch meetings around fasting colleagues, rather than expecting business as usual, report building outsized goodwill for a fairly small accommodation.

Austria's food culture at work is quieter but no less rule-bound. The FOOD Programme's cross-country barometer found Austrian workers prioritize proximity and quick service when choosing where to eat, but the deeper cultural fact is that lunch is protected time: a full hour, taken away from the desk, treated as a pause rather than an inconvenience to be minimized. [The Local](https://www.thelocal.at/20230516/the-austrian-eating-habits-the-world-could-learn-from) has specifically highlighted this as one of the Austrian habits worth importing elsewhere — the deliberate, unhurried sit-down meal rather than the eat-at-your-keyboard default common in more individualist, achievement-oriented work cultures. Fittingly, Austria's Hofstede individualism score (77) sits close to the opposite end of the spectrum from Indonesia's, yet the lunch hour isn't really about individual indulgence here either — it's a shared cultural expectation that everyone, not just you, steps away.

**The Reckoning:** Both cultures protect lunch fiercely, but for almost opposite structural reasons. Indonesia protects it as a communal ritual that reinforces group belonging — eating together, sharing dishes, greeting each other with food-based small talk are the connective tissue of a high-collectivism workplace. Austria protects it as personal recovery time within a culture that otherwise runs on individual autonomy and clearly bounded hours — the hour away from your desk belongs to you specifically, not to your coworkers as a group activity. A foreign hire who imports Jakarta's communal eating instincts to Vienna risks appearing intrusive around a colleague's protected personal hour; one who imports Vienna's solo-recharge instinct to Jakarta risks appearing standoffish in a culture where eating apart from the group reads as a small social withdrawal.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone describing daily life working in Jakarta noted that "sudah makan?" functioned less like a real question and more like a handshake — the honest answer mattered far less than showing you'd bothered to ask at all.*

> *Expat.com (Jakarta guide) — A guide to Jakarta's corporate culture warned that inviting colleagues to eat out without clarifying the bill split in advance meant you'd almost certainly be expected to pay for the whole table, a norm several new arrivals learned the expensive way.*

> *The Local (Austria) — An article on habits that show you've truly settled into Austrian life singled out taking a full, unhurried lunch hour away from the desk as one of the clearest tells that a foreign transplant had actually adapted, rather than merely relocated.*

> *Expat.com (Vienna guide) — A guide to Vienna's work culture noted that colleagues routinely left the office entirely for lunch, and that scheduling a working lunch meeting without checking first could come across as mildly inconsiderate rather than efficient.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Jakarta, budget your lunches for company, not efficiency — the meal is doing social work whether or not you notice. If you're moving to Vienna, budget your lunches for recovery, and don't mistake the hour-long break for laziness; it's protected time by design. The version Priya would tell a friend over a drink: in Jakarta, always ask if they've eaten; in Vienna, always let them actually eat.
