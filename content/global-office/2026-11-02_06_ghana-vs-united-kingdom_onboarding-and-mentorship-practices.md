**Welcome Aboard. Someone Will Find You Eventually: Onboarding in Ghana and the United Kingdom**

🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Gallup finds that only twelve per cent of employees worldwide strongly agree their organisation onboards new hires well, which means the other eighty-eight per cent are, to varying degrees, standing in a hallway wondering where the toilets are and who, if anyone, is responsible for them. Ghana and the United Kingdom arrive at this shared mediocrity from opposite directions: one country has spent decades perfecting an oral, relationship-first apprenticeship tradition that never quite migrated into the modern office; the other has spent decades perfecting the onboarding PDF, a document of tremendous legal thoroughness that nobody reads past page two. Neither approach reliably produces a new hire who knows what they are doing by week three, and both produce the same quiet casualty: the employee who spends their first ninety days too polite, or too junior, to admit they're lost. What follows is a guide to failing differently, on purpose, in two distinct registers.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn names, titles, and honorifics (Madam, Auntie, Chief) and use them from day one | Assume a quiet first week means no one is evaluating you — observation is the training |
| Ask a senior colleague to informally "show you round" rather than waiting for a packet | Expect a written 30-60-90 day plan; most knowledge transfers orally and relationally |
| Invest in small talk and personal rapport before expecting task detail in return | Correct a senior colleague's instructions publicly, even when they're incomplete |
| Treat whichever older colleague adopts you as your de facto mentor, title or not | Push for a feedback meeting on week one; trust is built before candour is offered |
| Show up early and stay visible; presence reads as seriousness in hierarchical offices | Mistake friendliness for permission to skip the hierarchy on real decisions |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for the written induction schedule and probation review dates on day one | Mistake "that's interesting" or "noted" for agreement — it usually isn't |
| Use a formal buddy or mentor scheme if offered; treat it as a resource, not decoration | Assume your line manager automatically doubles as your mentor |
| Keep your own record of probation objectives, since employers now must document theirs | Wait to be checked on after week one — flag confusion yourself or it goes unnoticed |
| Ask directly who else you can go to with questions beyond your manager | Expect enthusiasm for your questions; expect calm, slightly weary helpfulness instead |
| Read the handbook once, then ask a human the three things it didn't actually answer | Assume politeness at induction predicts warmth at month three; it often doesn't |

### Ghana: The Apprentice Never Really Leaves the Shop

Ghana's dominant model of skills transfer is not the HR department; it is the apprenticeship, and the International Labour Organization has long documented informal apprenticeship as "by far the dominant" route into trade skills for most of the working population, running on fixed fees, extended shadowing, and a master who corrects by example rather than memo. That template — an elder figure who absorbs you into their orbit, shows rather than tells, and expects deference before explanation — persists almost unconsciously inside Ghana's formal offices, banks, and NGOs, even where no apprenticeship contract exists. A new hire's "mentor" is rarely assigned on a form; it is whichever senior colleague takes an interest, usually after enough shared lunches and questions about one's family.

The gap this leaves is structural rather than interpersonal. Ghana's youth unemployment sits around 12.1 per cent, and more than 240,000 secondary graduates enter the job market each year without tertiary places to absorb them, according to commentary circulated via AllAfrica from Absa Bank Ghana's citizenship division, which cites a 2024 review of 73 studies finding mentorship meaningfully improves career clarity and job attainment. The argument being made across Ghanaian business pages, repeatedly and with some urgency, is that informal, improvised mentorship — real and valuable as it is — cannot scale to the number of young people who need it, and that corporate Ghana has been slow to formalise what has always worked person to person. Hofstede Insights scores Ghana at roughly 80 on power distance, among the higher end globally, which helps explain why guidance flows down through seniority rather than sideways through structured programmes: the hierarchy is the mentorship infrastructure, for better and for worse.

The one genuinely national onboarding mechanism is the National Service Scheme, which places most tertiary graduates into a mandatory year of placement before they're considered fully credentialed for permanent work. It functions, in effect, as a government-run mentorship bridge: a structured handover from classroom to workplace that the private sector itself rarely bothers to replicate once that year ends. The irony is that a system designed to smooth the transition into formal employment often produces graduates who are better onboarded during their national service than they will be at the first "real" job that follows it, where the elder-apprentice default quietly reasserts itself.

### United Kingdom: The Handbook Arrived, the Human Did Not

Britain's onboarding problem is almost the inverse: abundant process, uneven delivery. The CIPD's guidance to employers is unambiguous that induction should cover the organisation, the team, and the role, and notes that under the Employment Rights Act 2025 employers now face more formal scrutiny of how they run probation — paperwork has caught up with intention. Yet LinkedIn's Global Talent Trends research places onboarding among the top five talent priorities globally while also flagging it as one of the most neglected in practice, and Gallup's twelve per cent figure suggests the UK is not an exception to that neglect so much as a particularly well-documented example of it.

What distinguishes the British version of the problem is its politeness. The failure is rarely dramatic; it is a manager who is also, nominally, your mentor, too stretched to be either properly, dispensing vague feedback that sounds like approval and means "fix this quietly." Hofstede's individualism score for the UK sits near 89, among the highest measured anywhere, which tracks with a workplace norm that assumes competent adults will ask for help rather than wait to be offered it — an assumption that works beautifully for confident self-starters and rather less well for anyone raised to wait their turn.

Hybrid working has not helped. The Office for National Statistics has repeatedly found roughly a quarter to a third of British workers splitting their week between home and office, which means a new hire's "buddy," where one exists, is frequently a name on a calendar invite rather than a desk two feet away. The CIPD's own advice now explicitly tells employers to plan induction for remote and hybrid starters separately, which is a tacit admission that the old model — osmosis by proximity — has quietly stopped working for a large share of the workforce, formal mentor scheme or not.

### The Reckoning

The irony sitting underneath both systems is that the country with the thinner paper trail may produce the warmer mentor, and the country with the thicker one may produce the lonelier new hire. Ghana's informal, elder-led apprenticeship ethos means a new employee is rarely structurally alone, even if no one calls what's happening "mentorship" on an org chart; Britain's formalised, rights-documented induction process can leave a new employee legally well-protected and personally adrift, buddy scheme notwithstanding. One culture over-delivers relationship and under-delivers structure; the other does precisely the reverse.

The second irony is who actually benefits. Ghana's system rewards patience, deference, and the ability to read a room before asking a direct question — a skill most useful to someone staying years, building standing slowly. Britain's system rewards the opposite: direct, early self-advocacy, chasing the mentor scheme, naming the confusion out loud before anyone notices it for you. A new arrival who imports the wrong instinct into the wrong country will spend their first quarter being quietly misread — too passive in London, too presumptuous in Accra — for reasons neither handbook nor elder bothers to spell out.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — An American who worked UK contact centres and offices described thin, unclear training and weak communication as the norm rather than the exception, noting that instructions were often vague and colleagues did the bare minimum rather than fill the gaps.*

> *Blind — A tech employee, eight months into a new role, described their manager doubling as their assigned mentor and being chronically vague about expectations, leaving them guessing at tasks and still needing regular hand-holding well past the point they'd expected to be independent.*

> *Reddit, r/managers — A team lead at a small company with no formal HR admitted there had been no onboarding plan at all for a recent graduate hire: no software access arranged, no one assigned to check in, work handed off between meetings. The hire quit within three weeks, later confiding that every question had made him feel like a burden.*

> *University of Oregon Ghana field blog — An intern in Accra recalled sitting at what turned out to be an unassigned desk on her first day, having assumed it was hers, and being gently corrected on a work draft by a colleague who explained why her ideas didn't translate to the local context — a moment she described as one of several quietly humbling lessons in how much onboarding happens through conversation rather than instruction.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between these two failure modes, choose based on what you can tolerate being short of: structure or warmth. Ghana will likely hand you a person before it hands you a plan, and that person will teach you more than any induction deck could, provided you are willing to earn it through patience and proximity rather than ask for it outright. The UK will likely hand you a plan before it hands you a person, and that plan will protect your probationary rights admirably while leaving the actual question — what am I meant to be doing, and who do I ask — disappointingly open. Neither country has solved onboarding; they have simply distributed the unsolved parts differently, and dressed the gap in a culturally appropriate costume — hierarchy in one case, paperwork in the other. The practical test for a prospective mover is which gap you're better equipped to fill yourself: can you build trust quickly enough to earn guidance you weren't formally promised, or can you tolerate being formally promised guidance that doesn't reliably arrive? My honest advice, the kind I'd give over a drink: in Ghana, find your elder in the first week and attach yourself to them like a limpet; in Britain, ask the embarrassing question on day one, because nobody is coming to ask if you have one.
