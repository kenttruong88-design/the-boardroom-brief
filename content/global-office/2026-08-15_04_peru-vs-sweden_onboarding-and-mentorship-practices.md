**Being Introduced to Everyone vs. Being Handed a Login: Peru vs. Sweden**

**🇵🇪 Peru · 🇸🇪 Sweden**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Show up to a new job in Lima and you can expect to be personally walked around the office and introduced, by name, to nearly everyone on the floor before lunch. Show up to a new job in Stockholm and you may be handed a laptop, a login, and a calendar invite to a meeting where no one explains the org chart because, officially, there isn't much of one to explain. Both companies think they're being welcoming. Only one of them is thinking about your feelings while doing it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a personal, face-to-face introduction to colleagues rather than a written org chart | Treat onboarding as a checklist — Peruvian workplaces expect it to feel human, not procedural |
| Accept invitations to coffee breaks and lunch in your first days — this is where real onboarding happens | Eat alone at your desk in week one; it reads as standoffish, not efficient |
| Identify who your informal mentor is, even if no one calls it that officially | Assume a mentor will be formally assigned — many Peruvian firms rely on organic relationships instead |
| Show visible respect for hierarchy and tenure when meeting senior staff | Jump straight to first-name informality with senior leaders unless they invite it |
| Ask questions in person rather than over email or chat when possible | Rely solely on written documentation to learn how things actually work |

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take initiative to ask questions — flat hierarchies expect you to speak up, not wait to be told | Wait passively for detailed instructions; that's not how Swedish teams operate |
| Treat your manager as a facilitator, not an authority figure who hands down direction | Expect a manager to closely supervise your first weeks — autonomy is assumed quickly |
| Look for a mentor or buddy program explicitly, since many companies do run structured ones | Assume the absence of visible hierarchy means no one can help you — ask directly |
| Practice lagom — moderate confidence, not loud self-promotion, lands better | Oversell your achievements in intro meetings; humility is culturally rewarded |
| Expect consensus-based decisions even in your first weeks — your input will genuinely be sought | Mistake a slower, discussion-heavy decision process for disorganization |

---

Peruvian onboarding runs on relationship-building as a functional requirement, not a nicety. Employment law guidance for foreign employers explicitly warns against standardized, impersonal onboarding processes, noting that Peruvian workplace culture places real weight on visible faces, personal introductions, and human warmth from day one (Harris Gomez Group). Hierarchy remains present and respected — greetings, seniority, and the rhythm of coffee breaks and lunch pauses all carry social meaning — but it coexists with an expectation that new hires are folded into the group personally, not just administratively. Mentorship, where it exists, tends to be informal and relational: colleagues sharing "inside knowledge" about how things really work, rather than a structured program with defined milestones.

Sweden takes almost the inverse approach, built on the assumption that flat hierarchies and employee autonomy are themselves the welcome. An estimated 80% of Swedish workplaces operate with genuinely flat structures where junior staff are expected to speak up as freely as managers (Guestly Homes). Managers function more as facilitators of consensus than as directive authorities, and decisions typically require broad group buy-in even when a new hire is in the room (Commisceo Global). The cultural principle of lagom — "just the right amount" — shapes not just workload expectations but self-presentation: a new hire who arrives eager to prove themselves loudly will land worse than one who waits, listens, and contributes moderately. Structured mentorship programs do exist at many Swedish employers, but they sit inside a culture that otherwise expects new hires to self-direct almost immediately.

### The Reckoning

The paradox is that Peru's more hierarchical workplace culture often produces a warmer, more supported onboarding experience, while Sweden's famously egalitarian one can feel colder and more disorienting in the first weeks — precisely because flatness removes the built-in social scaffolding that hierarchy provides elsewhere. In Lima, someone senior takes it upon themselves to make sure you're introduced and oriented, because that's part of what seniority is understood to mean. In Stockholm, no one is formally responsible for that because responsibility itself is meant to be distributed — which works well for someone who already knows how to ask, and poorly for someone waiting to be told.

Hofstede's individualism scores capture part of this: Sweden ranks among the more individualist cultures globally, consistent with an onboarding model that assumes self-sufficiency, while Peru scores more collectivist, consistent with onboarding as a group project the whole office participates in. Neither produces a worse outcome — but they demand opposite instincts from a new arrival in the first month.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who relocated to Lima for a corporate role described their first week as almost overwhelming in a good way — being personally walked to every desk and introduced by name — and said it made the traffic and pollution elsewhere in the city easier to tolerate because work, at least, felt human.*

> *Reddit — An American data professional who joined a Stockholm company wrote that no one gave them a clear task list in the first two weeks, and it took a direct conversation with a colleague to learn that waiting for instructions was itself the mistake — initiative was simply expected.*

> *Internations Lima — A relocated marketing hire noted that skipping the daily coffee break in their first month, assuming it was optional social time not worth interrupting real work for, was quietly noticed and read as disengagement by colleagues who considered it part of the actual job.*

> *tobeadatascientist.substack.com — An American data scientist working in Stockholm wrote candidly that the flat hierarchy she'd been promised in interviews took real getting used to in practice — decisions that would have taken a single approval elsewhere required buy-in from a wider group, which felt slow until she understood it as the point, not a flaw.*

> *Quora — A Peruvian professional who later worked for a Scandinavian company observed that the hardest adjustment wasn't the language or the hours but learning that asking a manager directly for guidance, something normal in Lima, could read in Stockholm as not trusting your own judgment.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two, the honest question is whether you do your best work when someone is actively looking out for you, or when you're simply handed the keys and trusted to figure it out. Peru will make you feel cared for faster than almost anywhere, but expect that care to come bundled with hierarchy you're expected to respect. Sweden will hand you real autonomy almost immediately, but expect the silence in your first two weeks to feel less like trust and more like being forgotten — until you realize that asking is the whole system working exactly as designed.
