# Spain Argues About the End of the Working Day While the Netherlands Quietly Takes Wednesday Off

🇪🇸 Spain · 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Eurostat reports that Dutch workers averaged 32.1 hours a week in 2024, the shortest in the European Union. Spanish workers, according to Ara's reading of the same data, averaged 36.4. Four hours a week is not dramatic until it is multiplied by a career. It is, however, enough to suggest that the two countries mean different things when they say they value life outside the office.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Spain
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask early whether your employer runs a split schedule or the 9-to-6 European one | Assume that a long lunch means an early finish |
| Use the summer intensive hours, usually 8:00 to 15:00 with no lunch break, to claim your afternoons | Book a client call at 14:30 and expect cheerful attendance |
| Keep your own record of your hours; employers must log start and finish times | Work unrecorded overtime on the assumption that goodwill converts to pay |
| Take the menu del dia with colleagues between roughly 13:30 and 15:30 | Eat at your desk and wonder why nobody joins you |
| Treat deadlines as targets under negotiation | Mistake a relaxed tone for a lack of seriousness |

### Netherlands
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask about part-time or compressed weeks at the offer stage, since they are routine | Describe a four-day week as a lack of ambition |
| Leave on time and say so; boundaries are respected | Schedule a meeting at 17:30 without an apology |
| Say plainly when a workload is too large | Wait for someone to notice you are drowning |
| Read the overtime clause of any contract with care | Assume a Dutch address guarantees a Dutch workload |
| Keep lunch brief and punctual | Propose a two-hour lunch as a bonding exercise |

## Spain: A Long Day With a Very Long Lunch

The Spanish week is not short. Eurostat data reported by Ara put the average at 36.4 hours in 2024, just above the EU average of 36, and Euronews, using the latest Eurostat figures in June 2026, gave 36.3. The legal debate is about whether it should shrink. According to Euro Weekly News, Congress rejected a bill cutting the week to 37.5 hours in September 2025, and a government decree to make the working-hours record digital and unalterable was advised against by the Council of State on 19 March 2026. A general election called for 29 November will influence what happens next. The existing duty, in force since 12 May 2019, already obliges employers to record each worker's start and finish times and keep those records for four years.

The shape of the day varies. The Spain Handbook describes the horario partido, a morning block, a long midday pause and an afternoon block ending around 20:00, as still common in small businesses and some offices, while multinationals and major hubs favour the horario europeo, roughly 9:00 to 18:00 with an hour for lunch. From mid-June to mid-September, and often on Fridays, the jornada intensiva compresses work into 8:00 to 15:00. The lunch is a genuine institution, typically 90 minutes to two hours, and the menu del dia that accompanies it costs roughly 12 to 16 euros, according to the same guide. The guide also lists 22 working days of statutory leave, about 30 calendar days, and 14 public holidays split among national, regional and local calendars, though it is not wholly consistent on the entitlement. The point for the newcomer is that Spanish time is generous in the middle of the day and in the calendar, but not necessarily at its edges.

Overtime is where the brochure thins. The union federation CCOO, analysing Spain's labour force survey, reported that workers who put in unpaid overtime averaged 5.6 hours a week in 2025, across 441,000 people, or 2.3 percent of salaried workers. That is a small share, but it is also only the part that surfaces in the survey. An ADP survey of about 10,000 European workers, reported by IamExpat in 2019, found 66.9 percent of Spanish respondents regularly working unpaid overtime, against 54.2 percent in the Netherlands. The two sources measure different things, and the older one deserves hedging, but together they suggest that the long lunch coexists with a fair amount of unrecorded evening.

## Netherlands: The Part-Time Champions, Mostly

Dutch hours are shorter because Dutch jobs are smaller. Euronews put the Dutch part-time share of employment at 38.9 percent, second only to Switzerland among 33 European countries, though it did not state the year. Statistics Netherlands, as reported by IamExpat, found that about 1.9 million people worked part-time in the third quarter of 2025, defined as 28 to 35 hours a week, and that 31 percent of women with a partner and children worked part-time against 13 percent of fathers. The gender split is the part of the arrangement that the Dutch themselves discuss with less enthusiasm.

The arrangement does not reduce total contribution as much as one might expect. DutchNews.nl, reporting Eurostat data in July 2025, said the Dutch have the longest working lives in the EU at 43.8 years against an EU average of 37.2, and the highest net participation rate. In other words, the Dutch work fewer hours per week but more weeks over a lifetime, which is a form of balance that spreadsheets can verify. The same IamExpat report counts about 5.1 million people working 35 hours or more a week in the Netherlands in that quarter, so the part-time champions are not a majority of the workforce in the way the folklore implies, and full-time work remains the largest category. A newcomer should read the Dutch model as a menu rather than a mandate.

The culture supports this. A career coach writing in IamExpat described Dutch colleagues as leaving promptly, treating family and sport as seriously as work, pushing back when workloads grow and being trusted to manage their own time so long as they deliver. Hofstede Insights figures, as commonly cited, put the Netherlands at 38 for power distance and 80 for individualism, against 57 and 51 for Spain, which would account for the willingness to say no to a manager. This columnist could not confirm those scores on the institute's own site, so they should be read as a proxy. Uncertainty avoidance, commonly cited at 86 for Spain and 53 for the Netherlands, may help explain why Spanish employers favour documented hours and fixed schedules while Dutch ones prefer to negotiate case by case.

## The Reckoning

Spain offers a longer week, a longer lunch and an unresolved argument about how long the day should be. The Netherlands offers a shorter week, a cultural expectation that you will leave and an infrastructure of part-time work that treats reduced hours as ordinary. On the headline numbers the Dutch win: fewer hours, firmer boundaries and a labour market designed around the idea that adults have lives.

The headline numbers are not the experience, however. Spain's rules, with the record of hours and the mandated intensive summer schedules, are more visible than its practice, and its unpaid overtime is documented by unions rather than celebrated by employers. The Netherlands' reputation is strongest in Dutch-owned workplaces and weakest in the fast-growing, internationally owned ones, where some expats describe a pace unrelated to the national mythology. The honest comparison is between a country that debates its hours and a country that has mostly settled them, each with exceptions that the relocation brochure will not list.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — A poster who has worked in the Netherlands since 2015 across start-ups, scale-ups and large firms says highly competitive workplaces bring overtime and burnout, that they have rarely met anyone who works a consistent eight-hour day, and that some employers rewrite contract terms to extract more.*

> *Expat.com — In the thread on Spanish work culture, one poster found workdays surprisingly long compared with France's 35-hour week and said reduced schedules are rarely offered, while others praised the relaxed attitude to deadlines and the priority given to family and friends over work.*

> *Expat.com — A retiree a year into living in Spain says they resisted shifting their meals for a long time, then moved lunch to 13:00 or 14:00, dinner to a tapa or two, and bedtime to around midnight.*

> *IamExpat — A career coach says the Dutch work to live rather than live to work, leave promptly at the end of the day and trust colleagues to choose how and when they deliver.*

## Conclusion

Choose Spain if you value the rhythm of the day and can live with a longer week, a split schedule that may extend your evening, and a legislative debate that is not yet over. Choose the Netherlands if you value hours as a negotiable asset and want part-time or compressed work to be unremarkable. In either case, ask the employer, not the stereotype, which schedule applies, and ask what happens when the work exceeds the contract.

Before accepting an offer, request the written hours, the overtime clause and the name of someone who left the company recently. The Spanish will give you a long lunch and the Dutch will give you a short week, and the person who still manages to work late in both countries will be the one who did not read the contract.
