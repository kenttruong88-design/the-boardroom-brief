# Taiwan Says Nothing Unkind in Meetings, Japan Decides Before They Start

🇹🇼 Taiwan · 🇯🇵 Japan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Anyone who has attended a meeting in East Asia has eventually realised that the meeting was not where anything happened. In Japan, the formal session is largely a ceremony to announce a decision that was reached weeks earlier, one quiet conversation at a time. In Taiwan, the gathering is where nobody may criticise anybody, so the criticism arrives later by text message, ideally outside working hours. Both systems are polite, both are baffling to newcomers, and only one of them has been the subject of a government ruling on whether a chat message counts as overtime.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Praise colleagues in front of the group, which is welcomed | Criticise anyone in a team meeting, even constructively |
| Follow instructions from senior colleagues as given | Offer improvement ideas to your line manager too early; it may read as arrogance |
| Identify early who can make the executive decision | Spend weeks persuading someone who cannot approve anything |
| Move a terse written message to a face-to-face conversation quickly | Rely on text or email alone for sensitive feedback |
| Show interest in colleagues' lives and bring something back from trips | Gossip or raise controversial topics, since reputations travel quickly |

### Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Consult key people one-on-one before the meeting (nemawashi) | Introduce a new idea cold in a formal meeting |
| Wait for a natural pause before speaking | Fill every silence, which is a respected part of the process |
| Treat 'chotto muzukashii desu ne' (it is a little difficult) as a no | Press for a direct answer in the room |
| Send post-meeting summaries phrased as questions | Correct anyone publicly |
| Attend the nomikai at least early on; oolong tea is acceptable | Skip them repeatedly and be read as not a team player |

## Taiwan

Taiwan's official guide for Gold Card holders, published by the National Immigration Agency, is unusually candid about the culture it advertises. It says criticising someone in a team meeting is a major faux pas even when constructive, that praise before a group is welcome, and that offering improvement ideas to your line manager too early can be read as criticism or arrogance. The guide adds that many workplaces are conservative about flexibility, that presenteeism is common, and that KPIs may reflect attendance more than output. The meeting is therefore a place to display agreement, and the real exchange of views is postponed.

It is postponed, usually, to Line. Radio Taiwan International reported in 2019 that bosses commonly assign work over the messaging app after hours, and that under government rules such work counts as overtime, at time and a third for the first two hours and time and two thirds thereafter. Employers who fail to pay can face fines of up to NT$1 million, about US$32,000, though the report notes that even workers willing to sue often struggle to be paid. The same Gold Card guide warns that criticism often arrives by text or email, which avoids confrontation but invites misunderstanding, and advises arranging a face-to-face talk as soon as possible.

The result is a communication style that is warm, indirect and hierarchical, with decision-making that one longtime American resident, writing on a personal expat site, described as centralised and formal. The same writer says unpaid overtime is common, and that leaving at exactly the contracted hour could be read as a bad attitude. That is one person's account, not a labour statistic. It is also consistent with everything else you will hear in the office kitchen, assuming you are invited into it.

A foreign MBA student at National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University, writing on the university's student blog, adds a gentler complaint. He values the friendliness of colleagues but finds that courtesy and frequent email exchanges slow simple tasks, since staff often need to check with a supervisor first. He also says many employees stay late without pay, sometimes for twelve-hour days, to please bosses and avoid seeming less committed than colleagues, and that remote work is still uncommon in Taiwanese companies. The courtesy is sincere. It is also, in procedural terms, a queue.

## Japan

Japan has turned the meeting into a late-stage administrative formality. According to Japan Handbook, many decisions are reached beforehand through nemawashi, the quiet consulting of each key stakeholder, leaving the formal meeting largely to confirm and announce them. Introducing a new, unvetted idea there risks public disagreement and a loss of face. Modern Living Japan describes the ringi system, in which decisions are approved through a circulated document, as slow but thorough, and notes that colleagues pressed for a direct answer in a group may reply later by email.

Silence, in this system, is a feature. Japan Handbook explains that a senior executive's silence after a proposal usually means weighing, not rejecting it, and that repeating your point during the pause can seem to question the listener's understanding. A direct no is rare. The public face, tatemae, appears in meetings, while the private opinion, honne, tends to emerge in smaller groups or after work, which is why the izakaya is a working environment with a tab of roughly 3,000 to 5,000 yen per person. A commentary on the Japan Business Secrets newsletter adds a useful correction: hierarchy dictates who gets consulted, not who decides, and startups and owner-led firms decide faster.

The system is slower to start and, its defenders say, quicker to finish. It is also stubborn about location. According to the Japan Productivity Center surveys reported by Nippon.com, telework rose above 30 percent in May 2020 and drifted down to around 20 percent, with only 3.6 percent of employees fully remote by April 2021. A 2023 Persol Research survey, reported by HRM Asia, found 22.2 percent of employees using remote work, the lowest since the pandemic began, even though 81.9 percent said they wanted to keep it. The meeting, apparently, needs bodies.

## The Reckoning

Compare the two on the metric that matters to an arriving foreigner: how do you find out what people think? In Japan, you find out beforehand, if you have built the relationships that put you on the nemawashi list. Trust is the entry ticket, and the same source advises building personal relationships outside meetings for exactly that reason. In Taiwan, you find out afterwards, in private or in writing, when someone decides you have earned the sort of feedback that cannot be delivered in front of the group. Both reward patience. Neither rewards the person who says what they mean at 10:15 on a Tuesday.

Hofstede Insights offers little to separate them on one measure at least: the long-term orientation scores quoted on Wikipedia's summary of the framework put Japan at 88 and Taiwan at 93, which is to say two cultures equally willing to wait, and still capable of waiting in entirely different ways. The practical differences are structural. Japan is slower on the way in and more predictable once a decision is made; the formal meeting is a ritual you can learn. Taiwan is looser on the surface, with the office informal and the social gatherings frequent, but the rules live in the group chat, and the group chat does not stop at six. If you value clear working hours, the Taiwanese rule on after-hours messaging is appealing on paper, though the enforcement caveat is serious. If you value knowing how decisions get made, Japan's machinery is at least visible, even if the meeting it produces is mostly decorative.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Forumosa — paraphrased experience: A foreigner in Taiwan described offices as informal, with regular social gatherings among colleagues on good terms, but said some workplaces left him out of lunch invitations, and that being the only non-ethnic-Chinese person in the room creates distance even when the mood is friendly.*

> *Forumosa — paraphrased experience: A teacher in a Taipei private school said staff were expected to arrive twenty minutes before the contract time, with meetings running late or on Saturdays without time off in return. A reply claimed unpaid overtime is common for salaried staff and that people who challenge it are often pressured to leave.*

> *Taiwan Scene — paraphrased experience: An expat woman wrote that adjusting to indirect, high-context communication takes real effort, because voicing dissatisfaction means learning whom to approach and how to read an office's mood without being told. Work still spills into personal time through after-hours messages.*

> *Japan Today — paraphrased reader comments: Several people working for Japanese companies said meetings eat time without producing results. One described sessions lasting more than four hours that accomplished little, and said indirect communication stretches decisions to roughly twice the length at a Western firm.*

> *SoraNews24 — paraphrased experience: In an informal survey of seven foreign workers, one producer said meetings were overused, to the point of holding meetings to discuss other meetings. A publishing editor said foreign staff sat through long Japanese-language meetings mainly to be seen. Others praised colleagues for patiently explaining things.*

## Conclusion

Choose Japan if you can tolerate delay in exchange for a visible process, and Taiwan if you can tolerate ambiguity in exchange for an informal office. In both, learn who actually holds the decision, sit through the meeting without improvising, and find out which chat app your future manager considers a polite request.

Both countries have mastered the art of agreeing in public. The difficulty is finding out what happened to the disagreement.
