**Two Bosses, One Spreadsheet: A Relocation Guide to Brazil and Poland**

**🇧🇷 Brazil · 🇵🇱 Poland**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Hofstede's researchers rate Brazil and Poland almost identically on power distance — 69 and 68 out of 100, meaning both countries expect the boss to act like a boss and would find a Scandinavian consensus meeting faintly ridiculous. And yet an employee who thrives under a Brazilian manager can walk into a Warsaw office and mistake ordinary professionalism for personal hostility within the first fortnight. The hierarchy is the same height in both places. It is built from entirely different materials.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a relationship with your boss before you need anything from them | Expect a "no" to arrive by email; it usually arrives by delay |
| Save disagreement for a private conversation, not the group meeting | Publicly contradict a decision, even a bad one, in front of the team |
| Accept that your manager may ask about your family, weekend, or health | Treat that warmth as a sign the hierarchy has gone away |
| Dress and speak with visible respect toward senior staff | Assume a first-name basis means you're structurally equal |
| Let your boss make the final call, even after open debate | Push for a democratic vote on a decision that's already been made upstairs |

#### 🇵🇱 Poland
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use titles and surnames (or "Pan/Pani") until invited to drop them | Default to first names on day one, especially with anyone older or senior |
| Bring documented, structured answers to your manager | Show up to a 1:1 without a plan and expect it to read as confidence |
| Voice disagreement in writing or one-on-one, framed constructively | Contradict your manager in an open meeting, however diplomatically |
| Expect formality to loosen with tenure and trust | Mistake initial reserve for coldness; it usually isn't personal |
| Notice that younger managers increasingly want participatory input | Assume every Polish office runs like a 1990s ministry — some already don't |

---

Brazilian management runs on a logic best described as authoritative affection. Power distance is high — decisions are made at the top and cascade down, and junior staff are not generally expected to be in the room when the decision gets made (Hofstede Insights; Commisceo Global). But the same hierarchy that keeps a manager firmly in charge also obliges them to act like it, checking in on employees' families, health, and moods with what Global Business Culture calls a paternalistic style: the boss as a parent, guiding rather than merely instructing. Commisceo Global's management guide is explicit that feedback lands better delivered privately and wrapped in relational warmth, because public criticism — even gentle — reads as a breach of personal dignity, not merely a professional correction. The result is an office that feels emotionally close and structurally rigid at the same time, which is the exact combination that trips up newcomers from flatter, colder corporate cultures: they read the warmth as an invitation to informality the org chart was never offering.

Polish management runs on a different fuel: documented competence, delivered formally. World Business Culture describes managers who are expected to be authoritative and to already know the answer — issuing direct instructions, sometimes bluntly, and doing so through channels marked by title, surname, and the enduring "Pan/Pani" form of address (Cultural Atlas; World Business Culture). Contradicting a manager in an open meeting is, per multiple management guides, simply not done, even among Poles who otherwise pride themselves on directness. Statista's workplace-treatment survey data suggests a meaningful share of Polish employees have experienced public criticism or unrealistic short deadlines at work — evidence that the formality is not purely ceremonial; it sits atop a genuine expectation of deference. The reassuring footnote, repeated across sources, is generational: younger Polish managers are visibly warming to participatory, flatter teamwork, even if the median office hasn't caught up yet.

**The Reckoning.** Here is the part the Hofstede number doesn't tell you: two countries can share a power-distance score and still produce opposite failure modes for a transplant. Fail upward in Brazil and you'll likely be forgiven if you maintain the relationship — Brazilian hierarchy has a soft landing built in, because loyalty and warmth are partial currency against a misstep. Fail upward in Poland and the relationship buys you very little; competence and preparation are the currency, and warmth is something you earn afterward, not collateral you spend up front. Put bluntly: Brazil will forgive you for being unprepared if you're likeable. Poland will forgive you for being unlikeable if you're prepared. Pick your failure mode accordingly.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/brazil — one new hire recalled getting quietly pulled aside after questioning a manager's decision out loud in a team meeting; the decision itself was never revisited, but the lesson about where disagreement is allowed to happen landed immediately and permanently.*

> *Blind (Google Warsaw thread) — an employee weighing a Warsaw transfer was told the office runs mostly Polish, Russian, Ukrainian, and Belarusian, that international mobility from there is slow, and that the trade-off for lower pay than comparable roles elsewhere is a genuinely livable, low-cost city — a "golden cage," as one commenter put it, rather than a launchpad.*

> *Blind (Google/JPMorgan Warsaw threads) — another poster flagged the unglamorous physics of the role: syncing with US-based teams pushes some meetings into the evening, and low performers are managed out at a pace that surprised people expecting Central European work-life balance to mean something softer.*

> *Quora (on Brazilian social hierarchy) — a longtime resident argued that Brazilian offices can be warmer to your face and more status-conscious behind the scenes than newcomers expect, with subtle but real distinctions in how staff, drivers, and support workers are treated compared to management — a hierarchy that hides in plain sight behind the hugs.*

> *Quora (on Brazilian vs. Argentine business formality) — one answer advised that Brazilian meetings can look relaxed, even chatty, but the informality is a communication style, not a flattening of rank, and treating a friendly boss like a peer is the single fastest way to misjudge the room.*

### Conclusion

The practical upshot for anyone actually packing boxes: don't relocate on the strength of a single Hofstede number. Brazil and Poland will both hand you a boss who expects to be the final word, but you'll need almost opposite instincts to succeed under each one. In São Paulo, invest in the relationship first and the results will follow; in Warsaw, arrive with the answer already prepared and the relationship will follow instead.

Both systems work. Neither is interested in your opinion about whether it should.
