# Brazil vs. USA: Who Actually Tells You What You're Worth

## 🇧🇷 Brazil · 🇺🇸 USA

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Ask a Brazilian worker what they make and, thanks to a 1962 law, you get a number and a bonus month attached to it by statute. Ask an American the same question and you get a shrug, a change of subject, and possibly a fired conversation. Both countries insist their system is the fair one. Neither is entirely wrong, which is the annoying part.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm whether a quoted salary already includes the 13th-month bonus | Assume "monthly salary" means twelve payments a year — it's thirteen, by law |
| Understand CLT (formal contract) vs. PJ (invoicing as a contractor) before signing anything | Sign a PJ contract for a de facto full-time job without pricing in the lost benefits |
| Negotiate benefits and perks if base salary feels fixed | Push aggressively on numbers in the first meeting — it reads as confrontational |
| Expect vacation pay at 1/3 above your normal salary | Forget that FGTS (severance fund) accrues even if you never get fired |
| Ask HR directly about raise cycles and inflation adjustments (dissídio) | Expect transparent published pay bands — they're still rare |

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate the initial offer — it is expected, not rude | Accept the first number assuming it's final |
| Check state salary-transparency laws before an interview | Assume severance or notice is guaranteed if let go — at-will cuts both ways |
| Treat equity/RSUs as a real, if volatile, part of comp | Confuse gross salary with what lands in your account — withholding is aggressive |
| Ask peers or use Levels.fyi/Blind for real numbers | Discuss your exact salary casually at the office — it's still culturally taboo |
| Budget for employer-based health insurance as a compensation line item | Expect a mandatory year-end bonus — most "13th months" here are performance-based, not legal |

## Brazil: The Law Sets the Floor

Brazil's compensation system is built less on negotiation and more on statute. The décimo terceiro — the 13th salary — has been mandatory since 1962, paid in two installments between February and December, and cannot be withheld for performance, discretion, or a bad quarter (Wikipedia/Brazil Counsel; ADP). Add vacation pay at a mandatory one-third premium, FGTS severance contributions of 8% of salary accrued monthly regardless of eventual termination, and INSS social security, and a CLT (formally registered) employee's real compensation runs 70–80% above the quoted gross salary in employer cost terms (Asanify; Dupple 2026 guide). None of that is negotiated. It's owed.

What is negotiated, increasingly, is the choice between CLT and PJ (pessoa jurídica) status — invoicing as your own one-person company. PJ contracts commonly pay 30–40% more in headline terms because the worker forgoes the 13th salary, FGTS, and statutory vacation, taking on tax filing under a simplified regime that can run as low as 6–10% versus CLT's effective ~27.5% (Talenlio 2026 tech salary guide; ZS Advogados). It's a real trade-off dressed up as a raise. National average salary sits around BRL 3,679/month as of February 2026 (Boundless/wages index) — a figure that makes the CLT-versus-PJ math matter more than the sticker price.

## USA: The Number Is Negotiable, the Floor Isn't Guaranteed

The American system inverts Brazil's logic entirely: almost nothing is legally mandated, and almost everything is up for discussion. There is no statutory 13th month, no mandatory severance, and — under at-will employment, the default in every state but Montana — an employer can terminate a worker for nearly any reason, with no notice and no payout, unless a contract says otherwise (Ogletree; CNBC 2022). Median weekly earnings for full-time US workers hit $1,251 in Q2 2026, roughly $65,000 annualized (BLS), but that figure is a base-pay proxy — it excludes the bonuses, commissions, and equity that dominate compensation in finance and tech. Software developers alone post a BLS median of $133,080, while Levels.fyi's crowdsourced tech total-compensation median sits at $192,000, nearly triple the national figure once RSUs and bonuses are counted.

That gap between "salary" and "total comp" is why negotiation is expected, not just tolerated: the offer letter is a starting bid, not a legal entitlement, and platforms like Levels.fyi and TeamBlind exist to arm workers with data employers won't volunteer. Pay transparency laws now force salary ranges into postings in a growing number of states, but discussing your actual number with a coworker remains a quiet taboo even where it's legally protected — America mandates almost nothing about pay, then compensates with anxiety.

## The Reckoning

Put the two systems side by side and the philosophical split is almost too clean: Brazil legislates fairness and leaves negotiation at the margins; the US legislates almost nothing and expects you to negotiate everything, including your own job security. Hofstede's data frames why — Brazil scores 69 on Power Distance versus the US's 40, meaning Brazilian workers are culturally primed to accept hierarchy-set outcomes (including pay) rather than contest them, while the US's higher Individualism score (60 vs. Brazil's 36) rewards the self-advocating negotiator (hofstede-insights.com/country-comparison/brazil,the-usa). Neither posture is irrational; each is a response to what the state does or doesn't guarantee.

Globalization is quietly merging the two. Brazilian tech workers hired remotely by US firms now report senior-engineer pay around $84,000 — several multiples of the domestic median — creating a two-tier market where "Brazilian salary" means either the CLT floor or the dollar-denominated ceiling, nothing much in between (Hacker News discussion; Tremendous). Meanwhile American workers watching layoffs land with zero notice are rediscovering the appeal of a floor they never had. Both sides are eyeing the other's system and wondering if they picked the wrong one.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who'd negotiated a relocation package with their existing employer wrote that the biggest mistake was assuming the new-country salary would simply scale by cost of living; instead they had to separately clarify whether a Brazilian offer already folded in the 13th month, since companies routinely quote the number both ways depending on what makes the offer look better.*

> *Hacker News — A commenter comparing Brazilian and US tech pay noted that a senior engineer hired remotely by a US company from Brazil can land compensation several multiples above the domestic median, but warned that this creates two entirely separate labor markets inside one country — one paid in reals at CLT floors, one paid in dollars with none of the legal protections.*

> *TeamBlind — A tech worker described the platform's appeal as filling a gap employers deliberately leave open: without crowdsourced numbers, most engineers had no real sense of what a fair offer looked like, and many described negotiation itself as something they'd been culturally trained to feel guilty about.*

> *Expat relocation blog — An American who'd hired staff in Brazil admitted being blindsided the first time a bad quarter didn't excuse the 13th-month payment; used to discretionary year-end bonuses, they hadn't grasped it was a legal obligation with fines attached, not a gesture of goodwill.*

> *Expat forum — A Brazilian who'd relocated to the US described the shock of a first paycheck showing only net pay with no separate bonus line item anywhere on the calendar, and of realizing that "salary" in America is a number you can lose overnight, not a guarantee wrapped in a labor code.*

## Conclusion

Brazil hands its workers a compensation floor written into law and lets the ceiling sort itself out through the CLT-versus-PJ gamble; the US hands its workers a negotiating table and calls the absence of a floor "flexibility." Both produce real winners — the Brazilian CLT employee who never has to ask for a 13th month, the American engineer who talks their way into six figures of equity — and real losers, namely anyone in either country who doesn't know the rules well enough to work them. The honest takeaway for anyone moving between the two: in Brazil, read the labor code; in America, read the room, and then read Levels.fyi.
