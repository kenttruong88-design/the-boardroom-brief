# Austria Serves Lunch Like a Sermon. South Korea Serves It Like a Group Project.

🇦🇹 Austria · 🇰🇷 South Korea

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Both countries feed their citizens almost identically well. Austria's dietary energy supply sits above 3,500 kilocalories per person per day, and South Korea's runs close behind at roughly 3,430, according to FAO food balance data — a near-identical caloric budget delivered by two civilizations that agree on almost nothing else about what a meal is for. In Austria, food is a private ritual you're entitled to occupy alone for hours over one coffee. In South Korea, it's a coordination exercise, with order minimums, drink rounds, and a seating order set by age. Neither is wrong. Both will make a new arrival feel like they've wandered onto the wrong film set.

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy groceries by Saturday — shops close early on weekdays, all day Sunday and holidays | Assume a petrol station will bail you out on a Sunday |
| Say "Mahlzeit" before eating, even in a shared office kitchen | Rush a coffeehouse table — one coffee buys the chair all afternoon |
| Tell the waiter your order when paying so bills split cleanly ("getrennt, bitte") | Try to calculate a shared total and divide it evenly |
| Keep both hands visible on the table while eating | Order Schnitzel expecting veal by default — pork is standard unless it says "vom Kalb" |
| Expect a sit-down, multi-course culture even at lunch | Bring a laptop into a traditional coffeehouse and work from it |
| Tip by rounding up and stating the total to the server | Leave cash on the table and walk out |

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wait for the eldest person at the table to eat first | Pour your own drink — refill others' and let them refill yours |
| Use both hands when pouring for someone senior | Stick chopsticks upright in a rice bowl — it echoes a funeral ritual |
| Get comfortable with delivery apps like Baedal Minjok | Assume every dish comes in single portions — many carry a two-person minimum |
| Accept free side dishes (banchan) arriving unordered | Assume the bill splits evenly every time — one person often covers the whole tab |
| Seek out honbap-friendly chains for a low-pressure solo meal | Sit alone at a shared-grill restaurant and expect a warm welcome |
| Learn a few Korean food words — small effort reads well | Turn the meal into a loud negotiation |

### Austria

Austria's relationship with food starts at the checkout. According to Statistics Austria's 2024/25 Household Budget Survey, food and non-alcoholic beverages account for 11.6 percent of average monthly household spending of €4,170 — smaller than housing and energy, which alone eat up more than a quarter of the budget, but a category treated with disproportionate ceremony. The Viennese coffeehouse, recognized by UNESCO as intangible cultural heritage in 2011, still runs on the principle that one coffee buys a table, a newspaper, and hours of nobody's business but your own. Arrivals from cities built on table-turnover economics tend to find this either the most civilized thing they've encountered, or a mild affront to productivity.

The other adjustment is logistical. Retail hours assume Sunday isn't for grocery shopping: supermarkets close early on weekdays and shut entirely on Sundays and holidays, turning Saturday afternoon into a semi-mandatory errand. Combine that with a dining culture where "to-go" is treated as an American eccentricity, and Austria asks new residents to plan meals like a household manager, not a commuter grabbing lunch between meetings.

### South Korea

South Korea's food economy pulls the opposite way — toward speed, delivery, and volume — before circling back to hierarchy the moment people sit down together. Dining-out spending has recently been retreating: reporting in the Korea JoongAng Daily, citing the restaurant production index, found dining-out activity falling roughly 2 percent in 2024 and continuing to shrink into 2025 as households responded to food-price inflation by cooking more and ordering delivery instead. That barely registers as inconvenience, because South Korea's delivery infrastructure — apps like Baedal Minjok, cited among the fastest and most saturated anywhere — makes eating in nearly indistinguishable from eating out.

What delivery apps don't dissolve is hierarchy. Meals, especially after-work dinners known as hoesik, run on rules: the most senior person eats first, drinks are poured for others and never for oneself, and a glass is topped up before it runs empty. Hofstede Insights scores South Korea at 18 on individualism against Austria's 55 — a gap that shows up plainly at a dinner table, where a Korean meal functions as a small negotiation of rank and an Austrian one is a solo assignment completed in silence.

### The Reckoning

Put the two systems side by side and the counterintuitive part isn't the food — it's who ends up eating alone. Austria, the more individualist society by Hofstede's measure, built an entire cultural institution, the coffeehouse, explicitly to accommodate solitude in public. South Korea, the more collectivist society, had to coin a word — honbap — because eating alone was unusual enough to need naming, and at least one noodle shop has posted a sign effectively refusing solo diners, reasoning it didn't want to "sell loneliness." The individualist country protects your right to sit alone with a coffee for hours; the collectivist one still treats it, in some settings, as faintly suspicious.

The other reversal is about money. In Austria, checks are settled cleanly and immediately — "getrennt, bitte," separate please, is one of the first phrases any transplant learns — so nobody leaves owing anybody a favor. In South Korea, one person frequently covers the entire bill in group or work settings, with the informal expectation that the gesture rotates across future outings — a system running on memory and obligation rather than a splitting app.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who relocated to Seoul wrote that the hardest adjustment wasn't the taste of the food but its social architecture: many comfort dishes are portioned for two or more, so a quick solo dinner often meant hunting a chain built for single diners, or awkwardly paying for a meal meant for a group.*

> *An expat blog covering Austria (viennawurstelstand.com) described the pleasant surprise that a single small coffee genuinely does buy unlimited time at a café table — no server hovering to turn the seat, a pace that felt suspiciously generous after living somewhere with faster table economics.*

> *Quora — A contributor described a misread at an early company dinner in Korea: pouring their own drink without thinking, only to realize afterward it read as a breach of etiquette, since pouring is done for others, not oneself, especially toward anyone senior.*

> *A relocation-company blog cataloguing culture shocks in Austria (recom-relocation.at) noted new residents are regularly caught out by Sunday closures, assuming a petrol station would cover a grocery gap the way it might elsewhere, only to find most of those close early too.*

> *A traveler on the Rick Steves Travel Forum, discussing food customs in Austria and Germany, advised newcomers to learn the phrase for paying separately before their first group meal, since mentally splitting a shared total is a losing battle compared to telling the server each person's order.*

### Conclusion

The practical question isn't which country eats better — it's which discomfort you'd rather manage. Austria rewards people who can plan groceries around a Sunday shutdown and genuinely enjoy their own company at a café table for an afternoon. South Korea rewards people who can move fast, order for a group, and accept that a dinner table is also, quietly, a hierarchy chart. Get the logistics backward in either country — try a quick solo bite in Seoul's shared-plate restaurants, or expect Viennese retail hours to bend around your schedule — and the friction won't be about the food at all.

If a friend asked, Priya would say this: pick Austria if you want the right to be left alone with your lunch, and pick South Korea if you want lunch delivered to your door in fifteen minutes flat — just don't expect either country to apologize for making you learn its rules first.
