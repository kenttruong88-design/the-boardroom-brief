# Sweden Pays Fathers to Stay Home and Wonders Why They Leave Early, While Poland Waits for Them to Ask

🇵🇱 Poland · 🇸🇪 Sweden

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Sweden offers a new parent 480 days of paid leave and has spent roughly fifty years wondering why the other parent keeps coming back to the office early. Poland offers the mother 20 weeks of maternity leave at full allowance, a generous parental leave on top, and a nursery system in which, by one late-2024 policy brief, about one child in four could find a place. Both countries have solved the problem on paper. The question for the prospective emigrant is which paper to believe.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for a nursery place (żłobek) as early as you can, ideally during pregnancy. | Assume a place will be free when your leave ends. |
| Check which of your leave entitlements are transferable to the father, and plan the split with your employer in writing. | Assume the father's quota will be used simply because it exists. |
| Ask HR about the 81.5% option for combined maternity and parental leave and the deadline for requesting it. | Miss the 21-day window after the birth. |
| Look into the Active Parent payments if your child is 12 to 35 months old. | Treat the payments as a substitute for childcare. |
| Ask in advance how your team handled the last parent's return. | Announce a pregnancy before you understand how your contract treats it. |

### Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for a daycare place early and keep the confirmation; municipalities set their own rules. | Count on a place appearing on the day you want it. |
| Split the leave, and use your 90 reserved days. | Let them lapse; they cannot be transferred. |
| Tell your manager your leave plan well ahead, with dates. | Treat the statute as a guarantee of colleagues' enthusiasm. |
| Check the benefit ceiling against your salary before you plan the household budget. | Assume 80% of a high salary is what arrives. |
| Learn the vab routine for sick children, which is a normal absence. | Apologise for it at length. |

## Poland: The Long Leave and the Short Queue

According to the Polish government's family portal, maternity leave is 20 weeks for one child, with longer periods for multiple births, paid at 100% of the allowance basis. A mother who asks within 21 days of the birth can instead receive 81.5% across the combined maternity and parental leave. Parental leave then adds, in principle, 41 or 43 weeks. A policy brief from Free Policy Briefs adds that a 9-week portion is reserved for fathers, alongside 2 weeks of paternity leave.

The reservation has not changed behaviour as much as the statute intended. The same brief reports that in 2021 about 377,000 mothers claimed parental leave benefits against about 4,000 fathers. It also finds that mothers whose youngest child is between one and three have an employment rate of 62%, against 98% for fathers in the same position, and that in 68% of two-earner families the mother provides most of the care. Poland has a high female activity rate; it simply pauses it. Roughly 85% of mothers surveyed stepped away from work for more than eight months.

Childcare is the other half of the arrangement. The brief puts nursery availability at about one child in four aged up to three in 2023, with some 45% of municipalities lacking formal provision for the age group, and a 2024 audit called the expansion programme insufficient. Statistics Poland reports the supply is growing: 256,200 places at the end of 2025, and 5,122 nurseries against 4,776 a year earlier. The European Commission's Eurydice network adds that places are scarce and working parents are prioritised, which is a reasonable policy and an unwelcome discovery for anyone who is between jobs. The state's answer from October 2024 is Active Parent, offering up to 350 euros a month toward work-related care, which is a subsidy more than a solution.

The brief also notes that the programme will be judged on mothers returning to work, nursery enrolments and the growth of places in municipalities, and it records that fertility fell from 1.45 in 2017 to 1.16 in 2023. A country that pays generously for time at home, then offers few places in which to leave a child, has given parents a choice that is mostly made for them. The nursery sector, meanwhile, is largely run by private operators, which the Statistics Poland figures show: most of the nurseries counted in 2025 were operated by natural persons.

## Sweden: Everyone Is Equal, Some Are More Equal in March

The Swedish Social Insurance Agency sets out 480 days for one child. Of these, 390 are paid at the sickness benefit level and 90 at a basic rate of SEK 180 a day. Where two parents share, each has 90 days that cannot be transferred. An earlier guide from The Local put the benefit at almost 80% of salary up to a daily ceiling, and noted that parents may claim on the same day for up to 60 days while the child is young. The same guide says the mother must take two weeks around the birth, that up to 45 days can be passed to someone other than a partner, such as a grandparent, and that new fathers are entitled to ten days connected to the birth. This is the sort of detail that makes a Swedish benefits page read like a railway timetable. The figures change, and anyone relocating should check the current ones.

Uptake is where Swedish virtue meets Swedish practice. Researchers at the University of Gothenburg, writing with a colleague from Indiana University in 2019, found that Swedish fathers take less than a third of the leave mothers take. About 80% of working Swedish men are in the private sector, where many firms have no plan for covering absences and rely on colleagues' loyalty instead. Fathers, the researchers report, shorten their leave to avoid burdening coworkers and worry that being absent will mark them as less than the ideal employee. The generosity is real; so is the guilt.

Childcare is cheaper than it is punctual. In an Expat.com thread, a poster quoted Stockholm's fee caps at 3% of income for a first child, 2% for a second and 1% for a third, with a monthly maximum for each, and a free allowance of hours from age three. Another thread, from Gothenburg, described daycares as often overbooked. A cultural footnote: Hofstede Insights is usually cited for Sweden's masculinity score of 5 and uncertainty avoidance of 29, a pair of numbers consistent with a country that legislates caring and then worries about whether it is being too forward in using it. Poland's figures could not be retrieved for this piece, so no comparison is offered.

## The Reckoning

On entitlement, Sweden wins by length and by rate of replacement, and Poland wins by the clarity of its 20 weeks. Both countries reserve time for fathers, Sweden 90 days and Poland 9 weeks, and both reveal that reserved time is not taken time. The gap between them is not in the statute but in the infrastructure: a Swedish family can expect, eventually, a municipal daycare place at a capped fee, whereas a Polish family is more likely to be shopping for a private nursery, a relative or a subsidy.

The career effect is therefore different in kind. In Poland, the penalty falls largely on the mother, in the form of the long pause and the employment gap. In Sweden it falls on whoever takes the leave, in the form of a quieter question about commitment. Neither is a scandal. Both are the sort of thing a relocation brochure would summarise as a work-life balance.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Local Sweden — a call centre operator who took paternity leave said his colleagues, especially the men, were not very understanding, which sits oddly with the national self-image.*

> *Expat.com forum — a parent in Gothenburg described daycares in big cities as often overbooked, with some parents struggling to return to full-time work for lack of a place.*

> *Expat.com forum — a poster set out Stockholm's fee caps as a share of income, and added that the thread was about five years old, so everyone should check current rates.*

> *Expat Exchange — an expat who gave birth in Poznan called prenatal, labour and postnatal care excellent and the costs modest as an EU citizen, and advised new arrivals to learn the leave rules and childcare options before the birth.*

## Conclusion

If your priority is a long, well-paid leave with a daycare place at a capped fee, Sweden is the stronger bet, provided you apply early and plan to use your share of the leave rather than admire it. If you value a clear statutory 20 weeks and a high rate of female employment, and can arrange private childcare, Poland is workable, though the burden of the long pause will probably land on one parent. In either country, ask your future employer how the last person returned, and watch for the pause before the answer.

Both countries have achieved the rare feat of making parenthood legal. Making it ordinary is still pending in both, with no decision date.
