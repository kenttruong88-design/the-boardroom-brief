# Bulgaria Lists the Salary After You Ask Nicely; Britain Lists It and Then Pretends You Did Not Read It

🇧🇬 Bulgaria · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

On 11 September 2026, Bulgaria's Council of Ministers sent a draft law on pay transparency to the National Assembly, having missed the EU's transposition deadline of 7 June by a little over three months. In Britain, around 70 percent of job adverts on Indeed already state the pay, and 87 percent of workers are uncomfortable asking a colleague what theirs is. One country has the law without the habit; the other has the habit with a very specific exception. Neither will be of much use in your first salary negotiation, which you will conduct alone.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Bulgaria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| State a number; forum advice is to open high, around 3,500 to 4,000 lev for experienced engineers | Wait for the employer to name a figure first |
| Negotiate the package as well as the base, including hybrid work, relocation and a pay review | Assume the base salary is the only lever |
| Treat an ad with a stated salary as generally reliable | Treat a missing range as a sign of anything sinister yet; the mandatory range comes only once the law is promulgated |
| Interview in person where you can; forum posters say it works better in Bulgaria | Rely on a video call to carry a negotiation |
| Treat 3,500 to 6,000 lev ads from trading or crypto firms with suspicion | Mistake a best-case sales bonus for a salary |

### In the United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a pay range in the advertisement; Indeed measured roughly 56 to 71 percent of postings with pay, depending on the study | Treat a missing figure as normal; most workers want ranges in ads |
| Ask the recruiter for the range directly | Wait to be told |
| Prepare for politeness to stand in for agreement | Hear a vague answer as a yes |
| Keep your own salary to yourself unless asked by someone with a reason to know | Ask colleagues what they earn over lunch |
| Compare your offer with the range, and with the person who will sit next to you, privately | Assume contracts that discourage pay talk are enforceable in every case |

## Bulgaria

According to CMS Law's legal update from 14 September 2026, the Bulgarian bill would require employers to give applicants the starting salary or its range, to use gender-neutral job titles and to stop asking applicants about their pay history. Pay reporting and joint pay assessment would start on 7 June 2027 for employers with 150 or more staff and on 7 June 2031 for those with 100 to 149. It is a bill, not yet a law, and the missed deadline suggests that the usual European tradition of declaring a principle and then arriving at it later is in good health.

In the meantime, salary conversations in Bulgaria are negotiations in the older sense of the word. A thread on the Expat.com forum, from a hardware engineer planning to ask for about 3,650 lev a month in Sofia, drew advice that it was high but fine as an opening bid, that around 3,000 net was likely, and that an in-person interview at a Sofia business park worked better than a remote one. Another poster suggested opening at roughly 4,000 and negotiating down, noting that many experienced software engineers earn 4,000 to 5,000. A Reddit thread on r/Sofia, about a graduate who planned to ask for 2,500 leva net from a large US firm's Sofia office, produced the advice to frame the number as a minimum or to cite another offer, and to bargain for benefits. A reply also noted that foreign offices in Bulgaria often exist to cut costs.

Hofstede's scores, via The Culture Factor, give Bulgaria a power distance of 70 and uncertainty avoidance of 85, against the UK's 35 and 35, via Rework. In plain terms, Bulgarian employers hold authority and prefer to know what they are getting, which is a good reason to bring a number and a reason. The wider context is that Bulgaria's headline gender pay gap was reported by the national news agency as around 20 percent, against about 13 percent for women in the EU, according to the European Women's Lobby president quoted in the same article. Without published ranges, a person negotiating alone is negotiating with less information than the employer holds, and negotiation advice on Bulgarian forums is effectively an informal pay register.

## United Kingdom

British employers have moved quickly on transparency in advertising and slowly on everything else. Indeed Hiring Lab's December 2024 data found that 69.7 percent of UK postings carried salary information, compared with 19.3 percent in Italy and 15.8 percent in Germany. HR Review reported in March 2025 that 71 percent of UK job adverts include pay, the highest in Europe, and that 81 percent of workers and jobseekers want ranges in ads. More recent Indeed data reported by FE News in May 2026 put the figure at 56 percent, and the difference is probably method, so quote the study and not the vibe.

What has not changed is the taboo. In the same Indeed survey, 49 percent of respondents said discussing pay is still a social taboo, 87 percent were uncomfortable asking colleagues their salary and 84 percent were unwilling to share their own. Only 16 percent were comfortable sharing with colleagues, against 62 percent with a partner. When asked what they would do if a colleague asked, 26 percent would politely decline, 24 percent would be vague, 17 percent would share openly and 5 percent would be offended. The vague answer is the most British of the four, and the one with the longest shelf life.

A thread from 2014 on a British cycling forum, which should be treated as flavour and not evidence, shows how the debate runs. Some said that asking is bad manners; others said that contracts often ban pay talk and that such clauses are largely unenforceable when someone is checking an equal pay claim; several argued that secrecy mainly benefits employers. Hofstede's individualism score of 89 for the UK suggests the habit is as much about privacy as shame: a British person does not see why his pay is anyone's business, which is exactly why it is so hard to find out.

Both countries reward preparation, though the preparation looks different. In Britain the homework is mostly arithmetic: read the advertised range, work out where in it your experience sits, and be ready to explain why. In Bulgaria the homework is investigative: ask recruiters, read the forum threads, look at which firms post salaries and treat those listings as the most reliable anchor, since the Expat.com replies said ads with stated pay are generally dependable. Hofstede's high uncertainty avoidance score for Bulgaria suggests employers like to know what they are getting, so come with evidence of what you have delivered. In both places, the pay conversation goes better with a specific figure than with a general hope.

## The Reckoning

The two countries have opposite problems and a similar result. In Britain, you can see the number before you apply and cannot discuss it afterwards. In Bulgaria, you will probably not see a range until the law arrives, but the open online forums are blunt about what to ask for and what to expect. The British ad tells you where the negotiation starts; the Bulgarian forum tells you where it ends.

For a migrant, the practical consequence is that each country requires you to do the part the other has done for you. In Britain, ask the recruiter for the range if it is missing, and then do not expect colleagues to confirm it. In Bulgaria, build your own range from forum threads, recruiters and, ideally, someone in the job, and open higher than feels comfortable. The coming law will help, but a law passed by Parliament in 2027 will not negotiate on your behalf in 2026.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — an engineer planning to ask for about 3,650 lev a month in Sofia is told it is a bit high but fine as an opening bid, and that around 3,000 net is realistic. Another poster says to interview in person and open near 4,000 before negotiating down, and a third notes that 3,000 covers living costs but may not leave enough for flights home.*

> *Reddit (r/Sofia, read via a mirror) — a graduate asks about requesting 2,500 leva net at a large US company's Sofia office. Some say that is fine and others say it is low and suggest 3,000 to 3,500. One notes that language-skill call-centre roles pay around 2,000 to 2,100, and that foreign offices in Bulgaria often exist to save money.*

> *Expat.com forum — a German-speaking finance professional doubts jobs.bg listings of 2,500 and 2,800 leva gross. A reply says ads with stated salaries are generally reliable, but warns that trading or crypto firms advertising 3,500 to 6,000 leva may be scams or may be quoting best-case sales bonuses.*

> *SingleTrack World forum (2014, UK) — posters disagree on whether it is rude to ask a colleague what they earn. Some say asking is bad manners, others that contract clauses forbidding pay talk are largely unenforceable when someone is checking an equal pay claim, and several say secrecy mainly benefits employers.*

## Conclusion

If you are heading to Bulgaria, do your homework in the forums, bring a number and be ready to bargain on benefits as well as base pay. Do not trust an advert that promises a trading salary of 6,000 leva and a sales bonus in the same sentence. If you are heading to Britain, expect to see the number on the ad, expect no help in finding out whether anybody else gets more and prepare for a very polite response if you ask.

The law is catching up in one country and the culture is not catching up in the other. In both, the person who can state a number without apologising has the advantage.
