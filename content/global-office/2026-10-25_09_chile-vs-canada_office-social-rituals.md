# Chile Will Feed You for Three Hours. Canada Will Ask About the Weather and Go Home.

**🇨🇱 Chile · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Consider two facts, filed an ocean and a hemisphere apart. In Chile, the lunch break has historically run so long that the government has had to legislate around it, and even the new law compressing the workweek from 45 hours to 40 — phased in through 2028 under Law No. 21.561 — says nothing about touching the midday pause itself. In Canada, meanwhile, a 2017 OfficeTeam survey of more than 550 office workers found that only 29 percent of them actually enjoy the company holiday party, while 21 percent describe attending as an obligation rather than a gift. One culture built a legal architecture around eating together at length; the other built a quiet consensus that eating together should, ideally, be optional and brief. Anyone relocating between these two offices should budget their social calendar accordingly.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇱 Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the first asado invitation, even if "6pm" quietly means 9pm | Expect the lunch break to double as a quick errand-run; it is a social institution |
| Learn to eat standing, hand to hand, at a barbecue — cutlery is optional | Rush out of a work lunch the moment the food is served |
| Open meetings with family and weekend small talk before any agenda item | Raise politics, religion, or unflattering comparisons to other countries at the table |
| Greet female colleagues with a cheek kiss in informal settings | Assume one handshake style covers every greeting, every time |
| Expect office hierarchy to shape who initiates social invitations | Treat "once," the afternoon tea ritual, as just a coffee break to skip |
| Pace yourself — the asado is a marathon, not a transaction | Show up to a long lunch already mentally drafting your 1pm agenda |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to the team lunch even briefly — quiet absence gets noticed | Assume an "optional" social event carries no cost to skipping entirely |
| Bring a dish to the office potluck, ideally one from your own background | Bring up salary, immigration status, religion, or personal health near the coffee machine |
| Keep small talk light — weather, weekend plans, hockey, local restaurants | Decline every single happy hour; consistent absence reads as disengagement |
| Order a non-alcoholic drink at after-work drinks without a word of explanation | Assume alcohol is mandatory to belong at Friday drinks |
| Expect many social events to be informally organized, not lavishly catered | Overstay your enthusiasm at the holiday party; a large share of colleagues are quietly lukewarm on it too |

Chile's relationship to the office lunch is less a scheduling quirk than a structural feature of the economy. The country has for years ranked among the OECD's most hours-heavy labour markets — fifth among member states as of the most recent comparison, at 1,988 annual hours worked per employee, trailing only Mexico, Costa Rica, South Korea, and Greece, and running well ahead of Germany's 1,371. Legislators have responded not by shortening the midday pause but by trimming the bookends around it: Law No. 21.561, passed this year, phases the workweek down from 45 hours to 40 by 2028 without touching the lunch hour that, per labour-law guides covering the country, has long run well past the standard hour found elsewhere.

Hofstede Insights scores Chile at 63 on power distance and a mere 23 on individualism — one of the more collectivist readings in Latin America — alongside a notably high 86 on uncertainty avoidance, a combination that helps explain an office culture built on hierarchy, personal trust, and ritual rather than improvisation. In practice this means the asado, the weekend or after-work barbecue that functions as the country's real team-building exercise, is less a parenthesis to the working day than an extension of it. One traveler who lived in Chile recalls being warned that a 6pm invitation really meant food around nine, and that guests eat standing, passing dishes hand to hand with no assigned plates or silverware — a structure that would baffle most HR departments elsewhere but is, locally, simply how colleagues become something closer to family.

That emphasis on relationship before transaction extends into the office itself. Guides aimed at prospective movers describe Chilean workplaces as noticeably more hierarchical than flat, with meetings opening on family and weekend chat before any business item, and a midday break that one contributor flatly calls longer than "the standard one-hour break" expected elsewhere. The lesson for an arriving employee is not that Chileans work fewer hours — the OECD figures argue the opposite — but that those hours are organized around trust-building rituals that a northern calendar would likely file under inefficient.

Canada's office social calendar runs on a different operating system entirely: voluntary, itemized, and faintly anxious about obligation. An ADP Canada survey found that half of workers have employers who organize lighthearted social events at least quarterly, most commonly team lunches (29 percent) and seasonal or holiday gatherings (22 percent), and that employees link these events to stronger colleague relationships (48 percent) and a greater sense of belonging (40 percent). Yet the same culture that schedules these events seems faintly embarrassed by them: that OfficeTeam survey of Canadian office workers found only 29 percent actually enjoy the holiday party, 40 percent are lukewarm at best, and younger employees — 24 percent of those aged 18 to 34, versus 11 percent of those 55 and older — feel outright pressure to attend something officially described as optional.

The informal register is just as codified, if unwritten. Newcomer guides catalogue an approved small-talk menu — weather, weekend plans, hockey, local restaurants — and the topics kept firmly off it: religion, politics, salary, immigration status, personal health. Forum testimony backs this up with specifics that read like minor culture shocks rather than scandals: one contributor to Hacker News, a former IBM Canada employee, recalled offices stocked with little beyond "meh coffee," leaving staff to routinely pack their own lunch, while another described labmates who declined a shared coffee break in favour of sitting alone on Facebook. A third commenter, who had relocated from Canada to Germany, noted that unpaid overtime had been "fairly common" among peers back home, and that after the move they found themselves coaching new colleagues not to expect email replies outside office hours — a small but telling sign that Canada's social warmth at lunch does not always extend to respect for the clock after five.

Put side by side, the irony is almost too neat. Chile, scoring a mere 23 on that individualism scale, builds its most intense office bonding around a multi-hour, hierarchy-respecting ritual nobody would dare rush; Canada, scoring 80 on the same measure, builds its bonding around short, itemized, frequently declined events that a large share of the workforce would rather skip. The notionally communal culture produces a longer and less optional social obligation than the notionally individualist one.

The other reversal is temporal. Chile's long hours — among the OECD's highest — get spent partly recovering the pleasure of the table; Canada's shorter, more clock-bound culture often converts "work-life balance" into employees quietly logging unpaid overtime anyway, per forum accounts, while declining a company lunch because it feels like more work, not less. Neither country has solved the problem of making the office feel like somewhere people want to linger — they have simply distributed the discomfort differently.

### Conclusion

For someone actually weighing this move, the practical difference is not warmth versus coldness — both cultures like their colleagues well enough — but tempo. Chile asks you to accept a slower, more ritualized working day, where the long lunch and the asado are quietly part of the job description; Canada asks you to accept a faster, more optional version, where skipping the potluck is technically allowed but noticed, and where the real cost shows up later as unpaid overtime rather than a missed meal. Choose based on whether you would rather spend the extra hours at a table or alone at your desk.

If pressed for one honest line over drinks: Chile will overfeed you into belonging, and Canada will let you opt out of almost everything except the quiet expectation that you will answer the email anyway.
