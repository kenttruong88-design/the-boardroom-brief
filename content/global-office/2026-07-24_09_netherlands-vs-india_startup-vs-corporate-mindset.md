# Flat by Default vs. Flat by Choice: Startup and Corporate Mindsets in the Netherlands and India

**🇳🇱 Netherlands · 🇮🇳 India**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Amsterdam, a flat hierarchy isn't a startup perk — it's close to the cultural default, corporate or not. In Bangalore, flat hierarchy is a genuine differentiator, one startups actively market against the layered, brand-name stability of India's IT services giants. The same words, "flat" and "fast," mean structurally different things depending on which side of this comparison you're standing on.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect genuinely flat access even at large corporates — C-level executives regularly take one-on-ones with junior staff | Assume "startup energy" is unique to startups — much of Dutch corporate culture already operates this way |
| Join Friday "borrels" (informal after-work drinks) — they're a real, recurring social institution, not optional extra | Skip them entirely and expect no effect on how connected you feel to the team |
| Take advantage of Amsterdam's dense founder network — support between entrepreneurs is collaborative, not zero-sum | Expect Silicon Valley-style aggressive competitiveness — Dutch startup culture is comparatively low-ego |
| Recognize failure isn't heavily stigmatized in the Dutch startup scene | Assume a previous failed venture will count against you the way it might in more risk-averse cultures |

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Weigh brand-name MNC stability against startup compensation carefully — startups often pay more (35-45 LPA vs. 25-38 LPA at comparable seniority) but with less security | Assume a startup role protects you the way an MNC's formal termination process does — startups can let people go without the same due process |
| Expect real ownership and fast execution at startups, especially in hubs like Bangalore, Hyderabad, and Pune | Expect the same autonomy at a large IT services corporate — decisions move through more layers |
| Value the brand-name recognition of large MNCs in future job searches — recruiters still weight it heavily | Undervalue how much a well-known employer name helps get you the next interview |
| Ask directly about team structure before joining — flatter hierarchies are real but concentrated in tech hubs and startups specifically | Assume flat, hands-off management is the national norm — hierarchy-driven structures remain common outside these pockets |

### Netherlands: The Startup Mentality Isn't Confined to Startups

Amsterdam's startup ecosystem has genuinely outpaced much larger European hubs — the city recorded an average annual growth rate of 53% in its startup scene versus London's 8% over a comparable period, according to reporting compiled from ecosystem data. But the more distinctive fact for anyone comparing corporate and startup life here is how little the two diverge culturally. Dutch workplaces, corporate or not, tend to run on genuinely flat access: titles exist, but casual conversation across levels is normal, and Mindspace's guide to Amsterdam work culture describes C-level executives holding regular one-on-ones with junior employees as unremarkable rather than exceptional.

What does distinguish the startup layer is intensity and risk tolerance, not hierarchy. Amsterdam's entrepreneurial culture is collaborative rather than cutthroat — founders support each other through formal and informal networks instead of treating the ecosystem as zero-sum, per Forbes's reporting on the Dutch scene — and failure carries comparatively little stigma. The corporate-vs-startup choice in the Netherlands is mostly about pace and equity upside, not about escaping a rigid hierarchy, because the hierarchy most newcomers are picturing largely isn't there in the first place.

### India: The Divide Is Real, and It's About Structure, Not Just Pace

India's contrast is sharper and more consequential. Bangalore alone held 44% of India's startup funding in a recent NASSCOM-reported year, drawing an estimated $4.2 billion in investment, and the city runs alongside a deeply entrenched large-corporate IT services sector built around firms like TCS, Infosys, and Wipro. The cultural gap between the two is substantial: tech-hub startups run flatter hierarchies and faster decision cycles, appealing directly to Millennial and Gen Z workers who, per multiple 2026 workplace surveys, want meaningful work and regular feedback — yet 41% of employees at more traditional Indian companies still report active disengagement even after 53% of firms adopted hybrid models.

The tradeoffs are concrete and often financial. Startups in competitive hubs can out-pay MNCs at comparable seniority — reported ranges show startup base salaries running 35-45 LPA against 25-38 LPA at established corporates for similar experience — but without the same formal termination process or benefits cushion; startups can let people go without severance in a way MNCs generally can't. The corporate side offers brand-name credibility that continues paying dividends in future job searches, a currency that matters more in India's résumé-screening culture than it might elsewhere.

### The Reckoning

The Dutch comparison is mostly about intensity: startup versus corporate mainly changes your pace and equity exposure, not your day-to-day social experience of hierarchy, because flatness is already the cultural baseline. The Indian comparison is structurally deeper: startup versus corporate genuinely changes how decisions get made, how much autonomy you're handed, and how much institutional protection stands behind your job.

Hofstede Insights data explains why the same binary lands so differently: the Netherlands scores low on Power Distance across the board, meaning hierarchy is minimized everywhere, corporate or not, while India scores considerably higher, meaning the startup sector's flatness functions as a genuine counter-cultural choice rather than an extension of a national default. Choosing "startup" in Amsterdam is a bet on speed. Choosing "startup" in Bangalore is a bet on an entirely different operating philosophy.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *teamblind.com — In a thread comparing a ₹60 LPA startup offer against a ₹50 LPA MNC offer, several posters weighed in that the MNC's brand name would matter more for the next job search than the extra ten lakhs would matter now, while others countered that the startup's faster ownership and learning curve was worth the trade — a genuinely split debate with no clear consensus.*

> *teamblind.com — Someone weighing an MNC-versus-startup decision in India was warned explicitly that startups can let employees go "anytime without benefits," while MNCs follow a due process with severance — a distinction one poster said mattered more to them after watching a friend's startup fold with no notice.*

> *Quora — A respondent comparing IT work culture across Indian cities noted that flatter hierarchies and faster decision-making were most visible specifically in startup-dense hubs like Bangalore, and that the same title at a traditional corporate elsewhere in India could mean a much more layered, slower-moving reporting structure.*

> *InterNations — An expat entrepreneur based in Amsterdam described being surprised that Dutch corporate employees at large multinationals seemed just as comfortable challenging a senior executive's idea in a meeting as their friends working at scrappy five-person startups, concluding that "flat" in the Netherlands wasn't a founder's branding choice so much as a national habit.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between startup and corporate in the Netherlands, optimize for pace, equity, and personal risk appetite — the hierarchy question is largely already answered for you either way. If you're choosing in India, treat it as a genuinely structural decision about autonomy, security, and how much your next employer will care about the name on your last one. The honest version for a friend: in Amsterdam, the interesting question is how fast you want to move; in Bangalore, it's how much structure you're willing to trade away to get there.
