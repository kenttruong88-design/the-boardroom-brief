# The Ten-Year Handshake vs. The Two-Thirds Economy: Loyalty in Germany and Peru

**🇩🇪 Germany · 🇵🇪 Peru**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Germany, 41.7% of employees have been with their current employer for at least a decade, and switching jobs every few months — rather than every few years — is treated by recruiters and colleagues alike as a serious red flag rather than ambition. In Peru, the more pressing career-mobility question for most workers isn't how long to stay at a company; it's whether the job is even part of the formal economy in the first place. With 72.1% of Peru's employed population working informally as of 2024 — no contract, no benefits, no tax record, and by definition no tenure to speak of — the entire Western framing of "job loyalty vs. career mobility" assumes a stable, formal employment relationship that simply doesn't exist for nearly three-quarters of Peruvian workers. These two countries aren't just answering the loyalty question differently. Most of Peru's workforce isn't being asked the question at all.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Have a genuinely good, articulable reason ready if your CV shows frequent job changes | Assume 3-4 month stints will read the same as 3-4 year stints — Germans distinguish sharply between the two |
| Expect a formal, documented, contract-driven employment relationship from day one | Expect the informal, relationship-based hiring norms common in other markets — process here is thorough and paperwork-heavy |
| Lean into long-tenure norms if you're building a career here — it's genuinely rewarded, not just tolerated | Treat a 2-3 year stay as automatically insufficient; it's roughly the current national average, not an outlier |
| Know that switching employers while already employed is meaningfully easier than landing your first German job | Burn bridges with an employer even on the way out — reputational networks matter for the next formal role |
| Expect unskilled roles to show shorter average tenure than skilled ones — this is a documented national pattern, not a personal failing | Read short tenure in lower-skill sectors as a uniquely personal instability signal |

#### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand the formal/informal divide before evaluating any job offer — it changes everything about benefits, security, and mobility | Assume "employed" means "formally employed" — for most of the country, it doesn't |
| Target sectors with real formal-sector demand for foreign skills: mining, engineering, project management, data/AI | Expect to compete broadly for jobs locals can fill — legal caps limit foreign staff to 20% of a company's workforce |
| Base yourself in Lima if pursuing formal-sector work — it holds roughly a third of the national workforce and nearly all the qualified openings | Expect equivalent formal job density outside the capital |
| Use organizations like AmCham Perú or the Lima Chamber of Commerce to access the formal, internationally-oriented employer network | Rely on informal-economy job-search norms if you're specifically targeting formal, benefits-bearing employment |
| Recognize that informal workers get meaningfully less on-the-job training, which compounds mobility barriers over time | Assume informal work is simply a temporary stepping stone to formal work for most people who hold it |

---

Germany's loyalty culture is reinforced by the country's moderate-high uncertainty avoidance score of 65 on Hofstede's scale — a society with a genuine institutional preference for structure, predictability, and clearly defined rules, which shows up directly in employment as thorough contracts, strong worker protections, and a cultural expectation that changing jobs requires real justification, not just restlessness. The German Federal Statistical Office's own data shows this isn't just stereotype: over 40% of employees hit the ten-year mark with a single employer, and even among the more mobile third of the workforce, "mobile" in the German context usually still means years, not months, at each stop. The tradeoff is real: this system rewards demonstrated commitment and provides genuine security, but it can be a harder market to break into cold — Quora threads on German hiring consistently note that landing your first role is considerably harder than moving between roles once you're already inside the formal system and have a track record to point to.

Peru's picture is dominated by a structural reality that has nothing to do with cultural attitudes toward loyalty and everything to do with the shape of the economy itself. At 87 on Hofstede's uncertainty avoidance scale — even higher than Germany's — Peruvians show, if anything, a stronger cultural preference for structure and predictability than Germans do, which makes the country's informality rate a story about economic constraint rather than cultural indifference to stability. With informal employment holding steady around 72% and informal workers documented as 17 percentage points less likely to receive on-the-job training than their formal-sector counterparts, the mobility ladder that exists in principle — informal work as a stepping stone toward formal, secure employment — functions poorly in practice, because the very workers who need skill-building most to make that jump are the ones systematically excluded from receiving it. For a foreign professional, this creates a genuinely bifurcated labor market: a small, competitive, well-protected formal sector clustered in Lima and specific industries like mining, running alongside a much larger informal economy operating by entirely different rules.

The reckoning: Germany's loyalty-vs-mobility question is a real, individually navigable choice within a stable formal system — you decide how long to stay, and the system responds predictably to that choice either way. Peru's version of the same question is, for most workers, not really a choice at all — it's a structural sorting mechanism that determines who gets access to training, benefits, and a documented career history, and who doesn't. A foreign professional moving to Germany is choosing a tenure strategy. A foreign professional moving to Peru, unless working specifically within the formal, often foreign-company-anchored sector, needs to understand they're choosing which economy to work in altogether.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering how often Germans typically change jobs, one respondent flatly stated that job hopping "would be considered an absolute no-go" in Germany, adding that Germans are often described as "stubborn or loyal" — explicitly framing these as two sides of the same underlying cultural trait rather than separate qualities.*

> *Quora — Someone asking whether it's easy to change jobs in Germany as a foreigner got a nuanced answer: switching between qualified roles once already employed is genuinely easier than landing the first job, but any visible pattern of frequent moves still requires "a good reason and explanation" that a longer-tenured German candidate would never be asked to provide.*

> *Blind — A thread titled "Let's normalize job hopping" among mostly US and international tech workers pushed back explicitly on tenure-shaming, with posters noting that one cited example — moving from Boeing to Amazon — doubled their income in a single jump, a story that stands in sharp contrast to the German norm and illustrates how differently the same career move gets read depending on which country's labor market you're standing in.*

> *Expat.com (Peru forum) — Threads on finding formal employment in Peru consistently steer foreign job-seekers toward mining, engineering, and project-management roles specifically because those are the sectors where a legal cap limiting foreign staff to 20% of a company's workforce is least likely to bind, with posters warning that broader job-hunting approaches common in other countries simply don't translate to Peru's more restricted formal-sector opening.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing "job loyalty vs. career mobility" as a personal style question, you're already assuming Germany's frame, where that's a real and individually meaningful choice. In Peru, ask a different, more foundational question first: is this role even part of the formal economy, and if not, what does staying or leaving actually change for you? Germany rewards patience within a system built to reward it. Peru's formal sector can reward the same patience, but only if you can get inside it in the first place — and for most of the country, that's the harder and more consequential move.
