# The Suit Survives in Cairo. In Oslo, It Retired Early.

🇪🇬 Egypt · 🇳🇴 Norway

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Cairo, a man will put on a jacket in 38-degree heat to walk into a meeting and be taken seriously. In Oslo, a man will take off his jacket in a boardroom to avoid looking like he's trying too hard. Both countries will tell you their approach is about respect. Neither is lying. They have simply arrived at opposite conclusions about what respect requires you to wear, and a new hire who packs for one country using the other's rulebook will spend the first month either sweating through a suit nobody asked for or standing out, uncomfortably, as the only person in the room who dressed like he had somewhere important to be.

#### 🇪🇬 Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a dark suit (or at minimum jacket and tie) to any first meeting, even in summer | Assume "casual Friday" applies to your first two weeks — earn the casual first |
| Address people by title and surname until explicitly told otherwise | Wear gold jewelry as a man — silver only, per longstanding custom |
| Keep women's hemlines below the knee and shoulders covered, regardless of your own country's norms | Wear shorts, tank tops, or anything sheer to the office, even during a heat wave |
| Carry a light jacket for aggressively air-conditioned interiors | Show up underdressed to build trust quickly — appearance is read as a signal of seriousness |
| Budget real time for relationship-building before business gets transactional | Rush the small talk — it is not a delay, it is the actual work |
| Ask a local colleague before any client-facing wardrobe decision | Wear native or overtly religious dress as a foreign visitor unless invited to |

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to smart-casual: clean jeans or chinos, a good sweater, tidy shoes | Show up to an interview in a full suit unless you're in finance, law, or senior management |
| Buy quality basics — Norwegians notice good fabric, not brand logos | Wear anything overtly flashy or status-signaling — it reads as insecure, not successful |
| Use first names with everyone, including your manager's manager | Assume a startup's dress code applies at a bank, or vice versa — check first |
| Keep a bike or hiking layer handy — commuting by bike in work clothes is normal | Overdress for a client meeting hoping to impress — it can backfire |
| Save the tie for the specific handful of formal industries that still expect one | Mistake informality for a lack of seriousness — Norwegians dress down and still expect punctuality and preparation |
| Layer for weather, not hierarchy — the building's dress code is the same for interns and directors | Expect visible hierarchy cues (corner offices, dress-based rank) — they mostly don't exist |

### Egypt

Egyptian business culture treats appearance as a proxy for seriousness, and the data on who is actually in the workforce explains some of why. CAPMAS reported the country's labor force reaching roughly 34.2 million people in 2025, but male economic participation stood at 70.3 percent against 16.9 percent for women — a workplace that remains, numerically, dominated by men in suits, which keeps the sartorial default conservative by inertia as much as by rule. Commisceo Global and World Business Culture both describe the standard as trousers, jacket, shirt and tie for men in any formal meeting, with women expected to cover shoulders and keep skirts below the knee — modesty as baseline, not exception, in both local and multinational offices.

What makes this interesting rather than merely strict is the layer underneath it: Hofstede Insights scores Egypt at 25 on individualism against a global average of 64, marking it a strongly collectivist culture where loyalty and group standing outrank personal preference — including preferences about what one might rather wear in August. Business etiquette guides consistently note that managers are treated less like coordinators and more like commanders, with title and surname used well past the point a Western hire would expect first names. The dress code, in other words, is not really about clothing. It is a visible, low-cost way of signaling that you understand where you sit in the room, which matters more in Cairo than almost anywhere else on the org chart.

The one variable Egypt cannot regulate is the thermostat outside. Expat guides note that dress becomes "slightly less formal" in summer largely because the heat makes anything else untenable, and that linen or cotton jackets are the practical workaround for staying presentable without collapsing on the walk to the metro. The suit survives; it just gets lighter.

### Norway

Norway's dress code is, on paper, barely a code at all — which is itself the code. Statistics Norway and multiple relocation guides describe a workplace built on flat hierarchy: decisions made in groups, managers acting as facilitators rather than authorities, and titles that matter so little that interns and CEOs are on a first-name basis by lunchtime. Hofstede Insights places Norway among the lowest power-distance societies measured, and the wardrobe follows the org chart's lead — when status isn't supposed to be visible in the seating chart, it isn't supposed to be visible on your back either.

This is where Janteloven, Norway's unwritten social code against claiming to be better than anyone else, does more cultural work than any HR handbook. Relocation consultancies note that dressing flashily in a Norwegian office reads not as ambition but as insecurity — a tell that you feel the need to prove something. The result is a uniform of quality basics worn without ceremony: dark jeans, a good knit, clean sneakers, the fanciest engineer in the building arriving by bicycle in a rain jacket and changing at his desk. Suits are not banned so much as quarantined to a handful of industries — finance, law, senior client-facing roles — where the old formality survives as a specialized dialect rather than the office's native tongue.

The trap for newcomers is treating the absence of a stated rule as the absence of a standard. Guides for incoming hires are explicit that "casual" in Norway does not mean sloppy: neat, well-fitted, weather-appropriate clothing is still expected, punctuality is non-negotiable, and showing up in a full suit for a tech interview can read as more socially clumsy than showing up underdressed. The bar has simply moved from formality to competence signaled through fit and quality, which is a harder needle to thread than "wear the suit" ever was.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the irony sharpens: Egypt's formality functions as social lubricant — a fast, legible way to show respect in a hierarchy everyone already agrees exists — while Norway's informality functions as its own form of discipline, a constant low-grade performance of not performing status in a system that insists, sometimes against visible evidence, that hierarchy has been mostly dissolved. Both are exacting. Egypt just tells you the rules upfront; Norway makes you infer them from how little anyone will admit there are any.

The counterintuitive part is which one is actually more forgiving of a newcomer's mistake. Overdress in Cairo and you have simply confirmed you take the job seriously — a safe, correctable error. Overdress in Oslo and you have accidentally signaled insecurity, ambition, or a fundamental misread of the room, which is a slower thing to walk back. Ironically, the country with the stricter written dress code is the easier one to get right on day one.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — a respondent noted that Norwegian colleagues do care about how you dress, just not in the way outsiders assume: nobody polices formality, but showing up in visibly cheap or sloppy clothing gets noticed just as fast as showing up overdressed.*

> *Talesmag "Real Post Report" — a diplomatic spouse posted in Cairo described the local dress code as consistently formal even in blistering heat, with suits and ties standard for men in most office settings and only the most senior staff granted any real seasonal leeway.*

> *Expat.com Cairo forum — a longtime resident answering questions about summer dressing described the daily negotiation between staying cool and staying covered, recommending loose linen layers over anything sleeveless or fitted, even for expats given more social leeway than locals.*

> *teamblind.com — a poster working in tech described frustration with unclear, unwritten dress expectations at a casual-coded company, noting that "no dress code" often just means the code is unspoken and you find out you broke it after the fact.*

> *NLS Norway Relocation Group community notes — new arrivals are advised that seeing a senior engineer bike into the office in waterproofs and change at his desk is a genuinely reliable signal of how the workplace actually runs, more so than anything written in the employee handbook.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between these two postings based on wardrobe logistics alone, Egypt asks you to buy one good suit and wear it consistently; Norway asks you to buy several quietly expensive basics and wear them so unremarkably that nobody notices you own them. The deeper difference is what the clothing is actually doing: in Cairo it announces where you stand, in Oslo it tries very hard to announce nothing at all. Get that backwards and you won't be fired for it, but you will spend your first quarter being quietly recalibrated by colleagues too polite, in one country, or too egalitarian, in the other, to just tell you directly.

My honest advice, over drinks: pack the suit for Cairo and wear it like you mean it, and for Oslo, buy the good sweater, leave the tie in the suitcase, and try — genuinely try — not to look like you're trying.
