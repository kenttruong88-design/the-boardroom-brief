**You Can Take the Yam Out of Lagos, But Lagos Will Follow You to Abidjan**

🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

A Nigerian household spends, on average, 59 cents of every dollar on food — the highest share recorded in comparative data for the region, a figure Euromonitor International attributes to weak purchasing power and import-dependent supply chains, according to analysis published by Arbiterz. An Ivorian household, subsisting largely on home-grown cassava, plantain, and cocoa-adjacent trade, spends 38.5 cents. Both countries will tell you, with total sincerity, that they eat extremely well. Both are right. Only one of them is paying for it twice.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept at least a small portion of offered food in someone's home, even if you've eaten | Refuse hospitality outright — it reads as rejecting the host, not the dish |
| Greet the eldest person in the room first, with "sir" or "ma" | Skip the greeting ritual to get to the food faster — sequence matters more than speed |
| Dress conservatively, especially in the north, even for a casual lunch invitation | Assume a single national dress or dietary code — the Muslim north and Christian south differ meaningfully |
| Learn to recognize garri, egusi, and amala before you need to order them | Expect "plantain" to mean what it does at home — supermarkets may label it oddly or stock only the wrong variety |
| Budget separately for imported goods, which carry a real premium | Assume Lagos grocery prices are representative of Nigeria as a whole |
| Try Friday "traditional dress day" fare if your office observes it | Treat the custom as costume rather than a genuine, ongoing workplace ritual |

#### 🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Try attiéké, kedjenou, and foutou in your first weeks — they are the actual diet, not tourist fare | Treat Ivorian cuisine as a subset of "West African food" — it has its own distinct staples |
| Budget for Abidjan's wealthier-neighborhood restaurants if you want Western or Lebanese food | Expect budget options outside the local maquis stalls — that's where regular life happens |
| Pack light cotton and linen — there are only two seasons, dry and rainy | Pack for winter, or for four distinct seasons out of habit |
| Respect the blend of Christianity, Islam, and animist custom in daily life | Dismiss traditional rites or belief in magic as mere folklore in conversation |
| Use Abidjan's bars, clubs, and casinos as legitimate networking venues | Assume business socializing only happens in boardrooms |
| Learn basic market etiquette before haggling | Expect fixed prices outside formal retail — bargaining is the system, not a failure of it |

Neither country, it should be said, considers its own cuisine remotely negotiable. A newly arrived manager who suggests "simplifying" the office lunch order, or substitutes a continental breakfast for the real thing, will learn this within a week. Food here isn't catering — it's a running referendum on whether you've actually arrived or are merely visiting, and your colleagues will form their judgment well before you've worked out which market sells the good pepper.

In Nigeria, food is also a performance, and a well-reviewed one. The World Happiness Report 2026 ranks the country 106th globally — down a notch from the year before — which will surprise nobody who has read the pricing data for Lagos's own food basket. The city ranks as the fourth most expensive of ten African cities surveyed by a cost-of-food index cited by Pulse.ug, a city where garri and egusi are cheap by local standards but anything imported — cheese, decent chocolate, a recognizable brand of pasta — carries what amounts to a patriotism tax. Hofstede Insights data, reported by BusinessDay Nigeria, assigns the country a Power Distance score of 80 and an Indulgence score of 84, numbers that together describe a culture in which hierarchy is respected at the table and pleasure is pursued without apology once the greetings are done. Expats on Expat.com's Lagos forums report searching supermarket aisles for plantain only to find it mislabeled as "Indian banana," a small humiliation that captures the general experience of relocating one's diet several thousand kilometres and discovering the shelf has other ideas.

Côte d'Ivoire runs a quieter, cheaper operation. Its food spending share is roughly two-thirds of Nigeria's, propped up by domestic cassava, plantain, and cocoa trade that keeps the base diet both affordable and, crucially, local. Abidjan ranks a comparatively restrained eighth on the same food price index — more expensive than Accra, less punishing than Lagos. Attiéké, the fermented cassava couscous that anchors most Ivorian meals, and kedjenou, chicken steamed slowly in a banana leaf with minimal added fat, are not inflationary luxuries; they are what the country already grows. Hofstede Insights has, by its own account, never produced a verified standalone numeric profile for Côte d'Ivoire — the comparison tool simply will not render one — so the honest version of this paragraph ends with a shrug rather than a chart, with the qualitative literature gesturing toward strong communal and religious attachment rather than offering anything to put a number on.

The regional price comparison only sharpens the point. A food-cost index covering ten African cities, cited by Pulse.ug, ranks Lagos the fourth most expensive for groceries at an index score of 35.9, Abidjan a notably cheaper eighth at 32.2, and Accra — included here purely as a regional anchor — the cheapest of the ten at 29.7. The gap between Lagos and Abidjan isn't marginal; it's the difference between a city where the imported half of your diet is a budget line you watch carefully, and one where it barely registers because so little of the actual diet needs importing in the first place. Ivorian menus lean on cassava, plantain, and palm-wine-adjacent staples that the domestic economy already produces in volume, which is less a lifestyle preference than a direct consequence of how the two countries' food economies happen to be structured.

### The Reckoning

Both countries also treat the act of eating together as inseparable from doing business, which is where newcomers from more transactional office cultures tend to misstep first. Declining a meal, rushing through one, or treating a shared lunch as an interruption to the "real" meeting reads, in both Lagos and Abidjan, as a mild social failure rather than a scheduling preference — the meal is frequently where the actual relationship-building happens, with the agenda items functioning as the formality that makes the meal professionally acceptable in the first place, not the other way around.

The instinctive comparison is "expensive versus cheap," and the data supports it, but the more useful one is "imported versus grown." Nigeria's food culture is magnificent and visibly strained by its own logistics — a 180 million-person market still routing a meaningful share of its pantry through ports and currency markets, which is why a $150-a-week grocery bill for a couple, reported by one TalesMag diplomatic-spouse account from Lagos, reads as entirely plausible rather than extravagant. Côte d'Ivoire's food culture is smaller, cheaper, and structurally self-sufficient in a way that makes "eating local" not a lifestyle choice but simply how the economy works. Neither country is doing it wrong. One of them is just doing it with a significantly larger import bill and a significantly louder Friday.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — A West African diaspora member searching a Kuwaiti supermarket chain for garri, egusi, and plantain found the shelf only stocked something labeled "Indian banana," and had to track down an informal ethnic market instead — a small but telling lesson in how far a home ingredient list can drift once you've left the continent that grows it.*

> *TalesMag Real Post Report, Lagos — A diplomatic spouse on a ten-month posting reported Nigerians "love to dress up" for gatherings, alongside a steady $150-a-week grocery bill for two people and recurring food-related illness from unreliable imported goods — the glamour and the stomach bug arriving, somewhat unfairly, in the same week.*

> *ExpatExchange Q&A, Abidjan — A long-time resident fielding the recurring question of where to find good restaurants explained that solid Western or Lebanese food exists, but concentrated in Abidjan's wealthier districts and priced accordingly, leaving most newcomers to make peace with the local maquis stalls — not a downgrade, just a different economy entirely.*

> *Africanews — A feature on Nigerian immigrant bakers in Abidjan described a thriving cross-border business supplying Nigerian-style bread to an Ivorian market that simply didn't have it, suggesting that wherever one country's diaspora goes, its bread eventually follows, tariffs and borders notwithstanding.*

> *TalesMag Real Post Report, Accra — Included here as the clearest adjacent West African account found: a reporter noted that fruits and vegetables were plentiful and good quality, and that grocery delivery worked better than expected, with only certain imported items like broccoli and strawberries staying scarce and pricey — a reminder that "fresh" and "familiar" are two different problems for an arriving expat to solve.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between these two postings on food alone, the honest advice is to stop choosing on food alone — both diets are excellent, and the real variable is how much of your pantry you're willing to keep importing at a premium versus how quickly you're willing to fall in love with what's already growing outside your door. Nigeria will reward you with range and spectacle and a genuinely global food scene, at a price that fluctuates with the naira. Côte d'Ivoire will reward you with less drama and a lower bill, provided you can live without decent cheese for a while. Either way: learn to love attiéké or amala in week one, not month six — nobody respects a colleague who's still eating imported crackers for lunch in October.

And bring a sense of humor about your own grocery bill either way, because you will be comparing it with colleagues before you've finished your first pay cycle — in both cities, what you paid for lunch is a completely normal opening line, not a faux pas.
