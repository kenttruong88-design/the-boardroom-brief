# "Anything You Say Is Correct, Sir" vs. Cornering the CEO at the Christmas Party: Bosses in Nigeria and Denmark

**🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇩🇰 Denmark**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Nigeria, a well-documented workplace dynamic boils down to four words an employee learns fast: "Anything you say is correct, sir." In Denmark, a British manager relocated there put it just as plainly from the other direction: "Where I come from, there is more hierarchy, and you would never speak to your boss's boss. But they will in Denmark" — including, he noted, cornering leadership at the Christmas party to air a departmental grievance. Same job title, "manager," describing two completely different social contracts.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show clear, visible deference to age and seniority — it's a genuine cultural expectation, not just politeness | Publicly disagree with or correct a superior in front of others |
| Follow explicit instructions precisely as given | Assume initiative or unsolicited suggestions will be welcomed, even when you can see a flaw |
| Wait to be shown where to sit in formal meetings — seating reflects status | Sit wherever you like and assume it's a neutral, unimportant choice |
| Build a genuine personal relationship and trust before pushing back on anything sensitive | Raise a disagreement directly and immediately, especially with someone senior |
| Escalate real harassment or bullying carefully and in writing if HR response feels dismissive | Assume HR will automatically side with a junior employee over a senior one |

#### 🇩🇰 Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Feel free to take a good idea or concern straight to the top, even skipping your direct manager | Assume you need to go through a formal chain of command to be heard |
| Disagree with your boss directly when you have a real point — it's expected, not risky | Hold back a genuine objection out of concern it will damage the relationship |
| Approach leadership informally, including at social events, if something's on your mind | Assume social events are off-limits for raising real workplace topics |
| Expect a manager, especially one from a more hierarchical culture, to need time adjusting to this openness | Interpret a foreign manager's initial discomfort with direct approaches as personal rejection |
| Use first names and informal address with people well above your level | Default to formal titles or exaggerated deference — it can read as oddly distant |

---

Nigerian management culture runs on visible, structured deference. Business guides describe employees expected to "show proper deference and respect towards those in superior positions," with age and rank honored well beyond typical workplace courtesy — occasionally including a slight bow to a senior colleague. Seating arrangements in formal meetings reflect status, managers maintain a formal "official demeanour," and instructions flow one direction: staff are expected to follow them "to the letter" rather than push back, with ultimate decisions resting slowly with the CEO. One Nigerian writer captured the resulting dynamic in four blunt words describing the typical employer-employee relationship: "Anything you say is correct, sir." The same account describes a real case — an employee named Leye who correctly predicted a radio marketing campaign would miss its target demographic, was overruled anyway, and was then blamed personally when the campaign underperformed exactly as he'd warned. On Hofstede's power distance index, Nigeria scores 77 — high enough that this isn't a dysfunction unique to one bad manager, but a structural expectation the culture broadly reinforces.

Denmark occupies close to the opposite end of the same scale, scoring just 18. The practical result, according to multiple accounts, is a workplace where hierarchy is something close to optional in day-to-day interaction. One story making the rounds among cross-cultural management consultants describes a Danish executive visiting his company's US office, standing in the cafeteria expecting employees to approach him directly with feedback — nobody did, conditioned by American norms to route concerns through their direct supervisor instead. Run the story in reverse and it flips completely: a foreign manager posted to Denmark complained, with some genuine culture shock, "Whenever they have an idea, they come straight to me," bypassing the usual chain of command entirely. A British manager relocated to Denmark summed up the adjustment required bluntly: "Where I come from, there is more hierarchy, and you would never speak to your boss's boss. But they will in Denmark" — up to and including at the office Christmas party.

Both systems have a real cost attached, just on opposite sides of the ledger. Nigeria's structure can suppress genuinely useful feedback — the Leye story is a direct example of a correct prediction ignored purely because hierarchy discouraged pushing back — and separate accounts describe toxic dynamics compounding under that same deference, including routine put-downs like "you don't know what you're doing" directed at junior staff, and HR functions that, per one detailed account, labeled a bullying complaint "too sensitive" rather than act on it. Denmark's flatness, meanwhile, can genuinely overwhelm managers unused to it — the volume of direct, unfiltered access to leadership is repeatedly described as disorienting for anyone arriving from a more hierarchical system, even when the underlying intent (open feedback, real disagreement, genuine access) is basically healthy.

**The Reckoning:** The real gap between these two isn't just the size of the hierarchy — a 59-point spread on Hofstede's scale, one of the starkest pairings in this pillar's history — it's which behavior each culture treats as the safe default. In Nigeria, the safe move is silence and agreement, even when you're right, because being right doesn't outrank being junior. In Denmark, the safe move is speaking up, even to the very top, because staying silent when you disagree reads as withholding something useful rather than as respectful restraint. A manager or employee moving between these two countries isn't just adjusting their tone — they're inverting their entire theory of what "showing good judgment" at work actually looks like.

### Conclusion

If you're moving into a management role in either country, the honest prep work isn't learning new communication tactics — it's rewriting your assumptions about what silence and speech each signal. In Nigeria, expect deference by default and earn dissent slowly, through relationship, not through being correct. In Denmark, expect to be challenged directly and often, including by people several levels below you, and treat that as the system working rather than a discipline problem. Show up with the wrong instincts in either place, and you'll either silence the one person who saw the problem coming, or spend your first six months wondering why nobody seems to respect the org chart.
