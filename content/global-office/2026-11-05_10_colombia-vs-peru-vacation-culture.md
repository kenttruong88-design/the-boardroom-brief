# Peru Gives You 30 Vacation Days a Year. Colombia Gives You 15 and Actually Lets You Use Them.

🇨🇴 Colombia · 🇵🇪 Peru

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Peru offers the most generous statutory vacation entitlement in Latin America — 30 days a year, plus enough public holidays to add up to 46 total rest days, more than any other country in the region. More than half of Peruvian workers didn't take a single day of it last year. Colombia offers barely half as many statutory days, and its workers are, by a wide margin, better at actually disconnecting once they finally take one. On paper, Peru wins. In practice, Colombia is the one that works.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇴 Colombia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand your full compensation includes two mandatory prima payments (mid-year and year-end bonuses), not just base salary | Assume your salary figure alone represents your total annual pay |
| Expect your legal workweek to keep shrinking — Law 2101 is phasing hours down from 48 to 42 by 2026 | Assume the current legal workweek figure is fixed or final |
| Take your 15 statutory vacation days seriously — most Colombian workers who do take leave actually switch off from work | Assume a shorter statutory entitlement means less real rest; the data suggests the opposite in practice |
| Know that vacation pay accrues whether you take the days or not, and pays out proportionally if you leave a job early | Assume unused vacation simply disappears if you change jobs mid-year |
| Understand cesantías — a severance-fund payment equal to about a month's salary per year worked, paid out annually | Treat cesantías as a bonus; it's a legally mandated severance mechanism with its own payout rules |

#### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use your vacation days deliberately — roughly half of Peruvian workers take none at all in a given year, which is a documented problem, not a norm to copy | Assume a generous 30-day statutory entitlement means vacation culture there is correspondingly relaxed |
| Expect your employer to assume some availability during your time off — a large share of workers report bosses expecting partial check-ins | Assume a approved vacation period means genuinely full disconnection from work, the way it might elsewhere in the region |
| Ask directly whether your specific employer has a formal rest or disconnection policy — over a quarter of workers report having none at all | Assume a formal vacation policy exists just because the statutory entitlement is generous |
| Know you can cash out up to half your annual leave (15 of 30 days) rather than take it | Assume all unused vacation simply must be taken as time off with no cash alternative |
| Budget for Peru having the most official rest days on paper in the region, while also having the hardest time actually using them | Assume more days on the calendar automatically means more actual rest |

---

The headline numbers favor Peru decisively. Rivermate's country guides put Peru's statutory annual leave at 30 calendar days after a year of service, against Colombia's 15 — and Peru layers on another 16 to 18 national holidays depending on the year, versus Colombia's 18. Bloomberg Línea's reporting puts the combined total at 46 rest days a year for formal-sector Peruvian workers, the highest in Latin America, ahead of Brazil's 43 and Panama's 42 — generous enough, in fact, that the same reporting cites Macroconsult estimates suggesting each additional public holiday shaves roughly 1.3 points off monthly GDP growth, a real economic cost attached to all that generosity. Colombia, by contrast, sits in the middle tier regionally on paper, grouped with Venezuela, Chile, Ecuador, and Guatemala around 15 statutory days — a comparatively modest entitlement that's actively shrinking on the hours side even as it holds steady on leave, with Law 2101 of 2021 phasing the legal workweek down from 48 hours toward 42 by 2026.

What the entitlement numbers completely fail to predict is who actually rests. A detailed regional study by workforce platform Buk, surveying 1,019 workers across Chile, Colombia, and Peru, found that 62.5 percent of Peruvians won't take vacation at all during the traditional January-to-February period — the highest share of the three countries studied. Among Peruvians who do take time off, only 26 percent report not working at all during it, compared to 53 percent of Colombians — meaning Colombians, with half the statutory days, are roughly twice as likely to actually switch off completely once they do take leave. The same study found 45 percent of Peruvians rate their ability to disconnect from work as "none or barely possible," against 23.3 percent of Colombians — again, roughly double the difficulty, despite Peru's far larger leave allowance. A separate Bumeran "Vacaciones 3.0" survey reported by La República adds a striking behavioral detail: 45 percent of Peruvian workers surveyed said they'd rather simply shorten their workday permanently than take a conventional vacation at all — the highest share of any country in that particular regional comparison — and among those who did take leave, 71 percent reported checking work email or messages during it, with 32 percent describing real anxiety when they didn't check in and 17 percent feeling outright guilty about relaxing.

Colombia's own structural quirks explain some of the difference. An Expat.com forum thread on Colombian labor law, populated by a long-term expat teacher, lays out compensation mechanics that function almost like forced savings for rest: the mandatory prima (a bonus split into two annual payments, functioning like a 13th-month salary) and cesantías (a severance fund accruing roughly a month's pay per year worked, paid out annually) both create built-in financial rhythms around taking stock of the year, distinct from simply accruing vacation days one by one. The same poster notes vacation pay accrues "whether you take them or not" and pays out proportionally on early departure — a structural guarantee that may partly explain why Colombian workers report more confidence about actually using their leave, even though they have less of it to use. Peru's formal entitlement, by contrast, comes with weaker guardrails around enforcement: the Buk study found just 9.3 percent of Peruvian companies have a formal digital-disconnection policy, against Colombia's 11.9 percent, and 26.8 percent of Peruvian workers report no formal rest or vacation benefit policy at all.

InterNations' own Expat Insider 2025 survey adds an unexpected wrinkle to Colombia's half of the comparison. Colombia ranked second overall among 46 countries surveyed worldwide for expat satisfaction, with career opportunities ranked first and leisure options seventh — strong enough results that local press covered the ranking as a point of national pride. But the same survey's own data flags a caveat worth taking seriously: only 28 percent of surveyed expats in Colombia work full-time, against a 56 percent global average, with retirees making up 25 percent of the sample against 11 percent globally. A country that scores brilliantly on an expat survey weighted toward retirees and part-time arrangements isn't necessarily describing the same experience a full-time relocating employee should expect, a distinction worth keeping in mind before assuming Colombia's glowing survey results apply equally to a demanding corporate role.

### The Reckoning

The genuine irony here is structural, not cultural: Peru legislated generosity without legislating enforcement, producing a country with the best leave entitlement on paper and one of the worst disconnection cultures in practice, while Colombia legislated more modestly but built in compensation mechanics — the twin prima payments, the accruing cesantías — that seem to function as real behavioral nudges toward actually taking and using time off. Hofstede's data offers a partial explanation for the enforcement gap: Peru scores even higher than Colombia on uncertainty avoidance (87 versus 80), a dimension that, combined with weak formal disconnection policy, seems to produce more anxiety about stepping away from work rather than more comfort with a predictable, rule-bound rest period — the opposite of what high uncertainty avoidance usually predicts.

For someone relocating, the planning implication cuts against the obvious read of the numbers. Don't assume Peru's larger leave entitlement means a more relaxed relationship to time off — ask directly about your specific employer's actual disconnection culture, because the national data suggests you may need to protect your own boundary more actively there than the calendar implies. In Colombia, take your shorter allotment seriously and expect, per the data, that colleagues around you will actually respect it once you do.

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between the two, don't let the vacation-day count alone make the decision. Colombia offers less time on paper but a culture, and a compensation structure, that seems to actually deliver real rest once the time is granted. Peru offers more time on paper, backed by weaker enforcement and a documented culture of guilt around using it, which tends to erase much of the advantage before it's ever spent.

I'd tell a friend taking either job the same thing: don't count your vacation days until you've asked a colleague, honestly, whether they used theirs last year — in Peru especially, the answer is less of a given than the contract would suggest.
