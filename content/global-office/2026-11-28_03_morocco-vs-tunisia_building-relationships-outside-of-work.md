# Morocco and Tunisia Will Gladly Give You Tea, and Then Wait to See What You Do With It

🇲🇦 Morocco · 🇹🇳 Tunisia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Moving to North Africa for work is easy to romanticise, because the first week supplies everything the romance needs: mint tea, strangers who help, markets that seem to have been arranged for a documentary. The difficulty arrives in the weeks that follow, when a newcomer discovers that being welcomed and being befriended are different services, and that the second takes a language, a neighbourhood and a considerable amount of time. In Morocco and Tunisia, the first is on offer almost at once. The second is up to you.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept invitations to meals, a hammam or a wedding when they come | Decline repeatedly and then wonder why the invitations stop |
| Learn Darija greetings, or enough French to shop and chat alone | Rely on English outside the expat bubble |
| Choose a neighbourhood with a daily market and get to know the vendors | Spend your first months only in the places that already feel familiar |
| Bring pastries, sweets or something for the children when visiting a home | Arrive empty-handed and expect nobody to notice |
| Treat quickly declared closeness with some caution | Assume a warm greeting is a lifelong commitment, in either direction |
| Join language exchanges, cultural centres and expat meetups | Build a social life made only of other foreigners |

### Tunisia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn French and basic Tunisian Arabic, known as Derja | Assume English is widely spoken; only a small share of the population speaks it |
| Accept invitations for mint tea, coffee or a meal | Refuse several times in a row |
| Stay neutral on religion and gender equality in early conversations | Offer opinions on either topic within the first hour |
| Drink in licensed venues such as hotels and bars | Assume alcohol is available or tolerated everywhere, or on every day |
| Find an Arabic-speaking friend for administrative tasks | Wait for a meetup to organise itself |
| Build a local network early, especially if you are a woman living alone | Rely only on the international neighbourhoods and assume it is the whole country |

## Morocco: Hospitality at Speed

Morocco's reputation for hospitality is borne out by most of what expats write, with the usual caveats about who is writing. An Expat.com guide to Casablanca, last updated in July 2022, warns that friendships can form very quickly and advises caution about how fast someone calls you a close friend or family member. It also says social status matters, with family income a common selection criterion for international schools and sports clubs, and describes Casablanca as a city of extremes. In other words, a newcomer will be welcomed warmly and then, quietly, placed.

The expat community in the larger cities is real and organised, and not always inexpensive. The Casablanca guide notes that one leisure club requires co-opted registration and entrance fees, and that international school tuition costs roughly 20,000 to 20,500 dirhams a year, about double for Moroccan children. The social circuit, in short, has a door, and the door has a price list.

There are cheaper routes in. The same guide lists consular associations, international schools, sports clubs, meetups and social media groups, and a Marrakech local in an Expat.com thread suggested InterNations for expat events. A blogger on Taste of Maroc, in a 2017 post updated in 2021, said her American and European friends made a major difference, and that learning Darija or some French eased her isolation, with locals often pleased by the effort. Still, the standard advice from the forums is to avoid living entirely within the foreign circle, because the better stories involve a market vendor, a neighbour or a Friday lunch.

Behind the sociability is a young population with a restless economy. The labour market is also split by geography: the HCP figures show urban unemployment of 13.5 percent against 6.1 percent in rural areas, and women's unemployment of 16.1 percent against 9.4 percent for men, so a newcomer's urban, salaried social circle is not a sample of the country. Morocco's High Commission for Planning reported, via APA News, an unemployment rate of 10.8 percent in the first quarter of 2026, with 29.2 percent among those aged 15 to 24 and a broader underutilisation rate of 45.3 percent for that age group. Afrobarometer's 2024 survey, published in June 2025, found 28 percent of Moroccan youth had thought a lot about emigrating, up from 20 percent in 2017, though 73 percent also said the country was headed in the right direction. The young are both patient and packing, which makes them excellent company and imperfect long-term neighbours.

## Tunisia: Warm Welcome, Thinner Scene

Tunisia is described, in Expat.com's lifestyle guide, as a place of people who are naturally happy and like to get together, which is a pleasant sentence that does not mention how one joins in. Cyril Jarnias, writing in January 2026, says hospitality is central, that accepting invitations for mint tea, coffee or a meal is valued, and that refusing repeatedly can seem cold. Jarnias also notes that only about 8 percent of the population speaks English, that French is widely used in administration and business, and that learning French and Derja widens social circles and earns respect.

The social map is more varied than the postcard. Alcohol is sold, but tolerated mainly in licensed venues such as hotels and bars. Jarnias says affluent international neighbourhoods in Tunis can feel close to Europe, while conservative or rural areas keep more traditional expectations, and that sources describe harassment that rises in summer and in the evenings, with single women lacking a local network especially vulnerable. Gender roles remain pronounced in private life. The same Expat.com guide advises staying neutral on religion and gender equality, adapting to local life rather than expecting locals to adapt, and noting that Tunisians are generally punctual and professional and enjoy a break, particularly in warm weather. None of this makes friendship impossible, but it does mean the answer to where to meet people depends on who you are and where you live.

Young Tunisians share the Moroccan impatience, with fewer reasons to hide it. Afrobarometer's March 2024 survey, published in April 2026, found 57 percent of Tunisian youth saying the country was going in the wrong direction, and 51 percent having thought a lot about emigrating, up from 38 percent in 2018. Tunisia's statistics institute reported youth unemployment of 35.4 percent in the second quarter of 2026, as relayed by Tunisie Numerique, with an overall rate of 14.9 percent. The burden falls unevenly: the same report put female unemployment at 21.6 percent and graduate unemployment at 26.6 percent, with women graduates at 35.6 percent, figures that shape who has the time, money and independence to socialise. A country in which a great many young people are looking elsewhere is a strange place in which to put down roots, though it also means the foreigner is rarely the only newcomer.

## The Reckoning

If the question is which country makes the first month easier, Morocco has the stronger organised expat circuit, with consular networks, clubs and meetups well documented, and a social life in the larger cities that expats describe as lively. Tunisia has warmth in abundance and fewer organised footholds, and a language situation in which French is practically a social visa. Hofstede's dimensions, as reproduced in a University of North Carolina table, give Morocco a power distance of 70 and individualism of 46, both marked as estimates, and do not list Tunisia, which at least spares us a false comparison.

Where the two countries agree is in what they ask of the newcomer. Both reward the person who accepts the tea, learns the greeting and returns the invitation. Both punish the person who waits for the community to arrive, as one Expat.com meetup thread from roughly 2011 to 2012 illustrated when plans for an expat barbecue stalled for lack of an organiser. A relocation to either country succeeds or fails on the unglamorous matter of who rings you on a Sunday.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com (Morocco loneliness thread, paraphrased, c. 2018): Several posters said loneliness was rare, citing chats with market vendors, family nearby and a busy calendar of private gatherings in Tangier. One advised cutting back on social media and building local friendships rather than only expat ones, and another found the arts scene and locals in cafes easy to approach.*

> *Taste of Maroc (first-person blog, paraphrased): The author said adjusting was harder than expected, and that her foreign friends made the difference. She urged readers to accept dinner, hammam and wedding invitations, and to practise Darija in cafes and shops.*

> *Expat.com (Morocco introduction thread, paraphrased, recent): A poster suggested picking a neighbourhood with a daily market, learning a few Darija greetings, joining language exchanges and accepting invitations to tea or a Friday meal.*

> *Expat.com (Tunisia misconceptions thread, paraphrased, c. 2017): An American said locals paused what they were doing to help him, and that Tunisians appreciated attempts at the language; he recommended French. A British poster said his family had more friends in Tunisia than at home.*

> *Expat.com (Tunisia, what is it really like, paraphrased, c. 2018 to 2020): One poster found many people cynical and self-interested, felt overcharged compared with locals, and wondered what a kind dog-sitter really thought. The thread offered few accounts of lasting friendships, so it is a mood and not a verdict.*

## Conclusion

The practical advice is boring and effective. Learn some Darija or Derja, plus French, before you land. Say yes to the first dozen invitations, even when the schedule objects. Join one organised group, whether a language exchange, a club or an expat meetup, and one disorganised one, such as a neighbourhood market or a cafe. In Tunisia, build a local network early, especially if you are a woman living alone, and in Morocco, be prepared for friendships to start fast and to need a second look.

Employers sending staff to either country should treat social integration as part of the package, because a person with no one to call outside the office will eventually call a recruiter. Both countries offer plenty of tea. What turns it into a social life is, annoyingly, a person who keeps turning up.
