**One Country Shuts Down for August. The Other Just Drives to the Garden and Loses Phone Signal.**

🇷🇺 Russia · 🇬🇷 Greece

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Russian labor law guarantees 28 calendar days of paid annual leave, which can be split into two blocks of at least 14 days each (vacationtracker.io, citing Russian labor code). Greek law guarantees a comparatively modest 20 working days — but mandates that at least 10 of them be taken between May and September specifically (Legal 500 overview of Greek employment law), which is less a vacation policy than a national stage direction. The two countries arrive at roughly similar amounts of time off by very different philosophies: Russia tells you when the minimum block is, then leaves you to figure out where to go (increasingly, nowhere — just the dacha); Greece tells you exactly which months you're required to disappear in, because the whole country is disappearing together anyway.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇷🇺 Russia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan around the May holidays — many workers stretch them into a week-and-a-half break | Expect business to move normally during the first half of May |
| Understand that any leave block must be at least 14 days if split in two | Try to split your 28 days into several short, scattered breaks |
| Expect a dacha trip, not a foreign holiday, to be the default summer plan for many colleagues | Assume everyone is jetting off abroad — sanctions and currency issues have made that harder since 2022 |
| Budget extra travel time around the May holiday exodus — traffic jams to dacha country can run hours long | Schedule anything time-sensitive right at the start of a national holiday block |
| Treat the dacha as a genuine second home, not a quirky hobby | Dismiss a colleague's countryside plans as a minor weekend errand |
| Confirm your specific leave entitlement if you fall into a special category (teachers, disabled employees get more) | Assume the 28-day baseline applies uniformly to every role |

#### 🇬🇷 Greece

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan around mid-August — much of the country, including officials and journalists, genuinely leaves | Expect to reach anyone important by phone or email in the first two weeks of August |
| Take at least 10 of your leave days between May and September, as required | Try to front-load all your leave into winter and call it compliant |
| Enjoy the quieter city if you stay behind — lighter traffic, no bank queues, rooftop cinemas under the Acropolis | Expect normal service at every shop, office, or bank during peak exodus weeks |
| Check your payslip for the statutory vacation allowance — it's a real bonus on top of wages | Assume "vacation pay" is just your regular salary continuing |
| Book early or consider shoulder-season travel — June and September increasingly offer the same beaches for less | Assume August is your only viable vacation window just because it's traditional |
| Recognize that nearly half of Greeks are now skipping summer holidays entirely due to cost | Assume the August exodus still includes everyone equally — it increasingly doesn't |

### Russia

Russian vacation culture has quietly reoriented around the dacha — the family country house that one cultural account calls "even more" central to Russian holiday life today than in the past, thanks to currency controls and sanctions that have made foreign travel considerably harder since 2022 (folkways.today). The structure is practical: 28 calendar days of statutory leave, split into blocks of at least two weeks, with the May holidays functioning as the unofficial starting gun — enough people extend them with extra vacation days that the roads out of Moscow toward dacha country see hours-long jams every year.

What makes the dacha distinct from a generic "staycation" is how functional it still is. One longtime dacha owner, Tatyana, described living without electricity for years and still being pulled back every spring: "I always come back in the spring and want to do so much here and go into the woods." For a Moscow schoolteacher named Natalya, the appeal is explicitly about escaping the city rather than performing leisure: "This is a real vacation from the city, the noise, the people and the commotion" (reporting via NPR member station WUNC). Vegetable gardens at the dacha aren't decorative, either — they function as a genuine economic safety net in hard times, expanded and relied on more heavily whenever money gets tight.

### Greece

Greek leave culture runs on a stricter, more public calendar. The statutory minimum of 20 working days comes with an unusual condition: at least 10 of them must fall between May and September, converting "vacation" from an individual choice into something closer to coordinated national downtime (Legal 500). The August exodus around the Dormition of the Virgin Mary holiday (August 15th) is the most visible version of this — ministries, newsrooms, and union offices empty out, and the people left behind describe genuinely struggling to reach anyone at their desk for roughly two weeks (Al Jazeera, first-person account of Athens in August).

That tradition is under real financial pressure. Recent reporting puts the number of Greeks skipping a summer holiday entirely at roughly one in two, citing rising travel costs and competing financial obligations (To Vima). The response from the travel industry has been to nudge the ritual rather than abandon it — promoting June and September as cheaper alternatives to the traditional August slot — while the statutory vacation allowance (extra pay specifically tied to leave, on top of normal wages) remains a real, legally protected benefit regardless of whether someone can actually afford to use their days away.

### The Reckoning

Both countries treat extended time off as something close to a civic obligation rather than a personal perk — neither has the "take it if you can find the time" culture common elsewhere. But Russia's version has drifted inward, toward a private plot of land increasingly disconnected from the broader economy's troubles, while Greece's version has stayed stubbornly public and collective, built around a specific calendar window the whole country observes together, even as fewer people can actually afford to participate in it.

The practical upshot for an incoming employee: in Russia, don't be surprised if "vacation" means your colleague drops off the grid at an unheated cottage with no cell signal. In Greece, don't be surprised if your entire client base, government counterpart, and half your own office are unreachable for two specific weeks every August — and increasingly, don't be surprised if some of those same colleagues quietly aren't going anywhere at all this year.

### Conclusion

If you want colleagues who treat time off as a private, almost spiritual retreat to a specific plot of land, Russia will deliver that, dacha mud and spotty signal included. If you want a culture where the entire country synchronizes its downtime like a single organism — even as it increasingly can't afford to — Greece will deliver that too, assuming you don't need anything from a Greek office in the first half of August. Either way, block out your own calendar accordingly, because neither country is going to adjust its national rhythm for your deadline.
