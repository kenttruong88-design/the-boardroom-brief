# Confianza vs. Credentials: Building a Network in Peru vs. Slovenia

**🇵🇪 Peru · 🇸🇮 Slovenia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Lima, your professional network is built one dinner, one favor, and one shared football match at a time, and it will outlast any single job you hold. In Ljubljana, your professional network is built the same slow way, but with noticeably less flourish and considerably more punctuality — Slovenians will judge your trustworthiness partly by whether you showed up on time to find out. Both countries will tell you relationships matter more than your résumé. Only one of them will invite you to dinner to prove it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest time in "confianza" — trust — before expecting real business traction | Expect to close deals with people you've only met once or twice |
| Accept invitations to dinners, sporting events, and informal gatherings | Treat networking events as transactional; Peruvians read that as insincere |
| Use titles and surnames until explicitly invited to use first names | Assume a warm personal rapport means formal respect no longer matters |
| Expect to be asked for favors eventually — reciprocity is part of the system | Refuse a reasonable favor from someone in your network without real cause |
| Bring up family, local events, or football to open a relationship | Rush past small talk straight into the business agenda |

#### 🇸🇮 Slovenia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up on time — punctuality is read as a direct signal of respect | Be late to a first meeting; it can quietly end the relationship before it starts |
| Attend trade fairs and industry events — they're genuine relationship-building venues | Expect Slovenians to open up personally on the first meeting |
| Let warmth develop gradually through consistent, reliable interactions | Mistake early reserve or politeness for disinterest |
| Meet in person when you can — Slovenians read trustworthiness face-to-face | Speak critically of a third party — indirect communication avoids that entirely |
| Prepare thoroughly for meetings — precision is part of how trust is built | Treat a relationship as secure after one successful transaction |

---

Peru's professional culture runs on what BizLatin Hub calls *confianza* — a specific, earned form of trust that functions as the real currency of doing business. Commisceo Global's country guide describes Peruvian workplaces as formal and hierarchy-aware, where age, title, and education shape how people are addressed and treated, even as the underlying relationship, once it exists, becomes the thing that actually gets deals done. Hofstede Insights records Peru at an Individualism score of just 20 — among the lowest in the world, alongside Panama — confirming a deeply collectivist culture where group belonging, not individual credentials, opens doors. Networking here is explicitly reciprocal: contacts are expected to help each other when asked, and cultivating a wide personal network isn't an optional career skill, it's closer to a structural requirement, since Peruvians strongly prefer doing business with people they already know, like, and trust over unfamiliar counterparts with a stronger CV.

Slovenia's version of relationship-driven business is quieter and more procedural. BusinessCulture.org describes Slovenian business culture as a blend of German-style precision with Mediterranean warmth — punctual, well-prepared, egalitarian in its team structures, but still fundamentally oriented toward long-term relationships over short-term transactions. Rivermate's cultural guide notes Slovenes are indirect communicators who avoid speaking ill of others and prefer face-to-face meetings specifically because looking someone in the eye is how trust gets calibrated. Trade fairs and local industry events are cited repeatedly as the actual mechanism for expanding a network, rather than the more socially embedded, dinner-and-favor model common in Peru. Hofstede's research also places Slovenia within the collectivist range, though the expression is different: trust here is demonstrated through reliability and precision — showing up on time, following through exactly as promised — rather than through the more expansive personal disclosure and hospitality that defines Peruvian relationship-building.

### The Reckoning

Both countries reject the transactional, credential-first networking style common in more individualist economies, but they encode trust in almost opposite behaviors. In Peru, trust is demonstrated through generosity of time and willingness to participate in the reciprocal favor economy — skipping a dinner invitation or declining to help a contact who calls in a favor can genuinely damage a relationship. In Slovenia, trust is demonstrated through consistency and precision — arriving exactly on time, delivering exactly what was promised, and not overpromising in the initial, more reserved phase of a relationship. A Peruvian professional transplanted to Ljubljana who tries to fast-track intimacy through generous hospitality may find Slovenian counterparts polite but genuinely puzzled; a Slovenian professional transplanted to Lima who relies purely on punctual, precise delivery without ever accepting a dinner invitation may find doors staying firmly, politely closed.

The other divergence is pace and visibility. Peruvian networking is socially loud — dinners, sporting events, extended small talk before any business gets discussed — because the relationship has to be visibly, personally real before reciprocity kicks in. Slovenian networking is quieter and more venue-specific — a trade fair, a well-prepared meeting, a gradual thaw from reserved to warm over repeated professional contact — because the trust being built is more about demonstrated reliability than personal closeness. Neither is faster than the other; Peru front-loads the socializing and Slovenia front-loads the professionalism, but both expect the payoff to arrive only after sustained investment.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone describing life and work in Lima said most Peruvians they encountered were unusually welcoming toward foreigners, often inviting them into personal and family gatherings early on — a level of hospitality they hadn't experienced moving between other countries, and one that turned out to double as the fastest route into real professional relationships.*

> *Quora — A respondent asked directly how easy it is to find a job and build a life in Peru and was told that without either a sponsoring employer or a genuinely well-developed local network, the practical path forward narrows considerably — reinforcing how much weight relationships, rather than applications alone, carry in the local job market.*

> *Quora — Someone who moved to Lima as an English teacher noted that pay and job security improved noticeably once they had built a reliable circle of local professional contacts who would recommend them directly, rather than relying on cold job applications, which yielded comparatively little response.*

> *Quora — A poster comparing Slovenian and Austrian business norms observed that Slovenian colleagues took noticeably longer to become personally warm in a professional relationship than expected, but that once trust was established through a few reliably delivered projects, the relationship became durable and low-friction in a way that felt distinctly different from more transactional Western business cultures.*

> *Quora — Someone researching expat-friendly employers in Slovenia found that companies actively organizing structured networking opportunities, rather than leaving relationship-building to chance, were consistently rated as the easiest environments for foreigners to build a genuine local professional circle.*

### Conclusion

If your career runs on who you know, both countries reward the investment — they just charge different tuition. Peru asks for your evenings, your hospitality, and your willingness to be genuinely useful to people who will, eventually, be useful to you. Slovenia asks for your punctuality, your follow-through, and your patience while a more reserved relationship slowly proves itself reliable. Neither country will fast-track you on credentials alone. My honest line for a friend weighing the move: in Lima, say yes to the dinner; in Ljubljana, just show up five minutes early — either one, done consistently, is how you actually get in.
