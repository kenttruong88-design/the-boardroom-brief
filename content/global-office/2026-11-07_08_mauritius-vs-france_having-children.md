**Baby on Board: How Mauritius Pays You to Stay Home and France Pays You to Fill Out Forms About It**

🇲🇺 Mauritius · 🇫🇷 France

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Starting in January 2027, a new mother in Mauritius will be entitled to a full year away from her desk, the first half of it on full pay. A new mother in France, by contrast, gets sixteen weeks, a modest daily stipend from the state, and a congratulatory pamphlet about a childcare system everyone agrees is wonderful and nobody can actually get into. Both countries describe themselves as pro-family. Only one of them is treating it as an actuarial problem, and it is not the one you would guess from the GDP figures.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇺 Mauritius
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Start asking other parents for nanny referrals before you need one — word of mouth beats any listing | Assume a public crèche waitlist exists the way it does in Europe — for the most part, it doesn't |
| Confirm with HR whether your contract falls under the pre- or post-2027 maternity rules | Assume paternity leave is universal — it's currently reserved for married fathers with 12 months' service |
| Budget for private or informal childcare from day one — demand outpaces formal daycare capacity | Expect clear government guidance on returning to work after extended leave — the administrative side is still catching up to the law |
| Use the island's bilingual English/French norm to widen your pool of caregivers and schools | Expect unpaid domestic and care work to show up anywhere in the official statistics — it mostly doesn't |
| Lean on multigenerational and expat networks early — they do a lot of the work formal policy doesn't | Bank on the optional second six months of leave at half pay without checking what it does to your annual bonus |

#### 🇫🇷 France
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for a crèche place the moment the pregnancy test is positive — public waitlists open absurdly early | Expect a crèche place just because you applied — demand still exceeds supply in most cities |
| Register with the CAF immediately; PAJE birth and childcare allowances are generous but paperwork-heavy | Take the full three years of congé parental without checking the math — it pays roughly €160–€430 a month |
| Expect your partner to take real paternity leave — 59% of French fathers now take the full 25 days | Assume a day off work means a day of free childcare — school holidays require a separate, paid centre aéré |
| Learn the Wednesday half-day school schedule before you commit to a job full of Wednesday meetings | Badmouth the système too loudly — state-paid maternity leave beats what most expats left behind |
| Ask HR about collective-agreement top-up pay — base state maternity pay alone is thinner than people expect | Expect your career to simply pause gracefully — returning at week sixteen is the norm, not the exception |

Mauritius has just rewritten its maternity rules with the confidence of a country that has read the demographic charts and does not like what it sees. Under reforms taking effect in January 2027, paid maternity leave rises from 16 to 26 weeks of compulsory, fully employer-funded leave, with an optional further 26 weeks at half pay — a full year, if a mother wants it. Married fathers get six weeks, up from four, provided they have a year's service with the same employer, and employees who adopt a child under one are entitled to the same leave as biological parents. On paper, this is one of the more generous regimes anywhere in Africa, and arguably more generous in sheer weeks than France's.

The trouble, according to the World Bank's Women, Business and the Law 2024 assessment, is everything around the law. Mauritius scores a strong 75 out of 100 on the legal frameworks protecting parenthood, and a perfect 100 on expert opinion about women's actual treatment at work — but a flat zero on "supportive frameworks," meaning there is no single government process for claiming maternity benefits, no incentive structure nudging fathers to take their leave, and no published data on who does the unpaid care work at home. Childcare fares only slightly better: full marks for the legal framework, but just 50 for the support parents can actually access. The law, in short, is generous; the paperwork trail behind it barely exists yet.

This gap shows up in the numbers that matter most. Mauritius's female labour-force participation rate sat at 47.6% in 2023, according to World Bank data — respectable, but well below what a year of leave and a strong legal floor would predict. The likeliest explanation is the one every expat forum repeats before any ministry does: Mauritian families still run largely on informal infrastructure — nannies found through referrals, grandparents down the road — rather than on anything resembling a system, and a law with no application process is, for practical purposes, a law you have to lobby your own employer to honour.

France's approach is the mirror image: thinner headline leave, wrapped in far more bureaucracy, and considerably more institutional follow-through. Maternity leave totals 16 weeks (six before birth, ten after), paid by the state through the sécurité sociale at a daily rate between roughly €10.79 and €100.36, depending on prior salary, with many employers topping it up under collective agreements. Paternity leave, extended to 25 days in 2021, has become routine rather than exceptional: an Ined study found 59% of fathers now take the full allowance, and researcher Anne Solaz calls it "standard practice." A Senate-approved reform taking effect in January 2027 adds two further months of leave per parent, paid at 70% and then 60% of net salary, stacked on top of existing entitlements rather than replacing them. On top of all this sits the means-tested PAJE package: a birth bonus of roughly €1,019 paid in the seventh month of pregnancy for a modest-income couple, plus a basic allowance running until the child turns three.

What France does not do well is the long middle stretch. Congé parental lets either parent take up to three years off, but the PreParE allowance that funds it pays only €159.87 to €428.71 a month depending on hours worked — low enough that almost nobody with other options takes it, and almost nobody who does is a father: a 2021 OFCE study found just 0.8% of fathers took full-time leave for a first child, against 13.7% of mothers. Around 80% of mothers are back at work by week sixteen, which flatters the "generous France" narrative while quietly conceding that the generosity has a short shelf life. Meanwhile, INSEE-linked research has found that recent growth in crèche capacity has had little measurable effect yet on mothers' employment rates — supply is up, but not enough to move the needle. Even the parents who navigate all of this successfully then discover that school holidays are not covered by any of it, and a centre aéré place for July and August is simply another bill. France's female labour-force participation, at 52.1% in 2023, is higher than Mauritius's but not by the margin the policy apparatus would suggest.

Put the two side by side and the irony sharpens. Mauritius offers the longer leave and the thinner state; France offers the shorter leave and the thicker one. A Mauritian mother is legally entitled to a year away from work but must privately assemble the childcare, the nanny, and often the extended family required to make that year survivable. A French mother gets a fraction of the time but a dense scaffolding of allowances, registries, and employer top-ups designed to catch her on the way back down. Hofstede Insights' country comparisons capture something of why: France scores firmly individualist, built around formal institutions mediating between citizen and state, while Mauritius's more communal profile explains why so much caregiving there still runs on family networks rather than paperwork. Neither culture solved the problem so much as outsourced it to whatever it already does well — France to its bureaucracy, Mauritius to its relatives. Ask a new parent in either country who actually got them through the first year, and the honest answer is rarely "the policy."

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum (Mauritius) — A newcomer weighing a move with young children was told that nannies and childcare are "everywhere" on the island, but finding a trustworthy one runs entirely on referrals and word of mouth rather than any formal listing.*

> *Expat.com magazine (Mauritius) — A French expat, seven months pregnant during the 2020 lockdown, praised her clinic as excellent and easy to reach, but admitted she had never managed to visit a crèche or meet a nanny, and planned to sort childcare only once her leave actually started.*

> *Britishexpats.com forum (France) — A parent researching Clermont-Ferrand learned that a live-in "stagiaire aide-familiale" hired through an agency was vastly cheaper than a salaried French nanny, and that surviving school holidays meant budgeting separately for a centre aéré on top of everything else.*

> *Expat.com forum (France) — A remote worker who relocated with school-age children found that France's shorter week and Wednesday half-days genuinely enabled work-life balance, though the first six months were rough on kids who arrived without French and had to rebuild their friendships from nothing.*

> *University of Strasbourg field report, cheers.jsps.go.jp — An academic mother of twins described stitching together public nursery care, grandparents, and rearranged work hours as "a permanent race and a race in the head," and said it was her research output, not her teaching, that quietly paid the price.*

### Conclusion

Neither country has actually solved the problem of combining a career with a child; they have simply chosen different places to hide the cost. France hides it in the three-year stretch after the generous first sixteen weeks, when the state's enthusiasm evaporates into a monthly payment smaller than most rents. Mauritius hides it in the gap between an impressively long legal entitlement and the almost complete absence of institutional machinery to help anyone actually use it. The fact that female labour-force participation sits in the high 40s and low 50s in both countries, despite wildly different policy architectures, suggests the paperwork is not, in the end, where the real decision gets made. Whichever country you choose, the entitlement on the recruiter's slide deck is the opening offer, not the settlement.

For someone actually choosing between them, the practical test is less "which offers more leave" and more "what kind of help do you want when the leave ends": a thick bureaucracy that pays modestly but rarely drops you, or a generous law backed by your own network, nanny, and nerve. If you want the government's blessing and your mother-in-law's babysitting, take Mauritius. If you want the government's babysitting and your mother-in-law's disapproval, take France.
