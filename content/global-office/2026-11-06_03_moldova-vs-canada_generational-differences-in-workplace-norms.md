**Chisinau's Twenty-Somethings Are Leaving. Toronto's Are Just Asking for a Raise.**

🇲🇩 Moldova · 🇨🇦 Canada

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Moldova, the generational conflict at work resolves itself by one generation simply emigrating. In Canada, it resolves itself through a 44-country Deloitte survey, several corporate engagement platforms, and an unusually large number of workplace surveys asking Gen Z how they feel about returning to the office. Both are, in their own way, forms of protest. Only one of them shows up in the national unemployment statistics.

It's worth saying plainly: Deloitte's 2025 Gen Z and Millennial Survey, which covered 44 countries and 23,482 respondents, did not include Moldova at all — Romania served as the nearest regional proxy in this research, and even that comparison is offered with real caution. The absence itself is telling. A country doesn't get included in the large, well-funded workforce-sentiment surveys once its most surveyed demographic has already left for a country that does.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇩 Moldova

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget patience for bureaucracy and petty administrative friction — widely reported as the single biggest daily headache | Expect fast resolution on anything requiring an official stamp |
| Buy proper winter footwear — icy, uneven sidewalks cause real injuries even with ice cleats | Assume Chisinau's decent, cheap internet extends evenly to rural areas |
| Dress modestly and conservatively, in both social and work settings, especially outside the capital | Expect a large expat community for instant support — it's small, so your local network matters more |
| Ask directly about pay structure in any job offer — wage dissatisfaction, not culture clash, is the headline reason young staff leave | Assume a generational "attitude problem" explains youth attrition — the data points to pay and absent career counseling instead |
| Expect younger colleagues to be genuinely overqualified for their role — nearly three in ten are, by national reporting | Treat an overqualified junior hire as a red flag rather than a structural feature of the labour market |
| Recognize that almost no formal career-counseling infrastructure exists for young workers | Assume HR will offer the kind of structured mentorship pipeline standard in larger Western markets |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Offer mentorship proactively if you manage Gen Z staff — about half want it, and most feel they aren't getting enough | Assume younger staff want remote work by default — Gen Z actually leans toward structured, office-anchored hybrid more than Millennials do |
| Expect to discuss compensation explicitly if you mandate more in-office days | Assume a return-to-office push will land quietly — 95% of Gen Z and most other generations expect a raise attached to it |
| Treat job security as a genuine, stated priority for younger hires, not just an older generation's concern | Assume Gen Z's job-security anxiety reflects entitlement rather than economic conditions they didn't create |
| Expect general comfort reporting to a younger manager or managing an older employee | Assume age-based hierarchy functions the way it might in a higher-Power-Distance culture |
| Watch for Millennials, not Gen Z, as the generation most likely to quit over lost hybrid flexibility | Assume the loudest flexibility demands always come from the youngest cohort — the data says otherwise |
| Build career-development pathways visibly — Gen Z rates this a "must-have" nearly twice as often as Boomers | Assume a single generic onboarding track will satisfy every generation equally |

---

Moldova's labour force contracted 4.3% year-on-year in 2024, per the National Bureau of Statistics, with unemployment actually falling to 4.0% — a figure that looks healthy until you learn, via IPN.md's reporting, that nearly 29% of employed young Moldovans are overqualified for their jobs, youth unemployment separately measured at 18.6%, and the average salary offered to young workers (2,771 lei) sits meaningfully below what they consider acceptable (3,569 lei). More than 70% of young respondents reported having rejected a job offer specifically over pay. Roman Banari of Moldova's National Youth Council put the structural gap bluntly to IPN.md: there are, in his words, no counseling mechanisms or services to help young people plan a career. Hofstede figures for Moldova, sourced from a working paper by the Academy of Economic Studies of Moldova, show a Power Distance of 90 and Individualism of just 17 — a profile that helps explain why the generational dispute here isn't conducted through dialogue so much as through departure: when hierarchy is this entrenched and institutional voice this limited, leaving the country reads less like rebellion and more like the only lever available.

The gender layer compounds it further: young Moldovan women reportedly earn roughly 15% less than young men across nearly all fields surveyed, per the same IPN.md reporting, and about a third of young workers nationally are informally employed — meaning no contract, no benefits, and no real recourse if the generational bargain simply doesn't materialize. Rural youth face an even starker version of the same problem, with job shortages concentrated outside Chisinau leaving many with effectively one real choice: move to the capital, or leave the country entirely.

Canada runs the opposite experiment: hierarchy is comparatively soft, institutional voice is abundant, and the generational dispute is conducted in survey form, repeatedly, by consulting firms with a vested interest in selling the resulting insights back to employers. A Humi survey of 539 Canadian employees found Gen Z the most office-anchored generation by preference (44% want mostly-in-office with occasional remote, versus roughly 30% for Boomers and Gen X), while Millennials are the most drawn to fully remote work (29.7%) — the inverse of the "young people want to work from home" assumption that shows up in most workplace journalism. The same survey found 54% of Gen Z rate job security a "must-have" versus 35% of Boomers, and separate IWG/Mortar Research polling found 95% of Gen Z would expect a raise if forced back to five-day office attendance, versus 61% of Boomers — a generational gap measured not in values but in negotiating leverage.

A separate Robert Half Canada survey, referenced via Newswire, found Canadian workers broadly comfortable reporting to a younger boss or managing an older employee — a detail that matters more than it first appears, since it suggests the generational friction in Canada is almost entirely about policy preferences (remote work, office days, mentorship) rather than about status or deference. Nobody in the Canadian data is fighting over who gets to sit at the head of the table; they're fighting, politely and at length, over whether Tuesday counts as an in-office day.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely counterintuitive finding, buried in the Canadian data, is that Millennials — not Gen Z — are the most willing to quit over lost hybrid flexibility (53% versus Boomers' 32%), inverting the media narrative that treats the youngest cohort as the most demanding one. Moldova has no equivalent plot twist to offer, because its generational story isn't a dataset of preferences — it's a one-way migration pattern that a PRI feature memorably labeled "Moldova's generation gone." The structural contrast is this: Canada's generations are negotiating the terms of staying; Moldova's youngest generation is negotiating the terms of leaving, and increasingly deciding the negotiation isn't worth having.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *TalesMag Real Post Report, Chisinau — An embassy-affiliated employee described posting morale as "very high," but noted spouse employment was essentially nonexistent, and advised conservative, Western-European-style dress for both work and public life — a small, practical detail easy to miss until you've already signed a lease.*

> *ExpatExchange Q&A, Chisinau — Responding to the recurring question of what expats find hardest, posters converged on bureaucracy, unreliable infrastructure, and above all low wages and instability as the real obstacles to finding well-paid work, rather than any particular generational friction.*

> *IPN.md — Roman Banari of Moldova's National Youth Council stated plainly that no counseling mechanisms or services exist to help young workers plan a career, framing the generational gap as an institutional absence rather than a cultural one.*

> *Eurasianet / The World (PRI) — Reporting on what the feature called "Moldova's generation gone" described young Moldovans leaving en masse for work abroad, with returnees citing the gap between the clear career paths and counseling they'd found elsewhere and what remains available at home.*

> *Humi survey writeup (aggregated Canadian employee data) — Read in context, Gen Z respondents rank professional development and job security far higher than Boomers do — thousands of data points converging less on "entitlement" than on visible economic anxiety, a useful corrective to the laziness narrative that dogs the generation in the press.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway, if you're the one relocating: in Canada, generational friction at work is a negotiation you can have, repeatedly, with data on your side, whatever side you're on. In Moldova, it's a negotiation that too often simply doesn't happen, because the preferred resolution for an entire cohort has become a plane ticket. If you're a manager moving into either market, the task is the same — build the mentorship and pay transparency that keeps a generation in the building — but in Moldova, you're not just competing with the office down the street. You're competing with Bucharest, Berlin, and everywhere else that offers a counseling mechanism that actually exists.

Managers relocating into either market should take one specific lesson from the data above: the generation you assume will be the flexibility problem is frequently not the one that actually is. Build a retention strategy around the evidence in front of you, not the stereotype that happened to be trending in the trade press the year you wrote the policy. The generation actually threatening to walk may well be the one your org chart least expects, and the data above exists specifically so you don't have to find that out the expensive way, mid-resignation, in an exit interview. For a Moldovan employer, the lesson is blunter still: the counseling infrastructure nobody has built yet is, at this point, cheaper than the recruitment costs of replacing everyone who leaves for want of it.
