# Buttoned Up or Barong Down: Dressing for Work in Saudi Arabia and the Philippines

🇸🇦 Saudi Arabia · 🇵🇭 Philippines

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries, two philosophies of getting dressed for work. One is drafting a national law specifying, in regulatory language, how much of an employee's body must remain covered and how loose that covering must be. The other has spent the better part of a decade debating whether jeans count as professional attire and has basically decided yes, probably, so long as nobody wears flip-flops. In November 2025, Saudi Arabia's labor ministry floated a draft "Code of Appearance and Conduct" that would put Saudi men in the thobe, non-Saudi men in a formal suit with no business-casual exemption, and women of every nationality into clothing that is neither tight nor transparent (Latestly, citing regional press, Nov. 27, 2025). In the Philippines, a 2016 Regus survey of nearly 40,000 office workers found 82 percent consider jeans perfectly acceptable at the desk, and the only near-universal taboo is the rubber flip-flop (GMA Network, 2016). Both countries, reassuringly, draw the line at tank tops.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇦 Saudi Arabia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep a dark, formal suit on hand if you're a non-Saudi man in any client-facing role — the 2025 draft code removes business casual as an option for expat men entirely. | Don't assume the 2019 easing of the abaya rule for foreign women means dress is unregulated now; "loose, covering, non-transparent" remains the working floor almost everywhere outside a handful of international compounds. |
| Do notice when your Saudi counterparts show up in thobe and ghutra for official business — it's been mandatory for civil servants since April 2024 (Saudi Gazette), even in meetings where you're expected in a suit. | Don't bring anything with political slogans, flags, or ambiguous imagery to the office; the draft code explicitly bans "ideological or provocative" prints. |
| Do carry a cardigan or light jacket even in summer — offices and malls are aggressively air-conditioned, and modest layering reads as effortless rather than overcautious. | Don't treat "casual Friday" as license for shorts or sleeveless tops; casual in a Saudi office means ironed cotton, not exposed knees. |
| Do ask your specific employer for its written policy before you land — compliance with the new code is enforced employer by employer, with fines for firms that skip it. | Don't be surprised if your actual daily office (engineering sites, tech firms, oil-and-gas campuses) is far more relaxed than the national law implies; enforcement varies by sector and city. |

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Do keep a barong tagalog or a blazer ready for the two or three days a year you'll genuinely need "business formal" — client visits, year-end events, a visit from Legal. | Don't show up to a BPO floor in a full suit on day one; you'll be the only person dressed for a different country, since most call-center floors default to business-casual-to-casual. |
| Do ask HR for the literal written dress code memo on day one — it varies wildly by company, from "semi-formal" to "casual/office attire" to "smart casual," with no shared industry standard (Prosple graduate reviews). | Don't wear rubber flip-flops or tracksuits; these remain the one near-universal no, even though jeans cleared 82 percent approval nationally (GMA Network, 2016). |
| Do pack a cardigan for the office even in a tropical country — aggressive air conditioning is less a comfort than survival gear. | Don't assume Manila's BPO-tower informality travels to government offices, banks, or provincial postings, where expectations run noticeably more conservative. |
| Do default slightly more formal than you think you need to be in week one; it is easier to dress down later than to walk back a bad first impression. | Don't assume "no legal dress code" means no judgment — Filipino offices self-police through manager expectations and client-facing roles rather than statute. |

Saudi Arabia is, on paper, liberalizing and formalizing at the same time, which is the kind of contradiction that only a government in the middle of Vision 2030 could pull off with a straight face. Female labor-force participation has climbed to 36.2 percent as of the third quarter of 2025, a record for the kingdom (GASTAT). The abaya requirement for foreign businesswomen was eased back in 2019, a change one Arab News columnist credited with removing a genuine obstacle to international deal-making, after years of being told to put one on for meetings and then told to take it off for dinner, sometimes in the same evening (Arab News, Ellen R. Wald). And yet the same government that is recruiting more women into offices is simultaneously drafting the most legally specific description of a sleeve this reporter has encountered: not tight, not transparent, covering the body, correct from the moment you enter the building until the moment you leave it (Saudi Gazette, April 2024; Latestly, Nov. 2025).

The practical result is a country running two systems at once. The law aims at codified national identity — Saudi men in thobes, expat men in suits, a written policy displayed in every workplace. The lived reality, per people who actually work there, is often looser: an Aramco office where open-necked polos outnumber ties, where a hijab or abaya is treated as an unremarkable personal choice nobody questions either way. New arrivals should pack for the law, not the anecdote, and adjust downward once they see what their specific office actually does.

What makes the draft code genuinely confusing, rather than simply conservative, is the company it keeps. It arrives after a decade of Vision 2030 loosening nearly everything else about Saudi public life — cinemas reopened, gender-mixed concert venues normalized, the guardianship requirement for women's travel abolished — which makes a new, more detailed appearance statute read less like a crackdown and more like a government trying to write down, for the first time, rules that used to be enforced by glance and custom rather than paragraph. A country that spent years being criticized for having unwritten, arbitrarily enforced dress expectations has responded, in true bureaucratic fashion, not by relaxing the expectations but by drafting them properly. Whether that counts as progress or merely better documentation is the kind of question a new hire should leave to the op-ed pages and not worry about before their first performance review.

The Philippines has no equivalent statute, and functions instead as a vast, uncoordinated experiment in self-regulation. There is no national dress code, no ministry drafting fabric specifications — just 40,000 surveyed office workers collectively deciding, apparently by consensus, that jeans are fine and flip-flops are not (GMA Network/Regus, 2016). What looks like permissiveness, though, sits on top of the region's most stressed workforce: Gallup's 2026 State of the Global Workplace found 50 percent of Filipino employees reported significant stress the day before being surveyed, double the Southeast Asian average of 25 percent, even as the same workers reported above-average engagement, 39 percent versus a regional 25 percent (Gallup, via The Philippine Star, July 2026). The collar may be loose; the deadlines are not.

That looseness also means no two employers agree on what "appropriate" means. Entry-level reviews collected on Prosple describe the same tier of company as "semi-formal," "casual/office attire," and "smart casual" within the same industry, sometimes the same job board listing (Prosple graduate reviews). The barong tagalog, the country's closest thing to formal national dress, surfaces mainly for client visits and embassy functions, where one crowdsourced account specifies "button-down, khaki, and tie" as the unofficial uniform (Talesmag Post Report, Manila). Outside those occasions, the actual rule is: ask your specific manager, because the national rule does not exist.

Much of that looseness traces back to what the Philippine office economy actually is: a country that staffs other countries' customer service, payroll, and back-office functions around the clock. A floor answering calls for an Australian insurer at 6 a.m. local time, or processing claims for a US client at 11 p.m., has little reason to enforce a dress code calibrated to in-person client meetings nobody on that shift will ever attend. The client sees a headset and a voice, not a collar. That logistical fact, more than any national preference for comfort, is why the industry that employs over a million and a half Filipinos in business process outsourcing settled on "presentable enough for a video call, irrelevant below the webcam" as its de facto standard, long before remote work made that compromise fashionable everywhere else.

**The Reckoning.** Here is the twist a brochure would never print: the country legislating dress down to the centimeter is, in day-to-day office life, frequently less fussy than the country with no law at all. A Saudi tech office can run on polos and jeans while the kingdom debates a formal written code; a Philippine BPO floor can enforce a stricter unwritten standard than the law requires, simply because a specific team lead decided so in 2019 and nobody has revisited it since. Formality, it turns out, is not where the statute lives — it is where the manager sits.

Hofstede Insights' power-distance data puts both countries in roughly the same high-deference range — the Philippines scoring among the highest worldwide, the broader Arab cluster that includes Saudi Arabia not far behind — meaning both cultures expect hierarchy to be visible. They have simply chosen different fabrics to make it legible: Riyadh through codified national dress, Manila through the unspoken hierarchy of who gets to wear jeans and who has to wear the barong.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/consulting (via public mirror) — one commenter, asked whether a Saudi posting was worth it, skipped the dress code entirely and warned instead about the heat and the traffic, a reminder that the clothing question is rarely the hardest adjustment.*

> *Expat.com, Riyadh forum — a long-serving member describes the real men's dress code as close to American business casual: dress pants, a collared shirt, decent shoes, no suit required and no sports coat expected, contradicting the sharper, more formal image outsiders bring with them.*

> *Aramco Expats community forum — members note that abaya, hijab, and even niqab are all simply accepted at work, with nobody questioning a woman's choice either way, which surprised newcomers who had braced for a much more adversarial, policed environment.*

> *Talesmag Post Report, Manila — several contributors independently describe the real rule as "relaxed because of the heat," with true formal dress reserved for embassy functions and high-level meetings, undercutting the assumption that a business trip to Manila requires a suitcase of blazers.*

> *Prosple graduate reviews, Philippines — multiple entry-level hires at comparable employers each received a different official answer — "semi-formal," "smart casual," "casual/office attire" — the kind of inconsistency nobody mentions until the new hire is already standing in the lobby, overdressed or underdressed depending on luck.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway is almost the inverse of what each country's reputation suggests. Moving to Saudi Arabia, pack for the written rule: a suit if you're a non-Saudi man, modest covering if you're a woman, and a willingness to let your specific employer's written policy, not the news cycle, be the final word — then relax once you see what your actual office does day to day. Moving to the Philippines, pack for the absence of a rule: ask on day one, because "smart casual" at one Manila office can mean something entirely different three floors up, and the stress you'll actually encounter has far less to do with your collar than with your inbox.

Between a country formalizing its dress code by statute and a country formalizing nothing and somehow producing the region's most stressed employees anyway, the only honest conclusion is that neither committee of lawmakers nor absence of lawmakers has ever once made anyone's Monday easier.
