# Morocco's Soft Landing, France's Hard Truths, and One Useless Annual Review

🇲🇦 Morocco · 🇫🇷 France

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In France, the annual performance review is not something an employer is legally required to hold, unless a collective agreement says otherwise. In Morocco, 81% of employees surveyed by the recruitment site Rekrute said they hoped to use the review to renegotiate their salary. The same meeting, with the same two chairs, is a legal procedure in one country and a pay negotiation in the other. Both countries also contain a large number of people who think it is a waste of everyone's time, which is perhaps the only point on which they agree.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat the review as the moment to raise salary and career development, since both sides expect it | Arrive without a clear case for a raise or a new role |
| Prepare a written self-assessment, as local HR advice recommends | Expect the manager to lead the conversation unprompted |
| Frame criticism of a colleague or manager indirectly and privately | Deliver blunt criticism in front of others, where loss of face is a real cost |
| Keep daily problems in mind and raise them in a measured way | Assume silence means there are no problems |
| Ask for follow-up on objectives agreed in the meeting | Accept a promise of a raise without a date |
| Use French in business settings, where it remains the main working language | Rely on a verbal agreement alone |

### France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the criteria in advance, since French law says they must be precise, objective and verifiable | Assume the manager will improvise a fair assessment |
| Take the interview seriously, because an employee cannot refuse it | Skip it or treat it as optional |
| Ask for the minutes and your results, which you have a right to see | Sign anything you disagree with without reading it |
| Expect plain disagreement and defend your view | Take a blunt remark as a personal attack |
| Ask for feedback during the year, not only at review time | Wait until December to find out what went wrong in March |
| Raise any dispute through the proper channel, ultimately the labour tribunal | Go over your manager's head without telling them |

## Morocco: Respect Is a Feature, Candour Is Optional

Morocco World News describes a hierarchical society in which people accept an order where everyone has a place, and in which subordinates expect guidance and direction. It calls the ideal boss a benevolent autocrat. The same article, citing Hofstede-framework figures from 2017, gives Morocco a masculinity score of 53 and a long-term orientation of 14, and notes that in collectivist societies offence leads to shame and loss of face. IOR World's cultural summary adds that difficult information may be conveyed indirectly, through stories, to preserve harmony. Put these together and you have a feedback culture in which the message is often present but the delivery is carefully padded.

Rekrute's surveys show what that looks like in practice. In a 2015 survey of 1,513 working people, 49% of employees and 52% of managers said they held one review a year, while 34% of employees said there was no set rule. Most interviews lasted 15 to 30 minutes. About 74% of managers believed employees left the meeting more motivated, while only about half of employees agreed, and 31% of employees saw the exercise as a mere formality. A later Rekrute survey of 2,308 Moroccans, mostly young professionals at large companies, found 78% of managers and 76% of employees wanted reviews more than once a year. Managers expected staff to raise daily workplace problems, yet employees ranked that topic only seventh, which Rekrute reads as a hesitation to speak openly for fear of negative consequences.

Le Matin's 2018 coverage was less diplomatic. It reports that employees often see the interview as useless, subjective and demotivating, and quotes one who called it a waste of time and energy because promised raises and promotions never arrived. The Expat.com guide to Casablanca says recognition and pay drive much of the corporate culture and that the pressure to achieve results is lower than in Europe. For a foreign hire, the useful translation is that the review is less a verdict than a negotiation, and that the real feedback tends to arrive sideways, in tone, in whom you are asked to work with, and in what you are not told.

The frustrations Rekrute lists will sound familiar to anyone who has sat through an appraisal elsewhere. Employees say agreed objectives are not discussed again, that there is little motivation or encouragement, and that improvement points are not actionable. In the 2015 survey, 37% of employees said managers most needed to improve at motivating staff, and 46% of employees and 45% of managers said the meeting ran 15 to 30 minutes, a duration 70% of managers considered about right. Timing deserves a note as well. The US commercial guide to Morocco says public and private sectors work reduced hours of six a day during Ramadan, so a review scheduled for that month will be shorter, or later, than planned.

## France: Criticism Is a Form of Attention

French law is more specific than the culture it governs. Service-Public says employers may hold appraisal interviews under their management power but need not, unless a collective agreement provides for them. Where they exist, the criteria must be precise, objective and verifiable, employees must be told the method in advance, and in companies with 50 or more staff the works council (the CSE) must be consulted first. An employee cannot refuse the interview and cannot bring a staff representative to it. The employee can see the minutes, may refuse to sign without invalidating the report, and may challenge the assessment before the labour tribunal. The results can inform pay, promotion or discipline up to dismissal.

The culture is another matter. INSEAD says the French are used to criticising passionately and providing positive feedback sparingly. The Local reports that colleagues tend to be blunt, that disagreements can break out openly, and that people often do not take criticism personally. A Maddyness opinion piece, however, argues that feedback goes poorly in France because the annual review is often the only feedback moment, employees arrive asking for a raise, managers hold back praise in response, and issues surface months after they happened. It cites a Gallup finding that 83% of millennials feel the feedback they receive is poorly supported. Welcome to the Jungle adds that 44% of French employees consider the annual review useless, according to an Elevo survey in 2021, and that many companies are moving to a hybrid of formal reviews and continuous check-ins.

The contrast with Morocco is partly one of expectation. Welcome to the Jungle reports that 70% of respondents, in a figure it does not attribute to a named source, say these conversations are not followed by enough concrete action, and that 38% want more reciprocal exchanges. The anthropologist Stephane Moriou, quoted there, argues that feedback should also flow upward and between peers, and advises against the feedback sandwich of praise, criticism and praise. Few French managers have read the memo. The American writer in the Business Insider piece found that managers asked about family and politics with ease, yet raises were rare and asking for one felt taboo. Personal curiosity and professional candour, it turns out, are separate departments.

## The Reckoning

France has the stronger rules and the weaker habit. The law protects the employee's access to the process, but cannot make a manager say what they mean in July. Morocco has the weaker rules and, on the evidence of Rekrute, a clearer shared wish for more frequent conversations, undermined by a reluctance to say the difficult thing upward. The French employee is more likely to hear the criticism and less likely to hear the praise. The Moroccan employee is more likely to be spared the criticism and less likely to know where they stand.

Neither pattern is fatal, and neither is a reason not to go. It is a reason not to read the annual review as the whole of the feedback. In France, bluntness is the default and usually carries no malice, but it is rarely balanced. In Morocco, courtesy is the default and often carries information that has to be decoded. Employees who arrive from the Anglo-American system of explicit praise, written targets and regular one-to-ones will find both countries underserviced, and will need to build their own feedback loop.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Local France — colleagues tend to be blunt, disagreements can erupt openly, and people generally defend their views without taking criticism personally.*

> *Business Insider via AOL — an American who worked in a Paris corporate job for two years found that raises were rare, asking for one felt taboo, and managers asked personal questions about family and politics.*

> *Maddyness — a French opinion writer describes a critical email sent to a team with all superiors copied as humiliating, and says managers often repeat the patterns they experienced themselves.*

> *Expat.com (Casablanca forum) — managers working in Morocco describe close supervision and daily follow-up as essential, and one poster of Moroccan origin says French managers are often seen as arrogant, adding that listening works better than imposing methods.*

> *Le Matin — an employee quoted in a Moroccan newspaper called the evaluation interview a waste of time and energy, citing raises and promotions promised and never delivered.*

## Conclusion

If you are moving to France, ask your manager early how and when feedback will be given, and do not wait for the annual review. Read the criteria, keep your own notes, and treat directness as a working style, not a verdict. If you are moving to Morocco, prepare your salary case, listen for what is said indirectly, and ask for specific follow-up on anything agreed.

In either country, build a feedback routine of your own: a short written summary after each meeting, sent to your manager, is unglamorous and works. The annual review will remain a ritual in both. Bring a pen.
