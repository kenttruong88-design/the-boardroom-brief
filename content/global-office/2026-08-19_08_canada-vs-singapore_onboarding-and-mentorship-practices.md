# Canada Gives You a Buddy. Singapore Gives You a Binder, a Buddy, and a Mentor With a Different Job Title Than You Expected.

**🇨🇦 Canada · 🇸🇬 Singapore**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A new hire in Toronto typically gets paired with a peer-level "buddy" for the first few weeks — someone to ask dumb questions to, informally, without it going on the record. A new hire in Singapore is more likely to get that same buddy plus a structured orientation program plus, separately, a formal mentor assigned for longer-term career development — three distinct relationships doing three distinct jobs, mapped out before you've even found the printer.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Lean on your assigned buddy for the informal, "no dumb question" stuff | Assume your buddy is responsible for your career development — that's usually a separate conversation with your manager |
| Take initiative to build your own mentor relationship if the company doesn't formalize one | Wait passively for a structured mentorship program that may not exist in a Canadian company culture that leans informal |
| Ask direct questions early — Canadian onboarding tends to assume some self-sufficiency | Assume silence from HR after week one means anything is wrong — check-ins are often lighter-touch |
| Treat the first few weeks as a two-way evaluation, not just being tested | Over-formalize requests for feedback; a casual "how am I doing?" chat is usually welcome |
| Build peer relationships across teams, not just your immediate one | Assume your onboarding buddy is your only route to institutional knowledge |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a structured, multi-week orientation with defined milestones | Assume informal, ad-hoc onboarding — the process tends to be more codified than in Canada |
| Treat your assigned mentor relationship as a real, ongoing career resource, not a formality | Let a mentorship pairing go unused; it's often intended for genuine long-term development |
| Clarify who to go to for what — buddy for logistics, mentor for career, manager for performance | Default to your manager for every question when a buddy or mentor role exists specifically to absorb them |
| Build relationships proactively outside your assigned pairings too — personal networks matter for advancement | Assume the formal structure alone will build the relationships you need to get ahead |
| Respect hierarchy in how you approach a senior mentor — formality matters even in a supportive relationship | Treat a mentor as a peer just because the relationship feels warm |

---

Canada's relatively low power-distance and high-individualism profile (a PDI of 39 against a global average of 55, and an individualism score of 80, per Hofstede Insights) produces onboarding that leans light-touch and assumes competence: a buddy for the social and logistical basics, and an expectation that the new hire will build the rest of their network and mentorship on their own initiative. Formal, company-run mentorship programs exist in Canada, particularly at larger employers, but they're often optional rather than default, and the overall tone assumes new hires will self-advocate for what they need rather than having every relationship pre-assigned.

Singapore's higher power-distance and more collectivist orientation (PDI 74) shows up in onboarding as genuine infrastructure: research on Singapore employee onboarding practices describes structured orientation sessions as the norm, frequently combined with an assigned buddy for day-to-day navigation and a separate, more senior mentor focused on longer-term career development — a three-tier system some employers around the world, including Google and Deloitte, run everywhere but that shows up with particular consistency in Singapore's more formalized corporate culture. The distinction between buddy and mentor is treated as functionally real, not just organizational chart decoration: one relationship is peer-level and practical, the other is hierarchical and developmental, and conflating the two is a minor faux pas.

The reckoning is that Canada trusts the new hire to build their own scaffolding, while Singapore builds it for them — with both approaches carrying a hidden cost. A Canadian transplant in Singapore might find the structure reassuring at first and then slightly confining, waiting for permission to network organically the way they would at home. A Singaporean professional landing in a Canadian office might find the lack of a formal mentor disorienting, unsure whether the absence of a program means the company doesn't value development, when it more often just means development is assumed to be self-directed.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A respondent describing their first month working in Singapore as an expat said the orientation itself was thorough almost to a fault — HR walked through everything from benefits to reporting lines in detail — but that the real onboarding, in terms of understanding how decisions actually got made, happened much more slowly and depended on informal trust-building the paperwork didn't cover.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a question about finding a mentor in Singapore noted that a good mentor could fast-track a newcomer's learning curve significantly, but that finding one outside a formally assigned program required real effort — professional networking events were frequently cited as the more reliable route.*

> *teamblind.com — A poster comparing onboarding at a major tech employer described being paired with both an onboarding-focused buddy and a separate mentor outside their immediate team, and said the two-mentor structure meant they always had someone to ask "obvious" questions without it reflecting on their standing with their actual manager.*

> *teamblind.com — In a thread about feeling abandoned during onboarding at a different company, one poster described weeks of ambiguity about who was actually responsible for ramping them up, contrasting it with a previous, more Asia-based role where a formally assigned mentor made expectations explicit from day one.*

> *Quora — A commenter noted that finding a job in Singapore as a foreigner without existing local contacts was notably harder than expected, and that networking events functioned as a kind of unofficial onboarding into the professional community long before any actual job offer — connections mattered before the paperwork ever started.*

### Conclusion

If you're heading to Singapore, take the formal mentor relationship seriously — it's designed to do real work, not just check a box. If you're heading to Canada, don't wait for a program that may never materialize; build your own mentor and your own network deliberately, because nobody's going to assign you one. Either way, figure out in week one who's actually supposed to answer your questions — the org chart won't always tell you, and guessing wrong costs you time you don't get back.
