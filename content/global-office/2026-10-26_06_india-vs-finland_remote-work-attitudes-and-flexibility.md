**Logged In From Bengaluru, Logged Off at Four: Two Countries, Two Definitions of "Remote"**

**🇮🇳 India · 🇫🇮 Finland**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Finland, the law assumes you can be trusted to manage half your own working hours without supervision. In India, a sizeable share of the IT sector now ties your bonus to a swipe-card reader. Both countries call what remains of the pandemic experiment "flexible work." Only one of them means it literally.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat office attendance as a measurable KPI — at TCS it is literally linked to quarterly bonus pay | Assume a WFH-friendly job posting will survive the next leadership town hall |
| Build a home power backup and a stable internet plan before your first client call | Expect anyone to ask permission before requesting proof-of-presence during an outage |
| Get WFH days written into your offer letter, not promised verbally | Count on predictable hours once attendance becomes a scoring system |
| Budget commute time into your schedule like a fixed meeting | Underestimate how fast rent and traffic erode any savings once mandates reverse |
| Make selective, visible office appearances when hybrid is offered | Skip "face time" entirely on the theory that output alone will speak for itself |

#### 🇫🇮 Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Agree your flexitime band in writing — the law allows up to four hours of daily leeway | Mistake reserved colleagues for unfriendly ones; warmth arrives late and lasts |
| Use the 8-to-4 culture to actually stop at four | Expect a manager to track your hours for you — that's formally your own job |
| Accept that averaging hours over a four-month window is normal, not suspicious | Schedule meetings after 4 p.m. and expect full attendance |
| Take sauna and after-work invitations seriously as real team-building | Push for title-based hierarchy; flat structures are the default, not a trend |
| Let managers set goals and leave the method to you | Assume an English-speaking office means Finnish is optional long-term |

In India, "flexible work" has quietly become a euphemism under renegotiation. TCS, the country's largest IT employer, now links quarterly variable pay directly to office attendance — full pay above 85 percent attendance, half at 60–75 percent, none at all below 60, according to reporting in Business Today. Its chief executive has credited the return-to-office push with preserving "organisational culture," noting roughly 65 percent of staff now attend three to five days a week. Infosys and Cognizant have made comparable moves, trimming or ending blanket work-from-home options that were standard issue as recently as 2022. Disciplinary consequences for non-compliance are mentioned in company communications but rarely spelled out in public, which tends to make employees assume the worst rather than the best.

Yet the data cuts both ways. A Unispace study found 97 percent of Indian employees report satisfaction with hybrid arrangements, against a global average of 87 percent, with employer satisfaction running even higher at 98 percent versus 95 percent globally. That is not a workforce straining against hybrid work; it is a workforce that has been fairly content with it and is now being walked back toward 2019 anyway, largely for reasons framed around culture rather than output.

The cost of that reversal is the part policy memos omit. An academic interview project covered by the University of Bath's IPR blog, drawing on 51 Indian IT workers serving global firms, found employees buying their own battery backups at roughly a month's junior salary, staging video-call backgrounds in shared family flats, and scheduling television around calls because household bandwidth is shared with children studying online. In one case, a manager asked an employee for photographic proof of conditions at home during a power cut; in another, a tracking system logged hours and flagged lapses straight to a supervisor. Remote work, in other words, quietly outsourced the office's overhead — generators, desks, bandwidth — onto the household, a detail that rarely survives translation into a glossy "work from anywhere" press release. Meanwhile the sector remains largely exempt from statutory protections like a right to disconnect, and 2025 saw protests in Bengaluru over proposals to extend the maximum working day.

Finland's starting assumption runs the other way entirely. The 2020 Working Hours Act created *joustotyöaika* — flexible working time — letting employees who can work independently decide both when and where to do at least half their hours, so long as the weekly average doesn't exceed 40 over a four-month reference period. The same reform widened ordinary flexitime bands from three hours to four and raised the cap on banked overtime hours. Monitoring those hours, the law states plainly, is "primarily the employee's responsibility" — a sentence that would read as an invitation to abuse in most jurisdictions and reads, in Finland, as simply how work functions. Statistics Finland's Quality of Work Life Survey puts remote work at roughly 35 percent of wage earners in 2023, down from the pandemic peak but still above 2018 levels, and for the first time more common among women (37 percent) than men (33 percent).

None of this makes Finnish offices empty by design. Foreign specialists quoted by Yle News describe a culture that is flexible on hours but still deliberately social: a Nokia Ventures executive cites an "inclusive work culture" and room to grow, while a Wolt analytics lead notes simply that "no one forces me to work ten-hour days here." A colleague at the same company frames the office's real draw as what happens after five, recalling karaoke nights as part of belonging, not an optional extra. The expectation is autonomy on paper and continued, voluntary togetherness in practice — a combination that is harder to legislate than either half alone.

**The Reckoning.** The paradox is that India's workers, on paper, report higher satisfaction with hybrid work than Finland's workers need to report with full statutory flexibility — contentment, it turns out, is not the same as leverage. Finland's workers, who could legally work from a cabin by a lake for half their contracted hours, mostly choose not to, because the office there was never positioned as the enemy of flexibility in the first place. Hofstede Insights' country-comparison scores help explain why the two systems metabolize autonomy so differently: India scores high on power distance (77) and relatively low on individualism (48), meaning hierarchy and visible deference still carry real weight even when the manager is a face on a screen; Finland inverts both figures (power distance 33, individualism 63), making unsupervised, self-reported working hours culturally unremarkable rather than a managerial risk to be managed around.

The practical upshot: in India, "remote" is currently a negotiated exception that head office can revoke by internal memo and attendance spreadsheet. In Finland, it's closer to a statutory default that employees themselves are trusted, and legally obligated, not to abuse. Neither posture is more "modern" than the other so much as they are answers to two different questions about what an office is actually for.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit — A Bengaluru software engineer with over a decade of IT experience described moving back to the city as financially ruinous — citing rent, traffic, and commute costs that would erase most of their savings even with the same salary — and said years of remote work never once hurt their output.*

> *X (formerly Twitter) — A widely shared post argued that mandating office attendance in Bangalore and Mumbai when in-person presence isn't actually required simply burns employees' time and energy reaching the building before the workday even starts.*

> *Bath IPR Blog (academic interviews) — One engineer in his late twenties recalled his manager asking for photos of his house as proof during a power outage; another described an online system that logs his hours and flags any lapse directly to his supervisor.*

> *Druid.fi (expat blog) — An intern-turned-employee described Finnish colleagues as reserved at first, warming only gradually, while an 8-to-4 schedule let her pursue outside interests without carrying work home — a trade she hadn't expected to value as much as she did.*

> *Yle News — A Wolt visual-technology lead, originally from abroad, said the most pleasant surprise wasn't the flexible hours but the after-work culture that came with them, recalling evenings out singing karaoke with colleagues as a genuine part of belonging at the company.*

### Conclusion

If flexibility is the deciding factor in your move, read the fine print, not the recruiter's pitch. In India, ask specifically what percentage of attendance your bonus requires and whether that number has changed in the last eighteen months — it probably has. In Finland, ask less about whether remote work is allowed and more about how your own team actually uses the four-month averaging window, since the law grants latitude that office norms don't always claim.

Both countries will tell you they've figured out the future of work. Only one of them has put it in writing, and the other has put it on a scorecard.
