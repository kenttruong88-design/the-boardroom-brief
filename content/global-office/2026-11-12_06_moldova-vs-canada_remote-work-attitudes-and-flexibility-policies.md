# Moldova Wrote Remote Work Into Law in a Hurry; Canada Is Writing It Out of the Office on Purpose

🇲🇩 Moldova · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Moldova legalised remote work in 2020 in a matter of weeks and has been discovering what it forgot to include ever since. Canada, which spent the pandemic as an exemplary enthusiast, is now in a more reflective phase, one in which employers have recalled that offices have carpets. A reader hoping to move for a flexible job should therefore ask two questions: what the law says, and what the person who signs your payslip thinks. The answers do not always agree, and the order of surprise differs by country.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Moldova

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Put any remote arrangement in your individual employment contract or an addendum | Rely on a verbal "of course you can work from home" |
| Ask in writing who pays for internet, electricity and equipment | Assume the Labour Code splits home-working costs for you |
| Look at IT Park residency if you work in software and want the 7% turnover regime | Assume residency is a purely online process; applicants must visit Moldova once |
| Agree contact hours with your manager before the first evening message | Expect a statutory right to disconnect; none exists |
| Check whether your employer can switch you back to the office by order | Assume remote work is voluntary or reversible in law |

### In Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask the recruiter for the exact number of required office days and whether they are fixed | Accept "flexible" as a description without a number |
| If federally regulated, use the written right to request after six months | Expect a private-sector employer to owe you remote work |
| Learn your sector, since finance and the public service have moved hardest toward the office | Assume a hybrid offer today will stay hybrid next year |
| Budget for a commute, which averages 25 minutes by car and 44 by transit nationally | Choose a suburb by assuming the commute is optional |
| Negotiate remote terms before signing, not after | Count on a pandemic-era precedent |

## Moldova

Moldova's remote work framework is brief and, by the account of an ILAW report on the subject, hastily built. Remote work entered the Labour Code through Law No. 69 of 21 May 2020, in the first wave of pandemic emergency measures. The report finds that it must be set out in an individual contract or addendum, but that there are no explicit rules on voluntariness or reversibility, no statutory right to disconnect and no specific protection for remote workers against discrimination. Employers must provide equipment, but who bears costs such as internet and electricity is left undefined. Moldova also has not ratified the ILO's home work convention. The report recommended that remote work be voluntary and reversible, and floated restricting employer contact between 19:00 and 08:00; the sources I read do not show that these recommendations were adopted.

The reality is therefore sector-dependent. The national statistics bureau, according to the same report, counted about 40.5 percent of workers in home or remote work in the fourth quarter of 2020, though the authors question the method. The better-organised end of the market is IT. The Moldova Investment Agency says ICT contributed 8.3 percent of GDP in 2023 and employs 4.4 percent of the workforce, the second-highest share in emerging Europe after Estonia. The IT Park offers a single 7 percent tax on turnover and permits work without physical presence in Moldova, though applicants must visit once to obtain an electronic signature, register a company and apply for residency. Nearly 500 foreign citizens already work in that community.

Outside that perimeter, the attitude is hard to measure and the evidence is thin. A February 2025 Reddit thread on the Moldovan IT sector describes a saturated junior market concentrated in Chisinau, with remote work mentioned only in passing as an option that is easier in Romania. The honest conclusion is that Moldova is permissive in law, patchy in practice and strongly shaped by whether your employer is a foreign client.

Some practical details survive the vagueness. The normal working week is 40 hours with a daily ceiling of 10, according to the ILAW report, and Rivermate lists a flat 12 percent income tax on employment income. The same report notes that health and safety rules technically apply to a home workplace, yet employers cannot inspect one, and the monitoring provisions are ambiguous. In other words, your kitchen table is regulated in principle and unsupervised in practice. That suits most people until a dispute begins.

## Canada

Canada's attitude is easiest to read in its numbers, which have moved in one direction. According to Statistics Canada figures reported by the Canadian Press, 17.4 percent of employed people mostly worked from home in May 2025, down from 18.7 percent a year earlier, while 77.6 percent worked only outside the home and 5.1 percent were hybrid. By the second quarter of 2026, Robert Half Canada found that fully on-site roles made up 84 percent of new postings, up from 63 percent in the fourth quarter of 2025, with only 13 percent hybrid and 3 percent fully remote. A University of Toronto Scarborough researcher quoted in the HRD Canada coverage described a low-hire market in which employers hold the leverage.

Governments have led by example. The federal Treasury Board ordered executives back five days a week by 4 May 2026 and most other core public servants back at least four days a week by 6 July 2026, according to CP24; Ontario's public service moved to five days from January 2026. Workers dislike it. The Angus Reid Institute found in July 2025 that 59 percent of Canadians prefer remote work, and that among those currently working from home, 24 percent would likely quit rather than return full time. Another third would comply and look elsewhere. Public opinion is less one-sided than the grumbling suggests: in a September 2025 Angus Reid poll, 57 percent supported the then-current federal rule of at least three office days, and private-sector respondents were evenly split (45 percent in favour, 44 percent opposed) on ending hybrid work for federal employees entirely. Canadians, it appears, object chiefly to being the ones affected.

There is, however, a legal floor that Moldova lacks. Federally regulated employees may make a written request to change their hours, schedule or work location after six months; the employer must reply within 30 days and may refuse only on listed grounds, including cost and performance. Most workers, though, are provincially regulated and have no such right. Scoop data reported by BetaKit found that 84 percent of Canadian firms offer some location flexibility, yet Canadian companies require about 2.68 office days a week on average, and only 16 percent offer fully remote work.

## The Reckoning

In Moldova, the law is permissive, thin and silent on the points that matter once the honeymoon ends: costs, reversibility, disconnection. In Canada, the law is mostly silent for private employers and the market has turned from persuasion to instruction. Hofstede-based comparison data scores Canada at 39 for power distance and 72 for individualism; I could not retrieve a comparable Moldovan figure, so I will not invent one, but the Canadian pattern fits: the dispute is conducted by polite polls and petitions, not by open defiance.

For a prospective mover, the practical difference is whom you are working for. A Moldovan employer serving Western clients will often tolerate home working because the client does, and the IT Park regime formalises that for software. A Canadian bank or ministry will not. The question to ask in both places is not whether remote work is allowed but who can withdraw it and with how much notice.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/moldova — a junior developer asked about the IT market and was told it is saturated and heavily concentrated in Chisinau, and that the headline tax rate has not drawn as many outsourcers as promised. Remote work came up only as an aside about Romania, so this speaks to the job market more than to attitudes.*

> *Hacker News — a final-year IT student in Chisinau, posting in the November 2026 hiring thread, said they want remote-only work until mid-2027 and can overlap with US hours. This is a job-seeker's preference, not a comment on employers.*

> *ExpatExchange — one replier on Chisinau's challenges described an unstable local economy, high unemployment and low salaries, which makes well-paid local work hard to find. The post is undated and adjacent to the topic, but it explains why foreign remote income looks attractive.*

> *Reddit r/toronto (via BlogTO) — a poster vowed to minimise spending downtown in protest at office mandates, drawing over a thousand upvotes. Replies disputed whether that hurts employers or only the service workers who cannot avoid the commute.*

> *CBC News (human-interest piece, apparently 2023) — a Toronto senior accountant began job hunting when her employer required three office days, found two remote-friendly offers within a month and said her quality of life had improved. This is a press profile, not a forum post.*

## Conclusion

If you are a software professional who wants a remote arrangement that exists on paper, Moldova's IT Park is the more explicit structure, provided you accept a thin Labour Code and a small domestic market. If you want a deep job market and a federal right to request flexibility, Canada is attractive, but only if you are prepared for an employer to tell you how many days to attend and for the number to rise.

Ask both employers the same three questions before accepting: who pays for the home office, who can end the arrangement, and what was said to the last person who asked. The answers are more reliable than any policy page, and considerably more entertaining.
