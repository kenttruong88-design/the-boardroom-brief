# Argentina and Peru Have Achieved Gender Parity in One Respect: Both Disappoint Women Efficiently

🇦🇷 Argentina · 🇵🇪 Peru

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Argentina has elected a female president twice and sits 37th out of 148 countries in the World Economic Forum's latest gender gap index. Peru has produced a generation of women who, on average, have spent more years in school than the men they work beside. Neither fact has done much for the payslip. In both countries women earn roughly a quarter less than men, and in both the executive floor remains a place where women are visiting, not living. The difference lies in the style of the disappointment, which the relocating professional would be wise to understand before signing anything.

## Argentina: A Feminist Movement With a Spreadsheet Problem

According to the World Economic Forum's 2025 Global Gender Gap Report, as reported by Mercado, Argentina ranks 37th of 148 countries with a score of 0.762, down five places in a year. The overall figure flatters the economy. On the economic participation subindex Argentina scores 0.666, which places it 93rd, and on estimated earned income it ranks 102nd. This is the sort of result a country achieves by educating women excellently and then declining to pay them accordingly.

Domestic statistics are no gentler. Using official data from the third quarter of 2025, the think tank CEPA found that men's incomes were 27 to 29 percent higher than women's, and about 40 percent higher among informal salaried workers. Women made up 64.2 percent of the lowest-income group but 37 percent of the top decile. Female unemployment was 7.4 percent against 5.9 percent for men, and 38 percent of employed women worked informally. INDEC's time-use survey adds that women spend six hours and 31 minutes a day on unpaid domestic work, men three hours and 40 minutes. A second job, in other words, with no payroll department.

Leadership is slipping. Grant Thornton found women's share of senior roles in mid-market firms fell from 34 to 32.9 percent, while the proportion of companies with all-male leadership teams rose from 4.1 to 5.7 percent; at that pace, parity arrives around 2051. Expat.com's guide to Buenos Aires work culture adds a structural footnote: decision-making in traditional local firms and large organisations is concentrated at the top, while startups and some multinational teams are flatter. A woman's prospects therefore depend heavily on which kind of employer she joins, and on whether the person at the top has noticed her. In tech, women are roughly one in five workers and earn 81 cents per male peso. The mitigating evidence is real, though: World Bank researchers note that expanded childcare in Argentina raised mothers' employment by about 7 percentage points, which proves the problem is solvable and therefore a policy choice.

## Peru: Better Educated, Faster Rising, Still Behind

Peru's picture is a more orderly form of the same story. INEI, the national statistics office, reports that women's unemployment in 2024 was 6.6 percent against 4.7 percent for men, even as female income rose 6.3 percent against 4.9 percent for men. Faster growth from a lower base is a pleasant trend and an arithmetic one. Reporting on INEI data in March 2026, El Buho put women's average income at 75.9 percent of men's, about a 25 percent gap, in line with the Instituto Peruano de Economia's estimate of 24 percent. A regional report cited in the same article gives 11.9 percent, which suggests the answer depends heavily on whom you ask and what you adjust for.

The research explains why adjustment matters. A 2025 article in the US Bureau of Labor Statistics' Monthly Labor Review notes that Peruvian women's average schooling overtook men's from 2013, reaching 18.0 years against 17.4 in 2019. It also cites earlier work finding the raw gap roughly doubled from 6 to 12 percent between 2007 and 2011, and a Defensoria del Pueblo study attributing 60 percent of the gap to direct discrimination. More education, in other words, has not translated into proportionate pay.

At the top, the figures are blunter. According to the PAR Aequales 2025 ranking, women hold 15 percent of CEO positions in Peru, against a Latin American average of 24 percent. The World Economic Forum's 2025 report listed Peru among countries whose gender parity score fell by more than two percentage points in a year. Peru has a good story for HR to tell and, so far, a thin one for the shareholders' meeting. Context matters here too. INEI counted 17.3 million employed Peruvians in 2024, and in Lima and Callao informality still stood at 56.9 percent, meaning more than half of the capital's working life happens outside the protections that would make a pay gap visible, let alone fixable. A gap you cannot measure is a gap nobody has to close.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, Argentina has the louder politics and Peru the quieter ceilings, and neither has found a way to pay women the same for the same hour. Argentina's gap is better documented, more argued over and, per CEPA, wider among informal workers. Peru's is murkier, which is not the same as smaller: the estimates run from 12 to 25 percent depending on the study, and the 15 percent female CEO share trails the regional norm. Argentina's women are fighting a recent slide in leadership; Peru's are fighting a slow climb. The World Bank's finding on Argentine childcare is the most encouraging item in either dossier, since it shows a single policy shifting maternal employment by seven points. Peru's schooling lead shows the opposite lesson: education alone, however generous, does not pay for itself in the labour market. One country has evidence that policy works, the other evidence that patience does not.

For the newcomer, the practical divergence is in what you can expect to find out. Argentina gives you data and a debate, which makes it easier to walk into an interview with comparators. Peru gives you a rising income trend and more ambiguity, so the burden falls on you to ask directly about structures, sponsors and complaint procedures. Foreign women in both countries report a similar pattern of everyday dismissal rather than open hostility, which is harder to litigate and easier to endure for a while. Dismissal of that kind seldom appears in a statistic, which is precisely why it survives.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *BAExpats forum — a long-term foreign resident of Buenos Aires argued that Argentina feels more open to women than the United States, pointing to two women winning the presidency and a female chief executive at the national airline, and suggested that behaviour foreigners read as machismo may partly be routines locals are comfortable with.*

> *Yale Globalist — a student intern in Lima found her male co-workers friendly and never felt threatened, yet noticed women's issues were confined to one small department and was irritated by a male colleague's dismissive tone at lunch. She worried she might stop noticing.*

> *The Next Web — a writer who spent six years in Buenos Aires described networking events where her all-female team was complimented on "beauty and brains", some events turned flirtatious, and a colleague who was forcibly kissed at a pitch night was told to move on.*

> *Matador Network — a Western woman co-running a hostel in Cusco learned that local gossip assumed her male business partners and boyfriend were taking advantage of her. Her competence was being judged through her nationality and gender, not her accounts.*

> *Berkley Center, Georgetown University — a student who arrived in Argentina expecting pervasive machismo found a less consistent picture, with financially independent couples and single-mother households common, while still noting street harassment and a habit of crediting a president's success to her late husband.*

## Conclusion

If you are a woman considering either country, treat the offer letter as the beginning of due diligence, not the end. In Argentina, ask about senior-role representation, the salary grid and flexible hours, and use the available data as a negotiating comparator. In Peru, ask for the pay structure, find out who sponsors women into management, and prefer formal employers over the large informal economy that surrounds them. In both, keep your own written record of agreements.

The honest summary is that neither country will pay you less because you are foreign. They will do it because you are a woman, and they will be perfectly courteous about it.
