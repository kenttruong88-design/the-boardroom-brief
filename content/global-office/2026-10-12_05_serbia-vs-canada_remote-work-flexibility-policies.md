**Serbia Built an Entire Visa Category Around Never Making You Come Into an Office. Canada Just Spent 2026 Ordering Everyone Back In.**

**🇷🇸 Serbia · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Serbia will hand a foreign remote worker a one-year residence permit on the explicit condition that they never work for a Serbian company — the whole point is that your paycheck, and your Zoom calls, stay pointed somewhere else entirely. Canada, meanwhile, spent the first half of 2026 doing the opposite with real institutional force: Ontario's public service back in-office five days a week as of January 5th, Alberta's following in February, and the federal government ordering executives in full-time by May and everyone else to four days a week by July. One country is actively recruiting people to work remotely within its borders. The other is actively un-inviting the people already doing it. Pick your relocation accordingly.

### Serbia: The Country That Legislated You Out of Its Own Office

Serbia's temporary residence permit for remote workers, introduced in 2023, is almost comically precise about what it wants: proof of employment or freelance income from a foreign entity, a minimum of roughly $42,000 a year, valid health insurance, and an explicit rule that working for a Serbian company disqualifies you entirely. It is, in effect, an immigration policy built to import foreign currency and international client relationships without importing competition for local jobs — a neat trick that only works because Belgrade and Novi Sad already have the coworking spaces, fast internet, and English fluency to make remote work genuinely pleasant day to day.

The flip side, which the government's own marketing understandably skips, is what the domestic job market looks like for people who don't qualify for that arrangement. One longtime resident advising newcomers on an expat forum summed up the local outlook bluntly: high salaries are "very hard to get" inside Serbia itself, and his actual recommendation to arriving professionals was to find a foreign company willing to pay from outside the country rather than compete for domestic wages. Stay past 183 days in a given year, meanwhile, and you trigger Serbian tax residency regardless of your visa category — a detail that turns "digital nomad" from a permanent lifestyle into something you have to actively manage on a calendar. Hofstede Insights scores Serbia at roughly 86 on power distance, among the highest in Europe, which maps onto a more formally hierarchical domestic workplace culture than the footloose, laptop-on-a-terrace image the residence permit is selling to outsiders.

### Canada: Remote Work's Reversal, Told in Specific Dates

Canada spent the pandemic years as one of the most remote-friendly large economies anywhere, and the retreat since has been unusually well-documented, date by date. Statistics Canada figures show the remote-work share of the workforce falling from 22.4% in 2022 to 17.4% in 2025 — a drop of roughly 790,000 workers — while average daily commute time now sits at 53.4 minutes, highest in years. The federal government's own core public service, remote-friendly as recently as 2024 at a minimum of three office days a week, now requires executives in five days by May 2026 and everyone else at four days by July. Ontario's provincial public servants were ordered back five days a week starting January 5th; Alberta followed in February. Private employers piled on alongside government: BMO, Scotiabank, and RBC all moved staff to four in-office days, and Amazon's corporate staff were told to show up five days starting January 2nd.

The pushback has been loud and specific, not quiet or diffuse. OPSEU/SEFPO president JP Hornick, representing roughly 40,000 Ontario public servants, called the mandate "a slap in the face to the very workers who kept the OPS running" through the pandemic. CUPE separately estimated the affected agency would need at least two-thirds more office space than it currently has to physically accommodate the order, and Ontario Health atHome workers pointed out their employer had "sold off many of the office buildings" years ago and simply doesn't have the room. Not every province is moving in lockstep, either — Manitoba, British Columbia, and New Brunswick have kept more flexible hybrid rules, and Hofstede Insights scores Canada at roughly 80 on individualism against Serbia's 25, a gap that may help explain why Canadian workers are mounting organized, public, union-backed resistance to a policy shift that, in a more collectivist or hierarchical culture, might simply be absorbed without much visible fight.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't remote-friendly versus office-first — it's that Serbia is optimizing its remote-work policy around people who were never going to compete for local jobs anyway, while Canada is reversing a remote-work culture that millions of its own citizens had already built their lives around. Serbia's digital nomads are, almost by legal definition, economically separate from the domestic labor market; their presence doesn't displace anyone, which is exactly why the country can afford to be generous about it. Canada's return-to-office wave is the opposite kind of policy entirely — an attempt to claw back commercial office occupancy, commuter tax bases, and managerial visibility from a workforce that had already proven, for several years running, it didn't strictly need any of that to function.

There's a sharper irony for anyone actually relocating. Move to Serbia as a remote worker and the entire legal apparatus is designed to leave you alone — no local employer, no local manager, no mandate to show up anywhere. Move to Canada in 2026 expecting the reputation it earned in 2021, and you may find the flexible job you accepted has quietly become a four-or-five-day office commitment by the time you've unpacked. The paperwork in Serbia is more complicated to obtain. The outcome, once you have it, is far more stable than what a lot of "remote" roles in Canada currently guarantee.

### Conclusion

If flexibility is genuinely your top priority, the honest comparison isn't close: Serbia has built legal infrastructure specifically to let you keep working remotely, indefinitely, without friction, and Canada is actively building the opposite in real time, province by province and bank by bank, through 2026. That doesn't make Canada the wrong choice — the public pushback from unions like OPSEU suggests the fight over this is far from over, and plenty of Canadian employers haven't moved at all. But if you're signing an offer letter based on a company's 2021-era remote-work reputation, read the actual current policy before you sign anything, because in Canada this year, that reputation has a real chance of being out of date by the time you start.
