**Ask a Swiss Colleague Their Salary and Watch the Room Change Temperature: Switzerland and Canada on Pay**

**🇨🇭 Switzerland · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Switzerland, a 2018 campaign called Zeigdeinenlohn ("Show your income") made headlines simply by getting 350 people to publicly post what they earn — a genuinely novel act in a culture where, per the national employers' federation, firms deciding whether to reveal wages "based on their own business culture" is treated as a feature, not a gap. In Canada, meanwhile, British Columbia now legally requires a salary range on most public job postings, and a professional-networking app built on anonymity has quietly become more useful for finding out what your Canadian colleagues actually earn than anything your employer will tell you directly.

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect salary to be treated as a private, almost taboo subject among colleagues, even close ones | Ask a Swiss colleague directly what they earn — it's one of the more reliable ways to create an awkward silence |
| Negotiate your own starting salary firmly before signing — Swiss workers are documented as less likely than German or French neighbors to seek raises afterward | Assume strong post-hire pay negotiation is the norm — Michael Page's research found Swiss employees notably less likely to push for raises than nearby countries |

### Switzerland

Switzerland's wage secrecy is less an accident than a defended cultural position. Daniella Lützelschwab of the Swiss employers' federation told local media, as cited by The Local, that individual firms deciding for themselves whether to reveal pay "does not fit with the Swiss model" when transparency is imposed from above — a position her colleague Fredy Greuter extended by arguing the current system is already gender-neutral and effective. Michael Page's own research on the subject found something structurally consistent with that defense: Swiss employees are measurably less likely than their German or French counterparts to actively seek a pay rise at all, a pattern that fits Hofstede's relatively modest 34 power-distance score paired with a notably high 58 on uncertainty avoidance — a culture comfortable with established order and wary of the friction that open wage conversation might create.

The counter-pressure, when it appears, is organized and specific rather than diffuse. The 2018 Zeigdeinenlohn campaign persuaded around 350 people — among them Michelle Wildli, a cosmetician earning 3,800 francs a month, and Francesco, a digital marketing lead earning 9,500 — to post their real income publicly, with union organizer Isabelle Lüthi framing the goal plainly: "to lay everything bare and create a new culture in terms of talking about wages," arguing that women, foreign workers, and part-time staff are disproportionately harmed by the silence. On Expat.com's Switzerland forum, one contributor summarized the practical reality newcomers actually hit when they try to negotiate: "we are not on the market" — a blunt acknowledgment that Swiss pay, for many roles, is set by established scale rather than open haggling, whatever an incoming hire might have heard about Swiss negotiation norms being flexible.

### Canada

Canada's transparency story is moving the opposite direction, driven by law rather than activist campaigns. British Columbia's Pay Transparency Act, in force since November 2023, requires public job postings open to B.C. residents to state an expected salary or wage range, explicitly disallowing open-ended phrasing like "starting at $60,000" with no ceiling. Separate reporting from Benefits and Pensions Monitor found a genuine national surge in job postings including some salary detail — even as precise, specific figures remain comparatively rare outside provinces with hard legal requirements, meaning the shift is real but uneven across the country.

Into that gap has stepped Blind, the anonymous professional-networking app, which Globe and Mail reporting described as having more than doubled its Canadian membership to roughly 100,000 verified users in a single recent year. Career coach Kyle Elliott told the paper that Blind's crowdsourced salary data tends to be more current than typical job-board figures — "people are sharing salaries from 2024 rather than 2019" — and argued it lets historically disadvantaged groups "access data that was behind closed walls." CIOCAN board member Yasemin Sezer went further, framing the platform's popularity itself as an indictment of existing law: "why are we still hiding salaries in an organization?" Recruiter Travis O'Rourke offered the necessary caveat — anonymity produces honesty but also volume, since "people feel confident stating their true salary on the platform but that also creates a lot of noise." The Globe's reporting also described an unnamed Toronto-based Google software developer who used Blind's data to discover how underpaid he was relative to U.S. peers, and relocated as a direct result — a concrete example of transparency changing an actual career decision, not just a statistic.

### The Reckoning

What makes this pairing genuinely interesting is that Switzerland and Canada sit close together on several Hofstede dimensions — both comparatively low on power distance (34 and 39), both solidly individualist (68 and 80) — yet have produced almost opposite default postures on pay disclosure. The difference seems to hinge less on hierarchy or individualism than on each country's tolerance for ambiguity and its relationship to top-down rule-setting: Switzerland's high uncertainty-avoidance score lines up with a preference for letting individual firms decide quietly, while Canada's more fragmented, province-by-province legal patchwork has let legislation — however unevenly enforced — do the work that cultural consensus hasn't.

The practical irony for an incoming professional is that Canada's "more transparent" reputation is legally real but geographically conditional, while Switzerland's reputation for secrecy coexists with, and partly motivates, a genuinely organized activist counter-movement that simply hasn't won yet. Neither country's current state should be mistaken for its endpoint: Canada's pay-transparency laws are visibly expanding province by province, and Switzerland's employers' federation is explicitly playing defense against pressure it clearly feels, rather than ignoring it.

Worth noting too is who actually benefits first from each country's current arrangement. In Switzerland, the defenders of secrecy are, by their own framing, the employers; the beneficiaries of change, per union organizer Isabelle Lüthi, would disproportionately be women, foreign workers, and part-timers — precisely the groups with the least existing leverage to negotiate individually. In Canada, the early beneficiaries of transparency have skewed toward people with the technical savvy and professional networks to find and use a platform like Blind in the first place, which is its own quieter inequity sitting underneath the country's more flattering reputation.

### Conclusion

If knowing what you're worth matters more to you than most things in a new job, the two countries demand different strategies: in Switzerland, do your homework before you sign, because no one is going to volunteer a comparison afterward, campaign or no campaign; in Canada, check the legal requirements in your specific province and consider a platform like Blind for a second opinion, since the law's protection is real but patchy. The quietly funny part is that both approaches end up in the same place — you, alone, doing research your employer would rather you didn't — just with one country making you ask a stranger online, and the other making you read a statute first.
