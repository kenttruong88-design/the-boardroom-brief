# Suit Up in Pakistan, Strip Down in the Netherlands: A Relocating Professional's Guide to Dressing for Work

**🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇳🇱 Netherlands**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Somewhere in Lahore, a junior banker is ironing a crease into trousers that will spend the day under 40-degree heat and a cotton-poly blazer. Somewhere in Amsterdam, a senior partner at the same multinational is locking her bicycle outside the office in sneakers she will not change out of before her 9 a.m. client call. Both of them consider themselves appropriately dressed, and both of them are right, which is precisely the problem for anyone trying to pack one wardrobe for two very different definitions of professionalism. The Netherlands has spent decades quietly dismantling the tie, while Pakistan's civil service keeps issuing fresh memos about what its own staff are permitted to wear. Neither country is more "modern" than the other on this point; they have simply arrived at opposite answers to the same question of how clothing signals seriousness.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a tailored shalwar kameez or pressed Western formalwear for client-facing roles — both read as serious | Wear shorts in public or office, under any circumstances, heat included |
| Carry a shawl or dupatta even over long sleeves, as a modesty reserve for women across sectors | Assume a single "formal" option exists for women in eastern wear — most report improvising it themselves |
| Expect multinationals, large banks, and startups to accept Western business dress | Expect the same latitude at Islamic banks, some brokerages, or boards with older leadership |
| Switch to shalwar kameez and sandals on Fridays, even in diplomatically adjacent offices | Bare shoulders, wear sleeveless tops, or show legs above the knee in most corporate settings |
| Default to "business casual" as the diplomatic-corps baseline, and escalate to suits for government meetings | Treat local dress as a downgrade — it draws compliments, not demerits |

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat smart casual as the safe daily default — blazer optional, tie nearly extinct | Show up to a job interview in shorts; it is the one rule that survived the great Dutch casualization |
| Dress to your sector: suits still hold in Amsterdam's Zuidas financial district | Overdress on day one — juniors who out-formal senior leadership stand out, not up |
| Expect colleagues, including partners, in jeans and sneakers at tech and creative firms | Assume a suit signals competence in a creative industry; it can read as having misread the room |
| Start emails formal (surname, "kind regards") and mirror the tone that comes back | Send corporate correspondence over WhatsApp, especially in legal or client-facing work |
| Expect the office to empty by 4 or 5 p.m. — formality here is as much about schedule as fabric | Mistake casual dress for casual standards; direct, blunt meeting style persists regardless of footwear |

### Pakistan

Pakistan's workplace dress code is best understood as a layered compromise between global business norms, religious and social modesty expectations, and an unusually active state. According to the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, female labour force participation sits at roughly one in five working-age women, among the lowest rates the World Bank tracks in South Asia — a figure that shapes which offices even have an established women's dress norm to begin with, since so many professional spaces remain overwhelmingly male by default. Where that norm does exist, it is contested rather than settled: a Profit by Pakistan Today column by business journalist Ariba Shahid argues that men's office dress in Pakistan borrows cleanly from Western formalwear, while women's eastern-wear equivalents have no agreed formality tier, leaving many to default to the same lawn-cotton outfit they would wear running errands.

The state, meanwhile, keeps legislating the gap. Punjab's government has issued formal dress directives for its officers, and Karachi's education authorities moved in 2026 to bar teachers from wearing jeans, t-shirts, and tight-fitting clothes — the kind of institutional intervention that would strike a Dutch HR department as faintly medieval, and a Pakistani one as Tuesday. Expats fare better than the memos suggest: diplomatic-post debriefs collected by TalesMag's Real Post Report describe Islamabad as "business or business casual" day to day, with men commonly switching to shalwar kameez and sandals on Fridays, while Karachi postings lean more conservative, advising women to keep shoulders to ankles covered and to carry a shawl even over long sleeves.

The throughline is that formality in Pakistan is rarely optional and rarely just about the office. Dress reads as a statement about modesty, class, and institutional seriousness all at once, which is why a government bank and a Lahore tech startup can sit two kilometres apart and expect entirely different wardrobes from entirely different employees.

### Netherlands

The Netherlands has moved in the opposite direction, and it has the data to prove it is serious about relaxation. According to figures compiled from CBS Netherlands and Eurostat, roughly half the Dutch workforce works part-time, including about seven in ten working women — the highest part-time employment share in the OECD, and a labour market built around flexible, individually negotiated hours rather than a fixed office presence to dress for. Hofstede Insights, which publishes individually scored cultural profiles for the Netherlands but not for Pakistan, places the Dutch at a Power Distance score of 38 against a global norm closer to 60, meaning the boss in jeans is not an aberration but the expected shape of authority.

That egalitarianism shows up directly in wardrobes. DutchNews.nl, reporting on a 2019 dress-code controversy sparked by a government ministry's advice on hot-weather office wear, quoted recruiter Derk Rijntjes of Darwin Recruitment noting that while suits persist in Amsterdam's Zuidas financial district, ties have all but disappeared over the past several years: "We are getting less formal." Etiquette consultant Lilian Woltering, in the same piece, cautioned that the loosening has limits — dress for your clients, she advised, because a junior who shows up sharper than the partners invites more questions than respect, and a suit worn in the wrong creative context can read as a cultural misread rather than effort.

What survives the casualization is oddly specific: nobody interviewed thought shorts belonged at a job interview, hot summer or not, which suggests the Dutch have simply relocated the line rather than erased it.

### The Reckoning

Compare the two countries on paper and the contrast looks almost designed for a case study: Pakistan's Hofstede-style profile of higher power distance and stronger uncertainty avoidance against the Netherlands' famously flat, low-ceremony one, translated directly into two different theories of what clothing is for. In Pakistan, dress still functions as a visible marker of hierarchy, modesty, and institutional seriousness, actively reinforced by employers, boards, and occasionally government ministries. In the Netherlands, dress has been deliberately stripped of most of that signalling, to the point that showing up overdressed can cost you more credibility than showing up underdressed.

Neither system is more relaxed in practice, just differently distributed: Pakistan concentrates its formality in the wardrobe and loosens elsewhere, while the Netherlands concentrates its informality in the wardrobe and tightens elsewhere, in scheduling, directness, and a labour market built around part-time hours rather than full-time face time. A relocating professional who reads only the clothing half of either country's culture will misjudge the other half badly.

### Conclusion

If you are moving for work, pack for the country you are actually going to, not the one you assume from its visa requirements or its airport. In Pakistan, budget for formality: a tailored outfit for client meetings, a modesty reserve for humid-weather compliance, and patience for sector-by-sector variation that no single packing list will capture. In the Netherlands, budget for the opposite problem: resisting the urge to overdress, reading your specific office rather than the national stereotype, and accepting that "less formal" does not mean "less exacting."

Either way, the clothes are never really the point; they are just the most visible place each country decided to put its rules. Pack a blazer for both postings — in one you will wear it to a bank, and in the other you will wear it to the one interview where, mysteriously, everyone still agrees that shorts are a bridge too far.
