# The Baby Question: Taiwan Will Subsidize It, Peru's Grandmother Will Raise It

**🇹🇼 Taiwan · 🇵🇪 Peru**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Taiwan has one of the lowest birth rates on earth and is responding the way an engineering culture responds to any hard problem: with subsidies, flexible leave increments, and on-site childcare built directly into semiconductor campuses. Peru has never needed a national baby-subsidy strategy, because the extended family already runs one, informally, for free, one grandmother at a time. Both approaches produce children. Only one produces a line item in your relocation contract.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇼 Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your employer specifically whether they offer on-site or subsidized childcare — some tech firms do, most companies don't | Don't assume the national parental leave allowance (up to two years, 80% of insured salary for the first six months) applies automatically without paperwork through the Bureau of Labor Insurance |
| Confirm whether your maternity leave is paid at 100% (six months' tenure) or 50% (under six months) before you plan around it | Don't expect your spouse's dependent visa to include automatic work rights — check separately or risk becoming an unplanned stay-at-home parent |
| Budget realistically — expat families without a school allowance often need NT$300,000+ gross monthly in Taipei | Don't rely on public childcare spots opening up quickly — availability is genuinely scarce |
| Ask about the new flexible daily-increment leave rules that took effect January 2026 | Don't assume returning from leave resets your seniority or project assignments — ask directly what happens to your role |
| Look at whether your employer is one that visibly markets "parenthood won't hurt your career" (some semiconductor firms do this explicitly) | Don't underestimate how hierarchical, face-time-heavy office culture can collide with reduced hours post-leave |

#### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan around 98 days of fully paid maternity leave (49 before, 49 after birth), extendable for complications | Don't assume a formal "parental leave" category exists beyond maternity/paternity — Peruvian law does not provide one |
| Use your 10 days of paid paternity leave deliberately — it can start at birth or hospital discharge | Don't expect government-funded childcare unless your household qualifies for Cuna Más (aimed at low-income families) |
| Ask your employer about lactation room requirements — mandatory if 20+ women aged 15-49 work there | Don't assume a nanny or daycare is the default — grandparents and extended family remain the primary informal childcare system |
| Factor in real family involvement — over a third of Peruvian children co-reside with grandparents at some point | Don't schedule around a nuclear-family assumption — many households function multi-generationally by design, not by hardship |
| Negotiate flexibility explicitly if you don't have extended family nearby — you won't have the built-in support locals do | Don't assume urban, professional-class norms (nannies, hired help) apply outside Lima's wealthier districts |

Taiwan's approach to parenthood is a policy response to a demographic emergency. Births fell to roughly 129,897 in 2025, down from 134,856 the year before, and the government's answer has been an expanding menu of supports: a monthly NT$5,000 subsidy per child, more flexible parental leave that can now be taken in single-day increments for the first 30 days, and repeated public messaging that having children shouldn't derail a career. Some employers have taken this further than the law requires — TSMC, for instance, offers up to 20 weeks of paid maternity leave for a third child, on-site childcare near its fabs, and childbirth subsidies, and the results are visible: TSMC employees reportedly have a noticeably higher birth rate than the national average, occasionally described in local coverage as the "TSMC baby" phenomenon. But policy generosity coexists with an office culture that Al Jazeera and other outlets have described as deeply hierarchical and face-time-oriented, which means the generous leave on paper and the lived experience of using it without career cost can diverge sharply depending on your employer.

Peru solves the same problem without much government infrastructure at all. There's no dedicated "parental leave" category in Peruvian labor law — maternity leave (98 days, fully paid through social security) and paternity leave (10 days) are the extent of the statutory framework, and public childcare support through the Cuna Más program targets low-income households specifically rather than working professionals generally. What fills the gap is culture rather than policy: Peruvian family structure runs on extended-family involvement as a default, not a backup plan, and grandmothers frequently take on daytime childcare responsibility as a matter of course. For a foreign hire without that network nearby, this is the least obvious relocation cost — the system was never designed to substitute for what's normally an unpaid family arrangement, so hired childcare has to be built from scratch, at real cost, in a market where it isn't the norm.

**The Reckoning:** Taiwan is trying to engineer its way out of a birth-rate crisis with subsidies and legislated flexibility, treating parenthood as a policy variable to be optimized. Peru never needed to, because its social architecture already assumes multiple generations under one roof or in close proximity, sharing the work. The irony cuts both ways for a relocating professional: Taiwan will hand you a generous, well-documented leave policy on your first day, but you'll be navigating it inside a workplace culture that still prizes visible hours at your desk; Peru will hand you a much thinner legal framework, but the informal safety net around you — if you have any local family or the money to replicate it — is deeper than almost anything codified into Taiwanese law. Hofstede's individualism scores put both countries in similar territory (Taiwan 17, Peru 20, both strongly collectivist), which explains why in both places, "who takes care of the baby" is treated as a family or company obligation rather than a purely private one — they just resolve that shared obligation through opposite mechanisms, one bureaucratic, one familial.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone budgeting for an expat family in Taipei without a school allowance concluded they needed at least NT$300,000 gross per month to make the numbers work, and that anyone offered less should seriously consider a lower-cost city outside the capital instead.*

> *Quora — A response to "How expensive is it to raise a child in Peru?" noted that the sticker cost looks low compared to North America or Europe, until you price in private schooling and a nanny — the two costs that replace what an extended family would otherwise absorb for free.*

> *Blind — In a thread titled "TSMC's US engineers are babies," engineers who'd transferred between TSMC's Taiwan and Arizona operations described being genuinely startled by how much more built-in the Taiwan site's childcare and parental support felt compared to what they'd budgeted for and negotiated individually in the US.*

> *Expat.com (Taiwan guide) — A guide for relocating families noted that public childcare places are scarce enough that most expat parents end up choosing between private centers, at real cost, or leaning on family members if any happen to be nearby — with the trailing spouse's separate work-permit requirement often deciding which option is realistic.*

### Conclusion

The practical question to ask before moving isn't just "what's the leave policy" but "who is actually going to be present for the 9-to-5 gap." In Taiwan, that answer increasingly comes from the state and, at better employers, from the company itself. In Peru, it still comes from your family, and if you don't have one nearby, you'll be paying market rate to manually reconstruct what locals get for free. Priya's honest read: check the childcare line item before you check the salary line item, because in both countries, it's the one that will actually determine whether the job works with a small child attached to it.
