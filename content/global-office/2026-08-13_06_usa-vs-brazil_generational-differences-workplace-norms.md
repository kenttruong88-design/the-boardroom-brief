**Two Countries, Two Very Different Youth Problems: Generational Norms in the USA vs. Brazil**

**🇺🇸 USA · 🇧🇷 Brazil**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

By the end of 2026, Gen Z and Millennials will make up 74% of the American workforce, and only 17% of that Gen Z contingent says it's comfortable working across generational lines, versus 45% of Baby Boomers who claim no problem at all. In Brazil, the generational story is less about clashing values and more about sheer numbers: a quarter of workers aged 25 to 29 leave a job within a year, and among 18-to-24-year-olds that figure jumps to 38.2%. One country has a communication problem between generations; the other has a churn problem within one.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Spell out expectations explicitly across generational lines — assume nothing is obvious | Assume "professionalism" means the same thing to a 24-year-old and a 58-year-old colleague |
| Offer regular, direct feedback if managing younger staff | Equate visible hours at a desk with actual output |
| Ask what "loyalty" means to the specific person you're managing or being managed by | Assume Boomer-era tenure expectations apply to Gen Z hires |
| Invest in cross-generational communication training if available | Write off an entire generation's concerns as generational entitlement |
| Recognize AI fluency skews younger — use it as a two-way teaching opportunity | Assume older colleagues can't or won't adapt to new tools |

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect real churn among junior hires — over a third leave within a year | Take early departures personally as a management failure alone |
| Discuss pay and growth path openly with young hires — it's their top complaint | Assume loyalty will develop automatically over time |
| Recognize that 83.5% of employed youth are in entry-level roles by design | Expect fast promotion timelines to match what candidates may hope for |
| Build real networking and mentorship access for junior staff | Underestimate how much "lack of networking" (32% cite it) blocks advancement |
| Note youth unemployment concerns are real even amid falling headline rates | Assume young employees feel secure just because they're employed |

America's generational story is a story about values colliding inside a single open floor plan. Baby boomers, per workplace research compiled by O.C. Tanner and Korn Ferry, still largely measure dedication by hours visibly logged at a desk after closing time; younger employees operate on the premise that output matters more than visibility. The friction shows up in blunt numbers: a ResumeBuilder survey found 74% of managers describe Gen Z as challenging to manage, driven largely by communication style rather than actual work quality, and only 26% of employees overall report real "generational synergy" at their workplace. Technology divides along familiar lines too — 77% of millennials and 68% of Gen Z say adapting to new workplace tech is easy, versus 56% of boomers — which cuts against the old assumption that youth simply defers to institutional experience.

Brazil's generational tension is structural rather than attitudinal. The country logged 13.9 million young employed people in early 2026, with youth unemployment roughly half its 2021 peak, but the jobs those young people are landing are overwhelmingly entry-level: 83.5% work as salespeople, cashiers, receptionists, or administrative assistants, and 7.8 million earn up to one-and-a-half times the minimum wage. Nearly six in ten Gen Z Brazilians describe the labor market as closed and challenging, citing low salaries (48%), the demand for prior experience (39%), high competition (35%), and lack of networking (32%) as the core obstacles — which helps explain the churn: when a job isn't paying enough or leading anywhere, staying isn't loyalty, it's just inertia. Brazil's median age has climbed to roughly 35, up from 18 in 1960, meaning even a country famous for its youthfulness is aging into a more conventional demographic profile, with the labor-market squeeze on young workers likely to intensify rather than ease.

The Reckoning: America's generational gap is philosophical — different cohorts inside stable jobs disagreeing about what work should feel like. Brazil's generational gap is economic — young workers churning through unstable entry-level jobs because the value proposition simply isn't there yet, not because they've rejected the concept of a career. An American manager frustrated by a Gen Z report that "looked like a lot of work" is having a fundamentally different argument than a Brazilian employer watching a 22-year-old leave after eight months for a job that pays 20% closer to market rate. Both problems get labeled "generational," but only one is really about generations; the other is about wages.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *reddit.com/r/antiwork — A widely discussed post described a manager whose Gen Z cashiers all quit over what the workers considered a reasonable scheduling boundary, after which the manager declared she'd only hire "baby boomers" going forward because, in her words, Gen Z "doesn't know what work actually means" — a post that drew heavy pushback from commenters who read it as a management failure dressed up as a generational one.*

> *teamblind.com — In a thread titled "Gen Z report driving me insane," a manager described frustration not with the substance of a junior employee's work but with its tone and structure, illustrating the broader finding that much of the generational friction reported by managers is communication style rather than actual competence.*

> *Quora — Someone answering how to bridge the generational divide at work advised treating the gap as a communication-training problem rather than a character problem, and suggested structured mentorship pairing older and younger staff explicitly rather than hoping proximity alone would build understanding.*

> *Quora — A respondent describing the Brazilian job market for young graduates said first jobs commonly pay around 20% below market rate, and that landing a decent role depends heavily on personal connections — practical information the respondent said they wished someone had told them before they started applying.*

### Conclusion

If you're managing across generations in the U.S., the honest advice is to stop assuming your own generation's norms are neutral — ask directly what "respect," "loyalty," and "hard work" mean to the specific person in front of you, because those words don't mean the same thing to everyone in the room anymore. If you're navigating Brazil's youth labor market, the honest advice is to treat churn as a pricing signal, not a character flaw — young workers there are usually leaving because the math doesn't work, not because they've abandoned the idea of sticking around. Priya's honest take: America's generations are arguing about culture because their jobs are mostly stable enough to argue; Brazil's aren't arguing yet, because too many of the jobs aren't stable enough to be worth the fight.
