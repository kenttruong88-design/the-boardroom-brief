# Spain Will Keep You at Your Desk Until 8pm. Ireland Will Send You Home at 4 — After Five Minutes of Small Talk You Didn't Ask For.

**🇪🇸 Spain · 🇮🇪 Ireland**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Spanish office and an Irish office can both look, on the surface, relaxed — long lunches in Madrid, banter-filled meetings in Dublin — and both impressions are misleading in opposite directions. Spain's leisurely two-hour lunch is not leisure; workers routinely return to their desks and stay until 7 or 8pm, logging some of the longest average hours in the EU. Ireland's chatty, joke-cracking meeting culture is not informality without limits; bosses who leave by 4pm are fiercely protective of the line between work and everything else. The rule of thumb: in Spain, presence is the currency; in Ireland, boundaries are.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect the workday to run well past 6pm even if the pace midday feels slow | Assume long lunch breaks mean a short workday — total hours are among the highest in the EU |
| Use informal address (tú) even with senior staff, while still respecting clear hierarchy in decision-making | Confuse informal tone with informal structure — reporting lines and authority remain clearly defined |
| Build relationships over the long lunch or coffee break — it's part of how trust is established | Try to "optimize" the lunch break by eating at your desk; it reads as antisocial, not efficient |
| Bring patience for bureaucracy and paperwork — Spain has a five-century head start on it | Expect fast decision-making on formal processes; hierarchy and documentation slow things deliberately |
| Separate work life and personal life cleanly once you're off the clock | Expect colleagues to respond to work messages in the evening — off-hours are genuinely off |

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget five minutes of small talk before getting to business in any meeting | Jump straight into the agenda — it reads as rude, not efficient |
| Engage with political or current-events chat at the office; it's normal, not risky | Assume topics considered taboo in US offices are off-limits here too |
| Respect the 40–45 hour week as a real ceiling, not an aspiration | Expect senior staff to still be reachable after 4 or 5pm — many treat that boundary seriously |
| Use self-deprecating humor; it lands well and signals you're not taking yourself too seriously | Lean into visible self-promotion — it can come across as try-hard in a culture that prizes modesty |
| Give new hires time to settle before expecting full social integration | Take a quiet first few weeks personally — some describe a "soft cold shoulder" that thaws over time |

---

Spain's defining feature to outsiders is what locals call presentismo — a culture of visible time-in-office that persists independent of actual output. According to Eurostat and Statista figures, Spaniards logged around 1,665 hours annually as recently as the mid-2010s, more than workers in Germany or France, and weekly hours for the self-employed have run above 43. The Local Spain has covered this for over a decade: the long midday break creates a false impression of a short day, when in fact most employees return afterward and stay until 7 or 8pm. Hofstede's data puts Spain's Power Distance score at 57, notably higher than many of its northern European neighbors, reflecting a workplace where hierarchy is clear even as day-to-day address and manner stay informal — Quora threads on Spanish corporate life describe this precisely as a mix of "clear hierarchy and forced equality" that trips up newcomers expecting one or the other, not both at once.

Ireland sits almost at the opposite pole on structure, if not on warmth. Ireland's Hofstede Power Distance score of 28 signals a far flatter, more egalitarian workplace, and OECD-adjacent labor data puts average Irish working hours around 34–35 per week — meaningfully shorter than Spain's. Ireland has also been recognized in recent work-life balance rankings for strong performance on both working hours and broader quality-of-life measures. But the flatness comes with its own etiquette: meetings open with unhurried small talk as standard practice, and skipping straight to the agenda can read as brusque rather than professional. Political and social topics that would be carefully avoided in many corporate cultures come up routinely. None of this is a lack of boundaries, though — bosses who leave at 4pm are protecting a line, not slacking.

The reckoning: both countries resist the idea that visible effort equals real effort, but they resolve it in opposite directions. Spain keeps hours long and hierarchy formal while keeping tone casual; Ireland keeps hours short and hierarchy flat while keeping ritual — the small talk, the banter — mandatory in its own way. A newcomer who reads Spain's relaxed tone as low intensity will be blindsided by an 8pm meeting. A newcomer who reads Ireland's flat structure and jokes as a free-for-all will misjudge how firmly some colleagues guard their personal time.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — An employee at a major tech company's Dublin office described experiencing "soft cold shoulders" as a newcomer, noting that Irish colleagues were unfailingly polite in meetings but slower than expected to include a new hire socially, with real integration taking a few months rather than a few weeks.*

> *Quora — A foreign professional working in Spain described the disorienting mix of everyone, including junior staff, addressing the boss informally by first name, while still deferring completely to that same boss on any actual decision — calling it a paradox no orientation session had explained clearly.*

> *The Local Spain — A contributor noted that new arrivals often mistake the long Spanish lunch break for a shorter workday, only to realize months in that colleagues simply return to the office afterward and stay well past 7pm, making the total hours longer than back home, not shorter.*

> *r/expats — Someone who moved from Madrid to Dublin for a corporate role said the biggest adjustment wasn't the language, it was learning that a boss leaving promptly at 4pm wasn't a sign of low commitment but a firm, culturally normal boundary that everyone else respected without comment.*

> *Quora — An American who relocated to Dublin wrote that the requirement of small talk before every meeting felt performative at first, until they realized skipping it entirely with a new Irish colleague read as cold, and that the ritual itself was doing real relationship-building work.*

### Conclusion

If your priority is a shorter, more clearly bounded workday with a flatter hierarchy, Ireland delivers that structurally — you'll just need patience for the ritual small talk and a slower social thaw. If you're comfortable with longer hours and a clearer chain of command dressed in casual manners, Spain rewards those who can separate the day's length from its intensity and genuinely switch off once they leave. The version of this that catches people out is assuming either country's tone tells you anything about its actual rules. If a friend asked me over a drink: go to Ireland if you want your evenings back on paper as well as in practice; go to Spain if you can live with long days as long as nobody expects an email reply after 9pm.
