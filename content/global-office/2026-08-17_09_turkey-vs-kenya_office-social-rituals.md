**Two Cups, Same Rule: Never Refuse the Tea in Turkey or Kenya**

**🇹🇷 Turkey · 🇰🇪 Kenya**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Turkey drinks more tea per capita than any country on earth, over three kilograms a year per person, and a meeting that opens with çay and fifteen minutes of talk about football is not stalling — it is doing the actual relationship-building work that makes the business part possible afterward. Kenya runs the identical ritual on chai instead: offices, matatu stages, and negotiating tables all pause for it, and declining a colleague's offer reads less like a dietary preference and more like a small social refusal. Different leaf, same lesson — the drink is never really about the drink.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇷 Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept an offer of çay — declining is a mild social misstep, not a neutral choice | Don't skip the small talk before a meeting; family, food, and football come first |
| Treat lunch and after-work meals with colleagues as relationship infrastructure, not optional extras | Don't decline a dinner invitation from a business associate — it's considered impolite |
| Show up on time; punctuality signals respect even within a generally relationship-first culture | Don't rush past the tea-and-conversation phase to "get to business" — it undermines trust rather than saving time |
| Use titles and formal address until a colleague signals otherwise | Don't assume warmth and hospitality mean informality — hierarchy still structures the relationship underneath |
| Expect tea to appear in negotiations and meetings alike — its presence signals the host is ready for real conversation | Don't treat a tea break as downtime you can multitask through — it's the social work itself |

#### 🇰🇪 Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the shared lunch hour in a common space rather than eating at your desk | Don't decline chai when it's offered — it's a hospitality ritual, not a beverage question |
| Use full names and job titles when introducing or addressing senior colleagues | Don't skip greetings or small talk to jump straight into a work request |
| Expect an early start (many arrive by 5-6am) and a firm 5pm finish tied to commute timing | Don't mistake the hierarchy for coldness — warmth and formality coexist closely in Nairobi offices |
| Learn colleagues' family details and personal context — genuine familiarity is the norm, not an overstep | Don't assume junior staff will offer unprompted opinions in front of senior management |
| Treat chai breaks as a legitimate tool for defusing tense moments in a discussion | Don't schedule around lunch; the shared midday break is treated as fixed, not flexible |

---

Turkey's office rituals run on a foundation Hofstede's data makes explicit: high power distance combined with a collectivist orientation, a pairing that produces both real hierarchy and real relational warmth operating side by side. The Cultural Atlas and Commisceo Global both describe Turkish business culture as one where small talk isn't a preamble to the real conversation, it's a precondition for it — colleagues are expected to discuss family, food, and general well-being before substantive topics arise, and a business associate who skips that stage risks being read as cold or untrustworthy regardless of the quality of their actual proposal. Çay's ubiquity is the physical expression of this: tea culture guides note it appears in interviews, negotiations, and routine office breaks alike, with its very presence at a meeting signaling that the host considers the relationship ready for a genuine exchange.

Kenya's office culture layers a similarly warm, relationship-forward style onto a distinctly vertical hierarchy. Workplace guides describe Nairobi offices as places where hierarchy is taken seriously — junior staff defer visibly to senior colleagues, full names and titles matter in introductions — while simultaneously radiating a genuineness in daily interaction that outside observers consistently single out: colleagues know each other's family names, share food without much ceremony, and mark wins together in ways that read, per multiple workplace commentaries, as more organic than the team-building exercises other cultures pay consultants to manufacture. Chai occupies the same structural role çay does in Turkey — offered constantly, present at negotiations, used deliberately to defuse tense moments in a discussion — with the shared midday lunch hour functioning as a near-mandatory daily ritual rather than a scheduling convenience.

**The Reckoning**

The instructive overlap is that both cultures use a beverage ritual to do work that more individualist, low-context cultures assign to formal onboarding programs or scheduled team-building events: building trust, signaling openness, defusing conflict. The instructive difference is what sits underneath that warmth. In Turkey, the hierarchy is more explicitly acknowledged even amid the informality of shared tea — titles and formal address persist well past the point where the relationship feels genuinely warm. In Kenya, the hierarchy is, if anything, even more consistently observed in address and deference, even as the daily texture of office life — shared lunches, personal familiarity, name recognition — can feel, per multiple accounts, unusually close for a workplace this formally structured.

The practical trap for a newcomer is treating either ritual as optional once the actual work is understood. Declining tea repeatedly in Ankara, or skipping the communal lunch hour repeatedly in Nairobi, doesn't read as an efficient use of time in either culture — it reads as a quiet withdrawal from the relationship the whole office is built on, and the professional cost of that withdrawal tends to surface later, indirectly, in ways that are hard to trace back to a missed cup of tea.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Substack (Turkey culture blog, "32. Expec-tea-tion") — A long-term resident described the moment they realized declining a colleague's third or fourth tea offer in a single afternoon wasn't neutral politeness but was quietly read as standoffish, and that learning to simply accept and let it go cold was easier than trying to explain their reasoning each time.*

> *expat.com forum (Nairobi) — A foreign hire asked directly about Kenyan office culture and was told by several respondents that the shared lunch hour was effectively non-negotiable socially, even if the workload made a desk lunch tempting, because that hour was where real team cohesion happened.*

> *K24 Digital (Kenyan lifestyle outlet) — A piece on Kenyan work culture described the coexistence of a demanding, hierarchical structure with a genuinely warm daily rhythm as simultaneously remarkable and exhausting, noting that the emotional labor of constant relationship-tending was real even when it looked effortless from outside.*

> *Quora — A contributor comparing Turkish business meetings to more transactional Western equivalents noted that the fifteen minutes of tea and conversation before any agenda item wasn't wasted time at all, but functioned as a trust check that determined how candid the actual business discussion would be.*

> *The Filipino Expat (culture-shock blog) — An account of adjusting to Kenyan office norms described the early-morning start, tied to Nairobi traffic patterns, as a bigger practical adjustment than any of the social rituals — arriving at 5am to beat the commute reshaped the entire rhythm of the workday around lunch and chai breaks that followed.*

---

### Conclusion

If you're deciding between the two, the social contract is nearly identical in shape and almost entirely different in texture: both ask you to treat a hot drink and a shared meal as load-bearing parts of your job, not breaks from it, and both will quietly notice if you opt out. Turkey layers that warmth onto explicit hierarchy and formal titles that persist even after real rapport is built. Kenya pairs the same daily warmth with an early-morning rhythm and a hierarchy that shows up more in how you address people than in how close you're allowed to get. If a friend asked me over drinks: in either country, say yes to the tea — it was never really a question.
