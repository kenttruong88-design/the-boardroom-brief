**Czechia to Switzerland: Same Spreadsheet, Different Planet of Zeroes**

🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A median Czech employee takes home roughly CZK 45,523 a month, around €1,800. A median Swiss employee takes home CHF 6,788, around €7,200 — nearly four times as much, before anyone has mentioned cost of living. The Czech Statistical Office and the Swiss Federal Statistical Office agree on the numbers; what both countries agree on, bizarrely, is that you should never ask a colleague what theirs actually is.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research your market rate independently before any salary conversation. | Trust a job ad's salary range at face value — Czech postings are notoriously vague on pay. |
| Ask directly about gross versus net pay, and about tax and insurance deductions. | Assume the advertised number is what lands in your account each month. |
| Learn a second or third language if you can — it measurably raises your market value. | Assume English alone will top out your earning potential in most Czech roles. |
| Expect wage growth to lag behind rising rents and groceries, especially in Prague. | Assume an above-average salary means an above-average lifestyle once housing is paid. |
| Watch for the EU pay-transparency rules phasing in from 2027. | Expect transparency now — vague ranges remain the norm until then. |
| Negotiate, even modestly; most of your colleagues reportedly won't. | Assume a lack of visible negotiating is a lack of leverage — unemployment has stayed low for years. |

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat salary as a genuinely private topic, even with close colleagues. | Ask a Swiss coworker what they earn — it is a real, widely recognised faux pas. |
| Come to any negotiation with specific market data and a clear number in mind. | Expect to simply be offered a raise for good performance without asking. |
| Expect large companies (100+ employees) to run legally mandated pay-equity audits. | Assume those audits mean individual salaries are shared or visible to staff. |
| Benchmark offers against tech-industry sites if you're in that sector — the range is wide by canton. | Assume Zurich and Geneva pay identically, or that one employer's number is the market. |
| Shake hands, greet colleagues individually, and be punctual to the minute. | Open a gift in front of the giver, or schedule a work social event on a Sunday. |
| Accept that most of your peers won't ask for a raise this year either. | Read that silence as satisfaction — it is closer to norm-following than contentment. |

### Czechia

The headline number looks fine until you look at where it sits globally. Czechia posts one of the lowest average hourly wages among the OECD's 30 member states, at roughly €3.41, ahead of only Poland, Slovakia, and Mexico — while Czechs log some of the longest average monthly hours in the bloc, trailing only South Korea. The average gross monthly wage reached CZK 49,215 in 2025, up a healthy 7.2 percent year over year, and real wages grew faster than the EU average for a second straight year. None of that has stopped Prague rents and grocery bills from climbing faster still, which is why even above-average earners report trimming housing size or commute convenience to make the numbers work.

What makes the gap harder to close is the Czech reluctance to ask. A PwC global workforce survey found only 22 percent of Czech employees planned to request a raise this year — the lowest share of 46 countries surveyed, against a global average near 42 percent. One economist's read is blunt: Czechs are not natural negotiators, asking for more pay makes many of them visibly uncomfortable, and some employers have learned to count on exactly that. A second economist calls the pattern genuinely puzzling, since persistently low unemployment should hand workers real leverage they are mostly declining to use. Job postings do their own quiet damage here too — salary ranges in Czech ads are notoriously vague, pushing the burden of finding out what a role actually pays onto the candidate, a situation EU pay-transparency rules are due to tighten only from 2027 onward. Foreigners now make up roughly 11 percent of the Czech workforce, drawn partly by labour shortages the government is actively trying to fill, and the same vagueness that frustrates locals hits newcomers harder still, since they typically have no baseline sense of what a Czech role should pay before they arrive. Fluency in a second language beyond English — German, French, and the Nordic languages are singled out most often — measurably raises what recruiters are willing to offer, which is one of the few levers on this side of the border that doesn't require the discomfort of a direct ask.

### Switzerland

Switzerland's number is the Czech number's inversion in almost every respect. A median gross monthly salary of CHF 6,788 sits among the highest in the world, and the country's Hofstede profile backs up the general impression of calm, low-friction hierarchy: a power-distance score of just 34, alongside a comparatively high 68 on individualism, consistent with a workplace where people are expected to advocate for themselves rather than wait for a manager to notice.

Except, on salary specifically, they mostly don't. "You would never, ever talk about salary," one etiquette guide to Swiss social rules puts it flatly, describing the subject as a genuine taboo rather than a mild preference for discretion — a country with no legal wage transparency, where even close friends are expected to keep the number to themselves. Michael Page's own research backs this up with numbers: only 35 percent of Swiss employees attempted to negotiate a raise in the past year, against 48 percent across the EU, and of those who tried, only 37 percent actually got one, a lower success rate than the EU average of 47 percent. The one structural counterweight is Switzerland's 2021 Federal Act on Gender Equality, which forces companies with 100 or more employees to run independently verified pay-equity audits and share the aggregate results with staff — a rare crack of transparency in an otherwise famously closed system, and one that addresses the gender gap specifically rather than opening up salaries generally. Geography adds its own wrinkle: Zurich and Geneva both run genuinely high cost-of-living premiums on top of already-high wages, so a headline salary that looks enormous next to Prague's still has to clear rent and health insurance that are proportionately steep in their own right, and a newcomer who negotiates on the Zurich number alone, without pricing the canton they'll actually be living in, can end up less comfortable than the raw figure suggests.

### The Reckoning

Put side by side, Czechia and Switzerland arrive at the same quiet handshake — neither country's workforce negotiates much — by two entirely different roads. Czech employees mostly don't ask because asking feels culturally uncomfortable and the job market, despite genuinely favouring workers, hasn't taught them that leverage exists. Swiss employees mostly don't ask because the culture treats the number itself as a closely guarded secret, discussed with roughly the candour most people reserve for their medical history.

The gap this produces is almost comic in scale. A Google engineer in Zurich can expect a base salary north of CHF 190,000 before equity, according to specific figures traded on compensation forums for that exact role; a Czech employee earning the national median would need to work for well over four years to match a single year of that base pay. Both countries' employees, when you ask them directly, describe a job market that technically favours them — low unemployment in Czechia, among the highest wages on the planet in Switzerland — and both decline, at roughly the same modest rate, to actually cash that leverage in.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — A software engineer weighing a Google Zurich L5 offer got conflicting reads from commenters: one called the stock grant "a low ball" worth 20 to 30 percent less than a comparable Apple Zurich offer, while another pointed out that Zurich RSU grants run at roughly a quarter to a half of equivalent California grants, a gap neither commenter treated as negotiable without a competing offer in hand.*

> *Blind — A nine-year software engineer comparing a Geneva role's total compensation to a Swiss tech offer got a blunt tactical note back: without a competing offer, the only real leverage is naming a specific target number with a stated reason attached, since vague requests for "market rate" tend to go nowhere.*

> *thelocal.ch — A longtime Switzerland resident summed up the salary taboo for newcomers in one line: "You would never, ever talk about salary," adding that the country's lack of wage transparency makes the topic a sharper faux pas than it would be almost anywhere else in Europe.*

> *expat.com (Prague forum) — Answering a newcomer unsure whether a 30,000 CZK monthly offer was workable, one Prague-based commenter broke down the real math: about 23,000 CZK after tax and insurance, roughly 9,000 CZK gone to a single room, leaving about 14,000 CZK for everything else — enough for one person, she said, but with far less room to "enjoy life" than many foreigners arriving expect.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between a Prague posting and a Zurich one on salary alone, the arithmetic isn't close, and no amount of cultural nuance changes that. What the nuance does change is how you'll have to operate once you're there. In Czechia, the market quietly rewards anyone willing to ask for more, in a culture where almost nobody does. In Switzerland, the number is genuinely larger, but finding out whether it's fair requires doing your own research, because no one at the next desk is going to tell you, and asking them is the fastest way to make it awkward for both of you.

Bring a calculator to Prague, and bring your own benchmarking spreadsheet to Zurich. Neither office is going to volunteer the number first.
