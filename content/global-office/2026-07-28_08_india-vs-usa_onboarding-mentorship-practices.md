**One Country Assigns You a Buddy for a Year; The Other Might Not Assign You Anything At All**

**🇮🇳 India · 🇺🇸 USA**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

American onboarding, on paper, looks like a solved problem: 98% of Fortune 500 companies now run formal mentoring programs, up from 84% just two years earlier. In practice, only 12% of new hires rate their organization's onboarding process as good, and a full third leave within 90 days. India's approach is comparatively slower and more relational — a well-run onboarding stretches across an entire year, with a peer "buddy" doing daily check-ins in week one and weekly ones for the first month — but that structure exists mainly at larger, better-resourced firms, and plenty of new hires everywhere else are simply handed a laptop and a Slack invite.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask specifically whether you'll be assigned a "buddy" separate from your manager — it's a distinct, valuable role at well-run firms | Assume every Indian employer has this structure; it varies enormously by company size and sector |
| Expect onboarding to be framed as a year-long process, not a first-week event | Expect fast clarity on ambiguous instructions — asking directly for specifics is normal, not rude |
| Use scheduled one-on-ones to surface confusion early rather than guessing at unwritten norms | Assume silence from a manager means things are fine — check in proactively |
| Lean on peer relationships for the "unwritten cultural norms" a formal handbook won't cover | Expect a US-style rapid, transactional onboarding — the relational pace is often deliberate |

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly in your first week whether a formal mentor will be assigned — it often isn't, unless you request it | Assume a Fortune 500 name guarantees a good onboarding experience — company size doesn't predict quality |
| Push for concrete 30/60/90-day expectations if they aren't offered proactively | Wait passively for structure to appear — a third of new hires who fail to gel leave within 90 days |
| Treat the first 45 days as the highest-risk window and ask for feedback actively during it | Assume nobody will notice if you're quietly struggling — turnover data says plenty of managers won't catch it in time |
| Request an informal buddy yourself if one isn't offered — it demonstrably improves early performance | Interpret an unstructured first week as a sign the job itself is disorganized — sometimes it's just onboarding that's broken |

### India: A Year-Long Relationship, When It Exists

Well-designed Indian onboarding treats the first year, not the first week, as the real onboarding period. Best-practice guides for Indian companies describe a formal buddy system distinct from the reporting manager — a peer who runs daily check-ins through week one, tapering to weekly through month one, tasked specifically with explaining "unwritten cultural norms" that no handbook covers. Programs increasingly build in communication and diversity-awareness modules explicitly designed for India's multicultural workplaces, recognizing that a new hire in Bangalore and one in Kolkata may be adjusting to genuinely different regional office cultures within the same company.

The caveat, visible in accounts from workers across sectors, is that this thoughtful structure is unevenly distributed. Newer hires at large tech and multinational firms describe reasonably deliberate onboarding; graduates entering smaller IT services shops more often describe being handed assignments with major contextual details left out, piecing together expectations "like a puzzle in the dark." The gap isn't between India and elsewhere so much as between well-resourced firms and everyone else — a distinction sharper in India's more fragmented corporate landscape than in more standardized Western markets.

### USA: Formal Programs, Inconsistent Delivery

America's onboarding statistics tell a story of investment without execution. Mentoring program adoption among Fortune 500 firms has climbed to 98%, and companies with strong onboarding — mentoring included — report 82% better new-hire retention and over 70% higher productivity, according to workforce research. Yet only 12% of new hires describe their own onboarding as good, just 29% feel fully prepared to succeed in their new role, and 22% of all staff turnover happens within the first 45 days specifically because onboarding failed to land.

The mentorship layer specifically shows a stark gap between intention and experience: 70% of employees say a mentor during onboarding would have helped them feel more prepared, and 56% of those who did get one say it helped tremendously — implying a majority of new hires simply don't get matched with one despite near-universal program adoption on paper. The lesson for a newcomer is blunt: a big-name US employer with a stated mentoring program is not the same thing as being handed a mentor, and the gap between the two is where a third of new hires quietly wash out within 90 days.

### The Reckoning

India's system, where it functions, is built around sustained relational scaffolding — a buddy, a year-long horizon, regular informal check-ins baked into the process by design. America's system is built around formal program infrastructure that frequently fails to translate into an individual new hire actually being matched with a person who checks on them. The irony is that India's model, born partly of necessity in navigating a more regionally and culturally fragmented workplace, ends up closer to what US onboarding research says actually works — while America's more codified, metric-driven approach to mentorship still leaves a majority of new hires without the one intervention proven to move retention and productivity numbers.

Hofstede's collectivism-leaning profile for India versus the US's high individualism score explains the difference in default design: India's onboarding culture assumes a new hire needs to be folded into a network, while America's assumes a new hire needs a program to opt into — and the latter assumption turns out to be the weaker bet given how often the program goes unused.

### Conclusion

If you're moving to India, ask explicitly about the buddy system before you accept an offer, because where it exists it's genuinely one of the better onboarding designs in global use — and where it doesn't, you're in exactly the same guesswork as anywhere else. If you're moving to the US, don't be reassured by a big employer's name or a stated mentoring program; ask directly whether you'll be matched with a person, because the data says most new hires aren't, no matter what the handbook promises. The honest version, over a drink: India will hand you a buddy and a year to figure it out; America will hand you a Slack channel and hope for the best.
