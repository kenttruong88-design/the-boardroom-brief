**The Sarong and the Suit: What Malaysia and Switzerland Really Expect You to Wear to Work**

**🇲🇾 Malaysia · 🇨🇭 Switzerland**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Kuala Lumpur, a man can be denied entry to a government office for wearing shorts, then go home to find his neighbour has conducted an entire client call in flip-flops; in Zurich, a bank teller can be sweating through a 33-degree heatwave in a full suit because, according to a widely cited 2024 YouGov survey reported by Swissinfo, 72 percent of Swiss respondents consider flip-flops unacceptable office footwear even when nobody is watching. Both countries will tell you, sincerely, that they are relaxed about clothes. Neither is lying, exactly. They are simply relaxed about different things, and the gap between those two definitions of "relaxed" is where a surprising number of relocating professionals discover they have packed the wrong wardrobe.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇾 Malaysia
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep a blazer in the car or office for client meetings, even if you commute in a short-sleeved shirt | Wear sleeveless tops or short hemlines into government buildings, hospitals, or most corporate HQs |
| Dress noticeably more conservatively on Fridays, not less, ahead of prayer time | Assume "smart casual" means the same thing it does in London — it usually means closed shoes and sleeves |
| Greet the most senior person in the room first, dressed or not | Extend a handshake to a Malay-Muslim or Indian-Muslim colleague of the opposite gender — wait for their lead |
| Budget two hours for Friday lunch, not one | Correct a senior figure's mistake in front of others, however wrong they are |
| Watch what your boss wears before you decide how far you can dress down | Wear flip-flops to client-facing work even if colleagues wear them internally |

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Overdress for a first meeting; you can always loosen up once you've read the room | Wear shorts to the office even in a heatwave — only 27% of Swiss men say they would |
| Use surname-plus-title (Herr/Frau, Monsieur/Madame) until invited to switch | Expect casual Fridays, flexible hours, or spontaneous jokes with new colleagues |
| Leave the office at a reasonable hour and actually disconnect on holiday | Eat a sandwich at your desk through the lunch break — step out, it's noticed |
| Treat the office *apéro* as a real, if brief, work obligation | Bring up neutrality, the world wars, or the army before you know people well |
| Check a company's LinkedIn photos before day one — banking still skews suit-and-tie | Assume a quiet, undemonstrative manager is an unhappy one — it's the local register |

Malaysia's formality is really a formality of *rank*, not of fabric. Hofstede Insights scores the country at 104 on power distance — among the highest in the world — against an individualism score of just 26, meaning deference to hierarchy and group harmony outranks almost everything else, including how casually you're allowed to dress once hierarchy has been satisfied. That shows up less in neckties than in choreography: greeting the most senior person first, never publicly correcting a superior, and treating a boss's sartorial latitude as a privilege of rank rather than a company-wide policy. An Expat.com forum thread on business etiquette in Malaysia is blunt about this — one poster, describing years of local business dealings, notes that promotion often tracks "social status, not competence," and that even technically wrong explanations from a senior figure are best agreed with rather than corrected. Government buildings, meanwhile, enforce actual dress rules with some zeal; Malaysian news outlets have covered multiple incidents of women turned away from public offices and even hospitals over sleeveless tops or hemlines, a policy debate that resurfaces with enough regularity to count as a recurring national pastime.

And yet Malaysia is, by the numbers, a famously undemanding place to clock in. OECD-derived labour data puts the average work week at 40.8 hours, and Gallup's State of the Global Workplace finds only 20 percent of Malaysian employees reporting daily stress, well below the regional and global averages. The dress code tightens around status and occasion; the actual grind, by comparison, loosens. That combination — rigid about who outranks whom, relatively forgiving about everything else — is precisely what trips up newcomers who read "flip-flops at the next desk" as license rather than as an earned local exception.

Switzerland inverts the equation almost exactly. Hofstede puts the country at a modest 34 on power distance and a comfortably individualist 68, the opposite profile from Malaysia's — titles matter, but less as a marker of unchallengeable rank than as a marker of professional seriousness that persists uniformly, up and down the org chart. That seriousness is not performative for clients only; it is structural. The Swiss Federal Statistical Office's 2024 figures show full-time employees working around 40 hours a week, a number that has actually been falling, while banks such as Urner Kantonalbank and Bank Cler have formally dropped compulsory ties in recent years, according to finews.com — a relaxation driven, tellingly, not by staff comfort but by customers who found neckties off-putting. Even that loosening has limits: as The Local Switzerland's refresher on office norms notes, eating lunch at your desk rather than stepping away for a proper break is the kind of thing a Swiss manager will quietly flag to a newcomer.

**The Reckoning:** Put the two side by side and the counterintuitive finding is this — Malaysia enforces formality through people, Switzerland enforces it through process. Dress badly for your rank in Kuala Lumpur and you insult a specific person; dress badly for the occasion in Zurich and you've simply failed to read an unwritten manual that everyone else somehow received at birth. Malaysia will forgive an outsider's wardrobe mistake with a laugh and a nod toward the boss's example; Switzerland will forgive it eventually, but only after you've visibly adjusted, unprompted, without being told twice.

The other inversion is plain hours-logic hiding behind the clothes. Malaysians work longer average weeks and dress, on balance, more casually once rank is settled; the Swiss work fewer hours and dress more consistently formally regardless of role. Comfort with ambiguity, not comfort with heat, turns out to be the scarcer resource in Zurich.

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between these two postings on formality alone, choose based on which kind of discomfort you can tolerate: Malaysia asks you to read a room and defer to a person, which rewards emotional intelligence and social homework; Switzerland asks you to read a manual nobody wrote down and follow it before anyone checks, which rewards patience and a tolerance for being quietly, privately judged until you get it right. Neither is harder, exactly, but they are different muscles, and packing a blazer solves neither problem on its own.

What I'd actually tell a friend over a drink is this: in Malaysia, dress one notch above your peers and watch your boss, not the handbook; in Switzerland, dress one notch above the weather and assume the handbook exists even when nobody hands it to you.
