# The Friendship Gap: Why Argentina Adopts You and the Netherlands Schedules You

**🇦🇷 Argentina · 🇳🇱 Netherlands**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Buenos Aires, a colleague might invite you to Sunday asado with their extended family within a month of meeting you, and mean it. In Amsterdam, a colleague might be perfectly warm at the Friday borrel and still, eighteen months later, never have invited you to their home. Neither is rude. Both are following a system, and the systems are almost perfect opposites.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇷 Argentina

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept an asado invitation as a genuine gesture — showing up matters more than what you bring | Expect friendships to stay at "acquaintance" level once you're invited somewhere personal |
| Get comfortable with physical warmth — a cheek kiss greeting even among new colleagues | Rush the relationship-building phase before discussing business specifics |
| Learn Spanish beyond survival level — it visibly signals you're staying, not passing through | Assume an invitation to "get a coffee sometime" is a scheduling formality — follow up |
| Join a shared activity — tango, football, a course — to break into existing friend groups | Expect to be fully "inside" a friend group that formed in secondary school overnight |
| Treat being added to a group chat as a real marker of trust | Cancel plans last-minute without a real reason — it reads as a bigger insult than elsewhere |

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to the vrijmibo (Friday afternoon drinks) — it's the primary onramp to social life | Wait for a spontaneous invitation — Dutch social life runs on the agenda, not impulse |
| Join a club, sports team, or recurring activity — repetition builds trust here | Take directness personally — blunt feedback isn't a sign of dislike |
| Propose a specific plan with a specific date — vague "let's hang out" rarely converts | Expect a work friendship to migrate into home life quickly, or possibly ever |
| Be genuinely interested in integration — Dutch people notice who's trying and who isn't | Assume a great borrel means you've "made it" socially — it may just mean good colleagues |
| Give it a full year or more before judging whether it's working | Retreat into an expat-only social bubble — it's the easiest trap and the hardest to escape |

### Argentina: Friendship as an Open Door

Argentine social life runs on hospitality as a default setting rather than an earned privilege. The asado — Argentina's ritualized barbecue — functions, according to the City of Buenos Aires's own tourism office and multiple cultural guides, as the country's primary vehicle for building and maintaining relationships: slow, unhurried, built around hours of shared table time rather than a fixed agenda. Guides for newcomers describe being added to a group chat for a football match or a birthday as a genuine, legible signal of being welcomed into someone's inner circle — not a polite gesture extended to everyone.

Hofstede's cultural dimensions help explain why: Argentina scores meaningfully lower than most Western European countries on individualism, reflecting a culture where group belonging — family, close friend circles formed young, often in secondary school — carries more weight than individual independence. That collectivist streak cuts both ways for a newcomer. The initial warmth is real and fast. But cracking into a friend group that formed decades ago, per the Buenos Aires Expats Community forum, is a slower project than the warm invitations suggest — expats there specifically recommend recurring shared activities, like tango classes or intensive Spanish study, as the actual mechanism that converts a friendly acquaintance into a real friendship, rather than hospitality alone.

### The Netherlands: Friendship by Appointment

Dutch social life runs on the opposite operating principle: relationships built through repetition, scheduling, and shared structured activity rather than spontaneous warmth. The borrel — informal after-work drinks, especially the Friday "vrijmibo" — is described by iamExpat and multiple workplace guides as the real starting point for Dutch friendships, but starting point is the key phrase. Making the leap from borrel-friendly to actually-invited-home is a documented, well-known sticking point.

The numbers back up what feels, anecdotally, like Dutch reserve: InterNations' Expat Insider survey found that 52% of expats in the Netherlands report difficulty making friends with locals, against a 38% global average, and that 51% of expats there end up in friendship circles made up almost entirely of other expats, compared to 35% globally. Only 11% report being mainly friends with Dutch locals. Quora threads on the subject converge on a consistent explanation: Dutch people tend to form their core friendships early — school, university, sports clubs — and by adulthood, those circles are largely full, not because newcomers are unwelcome, but because the social calendar genuinely has less open space in it. Hofstede's data situates the Netherlands among the more individualistic cultures globally, and low power distance keeps interactions egalitarian and direct — but individualism here means self-sufficient social lives already in motion, not an openness to expanding them.

### The Reckoning

The counterintuitive part is that the country with the warmer first impression is not necessarily the easier one to build a lasting friendship in, and vice versa. Argentina's hospitality is immediate and can feel, to a newcomer used to more reserved cultures, like acceptance has already happened — when really it's the opening move in a longer, relationship-driven negotiation that still requires real investment (the language, the repeated shared time) to complete. The Netherlands' friendliness is more measured from the start, which reads as coldness to newcomers expecting warmth, but the actual mechanism — join the club, show up weekly, be patient — is more transparent and repeatable, if slower and less flattering along the way. Argentina asks you to accept an invitation and then prove you meant it. The Netherlands asks you to keep showing up until proving it becomes redundant.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Buenos Aires Expats Community — A longtime expat wrote that they never made real local friends until they committed to tango classes, adding that they wouldn't have met a single Argentine friend through work alone, no matter how many asados they were invited to.*

> *Quora — Someone who moved to the Netherlands for work described the specific sting of realizing, after a year of enjoyable Friday borrels with the same colleagues, that not one of them had ever proposed meeting outside of it — and that this wasn't personal, just structural.*

> *InterNations — A respondent to the Expat Insider survey singled out the Netherlands as a place where locals were friendly in the moment but rarely followed up, contributing to the finding that only about one in ten expats there end up mainly friends with Dutch nationals.*

> *Reddit — An expat in Buenos Aires described being genuinely surprised by how quickly a coworker's family folded them into a Sunday asado, but noted it took nearly a year of consistently showing up before conversations moved past pleasantries into anything resembling real friendship.*

> *Quora — A foreign professional in Amsterdam advised future arrivals to join a sports club rather than rely on work alone, arguing that Dutch friendships form through recurring structure — same time, same place, same people — far more reliably than through charm or effort in the moment.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Argentina, say yes to the asado, then keep saying yes — the invitation is sincere, but the friendship still has to be built on top of it. If you're moving to the Netherlands, join something with a recurring schedule and don't take the first year's slow going personally — the InterNations numbers say it's not just you. The honest version, if a friend asked me over a drink: Argentina will make you feel like you belong before you do, and the Netherlands will make you feel like you don't before you actually might. Neither feeling is reliable evidence of anything yet.
