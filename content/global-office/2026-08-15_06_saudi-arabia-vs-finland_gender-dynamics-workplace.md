**From 17% to 36% in a Decade vs. Already Equal on Paper: Saudi Arabia vs. Finland**

**🇸🇦 Saudi Arabia · 🇫🇮 Finland**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Saudi Arabia's female labor force participation has more than doubled in under a decade — from around 17% in 2016 to 36% by 2025, already past its original Vision 2030 target. Finland's female participation has hovered near parity with men for a generation. One country is in the middle of the fastest gender workforce transformation on earth; the other finished that transformation decades ago and is now arguing about the details. Both are legitimately interesting places to build a career, for opposite reasons.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇦 Saudi Arabia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research your specific employer's policies directly — practices vary widely between large multinationals and local firms | Assume nationwide reforms mean uniform workplace norms everywhere; implementation is uneven |
| Dress modestly and respectfully as a baseline, adjusting further based on your specific workplace and sector | Assume dress norms from Riyadh apply identically in every city or company |
| Take note of the sectors leading female employment — IT, telecom, finance, and Vision 2030 flagship firms | Overlook that women's participation in Saudi IT and telecom (28%) already exceeds the European average |
| Ask directly about accommodation, transport, and security arrangements if relocating as a single woman | Assume support infrastructure is identical to what's offered to male expatriate hires |
| Recognize progress as real and recent — colleagues who've watched the shift firsthand often have strong, specific perspectives worth listening to | Treat outdated stereotypes about the workplace as still accurate; the baseline has moved fast |

#### 🇫🇮 Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect genuine representation of women in management and on corporate boards as the norm, not the exception | Treat visible senior women as a diversity talking point — it's simply the ordinary makeup of Finnish firms |
| Take full advantage of shared parental leave if you're a father — uptake is actively increasing and encouraged | Assume parental leave is a "maternity" benefit only; Finland is deliberately shifting it toward shared use |
| Understand the labor market is still gender-segregated by sector even amid strong formal equality | Assume formal equality means no remaining pay gap — a real 16% gap persists on average |
| Discuss pay and career progression openly — transparency is culturally more normal here than in many countries | Assume a senior woman in the room got there through a quota rather than the standard path |
| Expect maternity-linked career breaks to still fall disproportionately on women in practice | Assume the generous leave system has fully closed the gap in actual time away from work |

Saudi Arabia's shift is one of the starkest workplace transformations happening anywhere right now. Female labor force participation rose from roughly 17.4% in 2017 to over 36% by 2025, blowing past the original Vision 2030 target of 30% years ahead of schedule, with a new goal of 40% now in sight (Arab News; vision2030.ai). The change isn't just about volume — women make up 45% of small and medium enterprise leadership in the Kingdom, and in IT and telecommunications specifically, women's workforce participation (28%) already exceeds the European average (17.5%). Private-sector employment of women (nearly 997,000 as of 2024) now outpaces public-sector employment, signaling that the shift is increasingly driven by market demand rather than government hiring alone (vision2030.ai). This is genuine structural change happening within a single professional generation, not gradual drift.

Finland, by contrast, is managing a mature gender-equality system with its remaining friction concentrated in subtler places. The country consistently ranks among the most gender-equal in the world, with women nearly matching men in overall labor force participation and strong representation in management and on corporate boards (Finland Toolbox). Yet a real gender pay gap persists — women earn on average 84% of what men earn across the labor market — and the system's generous parental leave hasn't yet translated into equal time away from work: mothers born in 1980 took career breaks 13 times longer than fathers by age 36, and even with recent reform, fathers account for only about 22.5% of reimbursed parental leave days (NordiskPost; Ministry of Social Affairs and Health). Finland's gender equality story, in other words, is less about access to work and more about what happens to careers around childbirth.

### The Reckoning

The counterintuitive contrast is that Saudi Arabia's headline participation numbers are moving faster than Finland's ever will again, while Finland's remaining gaps — pay, parental leave uptake, sector segregation — are the kind that tend to be the hardest and slowest to close precisely because the formal barriers are already gone. Saudi Arabia is fighting visible, structural battles with fast, measurable wins; Finland is fighting invisible, cultural ones that don't move with policy alone.

Hofstede's masculinity dimension is instructive here: Finland scores among the more "feminine" cultures globally, valuing consensus and quality of life over competitive achievement, which partly explains its emphasis on shared parental leave even where uptake still lags intention. Saudi Arabia's workplace gender dynamics are shaped less by that dimension and more by a rapid, top-down policy transformation overlaying a society still working out the cultural mechanics beneath the new numbers.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A foreign professional woman working in Riyadh described her security and living arrangements as more carefully managed than she expected, with employer-provided housing and transport, but noted the workplace itself, especially at a large multinational, felt far more conventional and merit-driven than pre-arrival assumptions had prepared her for.*

> *Quora — Someone who worked at a Saudi financial firm noted that female colleagues in analyst and management-consulting roles were increasingly common and taken seriously, but that this varied enormously by company — a state-linked traditional firm and a Western multinational operating in the same city could feel like entirely different countries.*

> *Internations Riyadh — A relocated European manager observed that many of her assumptions about workplace gender dynamics in Saudi Arabia were several years out of date by the time she arrived, and that colleagues who'd lived through the recent changes were often eager, not reluctant, to talk about how fast things had shifted.*

> *Reddit — A father who relocated to Finland for work said the biggest surprise wasn't the ease of taking parental leave but the social pressure in the other direction — colleagues and even his manager actively encouraged him to take his full share, treating a father skipping leave as slightly unusual rather than normal.*

> *Statista workplace perception surveys — Longitudinal data on Finnish men's perceptions of workplace gender equality show measurable but slow-moving improvement over two decades, reinforcing that formal equality and felt equality in Finland have converged unevenly, sector by sector, rather than all at once.*

### Conclusion

The practical distinction for someone deciding where to build a career is speed versus finish line. Saudi Arabia offers a workplace gender landscape that is changing quickly and unevenly — worth entering with your eyes open to real variation by employer and sector, and worth taking seriously as a place where being part of that shift is itself a career story. Finland offers a workplace that's already formally equal but where the remaining gaps are cultural and slow, embedded in who actually takes leave and who gets promoted after having children. If a friend asked which is the "safer" bet for gender equity, I'd say Finland on paper, Saudi Arabia on momentum — and either answer is a reasonable one, depending on whether you'd rather join a finished system or help write the next chapter of an unfinished one.
