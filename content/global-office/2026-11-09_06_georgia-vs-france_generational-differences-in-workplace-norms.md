# Georgia's Elders Still Run the Meeting. France's Elders Still Run the Country.

🇬🇪 Georgia · 🇫🇷 France

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Tbilisi, a 24-year-old analyst will defer to a department head twice her age without being asked to, because deference is simply how Georgian offices are wired. In Paris, a 24-year-old analyst will also defer to a department head twice his age — not out of instinct, but because French labor law, pension politics, and a civil-service seniority ladder have spent decades making sure he has very little choice. According to GeoStat, Georgia's unemployment rate fell to 13.9% in 2024, still disproportionately concentrated among workers under 30, while Randstad's October 2025 "La génération Z en entreprise" survey found French Gen Z workers arriving with a sharply different relationship to authority than their employers expected — ambitious, but unwilling to simply wait their turn the way the generation before them did.

## Georgia: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address senior colleagues formally until explicitly invited to do otherwise | Expect flat, Silicon-Valley-style hierarchy just because the office has beanbags |
| Treat family and personal introductions as a legitimate part of professional trust-building | Dismiss toasts and hospitality rituals as irrelevant to "real" business |
| Expect younger staff to absorb informal mentorship by observation, not structured onboarding | Assume a junior employee's silence in a meeting means agreement |
| Recognize that emigration to the EU and Gulf states shapes how younger workers value a job | Be surprised when a talented 26-year-old leaves for Germany within two years |
| Respect that many small and mid-size firms are still genuinely family-run | Push for rapid promotion on merit alone without factoring in tenure norms |

## France: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect younger hires to ask directly about work-life balance and purpose in the interview itself | Assume asking about the 35-hour week or RTT days signals low ambition |
| Respect the CDI/CDD contract distinction — it shapes how secure a young employee actually feels | Expect a junior staffer to casually override a senior colleague's decision in a meeting |
| Recognize that civil-service and large-corporate seniority ladders still move slowly by design | Assume quiet-quitting headlines mean Gen Z in France is simply disengaged |
| Expect unions and collective agreements to mediate generational workplace disputes, not HR alone | Treat a 24-year-old's hesitation to commit to unpaid overtime as entitlement |
| Use formal "vous" with senior colleagues until told otherwise, regardless of age gap | Expect generational candor about pay or promotion timelines the way it happens in Anglophone offices |

## Georgia: Deference Is the Operating System

Georgian offices run on an inherited hierarchy that has very little to do with how progressive the building's décor looks. Eurofound and the European Training Foundation's 2024 "Working Life in Georgia" report describes a labor market still defined by informal family and personal networks, where trust is built socially before it is built professionally — a dynamic that keeps older managers, often the firm's founders or their relatives, firmly in charge of both strategy and day-to-day decisions. Younger employees are expected to learn the job the way an apprentice learns a trade: by watching, absorbing unwritten rules, and not pushing too hard, too soon.

What complicates this picture is emigration. GeoStat's youth labor data and reporting on the 2024 unemployment figure show joblessness concentrated among under-30s, and a meaningful share of Georgia's most qualified young professionals simply leave — for Germany, Poland, or the Gulf — rather than wait out a seniority system they consider too slow. The result is a strange generational split: the Georgians who stay tend to accept the hierarchy as the price of opportunity at home, while the ones most likely to challenge it have already left for a labor market that will reward them faster. Emerging Europe's reporting on Georgia's labour market describes a workforce that is unusually young and increasingly multilingual relative to the region, which only sharpens the mismatch between what junior staff are capable of and how slowly they're actually promoted into using it. Hofstede's framework, which scores France at a notably high 68 for power distance, doesn't have an official score for Georgia, but the comparative pattern described in regional labor research points the same direction — deference to authority remains the default, just enforced through family and informal ties rather than codified rules.

## France: The Hierarchy Has Tenure, Not Just Age

French generational friction looks different because it's institutionalized rather than informal. Randstad's October 2025 survey of Gen Z workers in French companies found a generation that arrives ambitious and vocal about flexibility, purpose, and boundaries — yet lands inside a system of CDI contracts, seniority-linked promotion, and union-negotiated collective agreements that moves at a pace largely indifferent to individual urgency. Ipsos's Observatoire sociétal des entreprises reporting on Génération Z describes a workforce that wants dialogue with management far more than older French workers ever expected to receive, and often doesn't get it, because the chain of command — particularly in the civil service and large legacy employers — simply wasn't built for upward pressure from 24-year-olds.

Coverage of France's "quiet quitting" moment, widely discussed in French business media through 2025, reads less as generational laziness and more as a rational response to a system where visible ambition rarely accelerates anything. French business press has separately reported that roughly a quarter of French employees across generations say they're ready to leave their current job, a figure frequently paired with the observation that Gen Z workers are "ambitious but not loyal" — willing to walk rather than wait, precisely because waiting has historically been the job. Hofstede's power-distance score of 68 for France — high for Western Europe, closer to much of Asia and Latin America than to Scandinavia — helps explain why French Gen Z's demands for flexibility collide so loudly with a workplace structure that still formally defers to age and tenure, even as the generation gap in expectations widens every year.

## The Reckoning

Both countries run on hierarchy, but the mechanism differs in a way that matters enormously for anyone switching between them. Georgia's hierarchy is personal and familial — it bends for trusted relationships and tightens for outsiders, which means a foreign professional's path to real influence runs through social capital, not org charts. France's hierarchy is institutional and contractual — it bends for nobody regardless of relationship, and a foreign professional's path to influence runs through time served and the right contract type, full stop.

The generational tension plays out on different timelines, too. In Georgia, the pressure valve is emigration: dissatisfied young talent leaves the country rather than the company. In France, the pressure valve is quieter and more local: dissatisfied young talent disengages from ambition without disengaging from the paycheck, because the social safety net and contract protections make staying put, underwhelmed, a perfectly rational choice.

There's also a timing irony worth sitting with. Georgia's younger workforce is, on paper, exactly what a modern employer says it wants — young, multilingual, mobile — yet the promotion system wasn't built to move at the speed its own talent pool is capable of. France's younger workforce is arriving with the opposite complaint: a system built for patience, confronting a generation that was never taught to have any. Neither mismatch is going to resolve itself through better onboarding decks.

## Conclusion

If you're moving into either system, adjust your expectations about where influence actually comes from. In Georgia, invest in being introduced properly, be patient with a mentorship style that rewards observation over initiative, and accept that your fastest route to responsibility may run through a dinner table rather than a performance review. In France, learn your contract type cold, respect the "vous" until told otherwise, and understand that the collective-bargaining machinery moving slowly around you isn't indifference — it's the system working exactly as designed, for better and for worse. Neither country's young workers are wrong to feel stuck. They're just stuck in different-shaped boxes.
