# Portugal Stays Put Because Leaving Costs Too Much, Morocco Leaves Because Staying Pays Too Little
🇵🇹 Portugal · 🇲🇦 Morocco

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The average worker in Portugal has spent 11.9 years with one employer, which puts the country fourth in a Statista ranking of European tenure, behind Greece, Italy and Slovenia. In Morocco, a 2023 study by ReKrute and Boston Consulting Group found that roughly 70% of talent was actively looking for another job, against a global average of 40%. These look like two opposite philosophies of work. They are closer to one philosophy applied to two different sets of constraints, in which nobody is loyal to an employer so much as to a pay slip, and the pay slip decides the rest.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether your contract is open-ended (sem termo) or fixed-term (a termo). Fixed-term deals normally need an objective reason and have renewal limits. | Assume a fixed-term offer is a trial run for a permanent one. |
| Confirm whether an offer is monthly or annual. Salaries are usually paid in 14 instalments, so EUR 2,000 a month is about EUR 28,000 a year. | Compare a Portuguese offer with a 12-payment foreign one without doing the sum. |
| Ask HR whether a collective labour agreement applies. It can improve on the basic Labour Code. | Skip the question because the contract looks complete. |
| Ask early what the promotion path looks like. About a third of workers in a Randstad survey said they would leave without one. | Wait quietly for advancement to be offered. |
| Use titles such as Doutor or Engenheiro in writing and introductions, especially in law, finance and public administration. | Assume Lisbon's startup informality extends to the rest of the country. |
| Claim the 22 working days of annual leave and the 40 paid training hours a year. | Treat statutory entitlements as favours. |

### Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate pay and recognition explicitly. An expat guide describes loyalty as tied to both. | Expect gratitude to substitute for a raise. |
| Allow time for greetings, breaks and a relaxed pace. | Treat the slower start to meetings as a personal slight. |
| Bring foreign qualifications and make them visible. They get more recognition than Moroccan ones, according to Expat.com. | Assume any diploma guarantees a job. Unemployment among graduates was 19% in Q3 2025. |
| Dress for the role. Positions of responsibility and customer-facing jobs require proper clothing. | Turn up in sportswear or bold jewellery. |
| Expect colleagues to be open to better offers. About 70% were looking in the ReKrute and BCG study. | Read a colleague's departure as a betrayal. |
| Keep a long view on results. Being result-oriented is an asset but can distance you from colleagues. | Push a results culture harder than the people around you want it. |

## Portugal: Staying Put, Reluctantly

According to Statista, which drew on 2023 survey data, Portuguese workers spend an average 11.9 years with one employer, compared with 10.5 in Germany, 10.4 in Spain and 10.3 in France. It would be pleasant to attribute this to devotion. The evidence points elsewhere. A Randstad employer branding survey, reported by The Portugal Post, found 23% of professionals planning to change jobs in the next twelve months, down from 46% in earlier years that the article does not date. More than half said their pay was insufficient, a third said they would leave without a visible career path, and the paper described workers as feeling trapped rather than loyal.

The OECD's 2026 Economic Survey of Portugal supplies the structural explanation. Temporary and non-standard contracts remain widespread, particularly among youth and less skilled workers, and the OECD notes that repeated fixed-term contracts cause excessive job turnover. The minimum wage reached EUR 870 in 2025, about 59% of the gross median wage in 2024, and around 23% of employees earn it. Statistics Portugal figures cited by Fortune show about 250,000 residents held at least two jobs in 2023, roughly 5% of working-age adults and a record since 2011. A population that is stable and also holding second jobs is not content; it is cautious. The minimum wage rises to EUR 920 a month in 2026, according to an expat guide to Portuguese offices, with a government target of EUR 1,020 by 2028, which helps the bottom of the scale and does little for anyone hoping a new employer will double the offer.

For the young, the answer is not caution but departure. A Boston Consulting Group consumer survey, reported by Times of Madeira, found 34% of Portuguese respondents would consider emigrating and 73% of those aged 18 to 24, up from 64% in 2024, with 37% citing better professional conditions. Even the tech sector, the usual escape route, is not the bargain it appears. A developer's analysis of the 2026 market, using Eurostat data, found Portugal's ICT vacancy rate at 4.8% in the first quarter against 2.6% for the EU, yet mean ICT professional earnings in 2022 were EUR 39,232, about two thirds of the EU mean of EUR 57,522. Openings abound; so does the incentive to take them in another country. Hierarchy adds friction on the way up. An expat guide to Portuguese offices notes that decisions tend to move up the chain, that titles matter, and that flatter workplaces are mostly found in Lisbon and Porto tech. Hofstede Insights could not be reached for this piece, so no cultural scores are offered, and none are invented.

## Morocco: Loyalty Is a Luxury Good

Morocco's official labour data explain much of the churn. According to the High Commission for Planning, as reported by Morocco World News, the national unemployment rate was 13.1% in the third quarter of 2025, youth unemployment among 15 to 24 year olds was 38.4%, and graduates faced 19%. Underemployment rose to 11.1% from 10%, and 5.3% of workers were underemployed because of inadequate income or a mismatch between education and the job. Afrobarometer found 28% of young people had thought a lot about emigrating, up from 20% in 2017, with 54% of those who had considered it citing better job opportunities.

Against that backdrop, employee loyalty looks like an irrelevance. A 2024 Gallup study reported by Bladi found 69% of Moroccan employees ready to leave their current job and look for new opportunities, and only 16% saying they thrive at work. The ReKrute and BCG study, reported by Hespress, found only 1% of Moroccan managers described themselves as loyal, against 4% globally, and 52% of job seekers wanted a more interesting job or a higher level of seniority. An expat guide to Casablanca work culture describes loyalty as tied to pay and recognition and says behaviour of mercenaries paid at the highest level is not uncommon and in some sectors the rule. The guide also names the Moroccan paradox, where the more qualified one is, the less likely one is to be employed.

Young Moroccans are not simply restless. A Rabat Business School survey, reported by LNT, suggests they reject work without meaning rather than work itself, which is a sentence employers could usefully print and frame. Afrobarometer found that 21% of Moroccans aged 18 to 35 were jobless and seeking work, three times the rate among those aged 36 to 45.

The word mercenary is unkind, though not wrong in kind. When salaries are low relative to Casablanca living costs, competition for middle-management roles is intense, and the unemployment rate for the young is nearing 40%, an offer from a rival is less a disloyalty than a rational response. The same guide notes that colleagues spend more time on greetings and breaks, and that pressure for results is lower than in Europe, so the environment can be warmer than the churn suggests.

## The Reckoning

Portugal and Morocco differ in how the exit is blocked. In Portugal, tenure is long because pay is modest everywhere, fixed-term contracts discourage risk and the next employer will probably offer a similar number. The 23% planning to move is plausibly a figure shaped by fear of a bad landing, since the same survey found more than half of workers think their pay insufficient. In Morocco, tenure is short in intention because the employee has already priced in the possibility of leaving, but the reality is constrained by a labour market where 38.4% of young people are unemployed. Both populations are thinking about the same exit: abroad. BCG and Afrobarometer, using different methods, show the same slope, with rising emigration interest among the young in both countries.

For the person arriving from outside, the practical difference is the social contract around moving jobs. In Portugal, a long tenure is normal enough that a short one invites questions, and hierarchy and titles reward patience. In Morocco, movement is so common that staying put carries no particular merit, and a manager who expects deep devotion in return for a modest salary is likely to be disappointed. Portugal asks for patience it does not always reward. Morocco asks for little and gets about that.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — a Portugal-based poster says that while they do not work there, they see workers on construction sites without basic protective gear and farm workers handling chemicals unprotected, and read it as a sign of how little employers value worker wellbeing.*

> *Fishbowl — commenters in a Lisbon salary thread describe remote jobs paying EUR 100,000 and more for foreign employers, while others are weighing competing offers from consultancies and asking how hard to push on a counteroffer.*

> *Developer blog (danilchenko.dev) — a software engineer who tracks the Portuguese tech market concludes that it suits EU citizens early or mid-career, with plenty of openings but pay well below the EU average, and less so senior engineers with strong offers elsewhere.*

> *TelQuel — a 25-year-old in Meknes who has worked as a baker for eight years says he left school after middle school because there was no transport, and his father now depends on his income.*

> *TelQuel — a 25-year-old university student in Meknes delivers bread at dawn and works a bakery till to pay for his studies and living costs, and calls the work exhausting.*

## Conclusion

Portugal is the better choice if you want long-term contracts, collective agreements and statutory leave, and you can tolerate wages that stay modest and a hierarchy that likes its titles. Morocco may suit someone who negotiates freely, prizes a relaxed pace, brings a foreign qualification and expects colleagues to come and go. Before you sign either, read the contract for its type and its notice terms, and work out the pay in the currency and instalments you will actually receive.

In short, one country keeps you because there is nowhere obvious to go, and the other lets you go because there is nowhere obvious to stay.
