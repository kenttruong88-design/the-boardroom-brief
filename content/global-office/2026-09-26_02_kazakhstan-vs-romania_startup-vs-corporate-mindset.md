# Same Hierarchy Score, Two Different Bosses: Kazakhstan and Romania on Startup vs. Corporate Life

**🇰🇿 Kazakhstan · 🇷🇴 Romania**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Kazakhstan wants you to believe it is building the next Central Asian Silicon Valley, one state-backed accelerator at a time. Romania has already quietly become one of Europe's biggest corporate back offices, hosting Google, Microsoft, and Amazon teams who will never meet the people whose salaries they set. Both pitches sound like opposites — state-sponsored ambition versus multinational absorption — until you check the actual org chart in each country and find they're almost identically steep.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇿 Kazakhstan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect contracts in English, Russian, and Kazakh — get a translator involved early | Assume a handshake or verbal agreement covers you the way it might elsewhere |
| Invest time in relationship-building and small talk before business discussions | Rush straight to the agenda in a first meeting — it reads as disrespectful |
| Target Astana Hub-affiliated startups if you want a flatter, faster-moving culture | Expect that flexible culture to extend to government-adjacent or legacy industries |
| Accept that in-person presence is still the default | Push hard for remote work — under 1% of the workforce currently works remotely |
| Bring a gift to a first business dinner if one is offered | Treat business dinners as optional networking — they're where real decisions get discussed |

#### 🇷🇴 Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Give explicit, prescriptive direction to Romanian team members rather than vague autonomy | Assume silence in a meeting means agreement or lack of ideas |
| Build in one-on-one check-ins alongside group meetings | Rely solely on group brainstorms to surface disagreement or concerns |
| Recognize that technical execution is a genuine strength — trust the output | Mistake risk-aversion for lack of capability or ambition |
| Understand Bucharest and Cluj-Napoca operate like distinct tech hubs with their own norms | Treat every Romanian office as an interchangeable "outsourcing desk" |
| Ask directly for candid feedback if you want it — it often won't volunteer itself | Expect unsolicited pushback on a bad plan from someone several levels down |

---

Kazakhstan's startup story is a deliberate government project, and the numbers are real: Astana Hub-affiliated startups have generated $4.9 billion in revenue over five years and pulled in more than $910 million in investment, according to the hub's own figures, and the country cracked the top 10 "Rising Stars" in the Global Tech Ecosystem Index 2026, a ranking spanning 325 cities in 77 countries. The catch is that this ambition sits on top of a deeply traditional base. Corporate culture in sectors like oil and gas, government, and finance remains firmly hierarchical, per expat-focused labour guides, and that hierarchy doesn't dissolve just because a startup accelerator opened downtown — it just gets a separate lane. Remote work, tellingly, accounts for under one percent of the national workforce, and even routine employment contracts are commonly issued in three languages.

Romania's version of tech ambition took a different route: rather than building a homegrown startup brand, it became indispensable to everyone else's corporate machine. The country now counts roughly 192,000 software developers, up from 140,000 in 2020, ranks sixth globally in certified IT specialists per capita, and hosts Bucharest offices for Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Oracle, Dell, and Adobe that account for 63% of national software-development revenue. Cluj-Napoca alone employs over 220,000 IT professionals. None of this reads as scrappy — it reads as a country that industrialized outsourcing the way other countries industrialized manufacturing, complete with the management style that implies: local coverage on Romanian workplace norms describes a preference among managers for prescriptive, one-on-one instruction over open group debate, a pattern researchers trace partly to Communist-era habits of concentrating information and authority.

That management style is where Hofstede's numbers become genuinely interesting rather than decorative. Kazakhstan scores 88 on power distance; Romania scores 90 — statistically almost the same country on paper, both firmly in "very hierarchical" territory. That similarity cuts against the entire premise of "startup versus corporate," which assumes one culture is inherently flatter than the other. It isn't. What differs is *where* the hierarchy sits: in Kazakhstan it's concentrated in state-adjacent industries while a genuinely flatter startup subculture grows in Astana Hub's shadow; in Romania it's baked into the outsourcing relationship itself, where a Romanian team often reports up through a foreign client's org chart as much as its own.

**The Reckoning:** The honest comparison isn't hierarchy versus flatness — it's who's building the ladder. Kazakhstan is trying to construct an entirely new, flatter economic sector next to its old hierarchical one, betting that state investment can grow a startup culture from scratch. Romania inherited its position by being extremely good, and extremely cheap, at executing someone else's roadmap — a mid-level Romanian engineer earns $18,900 to $37,800 a year against $94,000 to $154,000 for a US counterpart doing comparable work, which is precisely the arithmetic that built Bucharest's tech district. A newcomer chasing "startup energy" in Kazakhstan should verify which lane they're actually joining; a newcomer joining a Romanian team should expect excellent technical execution paired with a workplace that rewards clear instructions over open dissent.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A recurring question from foreigners eyeing Kazakhstan asks whether it's realistic to get hired as an English-only speaker with no Kazakh or Russian; the consistent answer is that international and startup-adjacent roles can work in English, but anything touching government, legacy industry, or most local corporate life effectively requires Russian.*

> *Expat Arrivals (Kazakhstan) — One American professional described the bureaucratic reality behind the flexible-sounding pitch: their employment contract had to be produced in three separate languages, English, Russian, and Kazakh, before it was considered valid — a detail no recruiting email had mentioned.*

> *Romania Insider (a Bucharest-based English-language outlet, standing in for the harder-to-source live Reddit/InterNations threads on this pairing) — Foreign managers quoted on Romanian workplace habits described a consistent pattern: staff who execute instructions precisely and well, but who default to one-on-one conversations with the boss rather than raising concerns in a group meeting, and who rarely volunteer an unsolicited critique of a plan they've been handed.*

> *Expat Arrivals (Kazakhstan) — The same guide noted that skipping the small talk and relationship-building that typically precedes a Kazakh business discussion is read as a real social misstep, not just an inefficiency — several respondents linked slower initial deal-making directly to newcomers rushing past this stage.*

### Conclusion

If you're chasing "startup culture" as an escape from hierarchy, neither of these countries will hand it to you for free — you have to go find the specific company, team, or hub where it actually lives, because the national default in both places still runs steep. Kazakhstan rewards someone willing to learn the language and build relationships before extracting value from its genuinely fast-growing startup scene; Romania rewards someone who gives clear direction and doesn't mistake quiet execution for disengagement. Neither is the flat, egalitarian tech utopia the "startup" and "outsourcing" labels separately imply — they're both hierarchies, just wearing different uniforms.
