# Dinner at Midnight or Cheese on Nothing: Eating Your Way Through Argentina and Italy

🇦🇷 Argentina · 🇮🇹 Italy

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Argentines once averaged about 100 kilograms of beef a year; the figure now stands at roughly 47, the lowest in more than two decades. Italians, meanwhile, eat about 23.3 kilograms of pasta a year, the highest in the European Union, and regard the matter of which cheese goes on it as settled law. Both countries take food seriously. They differ in what they are prepared to be rude about.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Argentina

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Eat dinner at 9pm or later, and treat 10pm as normal | Book a table for 7pm and wonder why the staff are still setting up |
| Treat Sunday lunch as the main event of the week | Schedule errands for the Sunday family lunch |
| Accept mate when it is passed, and say "gracias" when you have had enough | Wipe the bombilla, ask for your own gourd or refuse out of squeamishness |
| Plan around the merienda, the afternoon coffee and pastry | Expect a proper meal between lunch and dinner |
| Learn the order of an asado, from offal and provoleta to the main cuts | Compare the beef unfavourably with anywhere else |
| Budget for beef as a luxury item | Assume the steak is still the cheap option |

### In Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Order cappuccino at breakfast only, and espresso afterward | Order a cappuccino after a meal and expect no reaction |
| Keep cheese for pasta and risotto | Grate parmesan onto a main course or onto seafood pasta |
| Sit down to eat, even for street food | Wander the street with a supplì |
| Take lunch seriously and plan around shops closing | Assume everything stays open through the early afternoon |
| Ask for coffee after the meal | Order a hot drink with your food |
| Eat the salad last | Order a leafy salad as a starter |

## Argentina

The Argentine day is organised around a late dinner and a large lunch. According to the culture entry compiled on Wikipedia, breakfast is small, lunch is often the largest meal outside the big cities, and dinner is frequently not eaten until 9pm or later. A journalist writing for The New World put dinner at between 10pm and 11pm, with merienda between 5pm and 7pm to bridge the gap, and once ate at midnight when her partner arrived at 11pm. The same entry calls Sunday family lunch the most significant meal of the week, usually an asado or pasta.

The asado is the national liturgy. It begins with offal, morcilla and provoleta, moves to choripán, and then to cuts such as vacío, entraña and matambre, all accompanied by chimichurri, which contains no chilli. Italian immigrants ensured that the other half of the table is covered: pizza, ñoquis, ravioli and tallarines are standard, and gnocchi are traditionally eaten on the 29th of each month. Mate, shared from one gourd, completes the ritual. The same Wikipedia entry credits Argentines with about 141 cups of coffee per person a year and, citing a 2017 study, with consuming more than 75 percent of the world's fernet. The country took in 6.6 million immigrants at its peak, which is why the national dish is an argument between two continents.

The country is also less uniformly carnivorous than its reputation. Wikipedia notes that in Patagonia lamb and goat are eaten more often than beef, often cooked on a stake over an open fire, and that fainá, a chickpea-flour dough, is served alongside pizza in Buenos Aires, Rosario and Córdoba. Business customs reported by Cyborlink follow the same schedule: business dinners are popular, lunches are uncommon outside Buenos Aires because many people go home to eat, and an 8pm meeting is not considered late. The same guide warns visitors never to compare American beef or wine unfavourably with the Argentine versions, which are treated as national symbols.

The difficulty is that the ritual has become expensive. The Buenos Aires Herald, citing the beef industry chamber CICCRA, reported that average consumption over the twelve months to March 2026 was 47.3 kilograms per person, the lowest in more than 20 years, while beef prices were up 68.6 percent year on year. Fortune, reporting on the same trend, put Buenos Aires beef at about 18,500 pesos a kilo against 4,900 for chicken, and said wages were rising more slowly than monthly inflation. A butcher quoted there said people were switching to pork and chicken. In Argentina, giving up asado is not a diet. It is a household budget.

## Italy

Italy is less a cuisine than a code of conduct. The pasta statistic is the easy part: Il Sole 24 Ore reports that Italians eat about 23.3 kilograms each a year, against 12.2 in Greece, and that Italy produces about 69 percent of the EU's pasta. The harder part is the grammar. The Local lists the rules foreigners trip over: no cheese on a main course or on seafood pasta, no cappuccino after midday, no hot drinks with food, no walking while eating, and no salad as a starter, because it is thought to aid digestion at the end. One writer was scolded at a Rome food stand for grating cheese onto stew.

The rhythm also matters. The Local notes that public offices, banks and most shops close in the early afternoon, so that almost everything is closed at lunch, and that Italians drink less than most Europeans and regard heavy drinking as unbecoming. For the practical expatriate, the useful discovery is that the prohibitions are mostly decorative, but the lunch break is not. The Local adds that there is no real tipping culture in Italy, and that rounding the bill up slightly is considered enough. Arriving up to half an hour late to an informal gathering is considered polite, though business meetings and dates call for punctuality. Newcomers should also expect the Italian table, on the whole, to be less flexible than the Argentine one: the Expat.com thread records restaurants sometimes requiring reservations even with empty tables.

Italy is also paying more to follow its own rules. Istat data reported by Il Sole 24 Ore show households spending 552 euros a month on food and non-alcoholic drinks in 2025, up from 533, with food's share of spending rising to 19.8 percent. Almost a third of households said they had cut back, and coffee prices rose 14.6 percent. The espresso, in other words, is still permitted. It has simply become a larger line item. Istat also found that the rises were not evenly spread: food spending went up significantly in the North and Centre, while in the South the increases were concentrated in housing and energy. Where you live in Italy therefore decides not only what you eat, but what you have left to eat it with.

## The Reckoning

Both countries have turned a meal into a social institution and both are now being stress-tested by prices, though the pressure falls differently. Italians are defending a canon with an extra 19 euros a month. Argentines are watching the canon's centrepiece leave the plate altogether: beef consumption has fallen from about 100 kilograms to about 47. Italy's cuisine is regulated by custom, and Argentina's by supply.

Hofstede Insights helps, slightly. Argentina scores 86 on uncertainty avoidance and 62 on indulgence, against Italy's 75 and 30, which suggests that Argentines are anxious about almost everything except dinner and the nation's inflation. Italy's 76 on individualism and 70 on masculinity point to a society with firm views, expressed over a meal. For the newcomer, the practical difference is that Italy punishes you for the wrong order, and Argentina punishes you for the wrong time.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Buenos Aires Expats forum — a Londoner living in the city missed cheap, fresh takeaway, and found the options mostly pizza, burgers, milanesas and empanadas, often in small portions or at high delivery-app prices. He relied on his retired mother-in-law, who he estimated spent around 25 hours a week shopping and cooking, and found the expectation that women cook stressful. Others argued over whether feedlot beef now dominates the steak market.*

> *Expats BA — one newcomer found the dinner schedule the hardest adjustment, because her early dinner was the locals' afternoon snack. Another noted that a casual dinner at 9pm usually means arriving around 10, though work appointments start on time, and a third said friends who loved the city left once high-end restaurant prices passed US levels.*

> *Expat.com — an American living near Rome was surprised that drinks arrive without ice, because locals reportedly believe cold drinks upset the stomach. Another American in Veneto said Italians are not interested in sauces, that northern food is mild even in Chinese restaurants, and that he had been knowingly overcharged. A poster from Sardinia suggested the south for spicier food.*

> *The Local — in its list of mistakes foreigners make, the writer advised newcomers that almost everything shuts at lunch, that milk alternatives in cafes are hard to find, and that arriving half an hour late to a party is polite while a business meeting is not. The article's only reader comment concerned pet sitting.*

## Conclusion

The practical choice depends on what you can tolerate. Argentina asks for stamina and a flexible stomach: late dinners, a long Sunday, and a steak that now costs more than the chicken by a wide margin. Italy asks for obedience: the right drink at the right time, the right cheese on the right dish, and a lunch break that is not an invitation to negotiate.

Pick the country whose rules you can live inside. Then buy a table for the correct hour, order the correct coffee, and accept that someone, somewhere, is already disappointed in you.
