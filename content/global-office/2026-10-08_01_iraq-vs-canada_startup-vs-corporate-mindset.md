**Who You Know, Who You Pitch: Startup Culture in Iraq and in Canada Are Not the Same Species**

**🇮🇶 Iraq · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Baghdad, roughly 95 percent of people say they needed wasta — a word that translates loosely to "connections," though "the entire operating system" is closer — to land a job, according to a 2019 Arab Barometer survey. In Toronto, the entrepreneurship rhetoric runs the other way: recent immigrants start businesses at more than double the rate of the Canadian-born, 37 percent against 17 percent, per Statistics Canada. Both countries will tell you they run on hustle. Only one of them means it as a compliment to the person doing the hustling rather than the person they happen to be related to.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇶 Iraq

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Spend the first ten minutes on tea, family, and small talk before any business gets mentioned | Pull out a laptop and start the agenda the moment you sit down |
| Treat "I'll see what I can do" as a soft no and plan accordingly | Treat "I'll see what I can do" as a yes and build a timeline around it |
| Build a real relationship with whoever can actually approve your hire or contract | Assume your CV or pitch deck will do the convincing on its own |
| Keep your temper flat even when a negotiation drags for the third meeting in a row | Show visible frustration — it reads as a loss of face for everyone in the room |
| Budget weeks, not days, for any decision that needs a senior manager's sign-off | Expect a verbal agreement to move at the speed of a signed Western contract |
| Ask open-ended questions when you need a real answer, not a polite one | Take "inshallah" as a scheduling commitment |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Flag your own mistakes to your manager before they're discovered elsewhere | Wait for a performance review to mention a problem you already knew about |
| Make eye contact and keep your presentation shorter than you think it needs to be | Read a long deck line by line in a meeting |
| Say directly what you need, including credit for work you did | Assume quiet competence will get noticed and promoted on its own |
| Expect the "compliment, critique, compliment" feedback sandwich and respond to the middle part | Take the opening compliment as the whole review |
| Apply for the regulated or incorporated path if the business plan is permanent, not a side hustle | Assume "self-employed" and "business owner" are interchangeable for tax or benefits purposes |
| Pick a lane: join an actual seed-stage startup, or join a bank, telecom, or the public service — the middle is thinner than it looks | Expect the unlimited-vacation, Slack-only culture of a 12-person startup inside a 40,000-person employer |

Iraq's working culture runs on a hierarchy so clean it could be diagrammed in one line: the most senior person present does the talking, everyone else supplies clarification, and the decision belongs to whoever outranks the room. Rivermate's guide to Iraqi labor practice confirms the formal skeleton underneath that informal rule — a standard 48-hour, six-day week from Sunday to Thursday, overtime capped at four hours a day, and a written contract required in Arabic or Kurdish. What the legal skeleton doesn't capture is the informal one: a 2017 survey of more than two thousand small and medium enterprises found up to 70 percent of their financing came from personal savings, family, or friends, against roughly 11 percent from bank loans, according to research from Kapita.iq. That is not a startup ecosystem shy of capital. It is a startup ecosystem where capital, like employment, still mostly travels through people you already trust.

Youth unemployment sits at 32 percent as of 2025, per World Bank data tracked by the St. Louis Fed — a number that reads less as "entrepreneurial necessity" and more as "entrepreneurial inevitability." Iraq does have a real startup scene: roughly 17 incubators, 9 accelerators, and 11 co-working spaces, concentrated in e-commerce and delivery, with names like Miswag and Talabatey doing for Baghdad roughly what their counterparts do elsewhere. But foreign ownership of an Iraqi company is capped at 49 percent, and Transparency International ranks the country 160th of 180 for perceived corruption. A founder here isn't choosing between bureaucracy and hustle. They're choosing between two different kinds of patience — one for the market, one for whoever has to approve the permit.

Canada's version of corporate life is, by comparison, almost suspiciously procedural. Resources aimed at newcomers — Windmill Microlending's guide among them — describe a workplace built on self-advocacy: flag your own errors before someone else finds them, make your contributions visible to management rather than trusting them to be noticed, and expect feedback delivered as a "sandwich," compliment first, critique in the middle, compliment to close. None of that is a startup posture. It is the posture of an economy where the dominant employers — banks, telecoms, the federal and provincial public service — have formal review cycles and formal everything else. And yet Canada's actual self-employment numbers tell a stranger story than its corporate reputation suggests: Statistics Canada's own data shows a business-ownership entry rate that fell from above 15 percent in 2006 to 12 percent by 2020, even as recent immigrants started businesses at more than twice the rate of people born in the country. The hustle is real. It is just disproportionately carried by people who weren't necessarily looking for a "startup lifestyle" so much as a workaround to a credentialing system that doesn't always recognize a foreign degree.

### The Reckoning

Here is the trick: both countries are, on paper, hard places to climb without the right relationships. Iraq's version runs through wasta, openly discussed, broadly resented, and almost universally used anyway — 95 percent of respondents call it necessary, and the public sector's headcount nearly tripled between 2003 and 2015 largely on the back of it, according to a 2017 World Bank assessment. Canada's version runs through something subtler and less discussed: credential recognition, professional networks, and a corporate culture that rewards the specific skill of narrating your own value out loud, in English or French, in a format a Canadian manager recognizes. Nobody calls networking in Toronto "wasta." Arguably, it's the same organ, wearing a blazer instead of a dishdasha. Hofstede Insights has no individual score for Iraq, which falls under its broader "Arab World" cluster — high on power distance (80) and low on individualism (38) — while Canada sits at the opposite end (39 and 80, respectively), a gap that lines up neatly with the hierarchy-versus-flatness divide described above.

The real divergence is what each system does to a startup once it exists. In Iraq, a new venture has to out-negotiate a trust economy that moves on personal relationships and lunar-calendar patience. In Canada, a new venture has to out-document a procedural economy that will ask it, repeatedly, to put its differentiation in writing, present it in under ten minutes, and expect pointed questions about execution rather than vision. Iraq's constraint is capital and connections. Canada's constraint is bureaucratic sprawl wearing a pleasant feedback sandwich. Pick your bottleneck; there is not a version of either country without one.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — An engineer who had worked at Amazon described the big-tech reality in blunt shorthand: "LP driven execution, too much bureaucracy, execution bogged down by tech debt" — not a complaint so much as a confirmation that the slow, process-heavy version of corporate Canada (and corporate anywhere) is not a myth employees tell each other to feel better about startup life.*

> *Blind — A different commenter, relaying a friend's move from a startup into Google, framed the trade plainly: the startup had "above average people, led by bright people, bias for action, no process," while big tech offered real work-life balance at the cost of execution speed — the kind of trade nobody mentions in the offer-letter negotiation.*

> *Rudaw (Kurdish news outlet, interview) — Abu Zeinab, a 60-year-old Baghdad retiree, said plainly of his five adult children's job search: "Poverty pushes people towards wasta," noting only one of them had found work, and it was through a connection rather than an application.*

> *Rudaw (Kurdish news outlet, interview) — Ahmed, 29, from Kut, got an education-ministry job after meeting a senior official's bodyguard — but only once he'd financed roughly $800 through a bank loan to pay for it. His own verdict: "There is no job without wasta," followed by the quieter admission that he "felt remorse" for having to pay a bribe to work at all.*

> *Expat.com forum — A long-time Canada-based poster going by Abdel noted that large companies and government employers were, in his experience, more consistent about pay regardless of gender than the comparisons other posters raised, and that what friction he did encounter with Québécois colleagues read as cultural rather than discriminatory — a distinction he clearly thought mattered.*

### Conclusion

If you're building something from zero, Iraq will test your patience with institutions and reward your patience with people — the money and the hires both tend to arrive through someone who already trusts you, which is slower than a cap table but, once it works, remarkably durable. Canada will test your patience with process and reward your patience with paperwork done right — the credentials, the incorporation status, the carefully-worded self-advocacy that turns a quiet contributor into a visible one. Neither country is lying about being entrepreneurial. They're just entrepreneurial about different bottlenecks.

The honest question to ask yourself before you move for either one isn't "do I have a good idea." It's "which gatekeeper do I already know how to talk to — the uncle with a cousin in procurement, or the manager who wants my wins typed up by Friday." Pick the country whose gatekeeper you understand. The idea was never really the hard part.
