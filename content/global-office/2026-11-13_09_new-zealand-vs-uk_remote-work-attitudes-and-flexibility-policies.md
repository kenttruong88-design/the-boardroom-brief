# New Zealand Will Let You Work From Home, Provided the Minister Is Not Looking; Britain Will Let You Ask Twice

🇳🇿 New Zealand · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Great Britain, 28 percent of working adults were hybrid workers in early 2025, according to the Office for National Statistics, and the government is busy drafting rules to make employers explain themselves when they say no. In New Zealand, the Public Service Minister has told civil servants that working from home is "not an entitlement," and 55 percent of them were not doing it anyway. Both countries have a legal right to ask for flexibility. Neither has a legal right to receive it, which is the sort of detail that tends to appear in paragraph nine of the relocation brochure.

## Do's & Don'ts

### New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Put any home-working arrangement into your written employment agreement as a variation | Rely on a verbal nod from a manager; an informal arrangement can generally be withdrawn |
| Make flexible work requests in writing and expect a written reply within one month | Take a refusal personally; employers may decline on a limited list of grounds |
| Agree regular office days with the people you actually work with | Assume the public service will be relaxed about it after the 2024 guidance |
| Check broadband coverage before choosing a small town for your remote life | Assume every pleasant rural address has fibre |
| Ask offshore employers about time-zone overlap before signing | Expect to be awake for European stand-ups without consequences |

### United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the statutory right to request flexible working from your first day | Confuse a right to request with a right to work from home |
| Ask in the interview how many office days are expected and which days | Accept the word "hybrid" as a policy; it is a mood |
| Get agreed days written into your contract or a letter | Rely on a team culture that a new director can reverse |
| Expect an answer within two months, and use your two requests a year wisely | Plan your life around the 2027 reforms before they exist |
| Coordinate home days with colleagues, because office days are often set by team | Stay home on the one day everyone else decided to come in |

## New Zealand

New Zealand's approach is best described as a legal right to ask, followed by good faith. Under the Employment Relations Act, employees can request changes to hours, days or place of work, and, according to Employment New Zealand, employers must reply in writing within one month. They may decline only on set grounds. Beyond that, a lawyer quoted by the HR trade press put it plainly: if an agreement does not give you a right to work from home, then "ultimately it's the employer's decision." Another employment specialist told Newstalk ZB that informal arrangements, being outside the contract, can generally be ended by the employer.

The state has set the tone. In 2024 the Public Service Minister issued guidance saying working from home must be agreed and monitored, and the Public Service Commission later reported that 33 percent of public servants worked from home at least one day a week, with an average of 0.9 days. Private sector sentiment points the other way. Robert Walters found that 90 percent of surveyed professionals would look for a new job if their employer required five days in the office, and InternetNZ reported in 2023 that six in ten workers had jobs allowing some home working, with 54 percent of those wanting more of it.

Public opinion is less combative than the politics. A University of Otago pandemic survey, reported by RNZ, found 67 percent of participants preferred a hybrid model. The politics, meanwhile, are local: the minister's stated aim was to revive a quiet Wellington city centre, and a city councillor called the directive a "sugar hit" that could not offset the loss of roughly 6,500 jobs in the capital.

Employers are not uniformly enthusiastic. The same InternetNZ research cited in The Listener found that 21 percent of employers do not encourage working from home at all, which means roughly one job in five comes with a dress code and a desk. Infrastructure matters too: the article noted that 64 percent of New Zealanders had home fibre in 2022, and that Cyclone Gabrielle and the January 2023 floods exposed how fragile regional connections can be. A remote career in a lovely small town is available, subject to weather.

For Hofstede Insights, which scores cultures so that consultants do not have to, New Zealand rates 79 on individualism and 49 on uncertainty avoidance, against 89 and 35 for the United Kingdom. Both are individualist and relaxed about ambiguity, which helps explain why each leaves the matter to private negotiation. It also means that New Zealand's main obstacle to remote work is not management but the price of a flight to the Wellington meeting.

## United Kingdom

Britain has more data and, accordingly, more arguments. The ONS found that hybrid working has risen steadily since 2022, but access is anything but even. Among workers earning £50,000 or more, 45 percent were hybrid, against 8 percent of those earning under £20,000. Degree holders were hybrid at 41 percent, those with no qualifications at 4 percent. Flexibility, it turns out, is a benefit that tracks the salary band with remarkable fidelity. Age and hours matter as well: 36 percent of workers aged 30 to 49 were hybrid, against 19 percent of those aged 16 to 29, and 34 percent of full-time workers against 18 percent of part-timers. Disabled workers were hybrid at 24 percent, against 29 percent of non-disabled workers, an uncomfortable detail for a policy sold as inclusive.

The law is also in motion. Flexible working has been a day-one right to request since April 2024, according to Remote Work Europe, and employers must answer within two months. The government's own factsheet says the next reforms, expected in 2027, will let employers refuse only if that is reasonable, and require consultation first, but that the system "stays request-based." The same factsheet estimates the net direct cost to business at under £10 million a year, which suggests Whitehall does not expect a stampede. Meanwhile King's College London found that only 42 percent of workers would comply with a five-day mandate, down from 54 percent in early 2022, and that 58 percent would quit or start job hunting. Actual behaviour has barely moved: a quarter of workers work remotely three or more days a week.

The researchers found that the average number of permitted home-working days rose from under one a week in 2022 to about 1.3 in 2024, and warned that rigid mandates risk a two-tier workforce. They also found women were more likely than men to say they would quit or job hunt (64 percent against 51 percent). In other words, the British bargain is individual, usually measured in days, and the number is still smaller than the brochure implied.

## The Reckoning

On paper the countries are twins. Each gives employees a statutory right to ask, each lets employers refuse for stated reasons, and neither guarantees an outcome. The difference is in who sets the tone. In New Zealand it comes down from the government and the individual employment agreement; in the United Kingdom the argument is conducted through the courts, the Acas code and a steady stream of surveys in which workers promise to quit.

If your priority is certainty, the United Kingdom has the more detailed process, with a longer timetable for its reforms. If your priority is a smaller labour market where a single employer's habits matter more than national policy, New Zealand rewards the person who negotiates well and writes it down. Either way, the contract wins over the mood, and moods in both countries have recently been changing.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — a New Zealander working remotely for a US company at Australian pay rates reports a roughly 40 percent raise after leaving a local employer, and now worries about having priced themselves out of the New Zealand market. They think local employers are out of touch.*

> *Hacker News — a commenter who negotiated flexible hours to work for six months while travelling recommends no more than four days a week, preferably three, because travel is tiring. Being an introvert, they found the long stretch alone lonely and booked hotels every couple of weeks to recover.*

> *RNZ — a Wellington ministry staffer says they prefer being in the office because full-time remote work felt isolating, a view the story set against colleagues who said the opposite.*

> *RNZ — a public servant who commutes by train says the return-to-office push, arriving as half-price fares ended, simply adds cost and shifts the problem rather than solving it.*

> *Startups.co.uk — a small business owner who moved to a coworking space reports saving about £1,200 a month, a reminder that in Britain the office is increasingly something people rent for themselves.*

## Conclusion

If you work for a New Zealand public-sector employer, assume a modest, supervised version of flexibility and get it in writing. If you work for a private employer, ask about the policy before you sign and put your arrangement into the agreement. In Britain, ask the day-count question in the interview, since the law gives you a right to be considered and nothing more, and remember that your pay band will probably predict your options better than the legislation.

Both countries will let you ask. Only a signed contract will let you stay home, which is why the sensible expat reads the contract before the brochure.
