# The Bolívar You Don't Get Paid In vs. The Number You Don't Mention: Salary Culture in Caracas and Zurich

**🇻🇪 Venezuela · 🇨🇭 Switzerland**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Switzerland, discussing your salary with a coworker is a quiet social violation — a 2025 Robert Half survey found 75% of Swiss employees don't feel confident arguing for a raise even when most believe they deserve one, in a culture where money talk is, by multiple accounts, simply "not appropriate for discussion." In Venezuela, the number on your contract has become almost beside the point: with inflation projected above 600% for 2026 and the legal minimum wage frozen at the bolívar equivalent of roughly $0.30 a month since 2022, the operative question isn't what you're paid, but what currency you're paid in.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇻🇪 Venezuela

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clarify immediately whether compensation is dollar-denominated, bolívar-denominated, or mixed | Assume a quoted salary figure means the same thing without checking the currency and payment structure |
| Understand the "ingreso integral" system (base wage plus bonuses) as the real take-home reference point | Rely on the legal minimum wage figure as meaningful — it hasn't moved since 2022 |
| Consider remote, dollar-paying freelance work as a genuinely mainstream local strategy, not a niche one | Assume remote/freelance income is unusual or informal by local standards |
| Budget and re-check compensation value frequently given inflation | Treat any single salary conversation as valid for more than a few months |
| Recognize skilled local professionals as a legitimate global remote-hiring talent pool | Assume economic hardship implies lower professional quality or capability |

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate with data — market benchmarks, your results, scarce skills | Cite a peer's salary directly in a negotiation, even if you know it |
| Raise compensation only after receiving an offer, never before | Discuss desired salary early in the interview process |
| Expect real regional pay variation — Zurich and Geneva pay a real premium | Assume a national average salary applies uniformly across cantons |
| Negotiate non-salary package items (leave, pension contributions, remote days) where base pay is fixed | Assume a "fixed" salary band means there's nothing left to negotiate |
| Keep salary conversations with colleagues rare and low-key if they happen at all | Compare your pay openly with coworkers as a normal office topic |

Venezuela's salary story cannot be told through a single number, because the system itself has effectively split into two currencies operating in parallel. AZA Lawyer's analysis of wage dollarization describes a workforce dividing into "those who work with remuneration in bolívares and those who receive dollars" — and the gap between the two experiences is enormous: Latinoamérica21 cites professional and technical monthly compensation in Caracas ranging roughly $340 to $531, while over 70% of the broader population earns under $50 a month. The government's own "ingreso integral" mechanism — a base wage supplemented by a food bonus and an economic-war bonus, raised to a combined $240/month as of May 2026 — functions as an acknowledgment that the legal minimum wage alone is no longer a meaningful reference point at all. Set against this, the remote and freelance economy isn't a workaround for a small number of people — dynamitejobs.com and Quora discussions both describe a well-established, mainstream pipeline of Venezuelan professionals earning dollar income through platforms like Upwork and specialized services connecting Venezuelan talent directly with foreign employers, a pattern serious enough that recruiting firms now market Venezuela specifically as an "inexhaustible source of remote IT talent."

Switzerland's salary culture, by contrast, operates inside one of the world's most stable currencies and one of its most rigid social taboos. JobCloud and multiple negotiation guides describe salary comparison among colleagues as something that "creates awkwardness" and actively damages relationships, shifting any negotiation "from your value to internal politics" the moment a peer's number gets mentioned. The negotiation norms that do exist are highly codified: raise compensation only after an offer is extended, never before; argue from market data and demonstrated results, never personal need; and expect meaningful regional variation, with Zurich and Geneva commanding a genuine 10–15% premium over smaller Swiss cities for comparable roles. Even the confidence gap is documented rather than assumed — that Robert Half figure (75% of Swiss employees unsure how to argue for a raise) suggests the taboo isn't just about discussing pay with others, but about advocating for your own.

### The Reckoning

The two countries land in nearly opposite relationships to the idea of a "salary" as a stable, meaningful unit. In Switzerland, the number is stable and precise, but discussing it is the taboo — the culture has built an elaborate etiquette around a figure everyone quietly knows matters enormously. In Venezuela, discussing pay carries little of that social weight, but the number itself has become genuinely unstable, requiring active renegotiation of what "salary" even means (which currency, which bonuses, denominated how) far more frequently than the actual conversation about it happens. A Swiss expectation transplanted to Caracas — expecting one clean number that holds — would be quietly absurd within a few months of inflation; a Venezuelan frankness about comparing pay, transplanted to Zurich, would likely land as a genuine social misstep.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A respondent to a question about Venezuelan freelancers on platforms like Upwork described the practice colloquially as "matar tigres" (hunting tigers) — informal, resourceful income-generation covering everything from translation work to home repairs — framing it as a widely understood, unremarkable part of how professionals there now supplement or replace formal-sector wages.*

> *Quora — A response comparing salary-discussion taboos across countries noted that in cultures like Switzerland's, the discomfort isn't really about the number itself but about what a comparison implies — that pay differences reflect judgments about relative worth that people would rather not confront directly with a colleague.*

> *gringojobs.substack.com — A newsletter aimed at relocating professionals highlighted Venezuela's dollar-income remote work scene as a genuinely underrated option for foreign employers, framing local talent quality as high and cost as favorable, without minimizing that this labor pool exists specifically because domestic formal-sector wages had become untenable.*

> *Upreer (Swiss salary negotiation guide, aggregated advisor commentary) — A recurring piece of advice for newcomers to the Swiss job market was to never mention a number before receiving a formal offer, with one advisor noting that international candidates who raised salary too early were sometimes perceived as poorly prepared for local professional norms, regardless of their actual qualifications.*

> *Quora — A response to a question about IT salary expectations in Geneva specifically flagged how sharply pay varies not just by role but by canton and sector, warning newcomers against anchoring expectations to a single national "average salary" figure they might have seen in a general cost-of-living article.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Venezuela for work, the practical priority is currency and structure, not the headline number — confirm whether you're paid in dollars, bolívares, or the blended "ingreso integral" system, and revisit that arrangement often, because the ground genuinely moves under it. If you're moving to Switzerland, the practical priority is etiquette — come to any negotiation armed with market data and your own results, never a colleague's number, and never raise compensation before an offer is on the table. The honest thing I'd tell a friend heading to either country: in Venezuela, ask about the money constantly, because it changes; in Switzerland, ask about it exactly once, at exactly the right moment, because how you ask matters as much as what you're asking for.
