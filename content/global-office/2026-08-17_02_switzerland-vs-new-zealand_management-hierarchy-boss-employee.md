**Two Flat Hierarchies, One of Them Lying to You: Switzerland vs. New Zealand**

**🇨🇭 Switzerland · 🇳🇿 New Zealand**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Both countries will tell you, sincerely, that they don't really do hierarchy. In New Zealand this turns out to be mostly true: you will call the CEO by her first name within a week and mean it. In Switzerland it is a more careful kind of true — you will also call your director by his first name, share a coffee machine with him, and watch him make the final decision alone anyway, exactly as planned, after listening to everyone with great sincerity. Flatness, it turns out, comes in a version you can trust and a version you should verify.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use last names and formal titles (Herr/Frau, Monsieur/Madame) until invited to switch | Don't present a finished plan without prior alignment — it reads as arrogance, not initiative |
| Treat deadlines as fixed; build in real buffer before commitments | Don't fill meeting silences — long pauses are thinking time, not discomfort |
| Expect a genuine consultation process before a decision, even if the boss decides alone at the end | Don't assume Zurich-style directness travels to Geneva; Romand offices run warmer and slower on trust |
| Bring documented, precise proposals — Swiss colleagues respect preparation over charisma | Don't expect quick friendship; workplace trust is built over months, not weeks |
| Match the formality of your specific canton and industry before loosening up | Don't mistake the flat-looking org chart for flat authority — the top still decides |

#### 🇳🇿 New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use first names with everyone, including senior leadership, from day one | Don't lean on a foreign job title or degree to establish authority — skills and delivery do that here |
| Voice disagreement with a manager directly if you have a reasoned case — it's expected, not risky | Don't micromanage or hover; it reads as a lack of trust and is disliked broadly |
| Keep work talk casual — jeans and sneakers are standard outside banking and law | Don't assume weekend or after-hours availability; the work/home line is firmly kept |
| Show up on time and leave on time — a 5pm finish is normal, not a sign of low commitment | Don't over-formalize emails or meetings; excessive process reads as bureaucratic, not professional |
| Build informal social credibility — a surprising amount of trust runs through shared drinks and small talk | Don't assume flat structure means no hierarchy at all — senior executives still make the final call |

---

Switzerland's paradox is structural. Hofstede Insights scores the country at 34 on power distance — genuinely low by global standards — and multiple business-culture guides, including Commisceo Global and Expatica, describe a workplace where employees address managers informally, sit near them, and are heard out in real consultation. But the same sources are consistent on where that stops: major decisions remain with senior leadership, and once made, they are received without further debate. iamExpat's comparison of Swiss and American offices notes that expats from top-down cultures often overcorrect by presenting finished plans unilaterally, which Swiss colleagues read as a breach of the consensus process rather than efficiency. The result is a hierarchy that is genuinely more accessible than, say, Germany's — federal statistics agency Destatis-adjacent guides regularly cite Switzerland as less title-obsessed than its northern neighbor — but no less final in its outcomes.

New Zealand's flatness runs deeper and further down the org chart. Hofstede puts the country at 22 on power distance, among the lowest scores globally, and the practical guides bear that out with more texture than Switzerland's: New Zealand employees are described across Commisceo, the Cultural Atlas, and recruitment-firm guides as expected to voice disagreement with a manager directly, skip hierarchical lines when a problem calls for it, and address the most senior person in the building by first name without a second thought. Micromanagement is flagged repeatedly, across nearly every source consulted, as one of the fastest ways to alienate a New Zealand team. The flip side, per Quora threads on Auckland's professional culture, is that some newcomers — particularly from more credential-driven markets — find that skill alone doesn't always carry the day; informal social ties and shared history at the pub can matter as much as the résumé.

**The Reckoning**

The instructive contrast is not who has hierarchy and who doesn't — both have it — but where each culture locates the illusion. Switzerland offers the appearance of flatness (first names, shared coffee, real consultation) while keeping the substance of hierarchy intact at the moment of decision. New Zealand offers the substance of flatness — real challengeable authority, real line-skipping — while retaining just enough formal structure that senior executives still, in the end, make the call. An American manager transplanted to Zurich who mistakes the informality for genuine flat authority will blindside a director by announcing something rather than proposing it. The same manager in Auckland who assumes the office is a free-for-all will be surprised at how much unwritten social capital, built outside the building, quietly decides who gets heard.

There's also a pace mismatch worth flagging for anyone choosing between the two. Swiss consultation is genuinely time-consuming — expat guides describe Swiss decision cycles as notably slower than the American norm, precisely because the input-gathering is not theater. New Zealand's flatness, by contrast, tends to speed decisions up, not slow them down, because there are fewer formal steps between an idea and a green light, even if the final signature still belongs to someone senior.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Team Blind — Someone comparing life in San Francisco to a move to Zurich described the Swiss workplace as formal in a way that initially read as cold, only to realize months in that the same colleagues who used surnames and rarely socialized after hours were also the ones who took the longest, most careful silences in meetings seriously as a sign of respect, not disengagement.*

> *Quora — A contributor answering a question about Auckland's professional culture pushed back on the idea that New Zealand workplaces are purely meritocratic, describing conversations that circled back to shared drinking history and old school ties in ways that felt, to an outsider, oddly disconnected from actual project performance.*

> *Upreer expat guide (Switzerland) — A relocation guide aimed at new arrivals flagged that expats coming from top-down corporate cultures tend to make the same mistake early: presenting a fully finished plan in a meeting instead of socializing it beforehand, which Swiss colleagues interpreted as trying to bypass the consensus process rather than showing initiative.*

> *Reddit — One poster weighing a Zurich relocation against staying in the Bay Area noted that while total compensation dropped, the calculation still favored Switzerland once lower effective taxes in certain cantons and a genuinely enforced end to the workday were factored in.*

> *iamExpat — A comparison piece on Swiss versus American offices highlighted that trust in a Swiss team has to be earned through consistency over an extended period, and that expats hoping to speed up that process through charm or fast wins were often left waiting regardless of performance.*

### Conclusion

If informal titles and first names are the only signal you're reading, both countries will tell you the same reassuring story. The one that matters is what happens after the meeting ends: in New Zealand, the discussion you had in the room is very often the decision; in Switzerland, the discussion was real, but the decision still belongs to one person, made afterward, on their own terms. Pick Switzerland if you want your input taken seriously within a structure that still resolves cleanly at the top. Pick New Zealand if you want the structure itself to bend around good arguments. And if a friend asked me which one to actually enjoy working in — that depends entirely on how much you like being told, gently, that you're wrong.
