# Latvia Wants a Month's Notice and a Reason; Ireland Wants Your Two-Year Update

🇱🇻 Latvia · 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Latvian employee who wishes to resign must, unless the contract or a collective agreement says otherwise, give one month's written notice, according to Eurofound. An Irish job hunter, according to a recruiter survey, is likelier than not to be thinking about the exit before the induction binder has been opened: the Cpl Employment Market Monitor found that nearly half of respondents expected to stay in their next role for no more than two years, and about a third expected to leave within the first twelve months. One country treats the employment contract as a formal arrangement with procedures. The other treats it as a pleasant introduction. Both are in the European Union, both use the euro, and both will hand you a laptop on day one. Only one will find your imminent departure unremarkable, and the answer depends on what you intend to do next.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Latvia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the probation clause before signing; since 2022 the contract must state whether there is a probationary period and how long it lasts | Assume a verbal promise about probation survives contact with the written contract |
| Put your resignation in writing and diarise the one-month notice period | Announce your departure by chat message and expect the clock to start |
| Check cv.lv and other listings for comparable salary ranges, which Latvian employers must publish | Accept the first figure on the grounds that it sounds large in euros |
| Keep your Latvian improving even if your office speaks English | Expect every well-paid role to be open to non-speakers |
| Ask about the written reason and the legal ground if a dismissal ever arrives | Treat a dismissal notice as final without reading what it cites |

### In Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep applying while employed and tell nobody until an offer exists | Resign first and negotiate afterwards |
| Collect a written reference before you leave, since large employers often provide only the minimum | Rely on a goodwill handshake for a later background check |
| Wrap up your projects tidily; the leaving is remembered longer than the staying | Disappear into annual leave during your final fortnight |
| Treat a pattern of short stints as the thing recruiters notice, and explain it | Assume one early exit is career-ending; one rarely is |
| Compare the pay uplift of switching against the cost of a shorter CV | Assume loyalty will be rewarded with a matching raise |

## Latvia

Latvian employment law is an exercise in procedure. The Labour Law requires a written contract before work begins, and its content is strictly defined, according to Eurofound's country profile. An employer who wishes to dismiss may do so only on one of eleven legally specified grounds, all tied to the employee's conduct or ability, or to economic, organisational or technological measures. The employer must give written reasons. Notice generally cannot be given while an employee is temporarily incapacitated for work, and a trade union member cannot be dismissed without the union's consent. For the expatriate arriving from a jurisdiction where termination is a Friday-afternoon event, this is a considerable adjustment in the direction of security.

The employee's side of the ledger is lighter. Resignation requires one month's written notice unless something shorter has been agreed. That is not a trap, but it does mean that the move from one Riga employer to another is rarely instantaneous, and a new employer has to be prepared to wait. The arrangement favours the person who stays, and gently inconveniences the person who does not. What it does not do is produce a culture of lifelong attachment. A study from Riga Technical University, using the EU-SILC survey database, found that job change in Latvia correlated more closely with an employee's age than with regional labour-market conditions such as the unemployment rate or the number of vacancies, though the authors describe the correlations overall as weak. In plain terms, Latvians move when they are young and settle when they are not, which describes most of Europe and a fair number of marriages.

Sentiment, however, is not enthusiasm. Gallup's 2025 data for Latvia puts employee engagement at 20 percent, level with the global average and well above the European regional average of 12 percent, while 37 percent of employees reported a lot of stress the previous day. Some 59 percent said it is a good time to find a job where they live, against 57 percent across Europe. The picture is of a workforce that is not unhappy, not euphoric, and aware that options exist. Hofstede's published country scores add a cultural gloss: Latvia scores 9 for masculinity, among the lowest in his data and against Ireland's 68, and 63 for uncertainty avoidance against Ireland's 35. A low-masculinity, moderately cautious culture will tend to value stable relationships over conspicuous career moves. The mobility is there. The swagger is not.

## Ireland

Ireland is where the career move has been professionalised. The Cpl survey, published in 2016, found that 49 percent of job hunters cited a lack of opportunities in their own organisation as a reason for looking, around a quarter blamed a bad manager, and 17 percent cited salary. The Irish Times reported in 2022 that an HR consultant at Mazars put average vacancy rates between 1 and 4 percent, which she described as quite significant, and that people were securing significant pay increases by switching jobs. Some candidates, she said, withdrew late in interviews after playing employers against one another. This is not loyalty in the Latvian sense. It is a market, and Dublin treats it as one.

The costs are not zero, and the same article is candid about them. It cites the phenomenon known as shift shock, in which the job turns out to differ from the one described at interview, and it advises candidates to protect the CV by avoiding moves that clash with their experience and long-term goals. A single short stint is forgivable. A pattern is a question. The Irish worker, in other words, enjoys an open exit and a keen audience, provided the résumé tells a coherent story. The same dataset supports the temperament: Ireland scores 35 on uncertainty avoidance, which describes a population relatively relaxed about the unfamiliar, and 68 on masculinity, which describes one that rewards visible achievement. Advancement is a reason to move, and moving is how it is demonstrated.

The working week is longer than in Latvia, which is a small surprise to those who assume Atlantic Europe is the leisurely one. OECD data, as compiled by TheGlobalEconomy.com, puts average annual hours worked at roughly 1,633 per worker in Ireland in 2023, against about 1,548 in Latvia. Mobility in Ireland is therefore paired with a heavier load, which may be the reason people are keen to find a better one. The global backdrop is similar: PwC's 2022 survey of 52,195 workers in 44 countries and territories found that one in five expected to change employers within twelve months and that 71 percent named pay as a main motivation. Ireland is merely the place where that statistic is on the induction slide.

## The Reckoning

On protection, Latvia wins and is unembarrassed about it. An eleven-ground dismissal test, written reasons, and a bar on dismissal during incapacity make a Latvian employee harder to remove than most newcomers expect. In Ireland the same employee is free to leave, free to be courted, and answerable for the pattern. The Latvian system rewards staying with legal certainty. The Irish system rewards moving with pay, and punishes it only through the CV. The first is a safety net. The second is a trampoline, with the usual question about who is holding it.

On everything else the comparison is closer than the stereotypes suggest. Both countries have workers who change jobs when pay or prospects stall, and both have employers who would prefer they did not. The practical difference is how much friction attends the departure: a month and a form in Latvia, a quiet conversation with a recruiter in Ireland. Latvia's advantage is predictability, and its disadvantage is a smaller market in which, forum commenters note, well-paid roles are limited. Ireland offers the reverse: scale, churn, and the occasional counteroffer, with no guarantee that the churn will land on you favourably.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/latvia — a poster advising on a Riga tech-support team-lead offer of 2,600 euros gross called it okay-ish, adequate for basic living but not generous for a role leading developers. They pointed out that Latvian employers must publish salary ranges, so comparable roles can be checked, and that groceries cost about what they do in Denmark while salaries are much lower.*

> *Reddit r/latvia — another thread on the IT market described junior roles as hard to land at the moment, with the high salaries going mostly to experienced people. Commenters said demonstrable skill counts for more than a degree, though some employers and clients still expect one, and that onsite support roles often require fluent Latvian in a small local market.*

> *Boards.ie — a poster weighing whether to leave a first job after six months for a move to Cork was told by most replies that it is not a career breaker for a strong graduate. The consensus was that a pattern of frequent changes worries employers more than one early exit, that one should search while employed and say nothing until an offer exists, and that a written reference is worth securing because large companies often give only minimal ones.*

> *ExpatExchange — a Riga-based member answering a newcomer's question about finding work said that foreigners find jobs across hospitality, IT, finance and education, with service work in restaurants, bars and hotels also common, and retail, construction and manufacturing offering openings as well.*

## Conclusion

The honest summary is that Latvia is the better country in which to be fired and Ireland is the better country in which to be hired away, and a sensible expatriate will establish which of the two risks is closer before signing anything. If the aim is a stable job with procedural protection and a manageable cost of living, Latvia offers the first two and a tight budget; the forum evidence on salaries suggests the budget deserves a spreadsheet before the contract deserves a signature. If the aim is to build a career by moving between employers, Ireland offers the open market, provided the story on the CV holds together.

Read the probation clause in Riga, collect the reference in Dublin, and remember that in both countries the employer is also making a decision about loyalty, and has a considerably larger team of people to help it.
