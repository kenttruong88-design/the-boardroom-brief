# Argentina Will Kiss You Hello, Spain Will Feed You Until Eight: The Office Social Calendar, Audited

🇦🇷 Argentina · 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Eight in ten Argentine workers say they have friends they met at work, yet barely a third say their employer organises anything to bring colleagues together. Spain runs the opposite experiment: the company calendar is thick with rituals, from the eleven o'clock café run to the Thursday round of beers, and attendance is treated less as a courtesy than as a performance review conducted in a bar. Anyone weighing a move should know which of these two countries expects you to build friendships on your own initiative and which expects you to turn up with an appetite.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Argentina

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet each colleague in the morning, usually with a single cheek kiss, and follow their lead on contact | Slip to your desk unannounced in an office of forty and hope the greeting round has been waived |
| Take your turn when the mate is passed around | Keep a private box of blueberries when everyone else is sharing snacks |
| Warm up with family, sport and weekend talk before any agenda | Open a first meeting with figures and a deadline |
| Use titles such as Doctor or Licenciado with senior staff | Contradict a superior in public as you might in a flatter office |
| Leave a note or small gift on a colleague's desk for their birthday | Assume your employer will organise a cake |
| Be punctual for interviews and formal appointments | Take offence when an ordinary meeting starts late |

### Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the mid-morning café run, usually around 10:30 or 11:00 | Skip it and eat toast alone at your keyboard |
| Buy the next round when a colleague buys one | Ask to split the bill |
| Take your lunch away from the desk, preferably as a menú del día | Eat a sandwich at your desk and call it efficiency |
| Address senior staff as usted until invited to use tú | Assume first-name informality in banking, law or public administration |
| Treat the Thursday or Friday caña invitation as an obligation, especially in your first six months | Treat it as an optional extra and then wonder why nobody tells you things |
| Schedule calls outside the early afternoon | Expect an answer between two and five |

## Argentina

The numbers suggest an office culture where the social life is organic rather than managed. According to a Randstad survey of 4,051 people across Argentina, Chile and Uruguay, reported by Los Andes in 2025, 81 percent of Argentine respondents had friends who began as colleagues, and 79 percent thought friendship at work was possible. Only 56 percent said their employer encouraged it, and just 36 percent said it regularly organised socialising such as after-office gatherings. Birthdays fare worse: 17 percent said they are always marked, 34 percent sometimes, and 48 percent never. The warmth is real, but it is largely self-service.

The texture of it is described with some bemusement by foreigners. An American who has lived in Argentina since 2007 told La Nación that colleagues greet each person every morning, usually with a kiss, which at nine o'clock with forty people she found demanding. Mate circulates, snacks are shared, and Friday may arrive with cumbia on the speakers. Arrival times are loose enough that a German intern was startled to find a message announcing a half-hour delay treated as unremarkable. The ILO's most recent figures, cited by Chequeado, put actual weekly hours at about 34.6 in 2022, well below the 48-hour legal ceiling, which suggests the office is a place where hours are less contested than relationships.

Those relationships carry weight beyond pleasantries. Expat.com notes that trust is built through informal conversation about family, sport and social life, and that decision-making is often concentrated at the top in traditional firms. The same American observed that personal ties can outweigh professional skill, citing a colleague promoted partly because he played football with the boss on Saturdays. The formal frame is conventional enough: Expat.com describes a standard day of nine to six with a one-hour lunch written into sector agreements, and says punctuality matters for professional appointments even if rapport-building lets meetings drift. Dinner begins around nine, and a poster on the ExpatsBA forum notes that working the morning after a late night is an adjustment of its own.

## Spain

If Argentina's rituals are informal, Spain's are scheduled, and the schedule is long. According to the OECD and European Commission figures reported by The Local, Spanish employees worked 1,658 hours per worker in 2023 against 1,556 in the eurozone, while GDP per hour worked was about 53 dollars against 61. The extra hours are not entirely spent on spreadsheets. Offices empty for lunch from around two, often into the afternoon, and the working day runs until seven or eight. A British columnist in The Local put it neatly: British colleagues are two hours into their afternoon when Spanish ones are still finishing lunch.

The social machinery is correspondingly dense. A mid-morning café run is routine, and one practical guide calls skipping it a networking mistake because favours and office politics are traded there. The menú del día, typically three courses with wine and coffee for 10 to 15 euros according to The Local, is how business is conducted, and eating a sandwich at your desk is frowned upon. A Seville resident writing on Substack describes Friday lunches that begin with drinks, run two to three hours and migrate to a bar, and even a Tuesday lunch among friends that ended at ten at night. Dinner is shifting earlier, but only slowly: Il Post, summarising the New York Times, reports half of dinner reservations are now at nine o'clock, up from 27 percent a decade ago.

The edges are firmer than they look. Summer hours, typically eight to three from mid-June to mid-September according to one expat handbook, soften the rota, while the Christmas dinner, a sit-down meal followed by an open bar, can end in a nightclub at four in the morning and is best attended with colleagues' Monday faces in mind. Hofstede Insights puts Spain's power distance at 57 against Argentina's 49, a modest gap that nonetheless shows up in forms of address, with usted expected in traditional sectors until a colleague says puedes tutearme. The Local also describes presentismo, the habit of remaining at your desk after the work is done until the boss leaves, and loud offices in which people shout across rooms. The ritual is generous, but it is also attendance-taking by other means.

## The Reckoning

Argentina offers the more forgiving apprenticeship. The rituals are physical and immediate, a kiss, a gourd of mate, a shared packet of biscuits, and nobody is formally keeping score. The risk is the reverse of Spain's: with less than half of employers organising anything, newcomers who wait to be invited may wait for a long time, and personal connection may matter more than the evidence of their work. Late starts and flexible time make the day pleasant, provided you do not mistake looseness for absence of hierarchy, which a Buenos Aires cross-cultural consultancy, Landing Pad BA, describes as marked by titles such as Doctor and Licenciado that signal authority and are expected to be respected.

Spain demands more of your calendar and your liver, but gives you a clear script. The round, the caña, the Christmas dinner and the long lunch are legible, and the unwritten rule is mostly that you attend. The cost is a day that stretches to eight in the evening and a dinner that arrives after your body has given up on the idea. Choose Argentina if you want company without a rota, and Spain if you want a rota with company.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Local Spain — a British columnist living in Spain says office life starts later and ends later than at home, with some colleagues guarding the long lunch as sacred while others would happily take thirty minutes and leave at six. Personal space is minimal, touching during conversation is routine, and British visitors tend to read it as flirting when it usually is not.*

> *The Local Spain — another column on Spanish offices lists the rituals nobody mentions at interview: the late-morning café trip for coffee, toast and gossip, deals done over a set-price lunch, a smoking contingent that gathers by a bathroom window, and employees who stay put after finishing work until the boss leaves.*

> *Substack — a resident of Seville describes Friday as the day work finishes around one or two, drinks come before lunch, and lunch drifts into a café and then into the evening, with groups of eight or more at every table. She reads the long meal as the actual mechanism for building connections, as opposed to a delay in getting back to work.*

> *La Nación — an American woman who has worked in Argentina since 2007 finds the morning kiss for every colleague hard to sustain, shares snacks because holding them back feels impossible, and respects the mate ritual while worrying about germs whenever someone is ill. She also thinks personal loyalties can count for more than competence when promotions come around.*

> *ExpatsBA forum — a Buenos Aires resident says meetings often begin late even when everyone confirmed the day before, though people arrive on time for job interviews and exams. They add that going to work after a late night out takes some getting used to.*

## Conclusion

Pick Argentina if you are comfortable with warmth that is unstructured and relationships that function as currency; bring snacks, learn the mate etiquette, and take the initiative on friendship because your employer probably will not. Pick Spain if you prefer a visible rhythm: attend the café run, accept the first caña, buy your round, and plan to eat dinner late whatever the clock says. In either country, the formal job description is only half the contract.

In both countries, the office party is optional right up to the moment you decline it.
