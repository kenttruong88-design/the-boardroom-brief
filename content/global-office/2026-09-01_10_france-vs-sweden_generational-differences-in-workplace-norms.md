# First Names and Formal Titles: France, Sweden, and the Generational Fight Over Who Runs the Meeting

🇫🇷 France · 🇸🇪 Sweden

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In France, a new hire spends her first month working out who she may address with the informal "tu" — and gets it wrong at least once, memorably. In Sweden, hers is spent remembering that the woman refilling the coffee at fika is the CEO. Both countries are quietly renegotiating what their youngest workers owe the org chart, and both are discovering the chart itself is the argument. One legislated a hierarchy and now watches junior staff test its edges; the other dismantled its hierarchy decades ago and now watches junior staff wonder what replaced it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇷 France
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use "vous" and full titles with new colleagues until invited to switch | Assume an early invitation to use first names means real informality has arrived |
| Learn the org chart early — who reports to whom shapes who speaks | Skip the two-hour lunch; real decisions happen there |
| Invoke the *droit à la déconnexion* if contacted outside working hours | Reply to after-hours messages "just to be safe" — it resets the norm for everyone else |
| Ask early whether your contract is a CDD or a CDI, and what the path to the other looks like | Treat a CDD as a formality; plan finances as if it could run for years |
| Dress with deliberate care; presentation reads as professional seriousness | Underestimate how much unspoken hierarchy governs who speaks first |

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use first names with everyone from day one, including the CEO | Default to titles or surnames — it registers as odd, deliberate distance |
| Protect fika time, both taking it and offering it | Skip fika to "look productive" — it reads as missing the culture, not diligence |
| Expect consensus-building before decisions are finalised, and speak up when asked | Push for a fast, top-down call; it stalls the process and marks you impatient |
| Take your full parental and vacation leave — genuinely expected, including of men | Assume visible overtime signals ambition; it's more often read as poor planning |
| Contribute in meetings regardless of seniority — flat structure expects input from all | Wait to be assigned every task; initiative is assumed, not requested |

## France

French hierarchy is infrastructure, not a vibe. Hofstede's Power Distance Index puts France at 68, well above the European norm, with Uncertainty Avoidance at 86 — formally ranked, procedurally cautious, allergic to junior improvisation. Into this has arrived a generation that, per EDHEC's pan-European Gen Z Career Mindset Study, actively favours flexibility, career pivots, and smaller employers over hierarchy — the opposite of what the org chart rewards.

The complicating factor is the contract. INSEE data shows France's employment rate at 69.3% in 2025, even as youth NEET (not in employment, education, or training) rose to 13.1% in Q1 2026, and over half of jobs held by 15-to-24-year-olds are fixed-term (CDD) rather than the permanent CDI that carries France's real protections. Younger employees are formally entitled to rights codified since 2017 in the *droit à la déconnexion*, but those rights apply fully only once they clear a CDD gauntlet that can run years — a delay landlords and mortgage lenders both notice.

What younger French workers have done is cite the hierarchy back rather than tear it down: the right to disconnect gets invoked by name, contract terms negotiated earlier. The structure survives; what changed is who quotes the rulebook back.

## Sweden

Sweden's org chart was mostly retired last century, leaving little for its youngest workers to push against. Hofstede scores Sweden at 29 on Power Distance — among the world's lowest — and at 5 on Masculinity, the lowest of any country it measures, reflecting genuine discomfort with displayed authority. Managers describe themselves as coaches, not commanders; fika, the twice-daily coffee ritual, functions as decision-making infrastructure rather than a break from it.

Sweden's generational story is less about hierarchy than about how much negotiating the state has already done. The "daddy quota," introduced in 1995, reserves parental leave exclusively for fathers on a use-it-or-lose-it basis; the share taking leave rose from 44% to 77% almost immediately, and nine in ten now take some. Yet researchers tracking the policy's slower second act find workplace culture still lags the law — disconnecting during leave remains the hardest part for many fathers, evidence that even a flat, generous system carries an "ideal worker" assumption younger employees now openly decline. The union structure reinforces the floor beneath them: per Lund University sociologist Anders Kjellberg's research for Institut Montaigne, 69% of Swedish workers are union members against 9% in France, giving even junior staff a collectively bargained baseline under most negotiations.

What Swedish Gen Z workers report struggling with, per Deloitte's Global Gen Z and Millennial Survey 2026, isn't hierarchy but something harder to name: average early-career tenure of 1.1 years, with roughly one in three planning to change jobs within twelve months. Flatness and consensus alone have not produced loyalty. Absent a hierarchy to resent, younger Swedish workers seem to be testing something else — whether the job is worth staying in at all.

## The Reckoning

The counterintuitive part is which system produces more open confrontation. France's rigid hierarchy generates a generation that fights through official channels — laws, contract categories, procedure — because the structure supplies the tools. Sweden's flat hierarchy, having removed those channels, leaves younger workers fewer levers to pull and, per the tenure data, a stronger tendency to simply leave.

There is a second irony: France's bureaucratic labour code produces a workforce that names its grievances fluently, while Sweden's egalitarian culture produces one that struggles to locate what's wrong when something is wrong — no visible villain to name. Rigid systems generate articulate complaints. Flat systems generate quiet exits.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Respondents to threads on working in France as a young person converged on one complaint: finding a first real job is markedly harder than in comparable European countries, and the CDI is discussed the way other cultures discuss tenure — years spent qualifying while your current position stays provisional.*

> *Blind — A tech worker previously in a competitive Asian market described Swedish flat-hierarchy culture as close to a dream by comparison, citing managers who genuinely deferred to team judgment rather than performing consultation before a decision already made.*

> *Tribune India / social media account — A widely viewed post from an Indian tech worker in Sweden detailing the harder edges of expat life — living costs, isolation, visa anxiety tied to employment — drew heavy backlash from expats and Swedes correcting specifics, notably the actual grace period after job loss. The exchange doubled as an unplanned tutorial in how differently "you're on your own" plays out inside a system built on collective bargaining.*

> *Personal essay, US professional in Paris — An American who took a corporate role in Paris expected after-hours email culture to mirror New York, and was struck instead by colleagues working calmly, without the coiled tension American overtime tends to carry, even when the hours logged weren't so different.*

> *Substack, US professional in Stockholm — An American data scientist recounted managers who trusted her judgment on unfamiliar problems without close supervision, treating consensus-seeking as how confidence in a new hire actually got built, not as a bottleneck.*

## Conclusion

The instinct is to call France the hierarchical country and Sweden the equal one and stop there, but the more useful distinction is where each system put its friction. France put it in the contract — a legible boundary between the protected and the not-yet-protected, which at least tells a 24-year-old what she's fighting for. Sweden dissolved that boundary and made its remaining frictions harder to name, which may be why so many of its youngest workers choose to leave a job rather than argue about it.

Neither brochure mentions this part, because neither government particularly wants to advertise it: a codified hierarchy, however maddening, comes with a map. A flat one, however pleasant, sometimes just comes with a coffee break.
