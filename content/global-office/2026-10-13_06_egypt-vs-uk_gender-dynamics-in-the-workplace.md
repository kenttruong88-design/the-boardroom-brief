**More Likely to Get the Callback, Less Likely to Apply**

🇪🇬 Egypt · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Egypt, a woman applying for a white-collar job is statistically more likely to get a callback than a man who applies for the same role — and statistically far less likely to have applied in the first place. In the UK, the gap isn't in who gets asked; it's in who ends up in the room that decides pay, and by how much, decades into mandatory disclosure. Two countries, two very different gender gaps, and in both, the paperwork says more progress than the office actually delivers.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇪🇬 Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a female candidate's application to be read closely — employer view rates run higher for women | Assume women are underrepresented in a given office because they're not getting looked at |
| Understand that unpaid care work, not hiring bias alone, is the bigger barrier to women staying in the workforce | Treat low female labor-force participation as proof that women aren't welcome at work |
| Ask directly about parental leave and childcare support if you're a woman relocating with a family | Assume formal nursery or childcare infrastructure will be there if you need it — coverage is thin |
| Notice that attitudes toward female bosses often come from peers as much as from management | Assume resistance to a female manager, where it exists, is only a top-down problem |
| Recognize software and IT as a genuine outlier — female managers there command a pay premium, not a gap | Apply one blanket assumption about pay across every sector |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to see a published gender pay gap figure — it's been mandatory since 2017 | Assume disclosure alone has closed, or is close to closing, the gap |
| Expect large employers (20,000+ staff) to be narrowing their gap fastest | Assume bigger company size means a bigger gap — the data runs the other way |
| Watch financial services specifically for a wider-than-average gap | Assume the private sector overall lags the public sector uniformly — it varies sharply by industry |
| Expect mandatory employer action plans on closing the gap starting spring 2027 | Treat the current reporting-only regime as the permanent rulebook |
| Ask where senior leadership actually sits by gender, not just what the average pay figure says | Take a narrowing average gap as proof women are reaching senior, higher-paid roles at equal rates |

Egypt's gender gap isn't really about whether women get hired when they apply — it's about whether they apply at all. Data from the recruitment platform Wuzzuf, reported by Enterprise, found that women's applications actually draw more employer attention than men's: view rates run 6.2 percentage points higher, and the "contact unlock" rate used as a proxy for interview invitations runs 1.4 points higher. Yet women make up only 29% of applications despite being 35% of active job seekers, and the gap widens hard with age — men apply at roughly double the rate of women by 25, triple by 36, five times by 43. The real constraint sits outside the hiring funnel: married Egyptian women spend seven times as many hours on unpaid care work as men, and nursery coverage for children under four sits below 8%, per World Bank and OECD figures. Labor force participation for women runs in the high teens to low twenties depending on the source — CAPMAS put it at 20.7% in 2025, OECD figures show it falling from roughly 23% in 2010 to about 16.5% in 2024 — against male participation above 70%, despite women making up 53% of university graduates. The World Bank estimates closing the employment gap entirely could grow Egypt's economy by as much as 56%.

What that gap produces inside the office is a culture split between formal opportunity and informal resistance. Hofstede Insights scores Egypt at 70 on Power Distance and a striking 80 on Uncertainty Avoidance — a culture that defers to hierarchy and strongly prefers predictable, well-worn roles, which tracks with how slowly workplace norms shift even where formal policy is neutral. A widely read first-person essay in Egyptian Streets described an office "full of women at every level" where female colleagues nonetheless criticized female leaders more harshly than male ones, with lines like "I just can't handle a woman for a boss" coming from other women, not just men. The author's read: every woman in a leadership position ends up carrying her entire gender's reputation on her back.

The UK's gap is better measured but no less stubborn. PwC's 2026 Gender Pay Gap Report puts the mean hourly gap at 10.7%, down from 11.2% the year before and 13.4% when mandatory reporting began in 2017 — real, if slow, progress. The largest employers (20,000-plus staff) are narrowing fastest, down 1.6 points, their lowest gap since reporting started; smaller firms move slower and more erratically. Financial services remains the stubborn outlier, a wider-than-average gap PwC attributes to women's underrepresentation in senior, higher-paid roles rather than unequal pay for the same job. At the current rate, PwC's own analysis says closing the gap could take over 30 years. Starting spring 2027, employers with 250-plus staff face a new requirement: not just publishing the number, but a mandatory action plan explaining what they're doing about it. Hofstede's numbers describe a culture built for individual, transparent accountability — Individualism of 89, among the highest measured, against Egypt's 25 — but transparency turns out to be a different achievement than closing the gap itself.

**The Reckoning**

Put plainly: Egypt's problem is mostly about who shows up, and the UK's is mostly about who gets paid once they're already in the room. An Egyptian employer can point to real, measurable openness to hiring women and still preside over a workforce roughly four-fifths male, because the barrier was never really the interview. A British employer can point to eighteen years of mandatory, falling pay-gap numbers and still be, by its own data, three decades from parity, because disclosure creates pressure but not promotion. Transparency and openness aren't the same lever, and mistaking one for the other is how both countries end up with progress on paper that doesn't yet match what their own data shows.

### Conclusion

If you're a woman weighing these two postings, the honest framing isn't "which country treats women better" — it's "which obstacle do you personally face." In Egypt, the fight is largely to be in the workforce at all, against unpaid care demands and thin childcare infrastructure that have nothing to do with whether any given employer wants to hire you. In the UK, the fight is to be paid and promoted at parity once you're already there, against a pay gap that keeps narrowing on schedule but, by the data's own math, won't close within most working lifetimes. Priya's actual advice: in Cairo, ask about childcare and flexible hours before you ask about the title. In London, ask to see where women actually sit on the leadership chart — the published pay-gap number is the headline, but the org chart is the real story.
