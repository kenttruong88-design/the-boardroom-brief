**Office Hours: India Mandates Them, Singapore Merely Asks Nicely**

🇮🇳 India · 🇸🇬 Singapore

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Here is the paradox awaiting the relocating mid-career professional: India, the country with no legal right whatsoever to request remote work, is where your employer is most likely to have spent 2026 arguing about it in a town hall, while Singapore, the country that passed an actual law obliging employers to consider flexible-work requests, is where your colleagues are most likely to simply show up five days a week anyway. Neither country is lying to you. Both are, in their own ways, telling the truth about what flexibility means once it meets a balance sheet.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your specific band or job-level's office-day requirement in writing before you sign — Infosys applies its latest mandate only from level 6A upward, so "hybrid" can mean different things two desks apart. | Assume your recruiter's verbal promise of "flexible, hybrid-friendly culture" survives past your six-month review; several large IT firms tightened policy mid-cycle through 2025 and 2026. |
| Ask explicitly whether variable pay or bonus is linked to badge-swipe attendance, as it now is at some firms — it affects your take-home, not just your commute. | Expect Indian labour law to back you if a manager informally reneges on a remote arrangement; India has no statutory right to request flexible work, unlike Singapore. |
| If offered hybrid, get the specific days of the week fixed in writing — unscheduled hybrid tends to drift toward five days once headcount pressure arrives. | Bank on a global captive centre's "remote forever" line surviving a memo from head office; GCC policy can and does reverse overnight on instruction from abroad. |
| Budget real commute time into your relocation math, not the fantasy fifteen minutes from the floor plan — Bengaluru and Gurugram traffic routinely erases any theoretical hybrid-day savings. | Treat a 2022-era "future of hybrid" survey as current; attendance mandates escalated sharply at major IT services firms between 2023 and 2026. |
| Clarify whether "work from office" compliance is tracked monthly, and by whom, before your first review cycle — several firms now run formal monthly attendance audits. | Assume every company in the sector behaves like the big services firms; smaller product companies and GCCs vary enormously, so negotiate per employer, not per industry reputation. |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| File a formal, written flexible work arrangement request under the Tripartite Guidelines if you want a changed arrangement — it legally compels a documented decision within two months. | Expect the request to be granted just because it was properly filed; approval is assessed on business grounds, and refusal carries no penalty for the employer. |
| Ask what proportion of your specific team already works a scheduled flexible arrangement — roughly seven in ten Singapore firms offer some form, so precedent usually exists somewhere in the building. | Mistake "Singapore has flexible work law" for "Singapore is a remote-work culture" — full five-day office attendance here is reported well above the global average. |
| Find out, before requesting, whether your manager visibly uses flexible arrangements themselves — workers report requests land far better with visible leadership buy-in. | Assume a request will be free of social cost; a large share of employees in recent local surveys cite fear of being seen as less committed as their main hesitation. |
| Confirm which categories your request falls under — flexi-place, flexi-time or flexi-load — since each is assessed and documented differently under the guidelines. | Expect a verbal "we'll sort something out" to count as a formal request; only a documented request triggers the employer's two-month response obligation. |
| Treat the 94% national approval rate on formal requests as a floor, not a ceiling — ask peers what "approved" actually looked like in practice, since outcomes can be modest. | Wait passively for policy to loosen on its own; take-up of the formal process has stayed low, so squeaky wheels get the data points, not quiet ones. |

India's corporate return-to-office movement is less a trend than a reconquest. A widely cited Cisco study found that 96 percent of Indian companies had required staff back into the office, and remarkably, 76 percent of employees reacted positively to the mandate, with 82 percent saying they wanted to be in the office at least a few days a week (People Matters, 2023). That data point tends to surprise outsiders who assume India's enormous IT workforce — the population NASSCOM's own Return to Workplace Survey once described as rapidly embracing hybrid operating models — simply stayed home after the pandemic. It did, for a while. Then the arithmetic of delivery-driven services companies reasserted itself: by 2026, Infosys had widened its in-office mandate down to job level 6A, Wipro required three office days with a six-hour minimum presence, and TCS tied variable pay to attendance compliance on a five-day office week.

None of this is accompanied by law. India has no statutory right-to-request framework for remote or flexible work; attendance policy is whatever the employer's HR circular says it is this quarter, which is precisely why the policy swings so hard and so often. For an incoming professional, the lesson is not that India is anti-remote — plenty of global captive centres and product firms remain genuinely hybrid — but that the floor under any flexibility arrangement is entirely the employer's goodwill, revisable at will.

That goodwill also correlates, inconveniently, with size. NASSCOM's own Return to Workplace Survey found organisations with more than 1,000 employees considerably more likely to formalise a hybrid model than smaller ones, which cuts against the instinct to treat "the IT sector" as a single employer. A ten-person product startup and a 200,000-person services major headquartered in the same city are, for scheduling purposes, operating in different countries. The early-2020s optimism that roughly half of India's white-collar workforce would settle into a comfortable three-office-days norm has not aged well; by 2026 the services majors had moved firmly the other way, and what one regional business outlet called "the battle over return to office" was still being actively fought rather than settled.

Singapore has done the opposite: it legislated the conversation rather than the outcome. The Tripartite Guidelines on Flexible Work Arrangement Requests, which took effect in December 2024, oblige employers to consider formal requests for flexi-place, flexi-time or flexi-load arrangements and respond in writing within two months, assessed against business needs (Ministry of Manpower). Roughly seven in ten Singapore firms already offered some form of scheduled flexible arrangement by 2025, per the Ministry's own tracking, with the Tripartite Alliance for Fair and Progressive Employment Practices and grant schemes such as Job Redesign+ and the EDGE Grant subsidising the job-redesign and remote-tooling costs of compliance.

The approval rate on formal requests sits at a striking 94 percent — except that formal requests remain rare, because, per a 2026 survey co-run by NTUC and the PAP Women's Wing, 36 percent of workers cite fear of appearing less committed as their chief reason for not asking, and 41 percent want to see their own managers model the behaviour first before they will risk filing. Meanwhile, Morgan McKinley's 2026 Workplace Trends Report found 37 percent of Singapore employees in the office five full days a week, against a global average of just 17 percent — second only to Hong Kong in the region. Singapore, in short, built the plumbing for flexibility and then largely declined to turn on the tap.

**The Reckoning.** Put the two side by side and the obvious read — India restrictive, Singapore progressive — inverts almost immediately. India's lack of legal protection produces chaos, not uniformity: ferociously strict at the big services firms, genuinely loose at many GCCs and startups, with your actual experience depending entirely on which letterhead you signed. Singapore's legal protection produces order without much change: a documented, two-month-guaranteed answer to a request that most people are too socially cautious to file in the first place.

Both countries, tellingly, score similarly high on Hofstede's Power Distance index (India around 77, Singapore around 74), which helps explain why in both places the practical answer to "can I work from home" still runs through a manager's personal comfort rather than a universal policy — Singapore merely wrote a clock into that conversation, it did not remove the manager from it. The Long-Term Orientation gap between the two is sharper: Singapore scores far higher, in the low seventies against India's roughly fifty, consistent with its instinct to formalise a process now that compounds predictability later, versus India's tendency to manage flexibility mandate-by-mandate, cycle-by-cycle, as quarterly delivery pressure dictates.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — A commenter described their India-based employer, which had closed its US, UK and EU offices entirely, floating a hybrid plan requiring on-site stints of five to seven days every one or two months, travel paid. At the town hall, of roughly 180 employees present, only four raised their hands in support, and even the managers running the meeting did not personally back full-time on-site work.*

> *Hacker News — Another commenter recalled a manager insisting, pre-pandemic, that people working from home "don't work," then later praising virtual tools as "equally effective" for a distributed US-India team-building exercise once it was convenient. When a company-wide return-to-office order arrived post-pandemic, this person's request to stay fully remote was refused outright, so they left for another job rather than contest it.*

> *Fishbowl (used as a disclosed substitute for an unreachable Quora thread) — In a thread about a major financial firm's US and India offices, one self-identified employee noted that fixed office-day requirements quietly punish anyone who relocated cities for the role, since they are still paying rent elsewhere to be physically present on mandated days, a cost nobody mentions during recruitment.*

> *Hacker News — A Singapore-based commenter described an employer that had studied work-from-home arrangements even before the pandemic and shifted deliberately toward remote work after finding employees measurably more productive off-site, directly contradicting the assumption that Singaporean firms default to presence-as-proof-of-work.*

### Conclusion

The honest advice for someone weighing India against Singapore on this one axis is to stop asking which country is "better" for flexibility and start asking which kind of uncertainty you can live with. In India, you are betting on a specific employer's current mood, reversible by memo, with no law behind you if it turns. In Singapore, you are betting on a specific manager's willingness to let you exercise a right that technically exists, in a culture where filing for it may cost you more socially than simply turning up. Pick the devil whose incentives you understand, not the one whose brochure sounds nicer.

If a friend asked me over a drink, I'd say: India will tell you no with a memo, Singapore will tell you yes with a form, and either way you are still buying the ticket to the office.
