# Thailand Offers Three Months and a Nanny; Sweden Offers Fifteen Months and a Pram Convoy

🇹🇭 Thailand · 🇸🇪 Sweden

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Sweden grants parents 480 days of paid leave per child and then expects both of them to use it. Thailand, until very recently, granted mothers 98 days, of which the final eight were unpaid, and expected the grandmother to manage the rest. Both systems work, in the sense that children are raised in each. They differ chiefly in who is invoiced: the state in Stockholm, the family in Bangkok.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Thailand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check with HR which leave rules apply to you, since the statutory leave is being extended from 98 to 120 days | Assume the full leave is paid in full; under the older rules, only 45 days came from the employer and 45 from social security, capped at 15,000 baht |
| Register with the Social Security Office early if you are in formal employment | Rely on a written promise that maternity leave will not affect your next promotion |
| Budget for a nanny or nursery before the baby arrives; playgroups and nannies exist at a wide range of prices | Expect a universal public nursery place for children under three |
| Ask about the 15 days of paid spousal leave if you are the father | Expect the office to have a nursing room |
| Decide early whether grandparents, a nanny or an international nursery is your plan | Treat international school fees as a footnote in the household budget |

### In Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply to Försäkringskassan within 90 days of your first leave day | Assume your employer pays; the state does, and you must register and apply |
| Give your employer at least two months' notice of your leave dates | Assume your employer will treat the dates as a suggestion |
| Learn the 90 reserved days per parent, which cannot be transferred | Plan around one parent taking all 480 days |
| Save days for later; leave can be used until the child is 12, with limits after age four | Claim the flat-rate days first without checking whether waiting would pay more |
| Join a parent group; it is where the social life of leave takes place | Be surprised by pushchairs in groups of ten |

## Thailand

The Thai arrangement is a patchwork with a ceiling. According to The Nation Thailand, maternity leave of 98 days was split three ways: the employer paid 45 days of wages, the Social Security Office paid the next 45 days up to 15,000 baht, and the final eight days went unpaid. The same report notes that low support pushes many working-class women back to work early, and that some women keep working until delivery to preserve leave for after the birth. A survey by a women's foundation and the Thailand Development Research Institute found that 38.4 percent of respondents feared financial problems from having a child, and 22.9 percent worried about losing bonuses or promotions.

The law is moving. DLA Piper reports that the Senate approved an amendment in September 2025 raising maternity leave from 98 to 120 days, with full wages for up to 60 days, and 15 days of fully paid leave for a spouse. It is the sort of reform that arrives as a headline and is then read very carefully by payroll. Anyone relocating should ask how it applies to their contract, rather than to the law's mood.

Childcare is the larger variable. Expat.com notes that Thailand has no mandatory preschool system, that English-speaking nurseries for ages one to three are in demand in cities, and that live-in nannies cost around 14,000 baht a month and part-time help around 7,000, with no regulation of the trade. International school nurseries can cost tens of thousands of baht monthly. Bangkok Post reporting, admittedly older, put the ratio at one childcare worker per 48 children against a target of one per 20, and found that about 21 percent of Thai children grow up away from their parents, often with grandparents. The same Bangkok Post piece records that many women take less than the 90 days the Labour Protection Law then guaranteed, because they do not want to lose overtime and allowances, and that a state hospital once circulated a memo telling female pharmacy staff to use birth control and to resign if they became pregnant. The hospital withdrew it after public criticism. Law and practice, in other words, can sit several floors apart. The career cost is otherwise quieter. One mother in that report kept the same bank job for a decade, having declined promotions that would take her further from her family, after being warned that maternity leave could slow her advancement.

## Sweden

Sweden's system is one that its administrators can recite from memory. According to Försäkringskassan, parents receive 480 days per child, 390 at the sickness benefit level and 90 at a minimum level of 180 kronor a day. Each parent has 90 days that cannot be transferred to the other. The Local adds that parents with qualifying income can receive close to 80 percent of salary, up to 1,116 kronor a day, that leave may be taken in fractions down to one-eighth of full time, and that the right to leave continues until the child's eighth birthday. Since July 2024 up to 45 days can even be transferred to a grandparent living in Sweden.

The system is built to move men as well as money. Fathers claimed 27.9 percent of parental leave days in 2017, per The Local's report of agency figures, up from about 10 percent in the mid-1990s, and the agency's own economist called a 60/40 split a distant goal. The state, in other words, has reserved the days, and the fathers have taken most, though not all, of the rest. Childcare is cheaper by design: the government announced in January 2026 that it was lowering the maximum preschool fee level and adding an income exemption, though the exact kronor figures are in an ordinance rather than a press release, and a newcomer should check the municipality's current schedule.

Newcomers are not locked out. The Local notes that those moving to Sweden with a child are usually still entitled to leave and benefits, with the amount depending on the child's age when first registered, and parents without qualifying income generally receive 250 kronor a day. One expat's account adds that anyone registered with a Swedish personal number can claim, regardless of where they or the child were born. The catch is administrative: you must apply, and then keep following up, because the agency will not do it for you out of sentiment.

The career consequence is the part Sweden has most deliberately engineered away. A parent of a child under eight can reduce hours to 75 percent, according to one expat's account, and employers are expected to agree to schedules submitted with notice. This is not the absence of a career penalty. It is the redistribution of it across both parents, which is more equitable and, for one's own manager, no less irritating.

## The Reckoning

Head to head, Sweden offers a longer, better-paid and more predictable leave, and charges the household very little for what follows. Thailand offers a shorter, patchier leave that is improving, and substitutes cheap domestic help for public provision. Expat.com's reporting puts full-time help in Thailand at roughly 400 to 800 dollars a month, against 2,500 to 4,000 euros for a private nanny in Germany, which explains why a household with a foreign salary can sometimes manage in Bangkok what it could not in Munich. It does not explain who looks after the nanny's children.

Hofstede Insights scores Sweden at 5 on its masculinity dimension, now called motivation towards achievement and success, against 34 for Thailand, and scores Thailand at 20 for individualism, a figure that squares with a system in which the family, not the state, is the first institution of care. Sweden's low score squares with a state that treats leave as the default. The practical conclusion is that Sweden asks you to follow a procedure, and Thailand asks you to build a household.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — a German co-owner of a Pilates studio in Phuket took two months off after the birth, because a longer break could damage a small business. A nanny minded the baby at the studio, and she had help at home with cleaning and cooking, which she says she likely could not afford in Germany.*

> *Expat.com — a Polish mother found that her Bangkok hotel job no longer fitted life with a baby, quit, and now works remotely in e-commerce customer support, a field she says she would never have considered before motherhood.*

> *Mumsnet — a UK mother-to-be with a Swedish partner asked whether either of them would qualify for Swedish parental benefits. Replies advised phoning Försäkringskassan about the individual case rather than trusting the website, and arranging the baby's personal number, birth certificate and passports quickly.*

> *Relocate to Sweden — an Irish expat accepted the low basic benefit rate when the agency contacted her soon after arrival, and now suspects waiting until she had a job would have paid more. Her main regret was not saving days to cover school holidays.*

> *Relocate to Sweden — a French expat who arrived in 1992 for what was meant to be two years says leave brought her into parent groups that walk, take turns hosting coffee mornings and, to the amusement of locals, fill pavements with prams.*

## Conclusion

If you are choosing on leave alone, Sweden wins and it is not close. If you are choosing on total household cost for someone earning a foreign salary, Thailand's cheaper domestic help can narrow the gap, provided you are comfortable with an unregulated market for it and a leave that is only now being lengthened. In either country, the practical step is to confirm your exact entitlements with the agency or employer before the pregnancy test, not after.

For the Thai side, ask your prospective employer three questions in writing: how many days are paid, who pays them, and whether leave is counted in performance reviews. The survey data suggest workers fear the third more than the first. For the Swedish side, ask when you will be registered, since the benefit rises with qualifying income and the order in which you claim can matter.

Plan the paperwork the way you would plan a mortgage. Sweden will reward punctuality with fifteen months of subsidised contemplation; Thailand will reward preparation with a nanny and a shorter break. Neither will reward optimism, which remains, as ever, unfunded.
