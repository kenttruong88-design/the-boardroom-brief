**Spain and Austria Both Pay You Fourteen Times a Year. Only One of Them Pays You Enough.**

**🇪🇸 Spain · 🇦🇹 Austria**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Ask a payroll clerk in Madrid and one in Vienna how many paychecks their employees collect each year, and you will get the identical answer: fourteen. Ask how much sits inside each one, and the conversation stops being identical very quickly. According to the OECD, the average full-time worker in Austria earned the equivalent of $78,301 in 2025, adjusted for purchasing power — roughly 35 percent more than the $57,779 collected by an equivalent worker in Spain. Both countries will hand you two "extra" paychecks you did not budget for in your first year abroad. Only one of them will make the other twelve feel adequate.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask upfront whether your quoted salary is paid in "12 pagas" or "14 pagas" — it changes your real monthly take-home by roughly a seventh. | Don't assume the annual figure on your contract arrives evenly; two of those "months" land as lump sums in July and December. |
| Negotiate hard before signing — national wages are genuinely low, so a few thousand euros matters more here than almost anywhere else in Western Europe. | Don't expect automatic cost-of-living raises; collective bargaining coverage has shrunk to roughly 14 percent of the workforce. |
| Budget around the two extra "paga" months for annual costs like taxes or travel, since many households structurally rely on them. | Don't bring up your salary casually with Spanish colleagues; discussing pay openly remains broadly taboo outside unionized sectors. |
| Target a public-sector role or a large unionized employer if stability matters more to you than upside. | Don't take the national average salary at face value; the median, and the single most common salary, are both well below it. |
| Check your region's and sector's collective agreement before assuming a national figure applies to your job. | Don't assume a foreign degree or specialist skill set automatically commands a premium; wage compression reaches skilled roles too. |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read job ads literally — Austrian law requires every listing to state a minimum gross salary, so treat that figure as a floor, not an offer. | Don't assume the posted number is final; candidates are expected to negotiate up from it, even when a figure is already published. |
| Divide any annual figure by 14, not 12, to estimate real monthly pay, since the extra two installments arrive before summer and Christmas. | Don't skip checking your sector's Kollektivvertrag; Austria has no single statutory minimum wage, only collective-agreement floors. |
| Run any offer through the Chamber of Labour's gross-to-net calculator before accepting. | Don't compare a quoted gross figure directly to a US or UK net salary; the payroll-deduction gap is larger than most newcomers expect. |
| Confirm whether an ad quotes full-time or part-time pay before judging the number. | Don't expect colleagues to discuss what they actually earn above the legal minimum; that conversation is as closed in Vienna as anywhere. |
| Factor in that median Austrian income runs roughly 50 percent above the EU average when budgeting for Vienna rents. | Don't assume performance bonuses are standard; most of the uplift in Austrian pay is structural, not merit-based. |

Spain's wage problem is not a perception issue; it is a documented one. Eurostat put the country's average annual salary at €33,700 in 2024, twelfth among EU member states and within hailing distance of Italy, but nowhere near France (€43,790) or Germany (€53,791). The OECD's purchasing-power-adjusted figures tell the same story from a different angle: Spain trails not only its northern neighbours but several economies most Spaniards would not think to envy.

The more interesting question is why, and The Local Spain, citing the economist Ignacio De La Torre, offers a tidy anatomy of the gap: unemployment that sat at 10.4 percent in February 2025 against an EU average of 6.1 percent, a union membership rate that has collapsed from 44.6 percent in the early 1980s to roughly 14 percent today, and a foreign-resident share that rose from 11.6 percent of the population in 2022 to 13.8 percent by late 2024, many arriving from economies where Spanish wages still look generous. None of these pressures push salaries up. All of them give employers room not to.

What survives this environment is a set of coping mechanisms longtime residents treat as common knowledge and newcomers discover the hard way. Pay splits into twelve regular months plus two "pagas extras" in July and December, already folded into the annual figure on the contract rather than a bonus on top of it, and talking about salary with coworkers remains quietly taboo outside heavily unionized workplaces. The jobs people compete hardest for are rarely the highest-paying private-sector ones; they are the most stable ones, a civil-service post or a seat at a large unionized employer, where the appeal is certainty rather than upside.

Austria sits at the other end of the same continent's pay scale. Statistics Austria puts median equivalised income at roughly €33,210, more than one and a half times the EU average, and third among the handful of major economies it is typically benchmarked against, trailing only Luxembourg and running essentially level with Ireland. The OECD's purchasing-power figures put Austria's average wage at $78,301 in 2025, among the highest in continental Europe outside the Nordics and Switzerland.

What makes Austria distinctive is not just the number but the machinery behind it. The Local Austria notes that job advertisements are legally required to state a minimum gross salary, with some senior roles exempt, and that candidates are nonetheless expected to negotiate up from whatever figure is posted. There is no single statutory minimum wage; pay floors instead come from sector-level collective agreements, or Kollektivverträge, which function as a dense safety net invisible to most outsiders. Salaries, like Spain's, are paid out fourteen times a year, with the two extra instalments arriving before the summer and Christmas holidays and taxed more favourably than ordinary pay.

Transparency, however, stops at the job ad. What an employer pays above the legal floor is rarely disclosed, and discussing actual take-home pay with colleagues is no more common in Vienna than it is in Madrid. Austria has simply legislated the floor, not the ceiling, and certainly not the conversation.

### The Reckoning

The two countries share a payroll quirk and almost nothing else. Both pay salaries in fourteen instalments, both expect new arrivals to budget around the extra months rather than treat them as windfalls, and both keep actual pay deeply private even where the law tries to pry it open. Hofstede Insights' cultural-dimensions data offers a tidy, if slightly misleading, explanation: Austria scores 79 on masculinity against Spain's 42, nominally predicting a more status- and performance-driven culture around pay. In practice, the opposite mechanism is doing the work — Austria's advantage comes from union-negotiated sector floors and legal minimums, not individual hustle, while Spain's compressed wages are offset by exactly the lifestyle factors the model would predict Spaniards to prioritise instead.

The irony that should actually inform a relocation decision is this: Spain's low pay is a known, measured, widely discussed national problem, which makes it unlikely to improve quickly but equally unlikely to surprise you once you arrive. Austria's high pay is real, but it is calibrated to a cost structure, particularly in Vienna, that quietly erases much of the advantage for anyone not also pricing in rent. Choosing between them is less a matter of which number is bigger and more a matter of which gap — between salary and cost of living, or between salary and your prior expectations — you would rather spend your first year managing.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *HackerNews — A developer who had worked in both the United States and Austria cautioned that a senior engineer's salary there lands only slightly above the national average income, not the multiple of it that American tech culture trains people to expect, though he added that on a smaller number, he still banked €1,000 to €2,000 a month without trying especially hard.*

> *Expat.com — A longtime contributor to the site's Madrid forum, posting as MadridMan, warned newcomers that landlords routinely charge foreign residents more for rent precisely because the lack of a Spanish residency card weakens their negotiating position, a markup that lands hardest on exactly the salaries least able to absorb it.*

> *ExpatArrivals — One expat quoted in the site's Spain relocation guide put the trade-off bluntly: in the United States, their income would count as poverty level; on a Spanish civil servant's salary, they live comfortably, a reminder that the number on a payslip and the life it buys are two different negotiations.*

> *Personio Community — An HR practitioner posting under the name Laura, in a payroll-software discussion forum, flagged a specific headache new finance hires in Austria keep rediscovering: standard twelve-month salary spreadsheets have no column for the 13th and 14th payments, so most companies simply log them as one-off bonuses in whichever month they land.*

> *The Olive Press — Summarising an HSBC Expat Explorer survey, the paper reported that Spain ranked almost rock bottom among expat destinations for income while landing near the top for quality of life, a trade-off longtime residents had made peace with long before any newcomer rediscovered it.*

### Conclusion

If the question is simply where the paycheck is bigger, Austria wins without much argument: its wages run roughly a third higher than Spain's by the OECD's own purchasing-power math, backed by legal minimums and collective bargaining machinery that Spain's weakened unions can no longer replicate. If the question is which country will make a modest salary feel survivable, or even comfortable, Spain's lower cost of living does real work that no payslip captures. The honest move is to price both sides of the ledger, salary and cost of living, rather than the headline number alone, and to plan your first year's budget around fourteen payments in either country, not twelve.

If a friend asked me over a drink, I would say this: move to Austria if you want your salary to make sense on paper, and move to Spain if you have already made peace with the fact that it won't — and that, in either country, nobody at the next table is going to tell you what they actually make.
