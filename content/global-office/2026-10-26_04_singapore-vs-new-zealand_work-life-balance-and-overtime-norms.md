**Singapore Clocks In Before Dawn; New Zealand Clocked Out Years Ago**

**🇸🇬 Singapore · 🇳🇿 New Zealand**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Singapore's Ministry of Manpower reports that full-time resident employees worked an average of 45.8 hours a week in 2021, plus 2.5 hours of paid overtime — figures the ministry presents, with a straight face, as evidence of improvement. New Zealand, meanwhile, has just topped Remote's Global Life-Work Balance Index for the third year running, scoring 86.87 out of 100 for letting its workers, in the index's own phrasing, "live and work well." Between these two Commonwealth-adjacent economies sits a genuine experiment in what a working life is for.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clarify in writing whether overtime pay applies to your role; most PMET contracts are exempt from the Employment Act's 44-hour cap | Assume a 6pm departure is unremarkable — in many offices it reads as mild defection |
| Use your annual leave in full; unused days signal poor planning, not loyalty | Treat a half-day medical appointment as routine — expect it quietly deducted from leave unless your contract says otherwise |
| Build slack into deadlines; consensus-driven meetings take longer than the calendar implies | Expect "flexible work" language in a job ad to survive contact with a literal-minded line manager |
| Ask colleagues, not HR, what time people actually leave before you accept an offer | Confuse looking busy with being productive — both are rewarded, only one matters |
| Negotiate a fixed-hours clause for shift or client-facing roles before signing | Assume a Saturday is automatically a weekend |

#### 🇳🇿 New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate salary hard before relocating; it rarely matches London, Sydney, or Singapore for equivalent seniority | Expect urgent sign-off on a Friday afternoon — it will wait until Monday |
| Take your full four weeks of statutory leave; abstaining reads as odd, not admirable | Mistake a relaxed pace for low standards — missed deadlines still cost you credibility |
| Expect a real 5pm stop outside Auckland's finance and legal sectors | Assume healthcare, visas, or public services move at the same unhurried pace as the office |
| Plan a life outside work early; after-hours socializing is assumed, not optional extra-credit | Expect overtime, where it exists, to go unpaid — ask about time-in-lieu policy upfront |
| Budget realistically for a smaller, less liquid job market before you commit | Interpret a slower promotion cycle as a dead end rather than a smaller pond |

Singapore's overtime data tells a quietly contradictory story. The Ministry of Manpower's own figures show weekly paid overtime falling from 2.9 hours in 2018 to 2.5 in 2021, which it credits to productivity gains and tripartite agreements limiting excessive hours. Kisi's Global Work-Life Balance Index nonetheless ranked the city-state 32nd out of 40 surveyed, and a Morgan McKinley survey found roughly two-thirds of respondents feel obliged to work beyond contracted hours, with nearly 90% of that extra time unpaid. Twelve percent were contracted above 46 hours a week outright — two hours past the statutory limit, a gap evidently negotiable in practice if not on paper. More than a third of that same survey said the long hours required genuine sacrifices in family life, which is the sort of finding that tends to get filed under "cultural context" rather than acted upon.

None of this is framed locally as a crisis so much as a known cost of doing business in a small, dense, resource-poor market that has spent six decades converting labour discipline into GDP per capita. The arrangement has worked, by most measures that matter to a finance ministry. It has simply also produced a workforce that, per the same commentary cited above, ranks third globally for hours spent appearing occupied — a statistic that would be funnier if it weren't also a fairly accurate description of a certain kind of 9pm email sent purely so the timestamp is visible.

The deeper issue, as a widely shared local commentary on Singapore's work culture put it, may not be hours at all: the city ranks third globally for the share of work time spent on what researchers term "performative work" — visibly busy, not necessarily productive. Presenteeism functions as a status display; staying later than your manager is a career strategy rather than a complaint. It is, in its way, a remarkably efficient system for producing exhaustion without the inconvenience of also producing proportionally more output.

New Zealand's reputation rests less on hours worked than on hours defended. Statistics New Zealand and the Remote index both point to a culture where a 40-hour week is treated as a ceiling, not an aspiration, and four weeks of annual leave are used without comment. But the Hays Salary Guide complicates the postcard: among New Zealanders who do work overtime, 48% say they go unpaid for it, and 30% of surveyed organisations reported overtime rising over the previous year. The lifestyle premium, in other words, is real — but it is subsidised less by employer generosity than by a smaller economy where wages simply sit lower, a trade-off every migrating professional eventually does the arithmetic on.

It is worth being precise about what the index is actually measuring, since "best work-life balance in the world" and "best-paid work-life balance in the world" are different claims entirely. Remote's methodology rewards paid leave, healthcare access, minimum wage, and general safety alongside hours — it is a composite of how humanely a labour market treats people, not a guarantee that the market itself is large or lucrative. New Zealand wins convincingly on the first measure. On the second, a long-running pattern of skilled emigration to Australia, the UK, and occasionally Singapore suggests plenty of locals have already run the same numbers themselves.

Hofstede Insights' cultural dimension scores offer a tidy shorthand for why: Singapore scores high on power distance and long-term orientation and notably low on indulgence, while New Zealand scores high on individualism and indulgence and low on power distance — meaning restraint and hierarchy are simply the Singaporean operating default, where boundary-setting is the New Zealand one. The counterintuitive part is that neither default reliably buys what it promises. Singapore's longer hours correlate with middling productivity rankings once cost-of-living is factored in; New Zealand's shorter hours correlate with wages that send a steady trickle of its own workers overseas in search of exactly the pay Singapore offers. Each country has, in effect, optimised for the thing the other one is missing.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/askSingapore — An expat posted that the work environment felt "demanding and stressful" compared to home, and that a friend who had moved to New Zealand described life there as "significantly better and less stressful," prompting the question of whether burnout was simply the local default here.*

> *Blind — A poster who spent three years in Singapore and three in the Bay Area called Singapore's overall balance better, but described a prior role with a mandatory Saturday workday, roughly 60-hour weeks, and a half-day medical appointment quietly docked from annual leave rather than waved through.*

> *r/newzealand — A nurse who left Auckland for Northern California described saving barely $1,000 a month on a new graduate's wage, at times unable to afford heating, against several thousand saved monthly abroad; she still called New Zealand home and beautiful, but said no one should have to choose between dinner and the thermostat.*

> *ScandAsia — A Swedish transplant said he wakes up daily grateful to live in Singapore, even as Singaporean colleagues around him quietly research jobs in Australia, Canada, and the US, citing cost of living, work culture, and thinner social safety nets as reasons to leave what he considers a privilege.*

### Conclusion

If the goal is maximising lifetime earnings inside a dense, status-conscious, overtime-ambiguous market, Singapore remains difficult to beat — provided the overtime clause gets settled before the contract is signed, not discovered the first Saturday everyone else is somehow also in the office. If the goal is a defended 5pm, leave taken without side-eye, and a Friday afternoon genuinely free of urgent pings, New Zealand delivers — on the condition that the paycheck is budgeted to match its market size rather than its scenery.

Singapore will pay you reasonably well to never quite finish the day; New Zealand will let you finish the day and quietly expect you to live within what it pays you for the privilege.
