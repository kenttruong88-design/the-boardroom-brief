**Taiwan Just Recorded the World's Lowest Birth Rate. Housing Costing Ten Times the Average Income Might Have Something to Do With It. Australia's Childcare Now Costs More Than Some Mortgages.**

**🇹🇼 Taiwan · 🇦🇺 Australia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Taiwan's Ministry of the Interior confirmed this year that the country has overtaken South Korea to record the world's lowest birth rate, with a 2025 fertility rate estimated around 0.72 births per woman and births down 20% from the year before. Taiwan's housing price-to-income ratio sits at 10.24 — nearly double the World Bank's upper benchmark of 5.5 for a developed economy. Australia doesn't have a birth-rate crisis of quite the same magnitude, but it has its own version of the same math: Sydney's price-to-income ratio of 13.8 is the second-worst of any major city on earth, behind only Hong Kong, and full-fee childcare in Sydney now runs about $875 a week before subsidy — more than plenty of mortgage repayments. Neither country is short on welfare ambition. Both are discovering that cash incentives are a weak match for a housing market that's already decided how many children you can afford.

#### 🇹🇼 Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Enroll in the National Health Insurance scheme as soon as you're employed — coverage starts fast and costs little | Assume NHI covers everything — eyeglasses, caregivers after surgery, and postnatal care centers are excluded |
| Budget for genuinely cheap street food and groceries relative to income | Assume cheap daily living means cheap housing — rent in Taipei runs high relative to local wages |
| Expect a NT$150 co-pay for most doctor visits | Expect every clinic to accept NHI — some don't, so ask first |
| Treat the 2026 newborn subsidy increase as real but modest relative to actual child-rearing costs | Expect a roughly $3,320 payment per baby to meaningfully offset years of costs |
| Factor in Taiwan becoming a "super-aged society" when judging long-term elder-care infrastructure | Assume youth-oriented infrastructure (childcare capacity, school placement) is keeping pace with demand |

### Taiwan: Cheap to Live, Expensive to Nest

Taiwan's day-to-day cost of living genuinely is low by developed-economy standards. South African expat Melanie put it simply: "Taiwan is more expensive than South Africa. However, everything is still affordable because the salaries are higher." Canadian expat Nick went further on food specifically: "street food, groceries, and eating out are overall significantly cheaper in Taiwan," cheap enough, he said, that "it's cheaper to eat out than cook at home." The National Health Insurance system backs that impression up — premiums run 4.69% of salary against a roughly 30% contribution ratio, most doctor visits cost a flat NT$150 co-pay, and the scheme carried a 91.6% public approval rating as of 2021. Melanie's own experience with it was glowing: "the healthcare is excellent... you take your card, they swipe it, and you pay hardly anything for your visit."

Housing is where that affordability story collapses. Taiwan's national price-to-income ratio of 10.24 is already nearly double the World Bank's upper comfort threshold, and a three-bedroom apartment in central Taipei runs roughly NT$52,000 a month — a serious sum against local wages, even with cheap noodles down the street. The government's response to a shrinking population has been direct cash: a standardized newborn payment of about $3,320 (nearly $7,000 for twins), expanded IVF subsidies, and a push the Minister without Portfolio says should help more than 120,000 families. It's real money, but it's arriving against a backdrop where births have fallen for ten straight years and the country became an officially "super-aged society" in 2025 — a demographic hole that a per-baby check, however generous, is unlikely to fill on its own while housing stays this disconnected from income.

### Australia: Cheap Insurance Fiction, Expensive Everything Else

Australia's welfare architecture looks generous on a brochure and gets considerably more complicated the moment you're actually using it. Medicare covers citizens, permanent residents, and nationals of countries with reciprocal agreements — notably not including the United States — so plenty of newly arrived skilled workers discover they need private hospital cover regardless, running roughly $100–$150 a month at the basic end before extras. Skip it past a certain income and the Medicare Levy Surcharge kicks in; delay buying it too long and Lifetime Health Cover loading raises what you'll eventually pay. Public emergency care remains genuinely free at the point of use, no insurance card required, which is more than several comparably wealthy countries can say — but it coexists with a system that otherwise assumes you're paying your own way.

Childcare is where the real financial shock lands. Full-fee daily rates in Sydney now average $175, and some inner-suburb centers in Mosman or Bondi have charged as much as $220 before subsidy — a figure that, annualized for one child, can exceed $42,000, with two children in care in Sydney's inner suburbs running past $36,000 a year even after subsidies are applied. Emily Hovette, a parent cited in a piece examining the government's "cheaper childcare" messaging, found herself out-of-pocket by $350 a week for one child's care, with a second child representing what the piece called "an impossible strain" on the family budget. The scale of the problem is structural, not anecdotal: a 2020 survey of 2,200 families found 76% said costs were too high for one or both partners to work full time, and a KPMG analysis found mothers working more than three days a week can face an effective marginal tax rate of 75% to 120% once lost subsidies are factored in. Hofstede Insights scores Australia at roughly 90 on individualism against Taiwan's 17, a gap that tracks with which country's family-support system assumes a household manages costs largely on its own, and which one has historically built more of its social expectations around extended-family and intergenerational support filling the gaps the state doesn't.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't rich-versus-poor or generous-versus-stingy — both countries are wealthy, and both governments are visibly trying to fix the exact problem they're each facing. It's that Taiwan's crisis shows up in a vital-statistics office as a fertility rate, stark and undeniable, while Australia's version shows up scattered across childcare invoices, Medicare paperwork, and KPMG spreadsheets about women's workforce participation — just as real, but far easier for any individual household, or any government press release, to treat as a collection of separate, manageable problems rather than one underlying affordability crisis. Taiwan's housing number alone, at double the World Bank's benchmark, explains a huge amount of its birth-rate number. Australia's childcare, housing, and underinsurance numbers add up to something just as structural, even though no single statistic carries the same demographic finality.

For anyone actually relocating, the practical lesson is to price the whole household, not the headline salary. A newcomer to Taiwan will likely find daily expenses shockingly low and then discover housing eats a disproportionate share of take-home pay regardless. A newcomer to Australia will likely find Medicare's free-at-point-of-use emergency care reassuring and then discover that reassurance evaporates the moment childcare, private cover, or a Sydney rental enters the picture. Both countries are trying to subsidize their way out of an affordability problem that, in both cases, the subsidy hasn't yet caught up to.

### Conclusion

If you're relocating for work and weighing these two on raw affordability, the honest framing isn't "which country is cheaper" — it's "which specific cost will blindside you." In Taiwan, day-to-day living is genuinely inexpensive and the healthcare system is both cheap and well-regarded, but housing has drifted so far from local incomes that it's now showing up in the country's own birth statistics. In Australia, free emergency care and a strong headline welfare state coexist with childcare and housing costs that can erase a second income entirely. Price your actual household, not the median statistic either country likes to put on its own tourism-adjacent welcome packet.
