# Estonia Says It Plainly, Finland Says Nothing at Length

🇪🇪 Estonia · 🇫🇮 Finland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Estonia and Finland both regard the meeting as a cost to be minimised, and both have arranged their offices accordingly. The agenda is circulated, the pleasantries are rationed and the clock is respected. They part company over what happens next. Estonia fills the saved time with process. Finland fills it with silence, which it regards as content rather than absence. The newcomer who mistakes either for a problem will spend a year being wrong in two languages.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Estonia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Send the agenda ahead and follow it in order | Open with an icebreaker or an anecdote |
| Arrive on time or slightly early, ready when formalities start | Treat the start time as a rough suggestion |
| Ask for feedback directly, since none may be volunteered | Read silence as a hidden objection |
| Get agreements signed in writing | Rely on a verbal "yes" |
| Address counterparts by family name until invited otherwise | Assume casual dress means a casual process |
| Expect several meetings before a decision | Push for sign-off in the first one |

### Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Be punctual, ideally a few minutes early | Arrive late and apologise at length |
| Keep to the agenda and the point | Fill pauses to reassure yourself |
| Save chatter for the coffee break | Open a meeting with small talk |
| Do what you promised by the deadline you gave | Offer vague deadlines out of politeness |
| Use first names, including with the boss | Wait for a title to be invited |
| Pick up the phone when email goes quiet | Assume an unanswered email is a refusal |

## Estonia: Brisk, Written Down and Faintly Formal

Estonia presents itself as egalitarian, and in places it is. According to the e-Residency programme's guide to local business culture, leaders invite input from all team members, dress is often casual, and the flat structure can strike people used to strong hierarchies as unstructured. The same guide notes that the working processes themselves can be quite formal, which is the sort of sentence that ends careers in mediation. Communication is described as low-context: direct, clear and explicit, with little reliance on implied meaning.

The formality shows up in the meeting room. Business Culture, a guide to European business practice, advises booking meetings in advance and sending the agenda beforehand. Meetings follow it in sequence and get straight to the point, with no icebreakers needed. The most senior person opens and sets the pace, junior staff speak when invited, and the guide warns that it can take several meetings to reach an agreement. Major decisions rest with management, so a meeting without the managing director is, in its words, an exchange of ideas. Verbal agreements are not considered binding. Estonians are described as direct but tactful, reserved and formal, and self-restraint is highly valued.

The part that catches foreigners is feedback. The e-Residency guide says feedback is often not volunteered and that silence generally signals that work is acceptable, so you may need to ask for input. Meetings begin on time and lateness is poorly tolerated. ExpatExchange adds that meetings tend to be short and to the point with little casual chitchat, and that employees are often trusted to work with little supervision. A British expat quoted by Raconteur described overtime in Estonia as a sign of poor time management, which is a view that travels poorly to most capitals.

## Finland: The Agenda Is Clear, the Silence Is Deliberate

Finnish meetings share Estonia's appetite for brevity but not its taste for rank. The Work in Finland portal says hierarchies are flat, people use first names even with the CEO, and meetings have a clear agenda that gets straight to the point, aiming at mutual agreement on an approach. MyHelsinki describes punctuality as sacred and lateness to a meeting as unacceptable, while noting that approachable leaders still expect employees to bring ideas and own their tasks. A flat structure, in other words, is not a relaxed one.

Then there is the silence. Michael Berry of the University of Turku, who has studied Finnish speech and silence, identifies silence and respect as typical Finnish characteristics. Finns, he notes, often show interest by listening quietly, where Americans tend to ask questions and interrupt, and each side can draw an unflattering conclusion about the other. Expat.com's Helsinki guide observes that Finns are comfortable with silence and can seem reserved, and that frankness can seem blunt without being meant as rudeness. A Canadian writing for TEK, the Finnish engineers' association, reports that meetings start with the topic and that chitchat is kept for the coffee break, which is offered at nearly every meeting. He also advises calling when an email goes unanswered, because emails get overlooked.

Finland adds a boundary that Estonia does not state as loudly. The same portal reports that the workday typically ends when people leave the office, and that employees usually receive four to six weeks of vacation. The TEK writer says people generally work the hours in their contracts and that unpaid extra hours are the exception. Meetings are short partly because there is somewhere else to be.

## The Reckoning

Hofstede Insights offers a rough map. On power distance Estonia scores 40 and Finland 33, which is a small gap by the standards of the other numbers. On individualism the scores are 62 and 75. The widest gap is indulgence, where Estonia scores 16 and Finland 57. A low score suggests a culture that restrains gratification, and the e-Residency advice to default to more formality fits that reading. Scores describe tendencies in whole populations, not your line manager, who will have read none of this and would likely regard a summary of it as a meeting that could have been an email.

In practice the difference is one of signalling. In Estonia, rank decides who speaks, writing decides what counts and silence is a quiet pass mark. In Finland, rank matters less in the room but accountability matters just as much, and silence is a form of attention. Estonia will ask whether you have read the agenda. Finland will assume you have, and wait to see what you do with it. The arithmetic of the two is therefore less about politeness than about who carries the burden of clarity: in Estonia the speaker must be explicit, and in Finland the listener must be attentive. Either country will happily forgive a short meeting. Neither will forgive a late one.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Relocate.me — a developer's account of seven years in Estonia*

A full stack developer who has spent seven years in Estonia says her current job came through a friend, and that referrals are common in a small country. She praises the low bureaucracy and the fact that nearly everything can be done online, and sees high English proficiency as a clear advantage for newcomers.

> *ExpatExchange — an editorial guide to moving to Estonia*

The guide's advice is that Estonians may seem reserved at first, then turn into loyal friends once trust is built. It suggests joining a choir or sports team, and notes that meetings are short with little chitchat.

> *TEK blog — a Canadian's notes on adapting in Finland*

A Canadian engineer recounts that coffee turns up at almost every meeting, that lunch at the desk is considered unhealthy and that the phone beats email for a quick answer. He was struck that Finns do not rely on hidden meanings, and that a plain statement is taken plainly.

> *Finnair Bluewings — foreign colleagues on working in Finland*

A Japanese colleague says Finns keep a comfortable distance and avoid small talk, which suits her. An Italian colleague who has worked in Finland for about 25 years says leaving at a set time for school or family duties is widely accepted, and far less common in Italy.

> *Tampere University student blog — a student on Finnish silence*

A student writer describes silence as a cultural value: people speak mainly when they have something useful to say, and colleagues turned out to be kind and helpful once asked for guidance. The writer found little need for supervision because trust is assumed.

## Conclusion

The practical advice is shorter than the meetings. In Estonia, read the agenda, arrive on time, ask for feedback aloud and get the decision in writing, because a nod is not a signature and quiet is not a review. In Finland, be early, keep to the point, save the chat for the coffee and learn to sit through a pause without apologising for it. In both, work out who actually decides before you start persuading anyone.

Neither country will hold your hand through the first month, since both consider that a waste of everyone's time. Each will, eventually, prove warm. It is simply that you will be invited to find that out on your own schedule, which is to say, punctually.
