**Germany Will Tell You Your Shirt Is Ugly; Finland Will Simply Let the Silence Do It: A Meeting-Room Guide**

**🇩🇪 Germany · 🇫🇮 Finland**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

A German colleague will tell you, in front of the room, that your shirt color doesn't suit you — this is not a cruelty, it is simply information being shared efficiently. A Finnish colleague, by contrast, will say nothing at all for several minutes after you finish speaking, and that silence is not disapproval, hesitation, or a slow internet connection — according to one newcomer's own account, it took him considerable time to realize it was simply "a cultural value." Two famously blunt, low-small-talk cultures, and yet their bluntness points in almost opposite directions.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat direct criticism of your work as being about the work, not a personal judgment — this is the stated cultural norm | Take blunt feedback personally, or assume it signals dislike — Germans distinguish subject-criticism sharply from character-criticism |
| Arrive with a clear agenda and expect the meeting to follow it precisely — structure is taken seriously | Mistake directness for coldness in every context — many expats report German colleagues softening considerably once a relationship forms |
| Speak up and contribute — silence in a German meeting is often read as disinterest, the opposite of Finland's norm | Be surprised if a colleague comments bluntly on your appearance, a sick day, or a personal choice — reported examples include direct comments on clothing and even working from home while ill |
| Arrive precisely on time — punctuality is treated as basic professional respect | Assume a direct comment carries hidden diplomatic subtext — in German workplace communication, it usually means exactly what it says |
| Match the directness over time if it suits you — several expats report this is the most effective long-term coping strategy | Expect an apology for bluntness — the cultural framing is that directness is a form of honesty and efficiency, not rudeness |

#### 🇫🇮 Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Let silence sit after you ask a question or finish a point — it usually means people are genuinely thinking, not disengaging | Fill every pause with chatter — Finns are documented as comfortable with silence and don't read it as awkward |
| Say less, and make what you do say count — Finnish communication rewards brevity and substance over volume | Pad statements with excessive softening language or small talk before getting to the point — it isn't especially valued here |
| Take a lack of visible enthusiasm as neutral, not negative — understatement is the cultural default | Assume that asking for help will be met with reluctance — newcomers report colleagues as consistently kind and supportive once asked directly |
| Expect written or slower follow-up for things not resolved verbally in the room | Interpret a quiet meeting as a bad or unproductive one — the opposite is frequently true |
| Value a Finnish colleague's rare but direct feedback precisely because it's rare — it tends to be considered and genuine | Assume frankness equals coldness — accounts describe Finnish directness as plain-spoken rather than harsh |

### Germany

Germany's reputation for directness is, per Hofstede's numbers, backed by a genuinely distinct cultural profile: individualism at 67 and uncertainty avoidance at 65, a combination that favors explicit, individually-owned opinions delivered without excessive hedging, inside a structure (agendas, punctuality, defined roles) that keeps the directness channeled rather than chaotic. The Cultural Atlas and multiple business-etiquette guides converge on the same framing newcomers are given early: criticism in a German meeting targets the work, not the person, and taking it personally is treated as a misreading on the recipient's part, not evidence of actual unkindness.

Real accounts from The Local's German readership show how far that directness extends beyond the meeting agenda itself. Jorge Bueso, a 38-year-old in Berlin, described colleagues who "are too direct... they feel like they have to share their thoughts as if its relevant or required," while Estelle Mankan, a French reader, recalled coworkers who would "bluntly tell you they do not have time to help you with a task" with no softening at all. Other readers described being told, in front of colleagues, that a shirt color "doesn't suit you," or being informed — twice, for emphasis — how unwell they looked while working from home sick. One reader, Suleman, offered the practical coping strategy that recurs across these accounts: "I just match the energy nowadays," after initially finding the adjustment difficult. None of this, notably, reads to Germans themselves as rude; it reads as information, delivered efficiently, exactly as the etiquette guides describe.

### Finland

Finland's communication style looks, at a glance, similar to Germany's — low small talk, minimal hedging, direct when it speaks at all — but it is built on a markedly different cultural chassis. Hofstede scores Finland at just 26 on masculinity, one of the lowest such scores globally, alongside a comparatively low power distance of 33, a combination associated with modest, egalitarian communication that avoids asserting dominance or filling space unnecessarily. Where German directness is active — stating an opinion plainly and often — Finnish communication is better described as conservationist: speak only when you have something that actually needs saying, and let silence carry the rest.

A first-person account on the University of Tampere's "Understanding Finnishness" blog captured the adjustment curve precisely. The author admitted his first impression was that "Finnish people were very introverted because they do not talk much," and that he initially read this as coldness — "but over time, I realized that silence is actually a cultural value," he wrote, adding that whenever he actually asked colleagues for help or guidance directly, "people were always kind, open, and supportive." Finnish workplace guidance from TEK, the engineers' professional body, reinforces the same point structurally: Finns are described as comfortable with conversational pauses rather than anxious to fill them, and limited use of softening language is better read as plain-spokenness than as frankness bordering on rudeness.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast, once you look past the shared reputation for bluntness, is where each culture puts its directness to work. Germany's directness is verbal and constant — it shows up in meetings, in casual comments about your shirt, in unsolicited observations about your sick day — because the culture values explicitly stated individual opinion as a form of honesty. Finland's directness is almost the inverse: it shows up in the absence of unnecessary words, in meetings that can sit in silence for genuinely productive stretches, because the culture values restraint and modesty (that low masculinity score) as its own form of honesty — saying only what's load-bearing.

For someone moving between these two, the adjustment runs in two completely different directions. Coming from Germany to Finland, the instinct to fill a silent room with clarifying chatter will read as noise, not helpfulness. Coming from Finland to Germany, the instinct to stay quiet and let a strong point speak for itself will simply read as disengagement — Germans expect you to say the thing, not trust that everyone already inferred it. Both cultures will tell you they're being straightforward with you. They mean two different things by it.

It's also worth separating the discomfort of the adjustment from any judgment about which style is "better," since both produce real, functioning workplaces by their own internal logic. A German meeting that runs loud and opinionated is not dysfunctional by German standards; it's working exactly as the culture's directness norms intend. A Finnish meeting that runs mostly silent is not failing either; the silence, per the Tampere account, is where the actual thinking happens, and the eventual short, direct statement is simply the visible tip of a longer internal process a louder culture would have narrated out loud the whole time.

The one thing both cultures would agree on, if either bothered to say so out loud, is that small talk is largely wasted motion. Neither German bluntness nor Finnish reticence leaves much room for the padded, relationship-softening chatter that cultures with higher power-distance or lower individualism scores often build into a meeting's opening minutes. An incoming hire from a more indirect culture may initially read both rooms as unusually cold for exactly this reason, when the more accurate read is that both have simply decided the meeting itself, not the preamble around it, is where the actual respect gets shown.

### Conclusion

If meetings are where you'll live or die in a new role, the honest advice diverges by direction of travel: heading to Germany, say the direct thing out loud, expect the same back, and don't take the shirt comment personally. Heading to Finland, say less, trust the silence, and don't mistake a quiet room for a disengaged one. Both countries are, in their own way, allergic to small talk — they've just built two completely different religions out of what to do with the silence that leaves behind.
