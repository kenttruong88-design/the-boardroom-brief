# Trust Fall: What "Flexible Work" Actually Means in Tashkent vs. Trondheim

**🇺🇿 Uzbekistan · 🇳🇴 Norway**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Norway will let you work from a cabin and trust you to actually do it; Uzbekistan is still deciding whether "core hours" means something you negotiate or something you're told. Both countries claim to be flexible. Only one of them means it the way you think.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇿 Uzbekistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm your remote-work arrangement in writing with HR — labor law here is still catching up to practice, and verbal promises evaporate | Assume "hybrid" means what it means at home; ask how many office days are actually enforced, not advertised |
| Budget for inconsistent mobile internet; keep a café or co-working membership as backup for video calls | Rely on mobile data for anything client-facing — switch to fixed wifi before a call that matters |
| Show up in person for your first few weeks, even if your contract is remote-friendly — face time still buys trust | Skip the in-office stretch entirely just because your contract allows it; you'll be the name nobody recognizes in the hallway |
| Expect a more formal management style, especially at legacy or state-linked firms | Mistake formality for inflexibility — many firms are actively rewriting policy to catch up with younger staff expectations |
| Keep a cash buffer and separate SIM for day-to-day logistics | Expect ATMs to reliably have cash, or English to get you very far outside Tashkent's international firms |

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take the flat structure at face value — your manager genuinely expects you to make calls without asking permission | Keep CC'ing your boss on routine decisions; it reads as either distrust or an inability to self-manage |
| Log off at a reasonable hour — leaving late can look like poor time management, not dedication | Treat a 5pm sign-off by colleagues as a sign no one is working hard | 
| Learn basic Norwegian fast, even in English-speaking offices | Assume strong English proficiency means the workplace will default to English long-term |
| Budget in NOK, not euros — the currency has weakened substantially against the euro over the past decade | Assume your nominal salary will stretch the way it would have ten years ago |
| Join the small rituals — Friday waffles, shared birthday cake — they're how belonging gets built here | Skip them as "optional extras"; opting out repeatedly reads as disengagement |

### Uzbekistan: Modernizing on Its Own Clock

Uzbekistan's labor code didn't formally account for remote work until relatively recently — employers are told to "define core hours and asynchronous expectations" rather than inheriting a settled default, per a 2025 practical guide to the country's evolving work culture (nnroad). The driver is foreign investment: IT Park Uzbekistan, the state-backed tech hub, now counts more than 40 foreign firms among its residents, each importing its own expectations about async work into a market still largely built around physical presence (Outsource Accelerator).

The result is a two-speed system: multinational and tech-adjacent employers are piloting hybrid arrangements with one-on-ones and outcome-based tracking in place of surveillance, while everyone else operates on a more traditional model, with leadership described as moving toward "performance focus with empathy" rather than command-and-control — a polite way of saying command-and-control was, until recently, the default. Uzbekistan has no individually published Hofstede score, but regional academic work on Central Asia consistently describes the broader culture as high power-distance and collectivist, which tracks with what that hybrid-policy guidance is working against.

For someone moving for a remote-friendly role, the practical reality is less about policy and more about infrastructure: mobile internet is serviceable but not reliable enough to anchor a workday, and co-working options exist mainly in Tashkent, Bukhara, and Samarkand rather than everywhere.

### Norway: Flexible, Expensive, and Not Always Kind to Outsiders

Norway's reputation for trust-based, flexible work is not marketing — Norwegians log some of the shortest average working hours of any OECD economy, around 1,418 hours a year, placing the country in the bottom tier of the 34 OECD members measured, well behind Mexico's 2,207-hour average (Visual Capitalist, drawing on OECD data). The OECD's own research on teleworking frames this as a trust-first model: Norwegian employers skew toward outcome measurement over hours logged, a pattern the OECD explicitly links to higher reported institutional trust.

What the trust doesn't fully buy you, as an incoming foreign hire, is pay parity. Lorelou Desjardins, the long-running Norway-based blogger behind *A Frog in the Fjord*, has written candidly that foreigners in Norway earn on average 18% less than Norwegian nationals — a gap that compounds with a currency that has roughly halved in purchasing power against the euro since 2010. The flat structure is real and widely praised; so is the arithmetic problem underneath it, which is pushing some skilled foreign workers to calculate that a lower nominal salary in a cheaper country nets out higher.

### The Reckoning

Here is the irony: Uzbekistan's remote-work culture is currently more honest about its limitations than Norway's. Tashkent doesn't pretend flexibility is universal — it's openly a privilege at multinational-adjacent firms, with everyone else negotiating something more traditional. Norway's flexibility is universal and real, which is exactly why its failure mode is subtler: a system that trusts you completely with your calendar can still quietly shortchange your paycheck.

The other inversion: Norway's flat hierarchy makes informality the formal rule — skipping Friday waffles or small talk about skiing costs more socially than it looks, since there's no hierarchy left to hide behind. Uzbekistan's more visible hierarchy can paradoxically make it easier to know exactly where you stand.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *andysto.com (digital nomad blog) — A remote worker who tried to anchor video calls on Tashkent's mobile network learned the hard way that "internet speed is still an issue here," particularly over mobile data, and now treats switching to fixed wifi before anything client-facing as a non-negotiable step, not a nice-to-have.*

> *SAIGA Tours blog, Eilidh Crowley — A co-founder working remotely from Tashkent while raising a child found that the city's café-and-coworking scene requires real reconnaissance: venues need "nice seating, relaxed vibe, free wifi and wall plugs, and somewhere for children to play," and at least one previously reliable spot had quietly stopped offering the facilities its reputation was built on — advice she wishes she'd had before, not after, committing to a location.*

> *A Frog in the Fjord (Lorelou Desjardins, expat blog) — A long-term foreign resident of Norway describes the flat management structure as everything it's advertised to be, and still flags that foreigners earn "18% less than Norwegians" on average — a pleasant structural surprise paired with a financial one that no amount of work-life-balance messaging fully offsets.*

> *Expat.com (Oslo work-culture guide) — New arrivals are warned that casual dress is the norm outside finance, but the sharper cultural misread is conversational: Norwegian colleagues keep small talk to neutral ground — weather, skiing — and treat "Waffle Fridays" and shared birthday cake not as perks but as the actual mechanism through which belonging gets built on a team.*

### Conclusion

If you want a government and employer that have already solved the flexibility question, Norway gets you there — just budget for the krone's slide and the quiet pay gap no policy document mentions. If you'd rather be part of a system still visibly writing its own rules, Uzbekistan is more honest about being unfinished. Either way: test your wifi before you test your assumptions. The version I'd tell a friend over a drink: Norway gives you the freedom and quietly adjusts your paycheck for having asked; Uzbekistan makes you ask for the freedom but won't dock you for wanting it.
