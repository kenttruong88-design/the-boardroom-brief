# The UAE will pay you on time and stare at you on the metro. Australia will underpay you and call you "mate" while doing it.

🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates · 🇦🇺 Australia

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Here is the paradox to pack alongside your visa paperwork: the United Arab Emirates, a federation built on a legal architecture that still assigns women guardianship-adjacent paperwork for some family matters, pays men and women the same wage by federal law since 2018. Australia, a liberal democracy that has had a female prime minister, a sex discrimination commissioner, and four decades of equal-pay legislation, still sends women home each year with 21.1 cents less on every dollar than men take, according to the Workplace Gender Equality Agency. Neither country is lying to you, exactly. They are just measuring different things.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask HR directly about the equal-pay provisions in Federal Decree-Law No. 33 of 2021 if your offer seems off | Assume the modest dress code applies only to women — it's gender-neutral on paper, enforced unevenly in practice |
| Use the women-only metro carriages and "pink" taxis without a second thought; they're mainstream infrastructure, not a workaround | Treat a female manager's quietness in a mixed meeting as passivity — status here often speaks through hierarchy, not volume |
| Negotiate school-fee and housing allowances explicitly in writing | Assume those allowances default to you if you're the female half of a dual-income couple; many packages still skew toward male "primary" earners |
| Build a network through groups like the Dubai Business Women Council | Expect promotion timelines to mirror home-market speed; most expats are told to plan on multi-year runways to prove themselves |
| Clarify your sponsorship status (own visa vs. spouse-sponsored) before signing anything | Wait until a work emergency to discover your NOC situation |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for the pay gap figure in your specific industry before accepting an offer; WGEA publishes it by employer | Assume "flat hierarchy" and "equal pay" are the same claim — they frequently aren't |
| Say yes to after-work drinks at least occasionally if you want to be read into decisions | Assume policy documents (parental leave, flexible work) describe the actual culture rather than the aspiration |
| Ask directly who else was considered for a promotion you missed | Assume self-deprecation in meetings is modesty rather than a social script everyone is expected to perform, unevenly rewarded |
| Negotiate bonus and overtime pay specifically; it's where the real gap hides | Assume "we're pretty equal here, mate" settles the question |
| Take paid primary-carer leave if offered — normalizing male uptake is still rare and visibly noted | Assume taking it will be cost-free to how you're perceived; only 20% of primary-carer leave is taken by men |

### United Arab Emirates

The UAE's labour-market numbers tell a story of rapid, top-down change layered onto a culture that Hofstede Insights scores at 90 for power distance and a comparatively low 25 for individualism — a context where deference to hierarchy and loyalty to one's in-group outranks assertive self-advocacy, male or female. Female labour-force participation sat at 53.0% in 2025, according to World Bank data, a figure that has crept up steadily from a pandemic-era dip and remains well below Australia's roughly 62%, but is nonetheless among the highest in the Gulf. Equal pay has been federal law since 2018, enforced through the Ministry of Human Resources and Emiratisation, and women now hold 10.8% of board seats across UAE-listed companies — modest by global standards, but double the GCC regional average and the highest in the bloc, per the 2024 GCC Board Gender Index Report from Heriot-Watt University and Aurora50. In some listed financial companies, women chair as many as 15.8% of boards, a figure that would raise eyebrows in plenty of G7 markets.

None of this happens because the UAE has become, culturally, a quietly feminist state; it happens because federal policy was set centrally and implemented with the same administrative speed the country applies to building islands. The effect for a foreign hire is a strange kind of whiplash: the paperwork protecting you is unusually explicit, even as the social context around it remains, by most comparative measures, one of the more hierarchical and collectivist environments a Western professional is likely to encounter.

What the law doesn't capture is texture. A 2018 ExpatWoman.com survey of 1,100 expat women found 29% reported facing workplace sexism — nearly double the rate they reported in their home countries — while a clear majority, 63%, said they'd faced none at all. More tellingly, 53% said being female hadn't limited their UAE career in any way, and 61% said they earned more than they had at home. Hofstede's own analysts, somewhat generously, attribute most gendered friction in the Gulf to religious and social custom rather than workplace culture per se — a distinction that matters less to the person filling out the form than it might to an academic.

### Australia

Australia presents as the mirror image: no legal gender distinctions to speak of, and a persistent, well-measured gap anyway, tracked publicly and by name. The national pay gap narrowed to 21.1% in the 2024-25 WGEA scorecard — women earn 78.9 cents for every dollar men earn, a $28,356 annual shortfall — but the gap at the CEO level actually widened, to 26.2%, even as the proportion of female CEOs held flat at 22%. Women hold 43% of manager roles and 33% of board seats, both inching upward year on year; discretionary pay is where the real asymmetry lives, with men collecting about 60% more than women in bonuses, allowances, and overtime, payments that make up roughly twice the share of men's total remuneration that they make of women's. Every one of these figures is published, by law, employer by employer — which is either the system working as intended or the system publicly documenting its own failure to fix itself, depending on your mood that week.

The more interesting data point is about confidence, not compensation. Industry surveys covered by IT Brief Australia found only 35% of early-career women felt able to ask for a raise, against 61% of early-career men — the widest gap the researchers measured at any seniority level — while 43% of entry-level workers overall described an entrenched "boys' club" at their workplace. As Women in Digital's Holly Hunt put it in that coverage, policy is not the same as culture: an organisation can have excellent parental-leave terms and still not feel inclusive day to day. Only 20% of primary-carer leave, per WGEA, is actually taken by men.

### The Reckoning

Here is the inversion worth sitting with: the UAE regulates gender equity explicitly and outperforms expectations informally, while Australia deregulates it entirely on paper and underperforms its own stated values in practice. A UAE employment contract is more likely to spell out exactly what you're owed; an Australian one is more likely to leave the real decisions — who gets the stretch project, who gets read into the after-hours conversation that actually allocates promotions — to unwritten social rules that favour whoever already looks like the incumbent.

The two systems also fail differently at the top. The UAE's board numbers are thin but rising from genuine scarcity; Australia's are thicker (33%) but increasingly decorative, since the CEO and discretionary-pay gaps suggest seniority itself doesn't close the gap so much as relocate it into bonus structures and informal networks. Neither country has solved this. They've just chosen which part of the problem to make visible.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — a woman job-hunting in Dubai was told bluntly by a poster with HR experience that her target salary was unrealistic given a saturated market, and that school-fee allowances were "normally given to male candidates" as a matter of company default rather than policy — a norm the poster flagged as unfair even while confirming it was standard.*

> *Khaleej Times interview — an HR executive who moved to Dubai a decade ago expected a conservative market with few openings for women and found the opposite: an interview within two weeks, steeper career growth than she'd likely have had at home, and government service counters that moved faster for her, which she credits partly to high market turnover creating constant opportunity.*

> *ExpatWoman.com community survey — among over a thousand expat women, workplace sexism was reported at roughly double home-country rates, yet a clear majority reported none at all, and most said relocating had not limited their careers — a split result that resists a single tidy verdict.*

> *IT Brief Australia, citing a Women in Digital-backed industry survey — early-career women reported feeling far less entitled to ask for a raise than their male peers, the widest confidence gap found at any career stage, prompting the observation that generous-looking policy and daily lived culture are not the same thing.*

> *HCA Mag, quoting an Executive Women Australia director — senior roles tend to open rarely and get filled through referral, and because incumbents are disproportionately men, they tend to refer other men; the advice distilled from this pattern is to build sponsorship relationships deliberately rather than assume merit alone will surface you.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing on paper, Australia wins: equal legal standing, transparent reporting, recourse through established channels. If you're choosing on lived outcome for a given individual, the picture gets murkier — UAE survey data suggests many expat women experience faster career acceleration and fewer felt limitations than Australia's steady-state numbers would predict for an equivalent local hire. The honest answer is that neither country has actually closed its gap; one has simply made its gap legible, publishing it in annual scorecards, while the other leaves you to discover it experientially, one missed bonus and one unreturned invitation to drinks at a time.

The practical takeaway for someone actually packing a suitcase: in the UAE, read your contract line by line, because the state has done the work of making gendered entitlements explicit, and the gap between you and a male colleague is more likely to be social texture than a missing legal right. In Australia, read the WGEA employer data for your specific industry before you negotiate, because the gap there is baked into bonus structures and informal sponsorship, not into anything your contract will admit to.

What I'd tell a friend over a drink: go to the UAE if you want your entitlements in writing and are prepared to read between cultural lines; go to Australia if you want the lines spoken aloud and are prepared to discover the entitlements aren't quite what the policy document implied.
