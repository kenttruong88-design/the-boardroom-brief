# Uruguay Eats Late and Carries Its Tea to Work; Spain Eats Later and Calls It Lunch

🇺🇾 Uruguay · 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries on opposite sides of the Atlantic have independently concluded that dinner before 9 p.m. is a rumour. In Madrid, the lunch service runs from about 1:30 to 3:30 in the afternoon and kitchens close around four, according to the city's tourism guidance. In Montevideo, Roads & Kingdoms reports, dinner "usually doesn't begin until after 9pm", and a restaurant at eight is an empty stage. For someone relocating for work, the differences between them are not in the hour but in what happens in the gap: Spain fills it with a long lunch, and Uruguay fills it with a thermos.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Uruguay

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the mate and drink the whole serving before passing the gourd back | Refuse it without a reason; it reads as impolite |
| Plan a merienda between 5 and 7 p.m., with mate or coffee and something sweet | Treat late afternoon as a dead zone |
| Book dinner for 9 p.m. or later, and later still on weekends | Show up at 8 p.m. and expect a crowd |
| Carry your own mate if you want to blend in at work | Be surprised to see one in a video meeting |
| Budget for restaurants and imported goods, which can be expensive | Expect Spanish prices |
| Share a chivito and drink local wine | Order three courses and an import |

### In Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Eat lunch between 2 and 4 p.m. and treat it as a proper meal | Eat a sandwich at your desk and call it lunch |
| Join the second breakfast around 11 a.m. with coffee and a pincho | Skip it and be hungry at 1 p.m. |
| Bring tupperware to work if you want a faster midday break | Assume everyone eats out every day |
| Expect a sobremesa, the lingering after the meal, to run two hours or more at weekends | Look at your watch |
| Avoid scheduling meetings during the 1 to 4 p.m. siesta window in smaller towns | Expect shops and offices to answer |
| Dine at 9 or 10 p.m. without complaint | Complain about the dinner hour; it seems rude |

## Uruguay

The central fact about Uruguayan food culture is the mate. Abstracta, a Uruguayan technology firm, writes that Uruguayans consume about 10 kilograms of yerba mate per person per year, making the country the world's largest consumer, and that people bring it to work and study and even to virtual meetings. This is not an eccentricity but an infrastructure: a communal gourd, a thermos of hot water and a code of conduct in which refusing the offered gourd without a reason is considered impolite, according to an ExpatExchange guide. An American writer for Expatsi noted that mate with a neighbour is part of everyday downtime, and that meals and conversations stretch longer than Americans expect.

Meal timing follows. Roads & Kingdoms describes Montevideo's merienda, between five and seven in the afternoon, as a ritual of mate, coffee or tea and something sweet, with a savoury version called a refuerzo. Dinner begins after nine. The same writer found restaurants pricey, especially compared with Buenos Aires, and attributed high prices to import duties. That fits what an expat writer reports about groceries and imported goods costing more than in the United States, and about shops that close earlier and a quiet Sunday. For a newcomer, the working day is a quiet rhythm with a pause in the middle, and a social calendar that starts when most countries are finishing.

Expats on ExpatExchange list language, a slower pace of life and differing cultural norms as the hardest parts of settling in, though one in Punta del Diablo said locals are patient with imperfect Spanish. We could not retrieve Hofstede's figures for Uruguay in time to compare them with Spain's, so we will not guess them, a restraint that Uruguayans would probably see as a dignified use of a mate break.

## Spain

Spain's reputation for a long lunch is supported by what little hard data exists. The OECD's time-use figures, reported by Connexion France in 2018, put the French at 133 minutes a day eating and drinking and the Spanish at 122. The Madrid tourist office puts lunch at 1:30 to 3:30 p.m. with kitchens open until about 4, and dinner from 8:30, with most people eating between 9 and 10:30, later in summer. The Local Spain says lunch is typically between 2 and 4 p.m. and dinner at nine or later, and that the sobremesa can run two hours or more at weekends.

It is easy to overstate the workday version of this. The Local also notes that many office workers still eat out on weekdays, while some bring tupperware, called a táper, to heat at work, and that there is a second breakfast around 11 a.m. A long-term resident commenting on the same article described the mid-morning almuerzo as a bocadillo, olives or peanuts, a drink, often beer or wine, and a coffee, and noted that the size of a caña varies by region. A Reddit thread on cultural norms, read through a mirror, found a poster who disliked dinner after 9 and going out after 1, and replies that said the siesta makes the late day logical. One of the replies, from someone working US hours from Spain, found a late dinner suited finishing work around 9.

On cost, Eurostat price levels reported by Euronews in January 2026 put Spain, with Portugal and Greece, noticeably below the EU average for hotels and restaurants. Hofstede's textbook scores for Spain, in a table citing the research, include uncertainty avoidance of 86 and masculinity of 42, which suggests a society that prefers a clear structure and a gentler pace, and which would explain why the lunch hour has survived the global trend to work through it.

The scheduling consequences are worth spelling out for anyone starting a job in either country. In Madrid, lunch service runs roughly from 1:30 to 3:30 p.m. and the second breakfast at about 11 a.m. already marks the morning, so a meeting at one o'clock is an act of aggression. In Montevideo, the merienda between five and seven is a social slot, a meeting scheduled across it will be conducted with a gourd in the room, and a dinner invitation for eight o'clock will be understood as a joke. Spaniards who want a faster midday heat their tupperware at work, and Uruguayans who want to be left alone bring their own mate. Both countries have a way of eating alone with dignity.

## The Reckoning

The two countries share a late dinner and differ on almost everything that leads to it. Spain organises the day around the meal: a second breakfast, a long lunch, an afternoon pause and a late dinner. Uruguay organises it around the drink, a gourd that starts the day, accompanies the work and returns at five o'clock with a pastry. A Spanish employee will say that she is going to lunch. A Uruguayan one will not say anything, because the mate is already with him.

The practical difference is in the cost and the audience. Spain is cheaper for restaurants than the EU average, and eating out is part of the working week. Uruguay is not cheap, and the social meal is more likely to be at home, with a neighbour and a thermos. Neither country eats earlier than you do. Both will eat later than you want to, in the company of people who see no reason to hurry.

## Conclusion

If you are relocating to Uruguay, buy a gourd, a bombilla and a thermos, learn the mate protocol before the first meeting and push your dinner reservation to nine. If you are relocating to Spain, accept that lunch is a legitimate part of the schedule, that the office will empty around two and that dinner is a late event with its own logic.

In both cases, do not arrive hungry at eight. You will be early for everything except your own hunger.
