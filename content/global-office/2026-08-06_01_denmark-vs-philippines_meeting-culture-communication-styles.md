# Say What You Mean, Mean What You Don't Say: Denmark and the Philippines on How to Run a Meeting

🇩🇰 Denmark · 🇵🇭 Philippines

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Copenhagen, a junior analyst who says nothing in a meeting is assumed to have nothing worth saying, and is quietly marked down for it. In Manila, a junior analyst who says nothing in a meeting is assumed to be doing exactly what a junior analyst should. Both offices would describe this as respect. Only one of them means it as a compliment to the person staying quiet.

*Illustration: The Alignment Times*

#### 🇩🇰 Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Speak up unprompted, even as the newest hire — silence reads as disengagement | Mistake the flat hierarchy for no hierarchy; someone still signs the budget |
| Show up on time, to the minute; meetings start and end on schedule | Oversell your title or credentials — Danes find self-promotion faintly ridiculous |
| Expect blunt, unfiltered feedback, and give it back the same way | Expect praise for doing your job; feedback defaults to what isn't working |
| Use first names with everyone, including the person who signs your contract | Let a meeting run over its slot; nobody will thank you for the overtime |
| Read the agenda beforehand — meetings assume the homework is already done | Take directness personally; it is not an attack, it is Tuesday |

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build rapport before business; small talk is not a delay, it is the work | Publicly correct or contradict someone senior in front of the group |
| Learn to read between the lines — "I'll try" or "maybe" often means no | Assume a room full of nodding heads means agreement has been reached |
| Address seniors as sir or ma'am, even in otherwise casual settings | Skip the pleasantries and launch straight into the agenda |
| Deliver criticism privately, one-on-one, never in the meeting itself | Treat a lack of pushback as a green light; silence can be disagreement in disguise |
| Build in buffer time; meetings rarely start exactly on the hour | Confuse warmth and hospitality for a lack of seriousness about the work |

### 🇩🇰 Denmark

Denmark treats the meeting room as a leveller. Hofstede Insights scores the country 18 on Power Distance, one of the lowest readings recorded anywhere, meaning subordinates are expected to challenge their superiors and superiors are expected to be challenged back. The World Economic Forum has ranked Denmark first among 139 countries for "willingness to delegate authority," a statistic that survives contact with an actual Danish office, where interns are invited to disagree with directors and penalized more for staying quiet than for being wrong.

None of this makes Danish meetings short. The same consensus model that flattens the hierarchy also obliges everyone present, from managing director to graduate hire, to have their say before anything is decided — efficient in that objections surface early, inefficient in that a thirty-minute meeting routinely becomes ninety. Denmark's annual working hours average around 1,380, well under the OECD average of roughly 1,700 (OECD Hours Worked), and Statistics Denmark records a standard full-time week of 37 hours, so the country compensates for its talkative meetings by working notably less everywhere else. Gallup's polling consistently places Danish workers among the world's highest for overall wellbeing, which suggests that arguing your way to a decision, however slowly, beats simply being handed one.

### 🇵🇭 Philippines

The Philippines runs on a different operating system. Hofstede scores the country 94 on Power Distance, among the highest in the world, and just 17 on Individualism, indicating a workplace where hierarchy is respected by default and the group, not the individual, is the unit that matters. In a Filipino meeting, the senior person's opinion functions less like one input among many and more like the answer key; junior staff are far more likely to signal disagreement through hesitation, a soft "I'll try," or silence than to say no outright, a pattern rooted in the value of pakikisama, or maintaining group harmony even at some personal cost.

This is not a culture of low effort. The Philippine Statistics Authority's Labor Force Survey puts average hours worked at roughly 41 to 42 per week, longer than Denmark's, and the country's outsourcing and services sectors run shift patterns built entirely around other time zones. What it does mean is that the meeting functions as a stage for consensus as much as a venue for decision-making: the real negotiation often happens afterward, in one-on-one conversations, over food, or relayed through an intermediary, rather than performed as dissent in front of the room.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the irony sharpens. Denmark, the country famous for hygge and unhurried coffee breaks, runs the more confrontational meeting: everyone is required to have an opinion and required to voice it, welcome or not. The Philippines, whose service economy is built on relentless, round-the-clock customer availability, runs the more conflict-averse one: opinions exist, but airing them where a superior might lose face is close to unthinkable. The country that works fewer hours argues more in the room; the country that works more hours argues less.

The counterintuitive lesson for anyone relocating in either direction is that directness and efficiency are not the same axis. A Danish meeting is direct and can still run long, because everyone insists on being heard before anything is settled. A Filipino meeting is efficient in the room and can still be slow overall, because the actual decision gets renegotiated afterward in side conversations nobody scheduled. Neither system is more honest than the other; they have simply moved the honesty to different points in the process.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — One respondent described the quiet decoding work of Filipino office life: colleagues would say "I'll try" instead of a flat no, and frustration was far more likely to surface later as an indirect, half-joking remark than as pushback in the meeting itself.*

> *Quora — A foreign hire recalled being unsettled in the first week by how quickly Filipino coworkers asked about marital status, family, and personal history, before realizing the questions weren't intrusive at all — they were simply how the team decided someone was worth trusting.*

> *The Local Denmark — A newcomer was advised outright to drop the instinct toward politeness and get blunt fast, after learning that staying quiet in meetings, which had felt respectful, was instead read by Danish colleagues as having nothing to contribute.*

> *Blind — A poster described the disorientation of watching an entire meeting nod along to a plan, only to see it quietly reversed days later with no one having voiced an objection in the room, and asked whether they had been too naive to take the agreement at face value.*

> *The Local Denmark — Readers discussing what it's like to work for a Danish boss noted that the same bluntness applied to layoffs as to everyday feedback, summed up by one respondent as: if you get fired, it's just business, delivered as plainly as a project update.*

### Conclusion

For a mover, the single fact worth internalizing is this: in Denmark, silence is read as the absence of an opinion; in the Philippines, silence is often the opinion. Prepare accordingly — speak early and often in Copenhagen, and in Manila, learn to hear what's being said in the gap between "I'll try" and "yes."

If a friend asked me over a drink, I'd say: go to Denmark if you want your ideas fought over in daylight, and go to the Philippines if you want your ideas gently redirected in the dark. Either way, nobody is lying to you. They are just using a different alphabet.
