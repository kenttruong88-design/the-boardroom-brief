# Pakistan Says We Will See and Britain Says With Respect, and Both Mean Something Else

🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom
*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Pakistan, "we will try" can be a polite no. In Britain, "with the greatest respect" is an impolite one. Both countries run on elaborate courtesy, and both conceal their real verdicts inside it, which makes the meeting room less a place for decisions than a place for interpreting them. The Pakistani version tends to unfold slowly and bow to rank. The British version starts on time, discusses its own punctuality and concludes that someone should circulate the minutes. A newcomer from either country will spend the first six months with the wrong decoder.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive on time, even if the meeting starts late | Treat your own punctuality as a lesson for others |
| Make time for small talk and a question about family before business | Open with the agenda and the deadline |
| Listen for what is implied when you hear "we will see" or "we will try" | Treat a polite phrase as a firm commitment |
| Address senior colleagues respectfully and let them lead the decision | Contradict a senior person openly in front of the room |
| Allow time for approvals, since several levels may need to sign off | Promise a client a decision by Friday |
| Follow up in writing after the meeting | Assume the verbal agreement will survive the weekend |

### United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive on time, since even a few minutes may leave a poor impression | Assume lateness is forgiven in the name of hospitality |
| Soften requests with phrases like "would you mind" or "could we possibly" | Issue instructions as bare commands |
| Treat "a bit tricky" as a serious problem | Take understatement at face value |
| Offer ideas spontaneously when invited to | Wait for the most senior person to speak first |
| Pay attention to praise mixed with criticism | Hear only the compliment |
| Use first names, including with senior staff | Insist on formal titles |

## Pakistan: rank, relationship and the pause

Pakistani meetings are built on relationship before agenda. Expat Arrivals notes that time is more flexible than in Europe or the USA, that meetings may start late or be cancelled at the last minute, and that foreigners are nonetheless expected to turn up on time. Workplaces place great emphasis on rank, senior leaders make the decisions and junior staff rarely disagree openly with their bosses. Approvals can take time because several levels of authorisation are often involved. Communication is courteous and indirect, and it protects face: "we will see" or "we will try" can mean no. Unhurried small talk, including questions about family, normally comes before business, since trust is built first.

The hierarchy is also reflected in the data. Hofstede Insights scores Pakistan at 55 for power distance and 14 for individualism, making it strongly collectivist, which is a technical way of saying that the meeting is about the group and the boss, and rarely about the individual who disagrees. A Dawn opinion piece on productive conflict makes the same point in management language: in hierarchical organisations, junior staff may hold back challenges to seniors for fear of career consequences, producing an echo chamber in which foreseeable problems go unraised. The piece is not a study of Pakistani offices, but its description is recognisable to anyone who has watched a room nod at a bad idea.

The efficiency of the meeting itself is a separate question. A 2022 knowledge brief from the Pakistan Institute of Development Economics, drawing on interviews with serving and retired officials, describes meetings that lack agendas and pre-circulated documents, include irrelevant attendees and pull staff from their tasks. The brief offers no quantitative data and concerns government rather than business, so it should not be generalised. A 2015 ProPakistani article describes an authoritarian management style in which employees fear for their jobs if they voice opinions. The article is dated, but the instinct it describes remains a plausible explanation for why meetings in a hierarchy can be long and still say very little.

The rhythm of the day adds its own rules. A guide from the language-learning site Ling, which should be treated as a secondary source, says strategic meetings are often held around ten in the morning and open with refreshments and friendly conversation, that the standard working week is up to roughly 48 hours and that Friday hours are shortened for prayer. Expat Arrivals similarly advises scheduling meetings for late morning or early afternoon, and a little earlier during Ramadan, when working hours are shorter and the pace slows. None of this is exotic, but it means a calendar invitation built around a British nine o'clock start may meet a polite, silent, and entirely rational lack of enthusiasm.

## United Kingdom: the meeting that contains no meeting

British meetings are frequent, punctual, polite and, according to the British themselves, mostly unnecessary. A Raconteur survey with Attest of 1,000 UK professionals, reported in 2025, found that almost one in ten spend more than 15 hours a week in virtual or in-person meetings, and 18 percent spend more than ten. A study by Brother UK, reported by HR Magazine, found that 55 percent of office workers feel they waste too much time in meetings and 81 percent believe shorter meetings could achieve the same result. Respondents identified waffling, excessive small talk and late joiners as chief offenders. The survey was commissioned by a vendor, so it should be read with suitable scepticism, though few readers will find it unfamiliar.

Style matters more than structure. Harzing's notes on British culture describe a high-context society where understatement is common, "not bad" can mean "very good" and "I hear what you say" can signal disagreement. "With the greatest respect" signals strong objection, and "I would suggest" can be a firm instruction. Remitly's guide for immigrants adds that performance feedback is often mixed with praise, and that "a bit tricky" may denote a serious obstacle. Interrupting is considered impolite, offering ideas spontaneously is generally welcomed, and first names are normal even for senior managers. A guide written for Pakistani newcomers by a money-transfer company adds that the British are generally more reserved about expressing emotion in public than Pakistanis tend to be, that non-verbal cues carry significant weight and that punctuality is highly valued. The advice to watch how colleagues talk to each other for the first few weeks is the most useful sentence in the genre, and also the most British: observe, say nothing and apologise if you are in the way.

The result is a culture that is flatter in form but no more direct in substance. Pakistani communication uses rank to avoid saying no. British communication uses politeness to avoid saying no. The two systems share a goal, which is to avoid a scene, and differ in method: Pakistan hides the objection behind hierarchy, and Britain hides it behind humour.

## The Reckoning

On punctuality and agenda, the United Kingdom wins, if winning is defined as starting when the invitation says. A Pakistani meeting may begin late, but it frequently produces a stronger relationship, and relationships are the mechanism by which things happen later. The British meeting starts promptly, ends politely and may produce no decision at all, because everyone has been far too considerate to insist on one.

On candour, neither wins. The Pakistani employee is constrained by rank, the British by manners. The practical difference is who can break the code. In Pakistan, the senior person can say what they like, and others interpret it. In Britain, nearly everyone speaks in code, and the senior person is expected to speak in it as well. A newcomer with a direct style will find Pakistan more forgiving of the boss and Britain more forgiving of the junior, provided the junior phrases the objection as a question.

## Conclusion

The practical approach is to treat the first few meetings as listening exercises. In Pakistan, note who speaks last, since that person usually decides, and take small talk seriously, because it is where the trust is built. In Britain, note who is smiling while saying that something is interesting, and translate accordingly. Ask for follow-up in writing in both countries. In the first it reduces the risk of a drifting commitment, and in the second it converts a polite suggestion into something that can be referred to later. It also helps to remember that the same sentence carries different weight in each room. A Pakistani manager who says the matter will be looked into may be offering a genuine gesture of respect, while a British manager who says the same thing may be closing the file. Asking a second, concrete question, such as who will do what by when, is the most portable skill either country offers.

Whichever direction you move, bring the patience that the receiving culture assumes you already have. The meeting will end, someone will say they will circulate notes and, in either country, whether anything follows will depend on who was actually listening.
