# Poland Has the Women Managers; Ireland Has the Maternity Leave and the Complaint Form

🇵🇱 Poland · 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Poland reports a smaller gender pay gap than the EU average and a larger share of women in management than Ireland's progressive reputation might lead you to expect, while Ireland offers longer statutory maternity leave and a louder public argument about discrimination. Both facts are true, neither is a verdict, and anyone relocating on the strength of either should read what follows with a pen. Gender in the workplace is one of the few subjects on which two EU members can differ on almost everything except the vocabulary.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to meet women in management, particularly in finance and administration | Assume a woman in the room is junior or supporting |
| Prepare documents and credentials thoroughly, as Polish employers value paperwork | Treat a detailed CV check as a personal slight |
| Ask a female manager directly about progression, since many report few women at board level | Assume mid-level equality means the top is equal too |
| Use formal titles and polite forms until invited to drop them | Open with first names and jokes in a first meeting |
| Ask about pay bands in writing, particularly in the private sector | Rely on the national average pay gap as evidence for your employer |
| Raise concerns about stereotyping through HR or a manager you trust | Expect your employer's diversity policy to be vigorously enforced |

### In Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask how a role's maternity and return-to-work policy works in practice | Assume the statutory minimum is the employer's full offer |
| Check whether the company has set a target for senior female representation | Take a diversity slogan as evidence of a pipeline |
| Document concerns about unequal treatment in writing and early | Let a remark that "was only a joke" go unrecorded |
| Be wary of casual gendered language, such as calling women "girls" in meetings | Mirror the local banter before you understand who uses it and why |
| Ask whether flexible arrangements are used by men as well as women | Presume flexibility is career-neutral |
| Use the formal channels available if discrimination persists | Wait on goodwill to fix a structural problem |

## Poland

Poland's headline numbers are better than its reputation. According to Eurostat figures reported by the Warsaw Business Journal, the country's 2023 gender pay gap was 7.8 per cent, against an EU average of 12.0 per cent, and among the lowest in the bloc. Experts quoted in the same piece caution that a small gap does not necessarily mean greater equality, since it may reflect lower female participation or part-time work. The Ministry for Family and Social Policy offers another view: the private sector gap was 12.9 per cent in 2022, while the public sector stood at 2.3 per cent. A national average, in other words, is a country-sized blur.

Women are well represented in management. The Polish Economic Institute found that 43 per cent of managers were women, compared with 35 per cent across the EU, and Eurostat places Poland third in the EU for women in management behind Latvia and Sweden. In finance and accounting, women hold about 60 per cent of roles but only 28 per cent of top posts, according to Careers in Poland. At the very top, the numbers shrink: at the end of 2024 women held 18.4 per cent of board seats in the 140 largest companies listed in Warsaw. In tech, Beetroot reports that women account for 16.7 per cent of those employed, even though about 43 per cent of STEM graduates are women.

The same sources add texture to the leadership figures. In a Hays Poland survey, 46 per cent of respondents had a female direct supervisor, yet women leaders were far more likely than male managers to lead mostly female teams, at 78 per cent. In finance, 62 per cent of women reported satisfaction with their roles, against 74 per cent of men, and many described being confined to specialist support positions rather than strategic ones. Seniority is available; it is simply not evenly distributed.

The cultural overlay is more traditional than the management statistics suggest. Hays Poland found in 2024 that 53 per cent of women reported gender-related career obstacles, compared with 25 per cent of men, and 54 per cent of women cited hiring or promotion decisions based on stereotypes. In a February 2025 survey, 46 per cent named discrimination and bias as the main barrier to women's career growth. Hofstede Insights scores, as compiled by one management reference, give Poland 64 on masculinity, 68 on power distance and 93 on uncertainty avoidance, which describes a workplace that values formality, hierarchy and rules. A foreign resident of Warsaw advises adjusting your manner to the person in front of you, which, in a hierarchical environment, is sound advice for everybody.

## Ireland

Ireland is more comfortable in conversation about equality than in the equality itself. According to the Central Statistics Office's Gender Balance in Business survey, reported by the Irish Times in October 2025, women made up 19.2 per cent of chief executives, 32.3 per cent of senior executives and about 27.9 per cent of board seats at large businesses with 250 or more employees. In construction, the share of women among senior executives was 15.2 per cent, and about a third of enterprises had set a target for female senior executive representation. Targets exist, which is progress, and they are not yet met, which is the usual ending of a progress report.

Surveys paint a more uncomfortable picture beneath the averages. A 2023 HR Buddy survey of 500 women, reported by the Irish Examiner, found that about one in three had experienced gender-based discrimination, 19 per cent reported sexual harassment, and seven in ten thought women were more likely to be passed over for promotion. It is a consultancy survey rather than a national census, and should be read as such. The Irish Times has also noted that a 2024 Web Summit survey of 1,000 women in tech found half had experienced sexism at work in the previous year.

Caring responsibilities do much of the rest. RTE, citing CSO data, reports that nearly a third of women starting maternity leave received no employer payment during it, that maternity benefit stood at EUR 274 a week, and that just over half of employed fathers did not claim paternity benefit in 2020. Statutory leave is generous by European standards, at 26 weeks plus up to 16 unpaid, but the income and the career gap fall on mothers anyway. The cost shows up later, too: the ESRI found male pension pots for ages 60 to 65 almost twice the size of female ones, a 35 per cent gap, which is the sort of figure that arrives long after the careers that caused it. Hofstede scores, as compiled by the same management reference, give Ireland 68 on masculinity and only 28 on power distance, so the hierarchy is soft and the competitiveness is not.

## The Reckoning

Poland offers more women in management and a smaller reported pay gap; Ireland offers more generous leave and a more openly conducted argument about discrimination. Neither is clearly ahead. Polish women are well represented in the middle of organisations and thinner at the top, and report stereotype-driven obstacles at a rate that sits uneasily with a pay gap of 7.8 per cent. Irish women face a pay structure shaped by caring, a top tier that remains overwhelmingly male at chief executive level, and a visible set of corporate targets that are, at least, public.

For a woman choosing between them, the practical questions are different. In Poland, ask about promotion beyond mid-level and about sector, since the private and public gaps diverge so widely. In Ireland, ask about leave top-ups, return-to-work support and whether flexibility is genuinely available to men. For a man, the lesson is simpler: in both countries, the workplace is no longer a place where gender goes unremarked, and the sensible response is to remark on it carefully.

## Conclusion

Neither country has solved gender at work, and both are willing to show you a favourable statistic while you are deciding. Poland gives you a high share of female managers and an unusually small pay gap on paper; Ireland gives you statutory leave, public targets and a legal framework that is easy to find. Check the sector, the specific employer and the way your own career path looks after children before treating either as an answer.

The honest summary is that both countries treat equality as a destination, and both have booked a very long journey.
