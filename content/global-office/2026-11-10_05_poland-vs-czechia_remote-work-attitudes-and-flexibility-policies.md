# Poland Legislated the Home Office, Czechia Negotiated It, and Both Are Waiting for the Boss to Agree

🇵🇱 Poland · 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

According to Eurostat, 5.5 percent of employed Poles and 6.6 percent of employed Czechs usually worked from home in 2024, against 8.9 percent across the European Union. By that measure the two neighbours are practically one country, a mildly sceptical one. The differences lie elsewhere: in what the law requires an employer to pay for, in how loudly employees object to being recalled, and in the gap between the number of office days workers want and the number their managers have in mind.

## Poland: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get remote or hybrid work agreed in your contract or an amendment, even though no written form is required | Assume that 24 occasional remote days a year is a right; the employer may decline |
| Confirm that the employer covers equipment, installation, electricity and telecom costs | Buy your own laptop without agreeing a cash equivalent first |
| Ask for the company's remote-work regulations or union agreement | Assume the hybrid pattern is fixed; teams often set their own days |
| Use the right to request remote work if you are pregnant or a parent of a child under 4 | Expect automatic approval; refusal is allowed if the work makes remote impossible |
| Plan on two to three office days as the market norm | Expect fully remote outside IT and business services |
| Remember a binding request to return to the office takes effect after 30 days if no date is agreed | Build a relocation around an arrangement your manager can revisit |

## Czechia: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Insist on a written teleworking agreement | Rely on a verbal assurance from a manager |
| Read the reimbursement clause before signing | Assume costs are paid; they can be waived in writing |
| Ask in the interview how many office days are expected | Assume a two-remote, three-office pattern will survive the year |
| Use your right to request if you are pregnant or caring for a child under 9 | Treat the right to request as a right to receive |
| Check the notice period for withdrawing the arrangement, 15 days by default | Plan a move around an arrangement either side can withdraw |
| Look at the sector: finance, shared services and administration are pulling staff back | Expect a bank to behave like a startup |

## Poland: The Law Is Clear, the Calendar Is Not

Poland amended its Labour Code to regulate remote work in April 2023, and the Ministry of Family, Labour and Social Policy describes the result with admirable clarity. Remote work may be agreed at hiring or later, with no written form required. The employer must supply the necessary equipment and cover its installation and maintenance, together with electricity and telecommunications costs. Employees may ask for up to 24 days of occasional remote work a year, which the employer may decline. Pregnant employees and parents of children under four may ask for remote work, and a refusal must be justified. Either side may ask to return to the office, with the request becoming binding after 30 days if no date has been agreed.

Practice is less tidy than statute. A Colliers Define survey reported by the Warsaw Business Journal in July 2025 found that Polish office workers spent 3.6 days a week on site while preferring 2.6, and that only 32 percent could choose their location freely. IT and business services enjoyed the most autonomy, manufacturing and the public sector the least. A JLL survey reported by the British Polish Chamber of Commerce in 2024 found average attendance of 2.4 days and a telling mismatch of expectations: 38 percent of employers expected four to five days in the office, while only a quarter of private-sector employees wanted that many. The same survey found that 58 percent of employees say home offers better focus, while 55 percent regard the office as essential to company culture, a pair of convictions that can coexist only in a survey. Staff in business services averaged a single office day a week.

Colliers Define's later report, covered by Eurobuild CEE in October 2026, put 93 percent of Polish organisations on hybrid models and only 1 percent on fully remote, with 58 percent requiring two to three office days a week. Poland has, in short, produced a nation of hybrids with a statutory entitlement to a laptop. Whether the arrangement endures depends largely on whether a given manager reads the survey that says 87 percent of employees consider hybrid effective, or the one that says 85 percent of employers disagree, both compiled by the payments-benefits firm Benefit Systems from Polish press surveys.

## Czechia: Flexible on Paper, Contractual in Practice

The Czech amendment, in force from the autumn of 2023 according to L&E Global, takes a more contractual approach. Teleworking requires a written agreement, and either side may withdraw from it on 15 days' notice unless the agreement says otherwise. The employer must reimburse costs for each hour worked, at a proven amount, a flat rate set by a ministry decree, or a higher agreed figure, but the parties may agree in writing in advance that nothing is owed. Pregnant employees and those caring for children under nine or another person long term may request teleworking, and a refusal must be explained in writing. Hofstede Insights scores the Czech Republic at 74 on uncertainty avoidance, which helps explain the enthusiasm for paperwork.

The attitudes are firmer than the law. In a February 2025 Expats.cz report, Agnieszka Pietrasik of the recruiter Hays described a clear trend towards office returns in finance, accounting, shared service centres, administration, construction and IT, with many large firms requiring three or four days. Business leaders in the piece saw a shift from the 2+3 model, two remote days and three in the office, towards 3+2 or 4+1 as inevitable, though recruiters warned that firms enforcing attendance too strictly risk losing young, skilled workers. A Předvýběr.cz survey reported in December 2025 found that two-thirds of Czech workers prefer remote or hybrid arrangements, that nearly four in ten hybrid workers would change jobs or disengage if the option were removed, and that over half admit to being more productive in the office. Both findings can be true, which is the usual condition of workplace surveys.

Eurostat adds a note of caution. In 2024 only 3.0 percent of Czech employees usually worked from home, against 26.8 percent of the self-employed. Czechia's home-office culture, at least as measured, is largely a freelancer's culture. The demand side is more emphatic than the supply side. The same Expats.cz report cites a StartupJobs survey in which IT and startup employees valued remote work above financial bonuses, and its chief executive, Filip Mikschik, warned that companies cannot enforce attendance too strictly without losing talent. Polish figures compiled by Benefit Systems point the same way: 62 percent of job applicants said they would apply only if they could choose their way of working, and 44 percent would never apply without a remote option. An open reader poll on Expats.cz, self-selected and unscientific with 993 votes, found 47 percent able to work remotely whenever they wished and 10 percent not at all, which says more about who reads Expats.cz than about Czechia.

## The Reckoning

On paper Poland gives the employee a firmer start. The employer pays for the equipment and the running costs by default; in Czechia they pay unless the contract says they need not. Poland's request right covers parents of children under four, Czechia's those under nine, a more generous threshold that the employer is nevertheless free to refuse. In both countries the real policy is whatever the individual employer will sign, and in both, a request right is a right to ask.

In practice the two markets converge on the same compromise of two or three office days, with the argument concentrated on the margins: whether it is three or four, and who decides. Poland's data suggest employers pushing against employee preference; Czechia's suggest workers prepared to switch jobs over it. A candidate with scarce skills holds the stronger hand in either country. A candidate without them holds a statute and a request form.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — paraphrased experience: A Polish commenter, writing in 2023, doubted claims that office attendance had returned to 70 to 90 percent of its former level. Most of their friends worked from home, remote jobs had grown relative to on-site ones compared with 2019, and most on-site jobs in their area were hybrid with two or three remote days a week.*

> *Hacker News — paraphrased experience: A subcontractor to a Polish mobile carrier recalled, in March 2020, that the company suddenly moved its staff to remote work, something it had never done before. The episode suggests how recent the idea still is in some large Polish employers.*

> *Hacker News — paraphrased experience: A Prague-based manager said in 2022 that their engineering team had been fully remote since long before the pandemic, and that they had years of experience running such teams from the Czech capital.*

> *Expats.cz — paraphrased experience: A hybrid worker with a long commute told the site that she found her work better and less stressful during full-time home working, and that the journey leaves her tired before the day begins. A freelancer in the same feature said she is much less efficient in an office, citing micromanagement and no control over the room temperature.*

> *Expats.cz — paraphrased experience: An employee at a large software company said he was unhappy at being asked to return to the office three days a week because of the extra cost and commuting time, while a colleague at a brewer said some flexibility survives because arrangements can still be agreed with managers.*

## Conclusion

If remote work matters to you, treat it as a negotiated term, not a national characteristic. In Poland, ask for it in the contract, confirm who pays for the equipment, and use the statutory request rights if they apply. In Czechia, get the agreement in writing, read the reimbursement and withdrawal clauses, and ask how many office days the team expects before you accept rather than after. In both, the sector matters more than the border: IT and business services remain the most flexible, and finance and manufacturing the least.

Neither country will offer you the fully remote life as a default, and both are happy to tell you about it in a well-drafted clause. The good news is that the clause is, at least, enforceable. The bad news is that the manager remains, as ever, outside the jurisdiction.
