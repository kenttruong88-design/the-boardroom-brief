# Seed Rounds and Siestas: Startup Versus Corporate Life in Greece and Cyprus

🇬🇷 Greece · 🇨🇾 Cyprus

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In 2025, Greek startups raised more than 732 million euros across 95 funding rounds, according to The Recursive. In 2026 to mid-October, Cypriot startups raised about 113 million euros across 28 deals, according to the Cyprus Mail. Both countries are in a mood of cautious celebration, and both are finding that capital is easier to acquire than a culture that knows what to do with it. The question for a professional is whether to bet on a startup or retreat to a corporate desk, and the answer depends on which island of the Mediterranean one lands on.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Greece

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read any overtime clause carefully; since Law 5239/2025, 13-hour days are permitted with the employee's consent, at a 20 per cent premium for the first extra hour and 40 per cent after | Treat "voluntary" as meaning what it says in a firm that sets targets around the extra hours |
| Look at startups in AI, health tech and shipping-related fields, where Greek ecosystem reporting points to the most activity | Expect local customers and cheap talent to be the advantage; one founder found both assumptions wrong |
| Ask whether a startup's funding round is seed or later; about 70 per cent of 2025 deals were pre-seed or seed | Assume a funded startup pays like a multinational |
| Compare take-home pay with Athens rent before accepting; forum posters describe 1,000 euros gross leaving about 800 net | Ignore the salary arithmetic because the project is exciting |
| Use corporate and multinational roles to build skills, since large firms are starting to treat startups as sources of R&D and talent | Expect bureaucracy to vanish because the company has a ping-pong table |

### Cyprus

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether the employer can sponsor a work permit before investing in interviews; an American newcomer in Limassol found it very difficult | Assume the open ecosystem means open hiring for every nationality |
| Consider a startup first and a larger company second, a route one Cypriot developer recommends for learning both breadth and procedure | Expect the same large-company career ladder as in a bigger market; 93.6 per cent of firms have fewer than ten employees |
| Look at ICT, which EURES lists among Cyprus's shortage occupations | Count on grant-funded roles as permanent; the Cyprus Mail says grants cannot replace a private venture market |
| Compare pay to housing costs; EURES notes many workers spend 40 per cent or more of monthly earnings on housing | Read the 2023 gross average of 2,224 euros without checking what the rent will take |
| Build a public portfolio, as the Cypriot developer urges, since hands-on work counts alongside a degree | Wait for a corporate employer to recognise potential without evidence |

## Greece: Capital, Law and the Slow Business of Trust

Greece is the larger market and carries the heavier past. The Recursive reports that 2025 funding rose 35 per cent on 2024, that the country reached a record 18 active venture funds, most backed by the Hellenic Development Bank of Investments, and that AI was the most active category, followed by health technology. Sifted cites Endeavor Greece as counting more than 1,000 startups worth over 12 billion dollars. The same reporting is candid about the obstacles: unclear rules for university spin-offs, a gap between entrepreneurship education and company-building, and, in one interviewee's phrase, a country that has excelled at operations and now needs a culture of creators.

The corporate side is shaped by the labour market. Mondaq reports that Law 5239/2025 lets a single employer schedule up to 13 hours in a day with the worker's explicit consent, for up to 37.5 days a year, and makes a four-day week of 10-hour shifts permanent where both sides agree. Scroll.in, reporting OECD data, says full-time Greek employees work about 1,900 hours a year against 1,510 in the UK and 1,330 in Germany, while a labour institute survey found nearly 60 per cent of workers rejecting the 13-hour day outright. To Vima's columnist called the overtime premium an institutionalisation of burnout instead of a pay rise. The same opinion piece observed that young workers increasingly weigh the security of a paycheque against the risk of striking out alone.

That choice is sharper because the corporate paycheque is not generous. Scroll.in puts the minimum wage at 880 euros a month after a 6 per cent increase. Posters on Blind describe junior software salaries around 12,000 euros a year and senior ones between 24,000 and 36,000, though these are anecdotes, not statistics. Founders interviewed by Sifted add that bureaucracy complicates company setup and that startups struggle to hold experienced staff they cannot afford to train for years. The startup mindset in Greece, then, is less a lifestyle than a calculated escape from a corporate system that is secure on paper and thin in practice.

There are signs that the two cultures are starting to meet. The Recursive reports that corporations have moved from treating startups as marketing or corporate-responsibility projects towards seeing them as sources of research, products and talent, and it points to early signs of brain gain, with a Pfizer digital innovation centre in Thessaloniki as one example. The obstacle is familiar to anyone who has sat in a budget meeting: reskilling and innovation pay off late, so they lose to immediate sales and operations. A corporate employee in Greece who wants a startup mindset will often find that the mindset is welcome and the budget is not.

## Cyprus: Small Island, Smaller Safety Net

Cyprus has a different problem: it is small, and everything is measured in tens of millions instead of hundreds. According to the Cyprus Mail, startups raised 112.8 million euros across 28 rounds in 2026 to mid-October, about 7 million short of the roughly 120 million raised in all of 2025, with the three largest rounds accounting for about 75 per cent of the total. The median investment was one million dollars. A separate Cyprus Mail report counts more than 150 deep-tech startups and almost one billion euros in competitive research funding over a decade, but concludes that it is much harder to show the ecosystem can repeatedly produce companies that scale internationally.

The corporate landscape is not one of towering headquarters. EURES reports that micro firms make up 93.6 per cent of enterprises and employ 39.8 per cent of workers, against 18.5 per cent in firms of 250 or more. Gross average earnings in 2023 were 2,224 euros against 3,417 in the EU27, though the employment rate of 75.2 per cent exceeds the EU rate and unemployment fell to 5.8 per cent in the first half of 2024. In such an economy, a startup is not an alternative to the corporate world so much as a variation on the family business. A Cypriot developer writing on Medium advises starting at a startup to do a bit of everything, then moving to a larger company to learn formal procedure, which is sensible and quietly assumes that the larger company exists.

For foreign workers, EURES notes that 21.7 per cent of the Cypriot workforce comes from outside the EU, which suggests the door exists. Yet the forum evidence is mixed. An American who moved to Limassol told other Expat.com posters that finding employers willing to sponsor a permit had been very difficult. The Cyprus Mail adds that the scaling gap needs investors willing to fund beyond the earliest stages, and that companies should buy from each other, which describes a market still learning to be a market.

Policy is pushing in the same direction. The Cyprus Mail reports a National AI Strategy 2032 that treats artificial intelligence as a national economic priority, an enlarged innovation funding framework approved in 2026, and an ambition for Limassol to become a regional meeting point for AI businesses and investors. The same article is careful to add that grants can help a company prove an idea but cannot replace a functioning private market. For an employee, the implication is practical: a grant-funded startup may offer interesting work and uncertain continuity, while a larger employer in tourism, shipping or healthcare, the sectors the article says startups should sell to, may offer the reverse.

## The Reckoning

Greece offers depth: more venture funds, more deals, a longer labour-law debate and a larger pool of both corporate employers and startups. It also offers a corporate culture that is protective on paper and demanding in practice, with long hours, thin pay and a government that, according to one DEV Community commenter, reverses policies when administrations change. The startup mindset there is a response to that environment, not a rebellion against a comfortable one.

Cyprus offers proximity and speed, but at a smaller scale. A startup employee is likely to be close to decisions, to the founder and to international customers; a corporate employee is likely to work in a small firm that looks like a startup with better tax planning. The honest comparison is that Greece asks what you will do with a larger, harsher market, while Cyprus asks what you will do in a smaller, friendlier one. Neither has yet shown that its startups routinely become its corporations.

The corporate mindset also differs in what it protects. In Greece, the argument is about hours and pay within established employers, with strikes and surveys marking the dispute. In Cyprus, the argument is about whether established employers of scale exist in sufficient numbers, with the evidence being that most workers sit in small firms. A Greek professional chooses a startup partly to escape a corporate system; a Cypriot professional may choose one because the alternative is another small firm.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — a Greek poster says a 1,000 euro salary leaves about 800 after tax, barely covering a small Athens flat, and compares it to what a waiter earns.*

> *DEV Community — a developer calls Greece a good place to live if you have above-average income, with strong engineering universities and multilingual colleagues, but says policy reversals, taxes and bureaucracy limit how far the software sector can grow.*

> *Sifted — a founder who moved a shipping-tech company back to Greece says the expected local customers and cheap talent did not materialise, and that most revenue comes from Norway, Singapore, Germany and Ireland.*

> *Medium — a Cypriot developer says Cyprus historically lacked a strong tech culture, that one now exists, and that starting at a startup before moving to a large company teaches both breadth and procedure.*

> *Expat.com — a recent American arrival in Limassol says employers willing to sponsor a work permit have been very hard to find, and another poster notes that some roles, such as manual software testing, are rarely offered locally.*

## Conclusion

If you want the largest local startup scene in the region, choose Greece, and read the overtime clause as carefully as the equity terms. If you want a small ecosystem close to international clients, choose Cyprus, and confirm your work permit before you confirm your enthusiasm. In both, compare net pay to housing, because a founder's optimism does not reduce rent.

The mindset worth bringing to either is the one the founders keep describing: build something, sell it abroad, and keep a corporate option in reserve. Both Mediterranean economies have discovered innovation. They are still negotiating the terms of employment.
