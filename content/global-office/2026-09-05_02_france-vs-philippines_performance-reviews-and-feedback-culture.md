# Why Getting Fired in France Feels Honest and Getting Praised in the Philippines Feels Like a Trap

🇫🇷 France · 🇵🇭 Philippines

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In France, a manager will tell you, to your face and often in front of the room, that your proposal was mediocre, then invite you to lunch. In the Philippines, a manager will tell you that your work was "quite good, maybe just a bit more polish needed," and you will spend the following week trying to work out whether you have been complimented or quietly informed you are being managed out. Both systems produce competent employees. Neither produces employees who enjoy the month their review is due.

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive at your entretien annuel with numbers, not adjectives — French managers respect a case, not a mood | Expect a compliment sandwich; positive feedback is rationed and negative feedback comes first, plain, and unpadded |
| Treat sharp criticism of your work as a critique of the work, not of you — colleagues who dismantle your deck often still take you to lunch after | Confuse the entretien annuel (performance) with the entretien professionnel (legally mandated career check-in every two years) — they serve different purposes |
| Push back with a reasoned counter-argument; debate is the currency of credibility here | Schedule anything requiring a decision in August — the office, and the decision, will not be there |
| Learn the difference between hierarchy on the org chart and hierarchy in the room — status is real, but open argument with a superior is normal | Over-smile or over-thank; relentless positivity reads as insincere rather than warm |
| Ask directly what you did wrong; a vague answer is worse than a blunt one | Assume no news is good news between reviews — France still runs on the single annual conversation more than continuous feedback |

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take corrective feedback into a private room, always, even for a minor error | Correct anyone in front of the team — public criticism triggers hiya (shame) and can end a working relationship, not just a project |
| Build pakikisama (rapport) before you need candor — trust is the precondition for honesty, not a bonus | Mistake a smile and "no problem, sir" for actual agreement that the plan will work |
| Learn to hear qualifiers as red flags: "medyo mahirap" ("a bit difficult") often means "not happening" | Expect a direct "I disagree" in a meeting, even when a subordinate can see a plan is flawed |
| Use "sir" or "ma'am" and title-based address; it is a hierarchy signal, not servility | Skip small talk before the business part of a one-on-one — it is not filler, it is the icebreaker that makes feedback land |
| Pair any critique with genuine, specific praise — the sandwich method is not corporate theatre here, it is the operating norm | Assume a good appraisal score means no underlying concerns; Filipino managers routinely soften scores to preserve harmony |

## France: The Cult of the Reasoned Attack

France's Hofstede Individualism score of 71 sits oddly next to a Power Distance score of 68, a combination Hofstede Insights itself flags as unusual, producing a workplace where hierarchy is respected on paper but argued with constantly in practice. The entretien annuel is less a morale exercise than a structured negotiation: employees arrive with self-assessed objectives and defend them, not simply receive a verdict. Since 2019, France has also required a separate entretien professionnel, focused on career development, at least every two years — a distinction newcomers usually learn by confusing it in front of HR.

The stereotype of French bluntness holds up better than most. In cross-cultural feedback studies cited by INSEAD Knowledge, France groups with Germany, the Netherlands, and Russia as cultures where managers are far more likely to "tell it like it is," delivering negative feedback directly while rationing praise — the inverse of the American instinct to cushion criticism in positivity. A 2015 survey of 2,200 non-French executives across 18 CAC 40 companies, reported by The Local France, found a majority reached for the word "hierarchy" to describe French management, with "arrogance" and "authoritarianism" close behind. Yet per Gallup's State of the Global Workplace, only 8 percent of French employees report being engaged at work, one of the lowest in Western Europe — suggesting the problem isn't the directness, but what rarely follows it: recognition.

## Philippines: Harmony as Infrastructure

The Philippines scores 94 on Hofstede's Power Distance Index, among the highest in the world, against an Individualism score of just 32 — a profile that makes the workplace function less like a marketplace of opinions and more like an extended family with a very clear org chart. Two concepts do the load-bearing work: hiya, the drive to avoid shame, and pakikisama, the imperative to preserve group harmony. Together they mean corrective feedback delivered in public isn't just impolite, it's structurally destabilizing — capable of undermining a working relationship, not just a project, according to HR guidance from workplace consultancies operating in Manila.

The management response has calcified into a well-documented technique: the sandwich, praise-criticism-praise, is not an imported gimmick but the default grammar for feedback in Philippine offices. The tradeoff shows up in the data. Filipino manager and essayist Jordan Imutan, writing on workplace communication in the Philippines, cites Gallup research finding that 58 percent of Filipino employees are uncomfortable sharing feedback upward — meaning the same harmony that protects dignity in the moment can suppress the early warning signs a manager most needs to hear.

## The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the paradox sharpens: France, the more individualist and outwardly confrontational culture, tells workers exactly what's wrong with them and still posts, per Gallup, some of Europe's lowest engagement. The Philippines, more hierarchical and outwardly deferential, rarely tells workers anything is wrong directly, and yet its call centers and BPOs are routinely cited by multinational clients for high customer-satisfaction scores — staff have absorbed, indirectly, exactly what's expected of them.

The honest reading is that both systems solve different failure modes. France optimizes against ambiguity: you will never wonder what your manager thinks, only whether you can survive hearing it. The Philippines optimizes against rupture: you will rarely be blindsided by a conversation, but you may be blindsided by a decision a quieter feedback loop failed to flag in time. Neither country would recognize the other's system as "giving feedback" at all.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — One respondent recalled a colleague who transferred from a French team to a branch in the American South and found the local habit of layering praise around every note "unbearable," reading it as insincere — while his own direct style left his new American reports feeling quietly discouraged.*

> *TheLocal.fr — Summarizing a Panthéon-Assas/Princeton survey of foreign executives at French blue-chip firms, the outlet reported most respondents reached unprompted for the word "hierarchy" to describe French management, with "arrogance" and "authoritarianism" close behind.*

> *Substack (Katherine Melchior Ray, cross-cultural communication consultant) — A case study described a French executive whose blunt, critical feedback style, standard at headquarters, landed on a team in South Carolina as arrogant and hurtful, though he believed he was simply being clear.*

> *Medium (Jordan Imutan, Filipino middle manager) — Reflecting on years managing teams across the Philippines' regions and dialects, he described feedback only landing once trust was built first, citing data that well over half of Filipino employees are too uncomfortable to raise concerns with managers directly.*

> *Expat.com forum — A Canadian who had managed agency teams in Manila described repeatedly discovering missed deadlines had been quietly re-routed through extra meetings and unspoken revisions rather than flagged early, requiring him to be, in his words, "fair but firm" to surface problems before they became crises.*

## Conclusion

If you are moving from France to the Philippines, the adjustment is not that feedback disappears — it's that you have to learn to hear it in qualifiers, pauses, and the gap between a compliment and a specific compliment. If you are moving the other way, the adjustment is not that your colleagues have become cruel — it's that they were always this direct, and the version of them that seemed reserved in the elevator was never going to be reserved in the entretien annuel. Bring data to Paris and patience to Manila, and you will survive either review cycle. Just don't expect either one to tell you, cleanly, whether you're actually doing a good job.
