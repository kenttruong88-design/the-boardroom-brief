**Closed for August: A Working Guide to France and Brazil's Very Different Definitions of "Off the Clock"**

**🇫🇷 France · 🇧🇷 Brazil**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

France logs roughly 1,505 working hours a year and considers that a compromise; Brazil logs closer to 1,709 and considers punctuality itself negotiable. Both countries will tell you, sincerely, that they have the healthier relationship with work. Both are partly right, for entirely different reasons — one has legislated its way out of the inbox, the other has simply declined to let the clock run the room.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take your full five weeks of paid leave — not taking it reads as poor planning, not dedication | Email colleagues on vacation expecting a reply; "le droit à la déconnexion" is a real, invoked right |
| Plan anything requiring a signature, a plumber, or a government office for anywhere but August | Schedule a big product launch for the first two weeks of August — half the country, and possibly your client, will be gone |
| Protect your full lunch hour; it is a working norm, not a luxury | Eat at your desk while working through lunch — colleagues will notice, and not approvingly |
| Track your hours past 35/week — RTT (extra days off) accrue and are yours to use | Assume unpaid overtime is normal; hours 36–43 carry a mandatory 25% pay premium by law |
| Expect debate before consensus in a meeting — disagreement is treated as rigor, not disloyalty | Mistake a long meeting for indecision; French colleagues are stress-testing the plan, not stalling it |

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build the relationship before the deal — a contract with someone who trusts you personally is worth more than one with a stranger | Expect a 9:00 a.m. meeting to start at 9:00; 9:15–9:30 is normal and not an insult |
| Learn to read a hesitant "yes" — it can mean maybe, or even no, delivered to protect the mood | Take a scheduling commitment at face value without a follow-up confirmation closer to the date |
| Greet colleagues with real warmth — the abraço and physical closeness are genuine trust signals, not informality creeping into hierarchy | Mistake warmth for flat hierarchy; the most senior person in the room still shapes the outcome |
| Use every day of vacation and every minute of a break you're entitled to under the CLT — colleagues do, without guilt | Chase a colleague for a fast reply outside work hours; once the day ends, most people stop thinking about the office entirely |
| Expect creative workarounds ("jeitinho") when a formal process stalls | Read a workaround as dishonesty; it's a different relationship to process, not an ethical shortcut |

### France: Legislated Rest

France's 35-hour week, introduced in 2000, rarely produces an actual 35-hour week in white-collar jobs — most professionals still work closer to 40 hours, per HR consultancy HReact, and are compensated for the difference either in cash (a mandatory 25% premium on hours 36 through 43, rising to 50% beyond that) or in banked time off known as RTT. What the law did succeed at is cultural: it made rest a default to be protected rather than a perk to be earned. French workers take a minimum five weeks of paid annual leave, and per France Today's account of OECD comparisons, French productivity per hour worked consistently outpaces longer-hours economies including the US, Japan, and South Korea — the quality-over-quantity argument the French have been making for decades, backed by the numbers.

The most visible expression of this is August, when — as one American who moved to Paris to babysit for local families discovered — "the majority of businesses" close outright. Her own account of the adjustment is blunt: "My American brain initially really could not comprehend the idea," she wrote, before eventually concluding that "the French see vacation as a right, not a reward" and that there's "no guilt attached to this rest." Roughly 70% of French workers take their main holiday in July or August, and Paris itself loses about a fifth of its population for the month — small shopkeepers hang handwritten signs reading "fermé pour congés annuels" and nobody treats it as a business emergency.

### Brazil: Relationship-Based Time

Brazil's labor code (the CLT) sets a standard 44-hour week with overtime capped at two hours a day and paid at a minimum 50% premium — rising to 100% for Sunday and holiday work — or banked through a "banco de horas" system that must be settled within six to twelve months. On paper, that's a fairly protective framework. In practice, the culture around time operates on a completely different logic than France's clock-based rest: Brazilian business culture treats punctuality as elastic and relationships as the actual currency. As one cross-cultural training site puts it, Brazilian negotiation happens "with people, not with organisations" — a contract means little without personal trust behind it, and a 9 a.m. meeting starting at 9:20 is not read as disrespect but as normal.

That flexibility, sometimes called jeitinho — a talent for finding a workaround when formal process fails — routinely reads as unreliability to colleagues from stricter, process-driven cultures. It isn't dishonesty; per one cross-cultural consultancy's framing, it reflects "a different relationship to process and structure that prioritises outcomes over procedures." The upside for work-life balance is real, though: forum accounts from foreigners working in Brazil consistently note that, once the workday ends, colleagues genuinely stop — Brazilians "don't start thinking about work until they get to their workplace, and stop thinking about it as soon as quitting time arrives," as one longtime expat on Expat.com's Brazil forum described it.

### The Reckoning

Both countries protect personal time fiercely; they simply disagree about what threatens it. France treats the inbox as the enemy and has built legal walls against it — the right to disconnect, the mandatory RTT, the untouchable August. Brazil treats the clock itself as the lesser concern and instead protects the relationship: punctuality bends, but once the workday is over, it is genuinely over, no lingering guilt, no phantom Slack notifications. An OECD hours count that puts France roughly 200 hours a year below Brazil actually understates the gap in lived experience, because a French 1,505-hour year comes with a hard stop nobody questions, while a Brazilian 1,709-hour year is punctuated by meetings that start whenever the room feels ready to start.

The clash comes when the two systems meet directly: a French manager sending a Tuesday-afternoon email expecting a same-day Brazilian reply will wait past the scheduled hour without cause for alarm; a Brazilian manager expecting a French colleague to "just check email" during the August shutdown will discover that the entire concept of "just checking" does not exist there in August, full stop.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com (Brazil forum) — A longtime foreign resident described the specific frustration of "the lateness to respond to messages, endless silence, especially when waiting for feedback" as the single biggest adjustment of doing business in Brazil, alongside relief that Brazilian colleagues genuinely never message about work after hours.*

> *The American in Paris (blog) — An American who moved to France to work with local families wrote that she initially could not process why "the majority of businesses" closed for the entire month of August, and that only after being caught out by it more than once did the shutdown become, in her words, "another part of my normal everyday French life."*

> *CultureFlow (cross-cultural consultancy) — A Brazil-focused trainer described watching a rule-oriented visiting executive treat a colleague's improvised workaround around a stalled bureaucratic process as evidence of unreliability, when the same move — jeitinho — was, to the Brazilian team, simply how competent people get things done.*

> *Commisceo Global (Brazil business guide) — The guide warns newcomers that a Brazilian "yes" in a meeting can mean yes, maybe, or a polite no, and that direct refusals are rare enough that hesitation, not a stated objection, is the real signal to watch for.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway for anyone weighing France against Brazil isn't which country works less — it's which kind of intrusion you'll tolerate worse. If a late-night email from your boss would ruin your evening, France's legislated boundaries will suit you; if a 9:15 meeting start would ruin your morning, Brazil will test your patience daily even though the actual workload may be lighter than it looks. Ask yourself honestly, over that drink: are you more offended by your phone buzzing at 9 p.m., or by the clock on the wall being treated as a suggestion? Whichever answer comes faster is the answer to where you'll actually be happy.
