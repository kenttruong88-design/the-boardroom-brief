# Tea Will Be Served Whether You Like It or Not: Turkey, Peru, and the Fine Print of Belonging

🇹🇷 **Turkey** · 🇵🇪 **Peru**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

---

In Turkey, 85 percent of people say they know someone they could rely on in a time of need — a figure that sounds reassuring until you learn it trails the OECD average of 91 percent, and that in a separate 2025 Pew Research survey, Turkey posted the lowest level of generalized social trust of the 25 nations polled, at 14 percent (Pew Research Center, 2025). Somehow these two facts coexist peacefully: Turks trust almost no one in the abstract and would still show up for you personally at 2 a.m. Six thousand miles away, Peru ranks 82nd out of 171 countries on the 2025 Global Social Progress Index, with particularly weak scores on citizen security and housing (Social Progress Imperative, 2025) — and yet a coworker you met on Tuesday may invite you to stay in his home, meet his mother, and eat a four-course lunch by Friday. Neither country's national statistics predict its personal warmth. This is, roughly, the entire story.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇷 Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the çay (tea) when offered, every time — refusal reads as a rejection of the relationship, not the beverage | Decline tea repeatedly to "save time"; there is no version of Turkish office life where this makes you look efficient |
| Learn colleagues' family situations before diving into business — small talk is due diligence, not delay | Launch straight into the agenda in a first meeting; it will read as cold and untrustworthy |
| Show up to the post-work gathering even briefly, especially in your first month | Treat afterwork socializing as optional networking — declining repeatedly gets quietly noted |
| Use formal titles (Bey, Hanım) with senior colleagues until invited to drop them | Assume first-name informality applies just because the office feels relaxed |
| Expect group harmony over blunt disagreement in meetings; read between the lines | Publicly contradict a senior colleague; raise concerns privately instead |
| Bring something to share if invited to someone's home or a meal | Arrive empty-handed to a home invitation — it's treated as a social event, not a courtesy call |

#### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept home invitations from coworkers — declining is read as disinterest in the relationship itself | Frame the relationship as purely professional; *confianza* (trust) is the actual currency of getting things done |
| Expect hierarchy to matter in meetings even amid personal warmth — deference to seniority and friendliness coexist | Mistake informality and hospitality for flat structure; decisions still flow top-down |
| Join the informal chat, coffee, or lunch ritual before business starts | Rush a first working relationship; rapport is the prerequisite, not a bonus |
| Lean on WhatsApp groups and coworking-space social calendars to build a circle beyond the office | Assume the office alone will generate your social life — colleagues often already have full family rosters |
| Show up to team celebrations (birthdays, anniversaries) — they are treated as genuine obligations | Treat a team-building event as skippable corporate theater; it is read as a measure of commitment |
| Reciprocate hospitality when you can — inviting someone back closes the loop | Let generosity go one-directional indefinitely; it eventually reads as extraction, not friendship |

### Turkey: Trust No One, Love Everyone You Actually Know

Turkey's collectivism is a documented, scored fact rather than a vibe: Hofstede Insights gives it an Individualism Index of 37, well below the global midpoint, meaning the "we" consistently outranks the "I" in workplace and social life (Hofstede Insights, Turkey-Peru comparison). What that number obscures is how selectively the collectivism gets applied. The Pew finding that only 14 percent of Turks say most people can be trusted is not a contradiction of Turkish hospitality — it is the mechanism that produces it. When generalized trust is low, particularized trust becomes the entire social operating system: you don't extend goodwill to strangers in the abstract, you extend enormous goodwill to the specific, named humans inside your circle, and getting into that circle is the whole professional and social project of a new job.

The office tea ritual is the clearest artifact of this. Çay is not caffeine delivery; it is a recurring, low-stakes opportunity to renew the specific-trust contract, several times a day, indefinitely. TUIK's 2025 time-use data shows Turks spending an average of four hours and thirty-four minutes a day on leisure, with visiting relatives accounting for 67.5 percent of social leisure activity and social media leisure use having roughly doubled since 2015, from 33.9 percent to 71.7 percent of leisure time (TUIK, Time Use and Daily Life Statistics 2025) — evidence of a culture that treats staying in touch as an active, near-constant task rather than a passive background fact. OECD data confirms Turkey punches above its weight on civic engagement specifically — voter turnout of 86 percent against an OECD average of 68 percent, driven partly by compulsory voting, but reinforcing a broader pattern of showing up (OECD Better Life Index). The lesson for newcomers: Turkish bonding culture asks little of your abstract faith in humanity and a great deal of your concrete, weekly attendance.

### Peru: Confianza as Load-Bearing Infrastructure

Where Turkish social life runs on renewable small rituals, Peruvian workplace bonding runs on a single, heavier concept: *confianza*, usually translated as "trust" but functioning closer to "personal collateral." Peru's Hofstede Individualism score of 20 — lower than Turkey's, and among the more collectivist scores globally — reflects a culture where the group, typically extended family first and close colleagues second, is expected to look after its own in exchange for loyalty (Hofstede Insights). This shows up structurally: INEI reports that the vast majority of Peruvians online use the internet chiefly for communication and staying connected, with WhatsApp topping the list of platforms used, ahead of Facebook, TikTok, and Instagram (INEI, ICT and Social Media Use Report) — infrastructure that maps neatly onto a culture organized around maintaining dozens of active personal threads rather than broadcasting to the abstract public.

This confianza-first system operates alongside, not instead of, hierarchy. Peruvian offices retain clear top-down decision structures and visible deference to seniority, even as personal warmth flows freely across those same ranks — a combination that can read as contradictory to outsiders used to equating friendliness with flattened structure. It isn't. And the warmth is not merely performative: Social Progress Index data placing Peru at 82nd of 171 countries, dragged down heavily by weak scores on citizen security and housing (Social Progress Imperative, 2025), arguably makes tight interpersonal networks less of a cultural nicety and more of a practical necessity — when public infrastructure is unreliable, the people you can call at short notice become the actual safety net.

### The Reckoning

Line the two cultures up and the apparent contradiction sharpens into a genuine finding: Turkey scores markedly better than Peru on formal civic engagement and considerably worse on generalized interpersonal trust, while Peru's Hofstede collectivism score is lower (more collectivist) than Turkey's despite weaker state-level social infrastructure to lean on. Both nations solve for connection, but at different altitudes — Turkey through frequent, ritualized micro-renewals of trust (the tea, the visits, the vote), Peru through fewer but deeper investments of confianza that then get asked to do more structural work, standing in for institutions rather than merely supplementing them.

For the newcomer, the practical difference is tempo. Turkish belonging accrues in small, frequent deposits — show up to enough teas and afterwork gatherings and the account fills gradually. Peruvian belonging tends to arrive in one disproportionate act of hospitality — the home invitation, the family meal — after which you are, somewhat abruptly, inside. Miss the frequent Turkish deposits and you stay a polite outsider indefinitely. Decline the single large Peruvian gesture and you may not get asked twice.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

*A Quora respondent describing a first work posting in Arequipa recalled a colleague, met only days earlier, offering a spare room, a city tour, and a four-course family lunch — hospitality so far ahead of the professional relationship that it took weeks to recalibrate what "coworker" was supposed to mean.*

*Another Quora thread on workplace culture wrestled with the opposite discomfort: colleagues who describe the office as "family" while some members of that office plainly do not feel like family at all, with posters divided on whether resisting the family framing makes someone antisocial or simply honest about professional boundaries.*

*On the Expat.com Türkiye forum, a longtime foreign resident responding to a thread on afterwork culture noted that the pattern felt nearly identical to one they'd already navigated in another country, needing barely any adjustment — a reminder that what feels like a uniquely Turkish ritual is, to well-traveled newcomers, a fairly recognizable genre of unwritten obligation.*

*A digital-nomad community resource on relocating to Lima advised that new arrivals should not expect the office itself to generate a social life, pointing instead to the dozens of WhatsApp groups organized around coworking spaces, sports, and shared-interest meetups as the actual entry point — advice framed as something the writer wished someone had told them before their first quiet month.*

*A separate account of Turkish workplace tea culture described the moment of realization that turning down çay was never really about the drink — that repeated refusals, however well-intentioned as a time-saving gesture, were quietly read by colleagues as a lack of interest in the relationship itself.*

### Conclusion

If you are taking a posting in Turkey, budget time for tea you did not ask for and relationships that accrue in small, frequent installments — and do not mistake low institutional trust for low personal warmth; they are separate ledgers. If you are taking a posting in Peru, be ready for hospitality that outpaces the professional relationship that technically justifies it, and understand that hierarchy and affection are not in tension there, whatever your instincts suggest. Both systems will eventually make you feel connected. Turkey will make you earn it one cup at a time. Peru will hand it to you all at once and quietly expect you to prove you deserved it.
