# Batik on Thursday, Khun on Friday: Dressing for Work in Malaysia and Thailand

🇲🇾 Malaysia · 🇹🇭 Thailand

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Malaysia has a government minister who announced, in 2023, that civil servants could wear batik at any hour of the working day, and that it was compulsory on Thursdays, though nobody would be punished for forgetting. Thailand has no such announcement and no such need. Its dress code is enforced the way most things in Thailand are enforced, by everyone noticing everything and saying nothing. Both countries sit within a tropical heat that makes a three-piece suit a form of endurance sport, and both expect you to turn up looking as though you made an effort anyway. The difference lies in who writes the rules, and who is expected to read between them.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear modest, tailored office clothing: collared shirt and closed shoes for men, suit or knee-length skirt for women | Wear shorts or flip-flops, even on casual Friday |
| Choose three-quarter sleeves as the safe default for women unless the company issues uniforms | Wear low-cut tops, see-through fabrics or hemlines above the knee |
| Check your employer's website or staff photos to see how people really dress | Assume sarees or kurtas are normal office wear; they are mostly for events |
| Expect batik on Thursdays in government offices and many companies that follow the lead | Treat the Thursday batik rule as a fine-bearing offence; it carries no penalty |
| Raise problems privately and indirectly to protect colleagues' face | Challenge a senior person in a meeting; the chain of command is real |
| Bring a light jacket, as government offices are set to 24-25C | Assume the air-conditioning matches the weather outside |

### Thailand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress conservatively for formal sectors: long trousers, long sleeves and closed shoes, with a tie for meetings | Wear flip-flops, tank tops, short shorts or miniskirts to work |
| Use "Khun" plus the first name until invited to do otherwise | Address Thai colleagues by surname; first names are the norm in speech |
| Return a wai, and let the senior person speak first and be greeted first | Challenge a senior colleague in public, or offer improvements without great care |
| Carry slip-on shoes, since some offices and all temples require shoes off | Wear black or bright red to a business meeting; black is associated with funerals |
| Wear yellow on Thai Father's Day if your office is government-linked | Ignore mourning periods; black and white can be customary for up to 100 days |
| Look neat and pressed; commenters link wrinkled shirts to a poor attitude | Assume casual means careless, even in an SME with no written policy |

## Malaysia: A Dress Code With a Dress Code

Malaysia's formality is layered. The Expat.com guide to Kuala Lumpur workplaces describes attire as modest and formal, with suits or tailored clothing, collared shirts, ties and closed shoes for men, and similarly covered-up options for women. Many companies allow a casual Friday of polo shirts and jeans, but shorts and flip-flops are not on the menu. A Kuala Lumpur resident answering a newcomer on Expat.com added that uniforms with printed corporate logos are a significant part of workplace life, and that bare skin is a serious misstep given Islamic influence on modesty. The government has added a layer of its own. According to BenarNews, civil servants may wear batik throughout the working day, it is compulsory on Thursdays, and the minister said the government would favour positive encouragement over penalties. The same announcement set government thermostats at 24 to 25 degrees Celsius, which means the one item the dress code actually requires is a cardigan.

The 2019 push began in Sabah, where the Rural Development Ministry, backing a state recommendation, set Thursdays for traditional wear. Employers have mostly followed the pattern of polite negotiation. In a 2019 Human Resources Online survey of 11 Malaysian HR leaders on traditional wear at work, most tied acceptance to the written dress code and the workplace context. PwC Malaysia pointed to its flex-wear policy, one firm noted that full-body coverage such as baju melayu is comparable to a suit, and one sceptic argued that overly comfortable clothing changes one's mindset. Notice what is absent: a rule that says traditional dress is forbidden. Formality in Malaysia tends to come from a strong chain of command and the habit of avoiding confrontation, which Expat.com's guide links to the idea of saving face. You can wear what you like provided it does not make your manager look bad.

The practical result is a country where the clothes are easier to get right than the conversations. A visitor who takes the advice of one FlyerTalk poster, to wear a full suit to meetings across Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia and Vietnam and remove the jacket once introductions are done, will not be criticised. A visitor who tells the boss in front of the team that the plan is flawed will be remembered for years.

## Thailand: Formality Without a Handbook

Thailand's formality is less a dress code than a social grammar. The Danish Trade Council describes Thai business culture as generally formal and both private companies and public offices as highly hierarchical, with little decision-making authority delegated below top management. For meetings it advises long trousers, long sleeves, closed shoes and a tie for men, and a skirt covering the knees for women. The Thaiger adds that professional titles plus first name are the rule until invited otherwise, and that "Khun" is the safe default. According to Expat.com's Bangkok guide, you are expected to wai to everyone on entering the office, and young people must show respect to seniors. The wai itself runs from lower status to higher, so you are not required to start it, only to return it gracefully.

The reality of dress is looser than the etiquette. JobsDB Thailand says many SMEs and modern corporates are casual, while banks, law firms, schools and hospitals lean towards formal wear or uniforms, and that unwritten rules apply everywhere. Expats quoted in Tales Magazine's Bangkok post reports describe business casual as the usual standard, with the jacket and tie reserved for embassies, corporate offices and government meetings, and with custom tailoring cheap enough that newcomers need not pack a wardrobe. Commenters on an ajarn.com piece about teachers' dress agreed that heat makes suits impractical but still expected neat, pressed, polished clothing, and some said that scuffed shoes and wrinkled shirts signal an attitude problem. The Danish Trade Council adds a practical note: shoes come off in some offices, so wear socks without holes.

The state occasionally joins in. In March 2026, as Pattaya Mail reported, a nationwide Proud to Wear Thai Attire campaign was launched in Bangkok, inviting people of all ages and genders to wear traditional Thai clothing or Thai fabrics. It was framed as an invitation, which in Thailand is a polite way of describing a hope. JobsDB adds the calendar: government offices often ask staff for yellow shirts on Thai Father's Day, and black and white is customary during royal mourning for up to 100 days.

## The Reckoning

Compare the two on rules and the contrast is stark. Malaysia writes things down: a batik day, a thermostat range, a casual Friday, a uniform with a logo. The expectations are public, and the penalties, as the batik announcement made explicit, are mild. Thailand writes almost nothing down and expects you to infer it from seniority, sector and the shoes of the person who greets you. The Malaysian newcomer asks what the policy is. The Thai newcomer asks who is senior, and then watches what they do with their hands.

On heat, they converge. Both countries accept jacketless shirts and slacks as practical, both expect closed shoes and covered shoulders, and both penalise flip-flops. The real distinction is where the formality lives. In Malaysia it lives in modesty and the chain of command, which mostly bite when you contradict someone. In Thailand it lives in age, status and ritual, which bite every morning at the door. Neither country is hostile to foreigners, and both forgive a clumsy first week. The forgiveness runs out around the time you stop noticing the details.

> *ajarn.com comments — Commenters on a piece about foreign teachers in Thailand said that tired shoes and creased shirts read as disrespect for the job, even though most accepted that suits are absurd in the heat.*

> *ajarn.com comments — One commenter said Bangkok offices have drifted from shirts and ties to slacks and polos, with full suits left mostly to executives, and a female teacher described a double standard in how women's clothing gets judged.*

## Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two on clothing alone, Malaysia is easier to prepare for and Thailand is easier to improvise in. In Kuala Lumpur, pack modest, structured office wear with covered shoulders and a layer for the air-conditioning, learn whether your employer observes batik Thursday and check whether the company issues uniforms. In Bangkok, pack less, buy tailoring locally, keep closed shoes polished, learn the wai and the word Khun, and work out the seniority map before you speak in a meeting.

In both countries, the best advice is the least glamorous: ask your new employer in writing what the expectations are, then look at what the most senior person in the room is wearing. The dress code is rarely the problem. It is merely the first place the hierarchy gets to grade you.
