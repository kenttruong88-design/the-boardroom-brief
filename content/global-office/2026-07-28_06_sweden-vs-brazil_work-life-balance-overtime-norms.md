**One Country Turns Your Overtime Into a Vacation Day; The Other Turns It Into a Lawsuit**

**🇸🇪 Sweden · 🇧🇷 Brazil**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Swedes work around 1,441 hours a year, roughly 18% below the OECD average, and in many companies unused overtime is simply converted into additional vacation time rather than paid out or banked as a badge of honor. Brazilians work a legally standard 44-hour week — longer than the European or North American norm — and when employers fail to pay the overtime premium the law requires, the dispute doesn't stay quiet: unpaid-overtime lawsuits are so common in Brazilian labor courts that employers bear the burden of proving they didn't happen. Two countries, two very different enforcement mechanisms for the same basic idea: your time after 5pm is not free for the taking.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a genuine 8am–4pm or similar structured day with a short lunch | Expect to be praised for staying late — it reads as poor time management, not dedication |
| Ask whether unused overtime converts to paid time off, as it does at many employers | Email colleagues after hours expecting a same-day reply |
| Use all 25+ legally guaranteed vacation days — nobody will silently judge you for it | Assume Swedish efficiency means understaffing; teams are built to finish on time |
| Take fika (coffee breaks) seriously as a real part of the workday, not a distraction from it | Schedule back-to-back meetings through the traditional fika slot |

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Track your hours carefully — CLT requires firms with 20+ staff to log time, and it protects you | Assume a verbal "we'll sort out the extra hours" is enforceable; get it in writing or logged |
| Expect a standard 44-hour week, typically 8am/9am to 5pm/6pm with an unpaid lunch hour | Assume Brazilians are "laid back" about work — most work a longer standard week than Europeans |
| Understand that overtime pay (50% premium on weekdays, 100% on Sundays/holidays) is a real legal right, not a courtesy | Waive documented overtime informally — Brazilian labor courts favor the employee when proof is disputed |
| Ask HR directly how your role is classified — "external" roles can be excluded from overtime tracking under specific conditions | Assume all salaried roles are automatically exempt from overtime; classification rules are specific and contestable |

### Sweden: The System Is Built Not to Need Overtime

Sweden's standard workweek is capped at 40 hours, and while the law technically permits up to 200 hours of overtime annually (50 hours monthly), the cultural default runs the opposite direction: overtime is rare enough that OECD figures put Swedish annual working hours nearly a fifth below the international average. Several employers convert unused overtime directly into additional paid time off rather than treating it as a badge of commitment, according to accounts of Swedish workplace policy, and the broader system — 25+ legally mandated vacation days, up to 480 days of shared parental leave, 90 of which are specifically reserved per parent and non-transferable — is designed to make long hours structurally unnecessary rather than merely discouraged.

The cultural enforcement mechanism matters as much as the legal one: colleagues in Sweden are widely described as genuinely respecting off-hours boundaries, with no expectation of after-hours email responsiveness. It's less that overtime is banned and more that a well-run Swedish team simply isn't supposed to need it, and asking for it repeatedly reflects poorly on management, not on the employee being asked.

### Brazil: The Right Is Legal, and Litigated

Brazil's CLT (Consolidação das Leis do Trabalho) sets the standard week at 44 hours — a bit longer than the European or North American norm most newcomers expect — spread across five days, commonly 8am or 9am to 5pm or 6pm with an unpaid lunch hour. Overtime is capped at two hours a day, pushing the legal daily maximum to 10 hours, and must be compensated at least 50% above the base hourly wage on weekdays and 100% on Sundays and public holidays.

What distinguishes Brazil isn't the cap — it's the enforcement culture. Employers with more than 20 staff are legally required to track working hours and breaks, and unpaid-overtime claims are frequent enough in Brazilian labor courts that the burden of proof falls on the employer to demonstrate hours weren't worked, rather than on the employee to prove they were. This produces a genuinely adversarial dynamic around timekeeping that a newcomer from a lower-litigation country may not expect: keeping your own hour log isn't paranoid in Brazil, it's standard practice, because your legal recourse depends on it.

### The Reckoning

Both systems ultimately protect the same principle — that unpaid extra hours are not simply the cost of employment — but they enforce it through nearly opposite mechanisms. Sweden achieves the outcome through cultural consensus and generous structural defaults, so thoroughly embedded that the legal overtime cap almost never gets tested. Brazil achieves a comparable outcome through adversarial legal enforcement, precisely because the cultural default assumes employers will under-pay unless a paper trail forces the issue.

Hofstede's uncertainty avoidance score for Brazil (76) towers over Sweden's (29), which tracks neatly with why Brazilian workplace culture defaults to documentation and legal recourse where Swedish culture defaults to mutual trust and informal norm enforcement. Neither approach is more "hardworking" than the other, despite a persistent international stereotype about Brazilian laid-back-ness — Brazilians work a longer standard week than Swedes by law, they simply fight harder to be paid properly for anything beyond it.

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Sweden, unlearn the instinct to treat visible lateness as a signal of dedication — the system is built to make it unnecessary, and using it anyway marks you as poorly managed, not hardworking. If you're moving to Brazil, keep your own hour log from day one and understand that the standard week is longer than its European neighbors, whatever the stereotype says — but that the overtime premium above it is a real, litigated right, not a courtesy your employer can quietly skip. The honest version, over a drink: Sweden trusts you to go home; Brazil will go to court to make sure you got paid for staying.
