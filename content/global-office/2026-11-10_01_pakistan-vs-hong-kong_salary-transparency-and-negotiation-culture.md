# In Karachi Nobody Knows What Anyone Earns; in Hong Kong Everybody Does, and Nobody Says

🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Pakistan and Hong Kong share one habit, which is to treat a salary as a private matter somewhere between a medical history and a family grudge. They arrive at this position by different routes. In Pakistan, according to Dawn, roughly 72 percent of the workforce sits in the informal economy, so there is often no payroll in which a number could be disclosed. In Hong Kong, the Census and Statistics Department can tell you the median monthly wage to the nearest hundred dollars, HK$21,200, and still hardly anyone will discuss theirs. One country lacks the data; the other lacks the nerve. Either way, the candidate negotiating an offer is largely guessing.

## Pakistan: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wait for a formal offer before naming a figure, and ask what range the company has budgeted | Volunteer your current salary; Pakistani recruiters now advise redirecting to the value you bring |
| Negotiate benefits when base pay is fixed: family health insurance, provident fund matching, fuel or transport allowances | Phrase demands as entitlement; "I'm hoping we can" travels better than "I deserve" |
| Confirm the currency, the working week and the real scope of the role before you compare offers | Trust a grand job title to describe the pay, or the duties |
| Agree an early review date if you accept below target, and write it down | Rely on a verbal figure; get the offer letter in writing |
| Check the sector: banks, exporters, multinationals and gig platforms pay in structured ways | Assume the rest of the market follows the same rules |

## Hong Kong: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for a raise; of Hong Kong workers who ask, 77 percent receive one | Expect the job advertisement to state a salary range |
| Negotiate the package, not just the base: medical cover for a spouse, a housing allowance, flights, moving costs | Accept a family relocation offer on base salary alone |
| Price rent and schooling before you counter; commenters put a three-bedroom flat at HK$21,000 or more a month | Compare the offer with your home salary without converting the cost of living |
| Be ready for the "what are you earning now" question and have a calm answer | Raise pay casually with colleagues; few of them will join you |
| Budget for provisional tax in your first year | Treat the low headline tax rate as the whole story |

## Pakistan: The Market Where Pay Is a Rumour

The most candid description of Pakistani pay comes from a Dawn analysis published in July 2025, which concluded that the compensation landscape is poorly understood because of limited data, few structured studies, employer reluctance and no regulatory push for transparency. Roughly 9 percent of the workforce is in the public sector and a similar share in top-tier private firms; the informal majority is paid whatever the market and the employer's mood allow. Majyd Aziz, a former president of the Employers Federation of Pakistan, told the paper that about 65 percent of formal-sector enterprises and fewer than 10 percent of informal ones pay the minimum wage. Employers, Dawn adds, are reluctant to share wage-bill data partly from fear of regulatory harassment, which is a reasonable thing to be afraid of in a country where labour policy is set province by province.

The official average is not much more illuminating. The Pakistan Bureau of Statistics Labour Force Survey for 2024-25, as reported by Profit, puts the average monthly wage at Rs39,042, up about 62 percent from Rs24,028 four years earlier. Formal-sector workers averaged Rs54,038 and informal workers Rs30,834. The bureau itself cites a margin of error of around 5 percent, larger for informal estimates, and inflation averaged 29.18 percent in 2021-22, so the nominal rise is less cheerful than it looks.

The public sector, meanwhile, offers its own version of transparency. Dawn notes that lower-grade government employees, who account for more than 75 percent of public staff, enjoy better pay, job security and post-retirement benefits than comparable private workers, with no clear link to performance, while promotions to the top grades are formally merit-based but, in the paper's account, undermined by nepotism and weak accountability. A salary scale that is published and a promotion system that is not is a useful thing to know before you accept a job that depends on either.

Into this fog walks the candidate. Pakistani recruiters, notably Taraki in a December 2025 guide, advise negotiating after a formal offer, declining to disclose current pay and proposing three numbers: a floor, a target and an opening ask five to fifteen percent above the target. The same guide recommends softer phrasing than "I deserve" and suggests non-salary levers such as family health insurance and provident fund matching. The advice is sound. It is also an admission that candidates are expected to bargain without a reference price.

## Hong Kong: The City of Quiet Numbers

Hong Kong has the opposite problem. The Census and Statistics Department's 2025 Annual Earnings and Hours Survey, released in March 2026, reports a median monthly wage of HK$21,200, up 3.5 percent, with the 10th percentile at HK$11,000 and the 90th at HK$51,300. The statistics are public, regular and precise. The conversation around them is not. A Robert Walters pay-transparency guide, issued as a press release in May 2026 and so to be read as employer-commissioned research, notes that salary disclosure in Hong Kong has traditionally been limited and treated as an individual matter, and that only 5.5 percent of professionals in its survey are comfortable discussing pay with colleagues. Hofstede Insights scores Hong Kong at 68 for power distance, which is consistent with a culture in which the boss's number is the boss's business.

Satisfaction is a separate matter from fairness. The same Jobsdb survey found that about 70 percent of respondents feel fairly or well paid, yet only 34 percent are satisfied with their salary, a gap that suggests plenty of people privately believe they are worth more. The C&SD figures bear out the modesty of the alternative: median wages rose in every age group, education level and industry section in 2025, but by only a few percent.

Asking is another matter. According to the Jobsdb Salary Pulse survey of 1,010 Hong Kong employees, reported in June 2026, 48 percent have asked for a raise and 77 percent of those received one. Yet Hong Kong workers were the least likely in Asia-Pacific to ask at all, and 40 percent of respondents described the employment outlook as pessimistic. The market rewards the question, then, and the workforce declines to put it. The same press release observes that asking candidates for their previous salary is drawing debate in the territory, which is a polite way of saying it still happens.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, the two markets suggest opposite strategies. In Pakistan, you are short of information, so the work is to build a reference price yourself: use recruiters, ask the employer for its budget, and refuse to anchor on your current pay. The reward is real, because employers there expect a counter and the structured sectors, such as banks, exporters and multinationals, tend to pay in clearer bands. In Hong Kong, information is plentiful and nerve is scarce, so the work is to ask. The statistics show that people who ask are usually paid more, and the foreign-package conventions, housing, medical cover and flights, are well established for expatriate hires.

There is also a difference in what each side seems prepared to concede. Pakistani guidance leans heavily on phrasing and tone, which suggests that how a request is made weighs as much as its size. In Hong Kong the evidence points to a readier acceptance of trade-offs. The Jobsdb data is instructive: 57 percent of respondents would accept some concessions in exchange for a 10 percent raise, so a candidate who arrives with a clear package request is negotiating with people who are already used to giving things up.

The shared hazard is the offer that looks adequate until you add the family. A Hong Kong bank offer of HK$52,000 that excites a Malaysian consultant becomes a different proposition once rent, tax and school fees arrive; a Karachi role billed as chief technology officer can pay less than a mid-level engineer expects. In both places the job title and the salary figure are the least reliable parts of the document.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (r/developersPak) — paraphrased experience: A developer offered a "CTO" post in Karachi, covering DevOps, backend work and a five-person team on a six-day onsite week, asked whether to negotiate or leave. Most replies said the money was far below the level of the title, and that the real job was team lead. The poster declined.*

> *Reddit (r/HongKong) — paraphrased experience: A Malaysian management consultant with twelve years of experience, offered HK$52K by a bank, was told it was fine for a single person and tight for a family of four. Commenters suggested asking for HK$60K to HK$70K, with help on housing or school fees, and warned that provisional tax bites in the first year.*

> *Expat.com (Hong Kong forum) — paraphrased experience: Asked what to negotiate when a spouse gets the job offer, one reply ranked the items: medical cover for both partners first, then a housing allowance even at the cost of base pay, then flights and moving costs, with visa help last.*

> *Blind — paraphrased experience: Commenters in a thread on Hong Kong technology jobs described the city as a finance hub where average tech pay is a poor match for long hours and limited paid leave, with Singapore regarded as the stronger regional tech market.*

> *Expat.com (Pakistan forum) — paraphrased experience: An older reply advised that Pakistani employers often open low expecting a counter, so a candidate who accepts the first figure leaves money on the table.*

## Conclusion

If you are choosing between these two, decide which problem you would rather solve. Pakistan asks you to negotiate in a market without public reference prices, where the structured private sector and the informal majority are close to different economies. Hong Kong asks you to negotiate in a market with excellent statistics and an etiquette against using them. In both, get the currency, the total package and the working week in writing before you resign from anything.

The practical rule is the same in each city: research privately, ask politely, and trade on benefits when the base will not move. It is not complicated, though it does require talking about money, a skill both countries have so far kept admirably under control.
