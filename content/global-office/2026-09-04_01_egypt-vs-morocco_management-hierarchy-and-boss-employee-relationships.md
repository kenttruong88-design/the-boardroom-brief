# The Boss Is Family in Cairo. In Casablanca, He's French Management With a Moroccan Accent.

**🇪🇬 Egypt · 🇲🇦 Morocco**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries, one Hofstede power-distance score of 70, and two entirely different ways of experiencing what that number actually feels like from a desk. In Egypt, a good boss is expected to ask about your mother's surgery, your cousin's wedding, and possibly your rent. In Morocco, a good boss is expected to be impeccably dressed, formally correct, and quietly, permanently in charge. Neither will ever admit they were wrong in a meeting. Neither expects you to say so either.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇪🇬 Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet your manager with real warmth — ask after their family before agenda items | Walk in and open with the numbers; it reads as cold, even hostile |
| Accept that decisions flow upward and wait for the final word from the top | Assume a manager's openness to discussion means the decision is now negotiable |
| Build a personal relationship with your boss — it is the actual operating system of the office | Treat the relationship as transactional or purely professional |
| Bring disagreements privately, after the meeting, not during it | Publicly contradict a superior in front of colleagues, even gently |
| Expect your manager to intervene in personal matters — a loan, a family crisis, a wedding leave | Refuse that involvement as "unprofessional" — it signals distance, not boundaries |
| Budget extra time for meetings to start late or be rescheduled last-minute | Read a delayed meeting as disrespect — it rarely is |

#### 🇲🇦 Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress formally and consistently — appearance signals seriousness before you've said a word | Show up underdressed, even for internal meetings; first impressions calcify fast |
| Address seniority and titles correctly, especially in front of others | Use first names too early with someone clearly senior in rank or age |
| Let relationship-building precede business — accept the slower runway | Push for a fast decision in the first meeting; it can read as presumptuous |
| Take written procedures seriously even when personal trust is high | Assume a warm rapport means formal process no longer applies |
| Save disagreement for a private, indirect conversation | Confront a manager's decision in a group setting — it costs both of you face |
| Recognize the French administrative influence in structure and paperwork | Expect Anglo-style flat hierarchy or open-door management by default |

### Egypt: The Office as Extended Family

Egyptian workplaces run on a hierarchy that is unmistakably steep but rarely cold. According to [World Business Culture](https://www.worldbusinessculture.com/country-profiles/egypt/business-management-style/), Egyptian managers consult widely before deciding, but the final call is theirs alone, and once made, it is not up for revision by a subordinate. What makes this bearable — even, for many expats, genuinely pleasant — is the paternalism built into the role. [Rivermate's cultural guide](https://rivermate.com/guides/egypt/cultural-considerations) notes that Egyptians often treat the office as a second family, with managers expected to take on responsibilities that look almost parental: checking in on a sick relative, helping navigate a personal crisis, showing up to a wedding.

That warmth coexists with real bureaucratic friction. Foreign hires need a work permit, employers must prove no qualified Egyptian could fill the role, and foreign staff generally can't exceed 10 percent of a company's workforce — a labor-market protection that shapes who gets hired and how. Meetings, meanwhile, operate on what [expat guides](https://www.jarniascyril.com/expatriation/install-egypt-expat-complete-guide/cultural-differences-expatriation-egypt/) describe as elastic time: start times shift, agendas move, and personal trust does more work than the calendar. New arrivals who read this as disorganization tend to struggle; those who read it as relational patience tend to thrive.

### Morocco: Hierarchy With a French Accent

Morocco shares Egypt's steep power distance — Hofstede scores both at 70 — but the texture is different. Decades of French administrative influence show up everywhere: formal titles, precise dress codes, and a management style [Commisceo Global](https://commisceo-global.com/management-guides/morocco-management-guide/) describes as "the benevolent autocrat" — a boss who gathers input but centralizes the decision, and who is expected to look the part while doing it. Appearance carries real professional weight here; being underdressed is not a minor faux pas, it's a first impression that's hard to walk back.

Face-saving governs almost every interaction. Direct confrontation, public correction, and blunt negative feedback are avoided by both sides, and disagreement with a manager's decision is something handled quietly, later, one-on-one — never in the room where it happened. [Expat.com's guide to Casablanca's corporate culture](https://www.expat.com/en/guide/africa/morocco/casablanca/20605-the-work-culture-in-casablanca.html) also flags a pace mismatch that catches Western hires off guard: results-pressure is lower than in Europe, and for some employees the job is simply a way to earn a living rather than a site of personal ambition — a very different relationship to work than the "second family" model next door.

### The Reckoning

Here is the trap: identical Hofstede scores suggest identical hierarchies, and they are not. Egypt's hierarchy is personal — you earn your boss's protection and give loyalty in return, and the relationship absorbs a lot of ambiguity about process. Morocco's hierarchy is formal — you earn your boss's respect by getting the performance of rank right, and the relationship absorbs a lot of ambiguity about timeline, not really about role.

The irony is that Morocco, despite its French bureaucratic scaffolding, can feel less predictable to a newcomer than Egypt's looser, warmer chaos — because Moroccan formality signals competence is expected, while Egyptian warmth signals forgiveness is available. An expat who nails the dress code and titles in Rabat but skips the relationship-building will plateau quietly. An expat who skips the paperwork in Cairo but invests in the friendship will often get more slack than the org chart implies.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com, Egypt forum — A European transplant described the real shock as not the hierarchy itself but how personal it got: their manager insisted on meeting their spouse within the first month, treating it as a basic condition of trust, not an intrusion.*

> *Expat.com, Casablanca guide — One contributor noted that a colleague who showed up to a client meeting without a jacket was quietly reassigned off the account within the week — no one explained why directly, and it took months to piece together that it was about the missing jacket, not the work.*

> *Quora — Someone who relocated to Cairo for a customer-service role wrote that their Austrian boss adapted fast to the late starts and rescheduled meetings, eventually admitting he'd never worked anywhere where being twenty minutes late to your own meeting was structurally normal, not a personal failing.*

> *Expat Focus — A returning contributor observed that Moroccan colleagues would never contradict a manager's plan out loud in a group setting, even when everyone privately knew it wouldn't work, and that the real conversation always happened afterward, quietly, in the corridor.*

> *Expat Arrivals — A long-term foreign hire in Cairo advised newcomers to accept the "second home" framing early rather than resist it, arguing that treating the office as strictly transactional was the single fastest way to lose an Egyptian team's trust.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two on management style alone: Egypt rewards emotional investment, Morocco rewards formal precision, and both punish the assumption that a high power-distance score means the same thing everywhere. According to the [World Bank](https://data.worldbank.org) and regional labor guides, both economies still run on relationship capital more than process capital — the difference is just which relationship, and which performance of respect, gets you there faster.

Priya's honest take, over a drink: if you like being taken care of and can tolerate ambiguity, go to Cairo. If you like knowing exactly which fork to use and can tolerate a slower yes, go to Casablanca. Either way, buy a good jacket.
