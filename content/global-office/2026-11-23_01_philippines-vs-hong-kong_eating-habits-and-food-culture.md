# Sharing the Rice in the Philippines, Guarding the Pantry in Hong Kong

🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong
*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Both cities agree that lunch should involve rice. They disagree on almost everything else. According to MyLifeElsewhere's cost comparison, a basic restaurant meal with a drink in Hong Kong averages US$8.28, against US$3.66 in the Philippines, and a fast food combo costs US$6.37 against US$3.23. A worker relocating from Manila to Central will therefore discover, within the first fortnight, that the salary uplift they negotiated so carefully has been quietly collected at noon, one set meal at a time. The food is recognisable. The arithmetic is not.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring baon, usually rice with one main dish, and expect to share some of it | Eat alone at your desk every day and wonder why nobody knows your name |
| Join the communal lunch table in the first week | Decline every offered spoonful, which reads as aloofness rather than dietary discipline |
| Ask whether your employer subsidises meals, since catered subsidy schemes are an ordinary budget line | Assume imported groceries cost what they cost at home |
| Treat the up-to-one-hour break as a social appointment | Turn the break into a working session without a very good reason |
| Eat where the office crowd eats and let someone else order | Order with the confidence of a tourist and discover the portion sizes later |

### Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the pantry etiquette before eating at your desk | Microwave steamed salted fish and walk away |
| Stock a desk drawer with snacks, because everyone else has | Arrive without a plan for the 30-minute lunch that sometimes happens |
| Budget roughly HK$50 to HK$100 for a casual local meal | Price your lunches in pesos and expect the habit to survive |
| Try cha chaan tengs, noodle shops and dai pai dongs before the imported options | Buy local cheese or milk as if it were a staple |
| Find the Filipino eateries and community groups early | Leave the pantry fridge full of weekend groceries |

## The Philippines: Packed Lunches and Shared Tables

According to SBS Filipino, Philippine office workers often bring baon, a home-cooked meal built around rice and a single main dish known as the viand. Breaks run up to an hour, and the occasion is communal. Colleagues eat together, share food, and in the process acquire the social bonds that other countries pay consultants to manufacture. Nobody has yet measured how many promotions were decided over a shared adobo, but the sample size is presumably large.

The economics reinforce the habit. Executive Gourmet, a Philippine corporate caterer, gives planning ranges of ₱90 to ₱105 for a practical employee meal and ₱110 to ₱130 for a standard one, with the usual caveat that these are general figures only. It also describes subsidy schemes in which an employer covers a fixed portion of the cost, for example ₱80 of a ₱120 meal, with the balance paid by the employee. A forum poster on Expat.com who lives in the Philippines reports that a large grilled chicken meal with rice and iced tea runs to about ₱250, and describes most local versions of imported food as disappointing, preferring the local dishes. The dietary norm, in short, is to eat the local thing and enjoy it, which is both cheaper and, by that poster's account, healthier.

There is also a quieter rule underneath the rice. Because the meal is shared, it doubles as a daily act of attendance: whoever brings food, whoever shows up, and whoever sits down is noted without anyone saying so. A newcomer who eats elsewhere is not breaking a regulation, but is skipping the one meeting in the day that nobody schedules. It is an efficient system, in the sense that it requires no software and cannot be rescheduled by a calendar invitation.

Hofstede Insights, for what a multi-dimension model is worth at lunchtime, scores both places as collectivist, at 32 for the Philippines and 25 for Hong Kong on individualism. The two diverge sharply on long-term orientation, at 19 and 96 respectively, which may explain why one office shares the rice and the other saves for the flat. This is an interpretation, not a finding, and the model has never been tested against a lunch queue.

## Hong Kong: The Desk Drawer and the Thirty-Minute Hour

Hong Kong offers a wider menu and a narrower wallet. The Filipino Hub lists street food, dim sum, dai pai dongs, noodle shops and cha chaan tengs, with a casual local meal costing about HK$50 to HK$100. MyLifeElsewhere's comparison puts Hong Kong groceries at roughly twice Philippine prices, with local cheese at US$12.52 for eight ounces against US$3.30, and milk at US$11.07 a gallon against US$6.50. Human Resources Online describes the one-hour lunch break as the norm, though a forum thread it reports shows the hour is not guaranteed, and the same article repeats a claim that Hong Kong has among the longest working hours anywhere, at 50 hours a week. That figure was cited from an earlier article and not independently verified here.

The result is a culture of eating where the work is. According to the South China Morning Post, the middle drawer of a typical Hong Kong office desk is stocked with snacks: matcha Kit Kats, dried squid jerky, potato chips and shelf-stable cakes, plus the inevitable souvenir pineapple cake from a colleague's holiday. Some larger employers stock healthier pantries, depending on how the year went. Human Resources Online adds that bringing a home-made lunch is rising, which it links, on the strength of forum anecdotes rather than statistics, to property prices and workers saving for deposits. The accompanying etiquette is exacting: no pungent food, no sauce on the microwave, and no raiding the shared fridge with a week of groceries.

A relocating worker should also expect the day to be organised around the clock rather than the table. The Hong Kong lunch is a window, not a gathering, and the window has a habit of closing early. Colleagues presumably still eat together on occasion, but the evidence assembled here suggests the default is efficient, quiet and brief. This suits people who prefer their meals without commentary. It is harder on those who arrive expecting a table to be waiting for them.

For the newcomer, the practical point is that kitchens are scarce. A commenter on an Expat.com thread, now about five years old, noted that many locals rarely cook because small apartments lack a Western-style kitchen. The Filipino Hub, for its part, notes that some neighbourhoods have Filipino eateries that ease homesickness, and recommends cooking at home where possible. The prudent worker will do both, and quietly resent the rent that explains why one of them is difficult.

## The Reckoning

On cost, there is no contest. Hong Kong's restaurant prices run to roughly double the Philippines' on MyLifeElsewhere's figures, its groceries to roughly double as well, and its cheese to something closer to a luxury good. A Philippine worker accustomed to an employer-subsidised meal in the ₱100 range will find that no one in a Hong Kong office is offering to cover any share of the bill. These are crowd-sourced estimates, but the direction is not in dispute.

On ritual, the divide is subtler and arguably costlier. The Philippine office lunch is a shared event with food passed around, whereas the Hong Kong equivalent is often a transaction conducted at speed, in a pantry or at a desk, with the snack drawer as a safety net. Neither is wrong. One simply requires you to be sociable by default, and the other requires you to be considerate by default, which is a quieter virtue and harder to put on a baon.

There is a practical corollary for anyone planning household finances. A Philippine salary stretches over lunch because lunch is cheap, shared and often subsidised, whereas a Hong Kong salary must stretch over lunch because lunch is none of those things. The relocating worker should therefore treat the daily meal as a fixed cost, as one would rent, rather than as a discretionary item to be trimmed in a lean month. The sums are modest in isolation and considerable over a year, which is how most budgeting errors are made.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/HongKong — A newcomer asked whether eating lunch at the desk is normal after a colleague's strongly scented meal filled a poorly ventilated office. Commenters said it varies by firm and district, that pantry eating is more usual in pricier areas such as Central, and that desk eating has grown since COVID and rising lunch prices.*

> *Expat.com Philippines forum — A long-term foreign resident says a large grilled chicken meal with rice and iced tea costs about 250 pesos, that he has never eaten healthier, and that local takes on international food are mostly disappointing, with Filipino dishes the safer order.*

> *Hong Kong Discussion Group, via Human Resources Online — A worker facing a 30-minute lunch break asked how to survive it. Replies suggested takeaway, cold food at the desk, eating very quickly, or quitting.*

> *Expat Arrivals interview — A Filipino living in Hong Kong ranks the restaurants just behind the transport system among the city's best features and advises newcomers to eat where residents eat, while describing the work culture as stressful, with a night that once ran to 2 a.m.*

## Conclusion

The practical advice is unglamorous. Budget for lunch at roughly double what you paid at home, and treat HK$50 to HK$100 as a sensible casual meal rather than a failure of discipline. Learn the pantry rules before the first reheated fish, stock the desk drawer, and do not expect a communal table unless you build one. Seek out Filipino food and groups early, because they are cheaper than homesickness, and keep the occasional home-cooked meal for the days when the arithmetic wins.

None of this should stop anyone from going, since Hong Kong's food is excellent and its restaurants are among the reasons people stay. It simply suggests that a relocating worker should read the lunch menu before signing the contract, as it tends to be the most honest document in the process.
