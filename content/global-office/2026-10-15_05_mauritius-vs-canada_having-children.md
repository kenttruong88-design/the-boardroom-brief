**Who's Watching the Baby: Mauritius's Grandparents vs. Canada's $10-a-Day Promise**

**🇲🇺 Mauritius · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Mauritius, the honest answer to "who's watching the baby" is usually a grandmother who lives twenty minutes away and considers it her job, not a favour. In Canada, the honest answer is a federal program that promised $10-a-day childcare nationwide by 2026 and is, as of this year, delivering that promise to roughly half the country. Both systems work. Neither works the way the brochure says.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇺 Mauritius

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect maternity leave of 16 employer-paid weeks (at least 8 after birth) and paternity leave of 4 consecutive weeks under the 2024 Work-Life Balance reforms | Assume those numbers describe pay rate as well as duration — the law specifies weeks, not necessarily full salary replacement |
| Lean into extended-family networks for childcare if you have them — it's the normal, expected model, not a sign you can't manage alone | Expect formal daycare infrastructure at Canadian or European density; quality options exist but informal family care is still the default for many |
| Expect real in-laws' influence on parenting decisions, especially education choices — consultation is cultural, not optional | Be surprised if moving without nearby family becomes the single hardest adjustment; multiple expat mothers describe exactly this as their biggest struggle |
| Ask other expat or local parents directly about international school options if raising children bilingually or biculturally | Assume "rat race" pressure on children is the norm; several expat parents specifically cite its relative absence as a reason they stayed |
| Budget for domestic help (nanny, housekeeper) as a real, common option if extended family isn't nearby | Assume a lack of local family support is unusual — it's the single most repeated complaint among expat parents specifically |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Choose between standard (up to 40 weeks total, 55% of insurable earnings) and extended (up to 69 weeks, 33% of earnings) parental leave carefully — you can't switch after applying | Assume $10-a-day childcare applies everywhere; as of 2026 only six provinces and territories have hit or are near that target |
| Check your specific province's childcare fee reality before budgeting — Toronto-area families save roughly $1,000-$1,300 a month versus pre-reform fees | Assume lower fees mean available spots; reduced cost has increased demand faster than new spaces have appeared in many regions |
| Apply for EI parental benefits early and understand your province's top-up rules, if your employer offers any | Expect your employer to top up EI's 55%/33% replacement rate automatically; it's common in some sectors but not legally required |
| Confirm with your partner, in writing if needed, exactly how many weeks each of you is taking before applying — the split is binding once chosen | Assume "35 weeks for one parent" is a fixed national rule; several real accounts show provincial and program-type variation that confuses even longtime residents |
| Get on daycare waitlists as early as possible, ideally in pregnancy | Wait until the fee drops to start looking; "child care deserts" are a documented, real risk in high-demand areas |

**Mauritius: The Childcare Plan Is Your Family**

Mauritius significantly strengthened its formal leave entitlements in June 2024: maternity leave rose from 14 to 16 employer-paid weeks (with at least 8 required after birth, and 2 extra weeks for multiples or premature births), while paternity leave jumped from a token 5 working days to a full 4 consecutive calendar weeks, according to Lockton's coverage of the reform. Those are real, meaningful gains on paper.

What the law doesn't provide is the thing that actually determines most families' day-to-day experience: nearby relatives. The Cultural Atlas's account of Mauritian family life describes young couples often living in an extension of the husband's parents' home until financially independent, with parents retaining real influence over major decisions, and some households increasingly hiring domestic help for childcare specifically because that extended-family model is thinning as more women work outside the home. For local Mauritian families, that network is usually already in place. For expats, its absence is the single most repeated complaint on record: multiple mothers interviewed by defimedia.info — spanning Singaporean, Indonesian, and French backgrounds — independently named missing nearby family as the hardest part of raising children there, even while praising the island's slower pace and strong sense of community as genuine compensations.

**Canada: The Policy Is National, the Reality Is Provincial**

Canada's federal child care plan promised an average of $10-a-day care nationwide by March 2026. A Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives report covered by Global News found the country will miss that deadline in a meaningful way: six provinces and territories (Nunavut, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, Quebec, P.E.I., and Newfoundland and Labrador) have hit or are close to the target, while Ontario, Nova Scotia, Alberta, B.C., and New Brunswick have no concrete plan to get there. Where it has worked, the savings are real and large — Toronto-area families are saving roughly $1,000-$1,300 a month — but economist David Macdonald called the federal goal of an "average" $10 a day a built-in "get out of jail free card" that lets provinces undershoot in specific cities while claiming success overall. Richmond, B.C.'s median infant daycare fee, at roughly $39 a day, illustrates the gap starkly.

Parental leave itself is generous on paper and confusing in practice. Parents choose between standard leave (up to 40 weeks total, 55% wage replacement, capped at 35 weeks for one parent) and extended leave (up to 69 weeks, 33% replacement) — a binding choice made before the baby fully exists as a person who sleeps. A Reddit r/daddit thread captured the resulting confusion well: commenters from different provinces described meaningfully different real-world experiences of the "35-week cap," genuinely disagreeing with each other about how the rule actually works, which says something honest about how unevenly understood the system still is even among people living inside it.

There's a cultural-values angle worth naming directly. Hofstede Insights scores Canada as highly individualist (80), a number that helps explain why the policy conversation there centres on a government program rather than a family network — the state is expected to fill a role that, in a more collectivist setting, relatives would simply occupy by default. Mauritius has no comparable, widely published individual Hofstede country profile, which is a real research gap rather than a cultural judgment, but everything in the Cultural Atlas's account of extended-family living arrangements and parental influence over major decisions points toward the collectivist end of that same scale.

**The Reckoning**

The deep irony is that both countries have built real infrastructure around the same problem and ended up equally dependent on something outside government control. Mauritius's generous new statutory leave means little if the informal family-care network it was built on top of keeps thinning as more women work outside the home — the policy modernised faster than the social structure under it. Canada's national childcare promise means little in a province that hasn't built the spaces to meet the demand lower fees created — the price dropped faster than the supply could follow.

Neither failure is really about parental leave law, which in both countries is now genuinely decent. It's about the thing sitting underneath the law: in Mauritius, a family network under quiet strain; in Canada, a physical-space shortage that cheaper prices made worse before they made it better.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *defimedia.info — Reneeta Sandhu, originally from Singapore and raising two daughters in Mauritius while working at an international school, said she finds herself "missing my family every day" and that "managing everything on my own can feel overwhelming without the support of extended family" — specifically around childcare, since both she and her husband work.*

> *defimedia.info — Marie-Laure Blaise, a French marketing consultant who moved to Mauritius eight years ago, called the lack of nearby family "one of the hardest parts of being an expat mother," while also describing a strong, specific sense of community that made up for some of what distance took away.*

> *Expat.com (Mauritius forum) — A family that relocated from Switzerland, posting twenty months after their move, said they had "no regrets whatsoever," praised their teenage son's international school for balancing local and expat students well, and noted feeling safer letting their kids out locally than they had in either Geneva or London.*

> *Reddit r/daddit — Responding to a thread about Canadian parental leave limits, one Ontario-based commenter described their own family split (52 weeks for the mother, 5 for the father, with funds swappable between options) in terms that directly contradicted another commenter's understanding of the "35-week single-parent cap" — neither side fully resolved the disagreement, a small but telling sign of how unevenly understood Canada's own leave system is even among people currently using it.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two for starting or raising a family, the real question isn't which country's parental policy reads better on paper — both now do, genuinely. It's which gap you'd rather manage: Mauritius's thinning but still-present extended-family safety net, or Canada's generous but geographically uneven childcare rollout. One asks you to rebuild a support system from scratch as an outsider; the other asks you to gamble on which province's math actually works out to $10 a day.

My honest line for a friend: if you have — or can build fast — a real local support network, Mauritius's pace of life and family-first culture are hard to beat. If you don't, and you're landing in Ontario, Alberta, or B.C. rather than Quebec or the Maritimes, budget like the $10-a-day promise doesn't apply to you yet, because for a lot of actual Canadian parents right now, it still doesn't.
