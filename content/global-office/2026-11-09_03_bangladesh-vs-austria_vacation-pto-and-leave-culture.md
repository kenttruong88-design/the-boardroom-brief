# Bangladesh Has 26 Public Holidays and No Time to Enjoy Them. Austria Has Five Weeks Off and a Panic Attack If You Haven't Booked Them by March

🇧🇩 Bangladesh · 🇦🇹 Austria

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

On paper, Bangladesh looks like a workers' paradise: the government approved 26 public holidays for 2025, more than almost any country on earth bothers to schedule. In practice, a large share of those holidays belong to people who are too busy meeting a shipping deadline to notice them. Austria, meanwhile, legally guarantees five full weeks of paid leave to everyone, then spends the back half of July privately calculating how many of its colleagues have already used theirs. Both countries, in their own way, have turned time off into a national anxiety.

## 🇧🇩 Bangladesh — Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Submit leave applications in writing, with a clear reason, well in advance | Assume a verbal request to your line manager counts as approved leave |
| Expect factory and garment-sector leave to be governed by the Labour Act's earned-leave formula, not a flat annual number | Expect every employer to interpret "earned leave" the same way — ask HR for the actual accrual rate |
| Plan around Eid-ul-Fitr and Eid-ul-Adha, when the whole country effectively shuts down at once | Book a holiday during Eid season assuming trains, buses, or flights will have room |
| Keep a paper or digital record of your casual and sick leave balance yourself | Trust that an informal workplace will track your leave balance accurately for you |
| Understand that public holidays and earned annual leave are counted separately | Confuse the 26 gazetted holidays with your personal vacation allowance — they are not the same thing |

## 🇦🇹 Austria — Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Submit your *Urlaubsantrag* for the August shutdown period by early spring | Wait until June and expect your preferred weeks to still be available |
| Treat your five (or six, after 25 years of service) weeks as fully used by year-end | Let unused leave quietly expire — some of it does not legally carry over |
| Expect entire offices, and sometimes entire towns, to close for the same two weeks | Schedule an urgent client meeting in the first half of August |
| Use sick leave for sickness; Austria's *Krankenstand* system is generous but monitored | Treat sick days as a flexible top-up to your vacation allowance |
| Respect that 13 public holidays are layered on top of statutory leave, not instead of it | Assume Austria's overall time-off generosity means spontaneous long weekends are culturally normal |

## Bangladesh: Generous on Paper, Negotiable in Practice

Bangladesh's Labour Act of 2006 sets out one of the more intricate leave architectures in South Asia: workers in factories and shops typically earn one day of annual leave for every eighteen days actually worked, on top of ten days of casual leave and fourteen days of sick leave a year, according to Rivermate's summary of the statute. Add the 26 public holidays the government gazetted for 2025, reported by Dhaka Tribune, and the headline number looks almost absurdly generous for a lower-middle-income economy.

The catch is enforcement, and it is not a small catch. Survey reporting from The Financial Express and Apparel Resources on the country's ready-made garment sector found that roughly one in five workers said they could not actually take sick leave when they needed it, regardless of what the Labour Act promised on paper. Earned leave is calculated against days actually worked, which means a slow season, an unpaid suspension, or a missed shift can quietly shrink an entitlement that looked fixed in the handbook. The Daily Star's commentary on what it called "the myth of the eight-hour work day" captured the broader mood: statutory rights exist, loudly, in the text of the law, and considerably more quietly in the lived experience of the people they are meant to protect.

For an expat professional arriving in Dhaka, the practical lesson is to separate three things that get casually conflated: the public holiday calendar, which is real and generous and genuinely observed at the national level; the earned-leave formula, which is also real but employer-dependent in its application; and informal workplace norms, which in many offices mean that senior staff set the tone on how much time off is actually acceptable to take, law notwithstanding. The ten days of casual leave and fourteen days of sick leave are legally separate pools, which sounds like flexibility until you notice how often employees quietly merge them in practice, treating the whole bundle as one modest allowance to be rationed across an entire year of family emergencies, illness, and the occasional actual holiday.

## Austria: A Legal Right That Everyone Actually Uses

Austria's leave system is less colorful but considerably more reliable. The Urlaubsgesetz guarantees every employee a statutory minimum of five weeks — 25 working days on a standard five-day week — rising to six weeks after 25 years with the same employer, according to multiple employer-compliance guides including Gloroots and Leavebalance. Layer on 13 public holidays, and the Austrian employee's time-off floor is one of the more comfortable in the OECD, a contrast Hofstede Insights' cultural-dimension data helps explain structurally: Austria scores among the lowest in the world on power distance, meaning leave is treated as an entitlement employees assert rather than a favor managers dispense, the inverse of Bangladesh's considerably higher score on the same measure.

What makes Austria distinctive is not the generosity but the predictability, and the mild cultural pressure that comes with it. The Local's reporting on the country's seasonal rhythms describes a widely observed pattern in which large stretches of the economy — manufacturing plants, government offices, even some restaurants — simply close for two or three weeks in August, by informal but near-universal agreement. Expat Exchange's community guide to moving to Austria flags the same thing from the newcomer's side: if you have not submitted your vacation request by spring, the good weeks are gone, not because anyone is hoarding them maliciously, but because everyone else filed theirs months ago.

Expat.com's feature on why time off abroad feels harder than expected makes the less obvious point: Austrians are comfortable using every day they are owed, and mildly puzzled by colleagues who are not. A foreign hire who tries to appear dedicated by skipping leave is not admired for it; they are quietly regarded as somewhat disorganized.

Austria also layers on a separate entitlement, *Pflegefreistellung*, for caring for a sick family member, kept distinct from both annual leave and personal sick leave — a level of bureaucratic precision that can feel excessive to a newcomer right up until the week they actually need it, at which point most expats report finding it one of the more reassuring features of Austrian employment law.

## The Reckoning

Put the two systems side by side and the real difference is not the number of days — Bangladesh's combined public-holiday-plus-earned-leave total can, on paper, rival Austria's — but who is expected to actually use them and who is expected to merely appreciate that they exist. Austria has built leave into the architecture of ordinary life, to the point that not taking your five weeks reads as mildly deviant. Bangladesh has built an admirably detailed leave code into the architecture of its labor law, and then left its application to the discretion of thousands of individual employers, with predictably uneven results.

For a professional comparing the two, the honest framing is this: Austria will tell you, credibly, exactly how much time off you are getting before you accept the job, and you can plan your year around it in March. Bangladesh will also tell you, but you should ask a second time, informally, how that number tends to work out in your specific office — because the gap between the Labour Act and the actual roster can be the difference between a real holiday and a very long shift that happens to fall during Eid.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — paraphrased experience: a longtime expat contributor describes feeling genuinely disoriented the first time a European colleague treated using every single vacation day as the obviously correct, unremarkable default — a stark contrast to years spent in workplaces where unused leave was quietly worn as a badge of commitment.*

> *Expat Exchange — paraphrased experience: a newcomer to Vienna recounts learning the hard way that Austria's famous "everyone's off in August" period isn't a suggestion; arriving in June with vague plans to "figure out vacation later" meant watching every good week get claimed by colleagues who had filed paperwork back in February.*

> *The Local Austria — paraphrased experience: a foreign professional working in Austrian manufacturing describes the mild culture shock of being handed a company-wide shutdown calendar on their first week — not a request, a notice — and realizing that "flexible scheduling" in Austria mostly means flexibility about which of the two mandatory closure weeks you'd prefer.*

> *The Daily Star (Bangladesh) — paraphrased experience: a commentator writing on the realities of Bangladesh's labor code describes employees who technically qualify for sick leave under the Labour Act quietly choosing not to use it, out of a shared understanding that doing so marks you, informally, as less reliable than a colleague who shows up ill.*

> *The Financial Express (Bangladesh) — paraphrased experience: a garment-sector worker interviewed for a workplace survey describes requesting sick leave during a production deadline and being told, informally, that leave requests during peak season were "not a good time" — despite having the legal entitlement on paper.*

## Conclusion

If you are weighing a move to either country, the leave calendar alone will not tell you much; the enforcement culture around it will tell you everything. Austria's promise is boring, bureaucratic, and almost universally honored, which is precisely its appeal: you can build a life, and a travel budget, around five dependable weeks. Bangladesh's promise is more generous on its face and considerably more contingent in practice, shaped by sector, employer, and how senior you are willing to sound when you ask for what the law already gives you.

The wry truth sitting underneath both systems is that a country's real vacation policy is never the one printed in the labor code — it is the one nobody has to explain to you after your first missed Friday.
