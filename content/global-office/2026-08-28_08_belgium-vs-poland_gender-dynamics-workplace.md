# Belgium Will Quota You Into the Boardroom. Poland Will Just Let You Run It.

🇧🇪 Belgium · 🇵🇱 Poland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Belgium wrote gender balance into corporate law in 2011 and has been legislating its way toward parity ever since, one committee at a time. Poland skipped the legislation and simply put women in charge of things — 43 percent of its managers are female, one of the more even splits in Europe — while quietly keeping their paychecks smaller once they get there. Neither country has solved the problem. They have simply chosen which half to leave for the coffee break.

#### 🇧🇪 Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect your employer to publish pay-gap data if it has 50+ staff | Assume a female colleague on the leadership team got there without doing twice the paperwork |
| Take paternity/co-parent leave seriously — it's a real entitlement, not a courtesy | Mistake the 33% board quota for a solved problem — executive committees are a different story |
| Read the quiet, indirect feedback style as substance, not indifference | Ask a Belgian woman why she's "still" not a partner — the math is not in her favour |
| Notice that part-time work is common and normalized for parents of both genders | Assume part-time automatically means "on the mommy track" — plenty of men use it too |
| Bring data if you want to argue about equity — Belgians love a spreadsheet | Confuse formal politeness with equal treatment; both can coexist |

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a woman to be your manager — it's statistically likely | Assume equal representation at the top means equal pay for the same role |
| Recognize that many women in STEM chose it deliberately, often young | Treat "so many women engineers" as evidence the gender problem is solved |
| Respect the directness — competitive, results-first communication applies to everyone | Read directness from a female manager as unusually harsh; it's the house style |
| Expect long, employer-protected maternity leave to be the norm | Assume long leave has no informal career cost — it often does |
| Ask about promotion criteria explicitly | Assume family-related career breaks go unnoticed at review time |

## Belgium: The Quota Worked. The Committee Didn't Get the Memo.

Belgium's 2011 board-quota law did what it said on the tin: female representation on the boards of Belgium's largest listed companies rose from roughly 8 percent in 2008 to over 33 percent within a decade, with the BEL 20 companies now sitting around 40 percent (Loyens & Loeff; Eurofound). It's one of the cleaner natural experiments in corporate gender policy anywhere in Europe, and Belgium likes to mention it.

What the quota didn't touch is the layer just below the boardroom door. Fewer than one in five Belgian firms has a female top manager, and roughly nine in ten CEOs are still men (Eurofound). The country's raw gender pay gap is genuinely small — Eurostat puts it around 0.7 to 4.5 percent depending on the year and method, among the lowest in the EU (UNRIC; Eurostat) — but that flatters the country, since it measures people already in comparable jobs, not the women who never made it onto the executive committee to begin with. Eurofound's analysis is blunter: organizations are still built around an uninterrupted, mobile, male-coded career pattern, and women who take breaks for caregiving pay for it in promotion odds, quota or no quota.

Hofstede Insights scores Belgium 54 on the Masculinity dimension — near the global midpoint, tilted mildly toward "quality of life over competition." That shows up as a workplace fluent in work-life balance, at least for the rank and file. The people negotiating that balance from the executive floor are still, disproportionately, men.

## Poland: Parity in the Middle, a Gap at the Top

Poland's numbers invert the pattern. Women hold about 43 percent of managerial roles, a genuinely strong showing by European standards, alongside an unusually high share of women in engineering and IT relative to regional peers (Careers in Poland). This isn't accidental — Poland's post-war education system pushed STEM enrollment for women decades before it was fashionable elsewhere, and the habit stuck.

The catch is what happens to that 43 percent once they're in the chair. The overall Polish gender pay gap sits around 8 percent by Eurostat's unadjusted measure (IBS) — respectable next to the EU average — until you isolate managerial roles, where the gap widens to a startling 27.7 percent (IBS). Poland handed women the title in unusually high numbers, then paid them less for holding it: a more efficient inequality than simply keeping them out of the room. Hofstede's Masculinity score for Poland is 64, a genuinely competitive, performance-first culture (Hofstede Insights) — and that ethos applies regardless of what's on the business card, which is either progress or just evenly distributed pressure.

## The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and you get a tidy division of dysfunction: Belgium has fixed representation at the summit and left the pipeline below it largely alone; Poland has built a wide, competitive middle-management layer stacked with women and quietly underpaid them once they got there. A Belgian woman is statistically more likely to see herself reflected on a board slide. A Polish woman is statistically more likely to already be somebody's boss — and statistically more likely to be paid less than the man in the identical role next door.

Neither arrangement touches the pattern that shows up in country after country: women in Belgium and Poland alike do roughly twice the unpaid household and caregiving work that men do, a burden McKinsey has flagged repeatedly as the actual bottleneck no boardroom quota fixes (McKinsey). Legislating the top of the pyramid and building a wide managerial base are both real achievements. Neither is the same thing as closing the gap — they're just different rooms in the same unfinished house.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone answering a question about whether Polish culture is "masculine" in the Hofstede sense wrote that competitiveness and results-orientation are real, but framed it as equal-opportunity intensity: women are expected to argue their case as bluntly as anyone, which newcomers sometimes mistake for hostility when it's just the house style.*

> *Quora — In a thread asking why Poland produces so many women IT engineers, a respondent explained it as a legacy of education policy that never carried the "math is for boys" stigma common elsewhere, so the pipeline was never as leaky to begin with.*

> *LinkedIn (standing in for the inaccessible InterNations/forum threads) — A professional described starting a new job in Poland and being pleasantly surprised at how normal it felt to report to a woman and see women across the leadership table, though she noted the pay conversation was conspicuously absent from onboarding.*

> *Independent culture blog (Substack, standing in for InterNations) — An expat in Brussels described initially reading a Belgian manager's blunt, task-only feedback as dismissive, only to realize months later it was simply the local register — the same feedback a male colleague received, word for word.*

> *TeamBlind — A thread about a European tech executive's dismissive comments on women engineers drew pushback from other women in the industry, several of whom said the real issue wasn't hiring bias itself but how casually it still gets said aloud in rooms with no consequences.*

## Conclusion

If you're weighing Belgium against Poland on this axis specifically, you're choosing between two unfinished renovations. Belgium has legislated the penthouse and left the stairwell as-is; Poland built a crowded, competent middle floor and priced the view differently depending on who's standing in it. Bring a calculator either way, read the fine print on "leadership representation" statistics, and remember that a country putting a number on its gender gap is, at minimum, more honest than one that isn't measuring at all.
