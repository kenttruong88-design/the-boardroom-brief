# Luxembourg Pays Everyone Well and Charges Them for It, While Portugal Keeps the Prices Low and the Pay Lower

🇵🇹 Portugal · 🇱🇺 Luxembourg

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

According to the OECD's Taxing Wages 2026 report, as relayed by the Brussels Times, the average gross wage in Luxembourg is 77,840 euros, the highest in the European Union. In Portugal, national statistics reported by Idealista put average total gross monthly earnings at 1,741 euros in 2025. The gap is large enough to suggest that the two countries are conducting separate experiments, one on how much a salary can buy and the other on how little it can be asked to. The more interesting question for the prospective migrant is what happens after payday, and on that subject the two systems have strong and rather different opinions.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Work out rent before accepting an offer. The national minimum wage was 870 euros a month in 2025, and Lisbon landlords are not obliged to consult it. | Don't trust generic cost-of-living guides. One expat reports that renting without a local guarantor meant several months of rent up front plus a deposit. |
| Register as a legal resident to use the national health service, which is free to residents, with co-payments of 2.50 to 40 euros. | Don't assume public care is quick. Expat.com notes waits of months, and in some cases years, for appointments, screenings and surgery. |
| Apply early for a public or social-sector crèche. Reference fees are estimated at roughly 100 to 250 euros a month. | Don't count on the free crèche for an older child. It applies primarily to children born from late 2021 onward, and places are limited. |
| Price a private health plan. Annual premiums run from a few hundred to a few thousand euros, and over four million Portuguese have some private cover. | Don't plan around a subsidised place in a central city. Demand exceeds supply, especially for children under one, so budget at private rates until confirmed. |
| Check each crèche's tariff locally. Fees are set locally and change every year. | Don't expect income to travel with you. Luxembourg's average gross wage is 77,840 euros, so a Luxembourg salary does not convert neatly at a Portuguese rate. |

### Luxembourg

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for the childcare service voucher. It covers ages 0 to 12 and applies to non-resident employees too. | Don't assume the voucher makes care free. Beyond the free hours, parents pay on a sliding scale by income and hours. |
| Use the 20 free hours of childcare for ages 1 to 4, available 46 weeks a year at approved facilities. | Don't price the unsubsidised alternative lightly. Expatica puts an average crèche at about 1,141 euros a month. |

## Portugal: The Land of Bargains for Those Paid Elsewhere

Portugal's wage numbers explain most of what follows, and the public health ranking is similarly modest: Expat.com cites a 23rd place in the 2024 World Index of Healthcare Innovation. The minimum wage was 870 euros a month in 2025, according to Idealista, a 6.1 percent increase on 2024. Average total gross monthly earnings were 1,741 euros, which Idealista's reporting of national statistics puts 6 percent above June 2024. Regular monthly earnings excluding seasonal bonuses were 1,368 euros. Those are figures one can live on if the rent is modest, and the rent, on the evidence of expat forums, is rarely modest in Lisbon.

The welfare system compensates in structure rather than in speed. According to Expat.com's health guide, the national health service is available free to residents, with co-payments between 2.50 and 40 euros, but patients have faced months or even years of waiting for appointments and surgery. Private insurance buys shorter queues, and more than four million Portuguese now hold some form. Childcare follows the same pattern. The Traveler's guide, whose figures are unofficial estimates, says free crèche places apply primarily to children born from late 2021, with social-sector fees of roughly 100 to 250 euros a month and private places in the major cities at 350 to 700, or up to 1,000 for international nurseries. The state is generous in principle. The waiting list is generous in practice.

The upside is the price list. MyLifeElsewhere's price data show Luxembourg 64.2 percent more expensive overall, with housing 95.2 percent higher and groceries 54.8 percent higher. The same source has restaurants 79.4 percent dearer in Luxembourg and utilities for a two-person flat 131 percent higher, so even the small pleasures arrive at a premium. For someone earning a Luxembourg-style salary remotely, or an international salary, Portugal is a bargain. For someone paid in Portuguese euros, it is merely a country where the bargains are priced for somebody else.

## Luxembourg: Where the State Pays for the Nursery and the Landlord Collects It

Luxembourg's answer to the childcare question is the chèque-service accueil. According to the government's own portal, vouchers cover children aged 0 to 12, give 20 free hours of childcare for 46 weeks a year to children aged 1 to 4 in the multilingual education programme, and provide free care from 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. in term time for school-age children, with five main meals. Residents and non-resident employees qualify, and fees beyond the free hours follow an income-based sliding scale. The portal does not publish the monetary value of the voucher, which is the sort of detail that keeps a paper trail honest and a spreadsheet blank.

Housing absorbs the dividend. Expatica reports average rent of 30.22 euros per square metre including service charges as of December 2025, which makes an 85 square metre rental about 2,838 euros in the Centre and 1,651 in the North. Basic private health packages start at about 80 euros a month on top of universal cover, groceries run around 611 euros a month for the average household, and an unsubsidised crèche averages about 1,141 euros. Forum parents in older Mumsnet threads described the same arrangement in different prices: expensive rent, crowded crèches, subsidies that mattered and the Luxembourgish-language nursery as the sensible budget option.

Expatica's household figures complete the picture. Average household spending was 6,027 euros a month in 2023, with housing alone, including utilities, at about 2,432 euros, and a single person needs roughly 4,318 euros a month in 2026 terms. It adds that public childcare costs about 4 to 6 euros an hour depending on income, though places are limited, against 7 to 9 euros an hour for private international care. In other words, the voucher is real, and so is the waiting for a place.

None of this is a trap, exactly. The OECD data say Luxembourg wages are the highest in the EU, and the benefits are designed for people who actually work there. It is a country that gives with one hand and, with the other, invoices you for a flat.

## The Reckoning

The two countries solve the same problem from opposite ends. Portugal keeps the cost side low and the wage side low with it, then offers a universal public system that is free at the door and slow in the corridor. Luxembourg lets prices float to a level only a high salary can sustain, then uses targeted instruments, chiefly the childcare voucher, to keep a family's costs from becoming ridiculous. The Portuguese system punishes impatience. The Luxembourg system punishes arriving without a contract.

The Brussels Times, reporting the same OECD data, puts Belgium's average gross wage at 62,348 euros, which makes Luxembourg's 77,840 a premium of roughly 15,000 euros for crossing one border. Portugal's equivalent figure is not in that league, and nobody should pretend it is. The decisive variable is where your income comes from. MyLifeElsewhere's figures put childcare in Luxembourg at roughly two and a half times the Portuguese price and housing at nearly double, but a Luxembourg salary is calibrated to that. On a Portuguese salary, the free crèche place, if it exists, is the one subsidy that matters, and a month of private insurance premiums is a non-trivial line in the budget. On a Luxembourg salary, the voucher and the free school-age care carry much of the childcare burden.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — an expat in Portugal said online cost-of-living guides understate the real bill, that renting without a local guarantor meant months of rent up front plus a deposit, and that the minimum wage cannot support Lisbon rent. They love the country anyway.*

> *Teamblind — a poster argued that a 50,000 euro salary in Portugal leaves room to save around half of what remains after rent and ordinary expenses, if you cook at home.*

> *Teamblind — another job-seeker said a 57,000 euro Portuguese offer felt low against Lisbon rent, which they expected to take roughly half their net pay, and that meal vouchers did not close the gap.*

> *Mumsnet — a parent weighing a move to Luxembourg City said rents had risen sharply and that suburbs such as Strassen and Bonnevoie were the realistic options, while another noted that many crèches were full.*

> *Mumsnet — a mother in an earlier thread said Luxembourgish-language crèches tend to be better staffed and cheaper than French-language ones, and that the state subsidy exists to soften the cost.*

## Conclusion

If your income is Portuguese, Portugal is a lifestyle country with a rent problem. Work out your rent before your offer letter, apply early for a crèche, and treat private insurance as part of the cost rather than an upgrade. If your income is international or remote, Portugal offers the same pleasant weather at a considerable discount, and you can afford to be patient with the waiting lists.

Whichever you choose, ask for the childcare, health and housing numbers in writing before you resign from anything. If your income is Luxembourgish, Luxembourg gives you the stronger family safety net, provided you register for the vouchers on arrival and accept that housing will take its share. The bottom line is this: one country asks what you earn, and the other asks where you earn it.
