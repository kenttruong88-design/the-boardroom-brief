# Bulgaria Has the Fiber-Optic Cable. Morocco Has the Excuse Note From God. Guess Who Works From Home More.

🇧🇬 Bulgaria · 🇲🇦 Morocco

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Bulgaria joined the euro and Schengen in 2025, wired much of Sofia with fiber cheaper than a bus ticket, and passed a digital nomad residence permit — and still recorded, for years running, the lowest share of people working from home in the European Union. Morocco, meanwhile, has no digital nomad visa at all, leaves remote workers to quietly stretch a tourist stamp, and yet has spent every March since 2020 nationally rehearsing a skill Bulgarian office managers have never needed: telling an entire workforce the hours are changing and everyone will adapt. Flexibility, it turns out, is not primarily an infrastructure problem.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇬 Bulgaria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register your address (adresna registratsiya) as soon as you arrive | Don't assume EU freedom of movement excuses you from local registration |
| Confirm your 183-day tax-residency status in writing before you settle in | Don't assume the flat 10% rate just applies itself without paperwork |
| Treat the fiber internet as a genuine perk — it is faster and cheaper than most of Western Europe | Don't skip a mobile backup for the occasional outage in older Soviet-era buildings |
| Show your face in the office periodically, even on a "remote" contract | Don't mistake a remote job title for an invisible one — attendance is still quietly tallied |
| Apply early for the new digital-nomad residence permit if you plan to stay | Don't expect a single-visit process — bring duplicate documents for the inevitable resubmission |
| Ask about social-security contributions, not just income tax | Don't budget only for the 10% headline rate and get surprised later |

#### 🇲🇦 Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your telework arrangement in writing, as the 2020 labor code amendment requires | Don't rely on a verbal understanding, however friendly the employer sounds |
| Base yourself in Casablanca or Marrakech for reliable infrastructure and coworking density | Don't assume a smaller town's advertised wifi speed will match reality |
| Build Friday prayer and Ramadan's adjusted hours into your calendar by default | Don't schedule a client call for 1pm on a Friday and expect anyone to pick up |
| Carry a local eSIM or backup hotspot as standard practice | Don't stake a client presentation on a single café connection |
| Track your 90-day tourist-visa clock carefully if you're working without a formal permit | Don't assume enforcement will always be as relaxed as it currently looks |
| Ask explicitly for the equipment reimbursement the labor code entitles you to | Don't wait for your employer to offer it unprompted |

Bulgaria's paradox is a matter of public record. According to Eurostat data reported by the Sofia Globe, Bulgaria posted the lowest rate of home-based work in the entire EU in 2020 — 1.2 percent of the working-age population, against a bloc-wide average of 12.3 percent — and it has stayed near the bottom of the table in every survey since, even as the country's IT and outsourcing sector embraced hybrid arrangements with more enthusiasm than almost anywhere else in the region. The OECD's 2025 labour market review of Bulgaria points to a structural explanation rather than a cultural one: a shrinking, aging population and an economy still weighted toward manufacturing and agriculture, sectors that do not lend themselves to a laptop and a balcony.

None of which stopped Bulgaria from making itself unusually convenient for the sliver of the workforce that can go remote. A flat 10 percent income tax, Schengen and eurozone membership as of 2025, and a newly approved digital-nomad residence permit (applications opened December 2025) have turned Sofia, Plovdiv, and the mountain town of Bansko into minor nodes on the global remote-work map, favored, per multiple expat accounts, for fiber connections running at a fraction of Western European prices. The catch is bureaucratic rather than technical: proving tax residency, timing the 183-day threshold correctly, and surviving a permit process prone to sending documents back for a second look.

Morocco took the opposite route: legislate the flexibility, skip the visa. The 2020 labor-code amendment was one of the more detailed telework statutes in the region for its time, guaranteeing teleworkers the same rights as on-site staff, requiring a written agreement, obliging employers to cover equipment costs, and enshrining a right to disconnect — protections several EU labor codes still handle more vaguely. A further reform, announced for 2025, aims to tighten the rules further. Yet Morocco has never bothered with a formal remote-worker visa; the operative workaround, per multiple digital-nomad guides, is a 90-day tourist stamp most people simply renew by leaving and re-entering. The IT sector now accounts for roughly a third of the country's teleworking job postings, concentrated in Casablanca's fiber-dense business district and Marrakech's coworking cluster around Gueliz.

**The Reckoning.** Put the two systems side by side and the counterintuitive part comes into focus. Bulgaria has every formal ingredient for a remote-work boom — EU membership, cheap high-speed internet, a favorable tax code, an actual visa product — and still can't get its own population to work from home at rates matching the EU average, a gap the OECD attributes to structural economics but which also reads, given the persistence of office attendance in fully remote-capable roles, as stubborn presenteeism. Morocco has none of that formal scaffolding and instead has the muscle memory: a country that nationally reschedules its working day every year, shifting government offices to a continuous 9am-to-3pm shift and building in accommodation for Friday prayer, has already normalized the idea that "when work happens" is negotiable. Bulgaria automated the paperwork of flexibility. Morocco practiced the choreography of it.

The comparison flatters neither side completely. Bulgaria's rigidity comes with genuine legal clarity once you clear the residency hurdle; Morocco's improvisational flexibility comes bundled with a visa status many remote workers simply choose not to examine too closely. Hofstede's cultural-dimensions data puts both countries in broadly hierarchical, collectivist territory relative to Northern Europe — which makes Bulgaria's low WFH numbers less surprising and Morocco's institutionalized flexibility more of an outlier worth noting.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — a Bulgaria-bound remote worker described the tax-residency question as more confusing than the move itself, with the 183-day rule and a UK-Bulgaria double-taxation treaty requiring more paperwork than anyone had budgeted time for.*

> *Quora — a poster asking about EU work-permit and insurance requirements for remote employees in Bulgaria noted that "remote" didn't exempt them from local registration and social-security enrollment, which took longer than the actual job search.*

> *Quora — someone asking about typical Moroccan office hours was surprised to learn how late the standard workday runs and how long the midday break stretches, a detail several remote collaborators only discovered after repeatedly scheduling calls no one in Morocco intended to join at that hour.*

> *A Bulgaria digital-nomad blog described the new residence-permit process as procedurally sound but slow, with documents sent back for revision more than once — the advice offered to future applicants was to bring extra copies of everything and expect a second trip to the migration office.*

> *A Morocco coworking guide noted that newcomers consistently overestimate wifi reliability outside Casablanca and Marrakech, and the seasoned advice passed along was to treat a local eSIM as non-negotiable rather than a backup plan.*

### Conclusion

Neither country is lying about what it offers. Bulgaria has the tax code and the internet speeds; Morocco has the legal protections and the lower cost of living. What both undersell is the gap between policy and habit. In Bulgaria, that gap shows up as a workforce with every reason to work from home and a persistent tendency not to. In Morocco, it shows up as a workforce with no formal visa track for remote status and a national talent for making flexible hours look routine anyway. Choose Bulgaria for certainty and Morocco for adaptability, and budget patience for whichever bureaucracy you land in — because in both countries, the paperwork, not the wifi, decides how remote "remote work" gets to be.
