# In Norway, Everyone Knows What You Make. In the Philippines, Nobody Dares Ask.

🇳🇴 Norway · 🇵🇭 Philippines

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Norway, any citizen can look up any other citizen's taxable income in a few keystrokes, legal and mildly awkward since 2001 — yet, per a ManpowerGroup survey cited by the union Tekna, roughly half of Norwegian workers accept a job offer before ever asking what it pays. In the Philippines, salaries are guarded like state secrets, discussed in whispers or not at all — yet a growing cohort of BPO and remote workers now negotiate with the confidence of people who've decided the old rules no longer apply. Two countries, two theories of what transparency is for.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Look up sector minimums under your industry's *tariffavtale* (collective agreement) before any interview | Assume a public tax lookup invites citing a colleague's exact number in negotiation |
| State a clear counter-figure in kroner, not a vague range | Leave the room without asking — see: the half of workers who don't |
| Weigh vacation (five weeks statutory), pension, and parental leave as part of total comp | Fixate on gross salary alone; packages are built around the extras |
| Keep your pitch calm, factual, and modest in tone | Oversell your CV — Janteloven punishes visible self-promotion |
| Loop in your union rep if one exists for your role | Skip the union conversation assuming it's only for factory jobs |
| Register for Skatteetaten's online services early | Be surprised when someone gets notified that you searched their tax return |

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research market rate via JobStreet, PayScale, or PSA wage data before an offer lands | Wait for an employer to volunteer a salary range unprompted |
| Frame a counter-offer around value and gratitude, not confrontation | Issue ultimatums; direct bargaining reads as aggressive |
| Ask specifically about 13th-month pay, HMO, and SSS/PhilHealth/Pag-IBIG contributions | Assume the quoted base salary is the whole offer |
| Use a trusted intermediary — HR, a mentor, a recruiter — if a direct ask feels too forward | Stay silent out of *hiya*; unasked raises are the norm, not the ideal |
| Compare BPO/remote-client rates separately from local corporate rates | Benchmark a Manila salary against a provincial one — the gap is real |
| Put any verbal counter-offer in writing afterward | Accept on the spot just to avoid an awkward pause |

### Norway: Radical Transparency, Reluctant Negotiators

Norway has published citizens' taxable income since 1863, and put the system online in 2001, per The Local and Daily Scandinavian. Since 2014, anyone who looks up another person's tax file gets logged and reported back to the person searched — a change that reportedly cut lookups by roughly 90 percent almost overnight. The country didn't abandon transparency; it added a small tax of its own, paid in social discomfort.

What's odd is how little this openness translates into individual assertiveness. Per SSB, average monthly wages in 2025 were NOK 62,070 against a median of NOK 55,800, and women's average earnings sat at 87.9 percent of men's — a gap that has persisted despite every salary being, in principle, public record. Radical transparency is not radical negotiation. Janteloven, the informal code against standing out, does quiet work here: everyone can see what everyone else makes, but asking for more yourself still reads as un-Norwegian. Norway is also bracing for the EU Pay Transparency Directive, which will eventually require employers to justify pay gaps with "objective criteria," per Ravio and Lewis Silkin — a compliance framework for doing what the country has done since the 19th century.

### Philippines: Guarded Numbers, an Emerging Negotiating Class

The PSA's 2024 Occupational Wages Survey put the national average full-time monthly wage at PHP 21,544 (roughly $370), with a striking spread by sector: ICT workers averaged PHP 43,676, while agriculture, forestry, and fishing workers averaged just PHP 14,615 — nearly a threefold gap within one labor market. Unlike Norway, there is no public ledger; salary information circulates informally, filtered through norms like *hiya* (reluctance to cause embarrassment) and *pakikisama* (group harmony), which recruitment advisories say lead many workers to accept the first number offered rather than risk an awkward exchange.

The calculus is shifting fastest where workers face foreign clients. The BPO and remote-work economy, built over two decades into a global talent pipeline, has quietly imported Western negotiating norms along with Western paychecks — entry-level remote roles now commonly range from $700 to over $6,000 a month, and advisory sites report workers who negotiate see an average raise near 19 percent. The message reaching a new generation of Filipino professionals, delivered mostly through recruiter blogs rather than any official source, is blunt: not asking isn't politeness, it's a discount you're giving your employer for free.

### The Reckoning

Here is the joke neither country would find funny: Norway built the most transparent salary system on Earth and still can't get half its workforce to ask a follow-up question, while the Philippines built one of the least transparent systems and is now producing, sector by sector, some of its most confident negotiators. Hofstede Insights scores Norway at 31 for power distance and 69 for individualism, against the Philippines' 94 and 32 — on paper, the flat, individualist culture should out-negotiate the hierarchical one every time. In practice, Norway's flatness discourages the self-assertion its openness should reward, while Filipino professionals in globalized industries negotiate not despite hierarchy but by opting out of it, dealing instead with foreign clients who expect a counter-offer as a matter of course.

The irony compounds further: Norway's transparency was meant, in part, to close pay gaps, yet SSB's own figures show one persisting regardless. The Philippines' opacity was assumed to entrench underpayment, yet the segment of its labor force most cut off from official wage data — remote and BPO workers reading Glassdoor and Levels.fyi instead of any domestic register — is arguably closing its own gap fastest, one counter-offer at a time.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — a commenter recounting Norway's public tax registry described the appeal of transparency curdling into unease once the 2014 rule kicked in, realizing that looking up a colleague's income now meant that colleague would know who'd been checking.*

> *TeamBlind — a software engineer weighing a Norwegian offer outside Oslo, in a smaller city like Tromsø, noted that headline salary numbers ignored cost-of-living reality entirely, with no meaningful adjustment for working far from the capital.*

> *Quora — a respondent addressing whether it's acceptable to negotiate salary in the Philippines answered plainly that it is, provided the person's credentials and track record actually justify the ask, a caveat that read as more permission-seeking than the question itself.*

> *Expat.com's Philippines forum — a newly arrived foreign employee described adjusting to a workplace where seniority was addressed formally as "sir" or "ma'am" by default, and where raising a pay question directly with a superior felt, initially, like a small act of insubordination.*

> *InterNations' Manila expat survey, cited in secondary reporting, found that a large majority of relocated professionals felt at home quickly and found it easy to build relationships with Filipino colleagues — a social ease several respondents said made it harder to bring up something as transactional as pay.*

### Conclusion

The practical advice inverts neatly by country. In Norway, the data is sitting in public view — use it, cite it, and still ask the question out loud, because apparently half the workforce forgets that last step. In the Philippines, the data mostly isn't public, so build it yourself from job boards and recruiter reports before sitting down at the table, and treat a counter-offer as basic professionalism rather than a breach of harmony. Both countries, for different reasons, have made it easy to know what everyone else earns and hard to ask for what you're worth — a strange coincidence, or an accurate description of how salaries work everywhere.
