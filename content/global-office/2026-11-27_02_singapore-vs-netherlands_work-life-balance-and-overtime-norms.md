# Home by Five in Amsterdam, Still Online at Nine in Singapore

🇸🇬 Singapore · 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Singapore's Ministry of Manpower reports that paid hours averaged 42.9 a week in early 2026. Eurostat says the Netherlands averaged 32.1 in 2024, the shortest working week in the European Union. A gap of roughly ten hours a week, compounded over a career, is either a rounding error or a second life, depending on whether your visa is stamped at Changi or Schiphol. This guide explains what each country expects after five o'clock, who is legally entitled to object, and what your contract may or may not say.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the overtime clause in your contract and check whether your grade is covered by the Employment Act | Assume a manager or executive title comes with overtime pay |
| Agree expectations on weekend and midnight contact with your manager early | Wait until the second month to learn that messages are expected at all hours |
| Keep timestamped records of hours if you ever expect to raise a dispute | Rely on a verbal promise of time off in lieu |
| Ask the team how long people actually stay, not how long the contract says | Judge a company by its stated office hours |
| Compare foreign employers with local small firms, since reported cultures differ widely | Treat every Singapore employer as identical |

### Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Log off at the agreed time and expect your colleagues to do the same | Show loyalty by answering messages at eight in the evening |
| Ask about the contract hours and whether four days is possible | Assume a full-time role means five days |
| Use your full leave entitlement, since colleagues expect it to be used | Offer to cancel a holiday as a gesture of commitment |
| Give direct feedback and expect to receive it | Dress criticism in three layers of diplomacy |
| Check the collective agreement for overtime pay | Assume the law guarantees extra pay for extra hours |

## Singapore: Forty-Four Hours, Plus Whatever the Midnight Email Wants

The Ministry of Manpower's own data show a long, slow retreat from overwork. Paid hours averaged 45.5 a week in 2016 and 43.1 in 2025, while paid overtime fell from 3.3 hours a week to 1.9. An earlier ministry analysis put the usual weekly hours of full-time resident employees at 45.8 in 2021. These are averages for private establishments with at least 25 employees and the public sector, so smaller firms, which several voices below find the hardest, are not fully captured. The ministry also publishes the rules. The Employment Act sets a normal week of up to 44 hours, requires overtime at no less than 1.5 times the basic hourly rate, and caps overtime at 72 hours a month.

The catch sits in the salary thresholds. Overtime pay applies to non-workmen earning a basic salary of $2,600 a month or less, and workmen earning $4,500 or less, while managers and executives fall outside that part of the Act, and their terms follow the contract. In practice, the more senior and better paid the role, the more the working day is a matter for negotiation. Singapore still fares respectably in comparisons: Time Out Singapore reported in August 2025 that the Remote.com Global Life-Work Balance Index ranked it 25th of 60 countries and first in Asia, a distinction roughly like winning the quietest room in a busy kitchen. The index weighs leave, sick pay and maternity provisions as well as hours, so a respectable rank is compatible with a long working day. The ministry's own trend line is the better evidence: overtime has generally declined over the last decade, and the 2021 figure of 2.5 paid overtime hours a week was lower than the 2.9 recorded in 2018. Improvement, in Singapore, is real and measured in tenths of an hour.

## Netherlands: The Shortest Week in the Union, Hosted by Part-Time Work

Eurostat reports average actual weekly hours of 32.1 for Dutch workers aged 20 to 64 in 2024, against 36.0 for the EU as a whole. Statistics Netherlands, via DutchReview, puts hours worked at 1,439 a year in 2023. The explanation is largely arrangements, not idleness: the OECD notes that nearly 60% of Dutch women work part-time, roughly three times the OECD average for women, although its excerpt does not state the data year. Older OECD data, from 2015 and so flagged as old, found only 0.45% of Dutch employees working 50 or more hours a week, against an OECD average of 12.51%.

The law is more permissive than the culture. Business.gov.nl states that employees may work up to 12 hours a day and 60 hours a week, with at least 11 hours of rest between working days and 36 hours of weekly rest, and that the law does not set overtime pay, leaving it to contracts and collective agreements. The shorter week is therefore a social settlement, not a statutory one. It does not abolish burnout: a 2023 union survey cited by DutchReview found more than one in five workers facing burnout from work stress or overwork. Hofstede Insights offers cultural comparisons for those who want them, but its scores could not be retrieved for this piece, so none are quoted.

The daily reality is softer than the statute and the survey alike. An Expat.com guide updated in October 2024 notes that a contract may say 40 hours while the employee works four days or part of the week from home, and that the Flexible Working Act lets staff with six months' service at employers of at least ten people request part-time remote work. A 2022 first-person piece on IamExpat, written as a paid partnership with a language school and so worth reading with a raised eyebrow, describes an expat's colleagues asking why she was online at 8 p.m., and her manager promising to work on that in the future. Her advice to newcomers was to adopt a nine-to-five mentality, which in Singapore would be filed under ambition deficit. The Dutch workplace, in short, treats unnecessary availability as a management problem, not a virtue.

## The Reckoning

Put the figures together, with the caution that Eurostat's measure of actual hours for workers aged 20 to 64 and Singapore's measure of paid hours at larger establishments are not identical, and the direction is unmistakable. Singapore workers log roughly ten hours more a week, even as the trend improves, and the protections stop at a salary line. The Netherlands built its short week from part-time contracts, a norm that leaving on time is unremarkable, and legal rest periods, though its statutory ceiling is generous and its overtime pay is a matter of negotiation. The Netherlands also has the cleaner cultural signal: a Dutch employee's Reddit post about leaving at five became news in January 2026 precisely because the American manager expected something else.

The practical consequence is who carries the burden of limits. In Singapore, a professional or manager is expected to negotiate boundaries personally, one manager at a time. In the Netherlands the boundary is largely the default, which means the awkwardness falls on the newcomer who does not know it exists, and on the American manager who does not respect it. Neither country is a paradise, and nobody should relocate on the strength of a ranking: Singapore pays for its intensity in hours, and the Netherlands pays in part-time earnings and a household model that quietly assumes someone else is working more.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/askSingapore, paraphrased experience: a tech employee said staff at his firm were expected to work more than 15 hours a day plus long weekend shifts, and commenters mostly advised finding another job because a complaint to the ministry would be slow and require heavy proof.*

> *Blind, paraphrased experience: replies to a Singapore versus USA thread described rigid Saturday work and little flexibility, a face-time culture and roughly 60-hour weeks, while one commenter who had worked in both places said Singapore actually offered better balance than the Bay Area.*

> *Expat.com, paraphrased experience: in a thread about a tech employer in Singapore, one member said it was not always the extreme 996 pattern but still quite demanding, and another said overtime is common and rarely paid, with leave often low.*

> *Reddit, paraphrased experience: a Dutch employee told his American manager that being unreachable after five did not signal a lack of dedication, and his HR representative reportedly laughed and told him to ignore the complaint.*

> *Expat.com, paraphrased experience: a member who has worked in the Netherlands since 2015 described a competitive tech scene with overtime and burnout, warned that direct talk can mask toxic behaviour, and advised checking contracts carefully.*

## Conclusion

If you want the evening as a default, the Netherlands is the better bet, with the caution that its law and its culture are not the same thing and that international employers sometimes import their own. If you can use Singapore's higher intensity for faster career progress, pay and a tax-friendly base, go with open eyes: confirm your overtime status, ask the team how long the day really runs, and treat the contract hours as a starting position.

In both places, the written contract and the unwritten team norm decide your working life, and the second is rarely in the offer letter. As a rule of thumb, ask which of the two you will actually be asked to follow, and then wait to see who answers before you sign.
