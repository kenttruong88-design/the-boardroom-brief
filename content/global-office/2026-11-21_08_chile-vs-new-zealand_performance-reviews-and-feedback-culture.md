# Chile Delivers Its Criticism Upward, New Zealand Delivers It Sideways, Both With an Apology

🇨🇱 Chile · 🇳🇿 New Zealand

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Gallup counts 33% of Chilean employees as engaged at work and 23% of New Zealanders, which would suggest the Chileans are the more satisfied. Gallup also counts 43% of Chileans as thriving in their lives overall against 56% of New Zealanders, which suggests the reverse. Both findings are from the 2025 State of the Global Workplace, and both are compatible with a single observation: performance feedback in these two countries is delivered by people who would rather be doing something else. The arrangement is relevant to anyone who proposes to be judged by it.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your manager in advance what criteria the review will use | Assume the criteria exist in writing because the form has a column |
| Frame criticism of a process in positive terms, "en positivo" | Deliver a blunt critique in a meeting and expect applause |
| Ask for feedback during the year, since reviews may be the only formal occasion | Wait for the annual conversation to learn how you are doing |
| Treat your direct manager as the main gatekeeper to your career | Go around your boss to someone more senior |
| Keep the salary conversation separate from the development conversation where you can | Walk into a review expecting the two to be unrelated in the manager's mind |
| Learn how severance works before accepting an indefinite contract | Assume a review is the only route by which a job can end |

### In New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat "not bad" and "good, thank you" as information, and confirm what was meant | Hear polite as meaning satisfied |
| Ask for a review cycle in writing, since the law does not require one | Assume an annual review is guaranteed |
| Use first names with your manager, including the senior one | Call anyone "sir" or "boss" |
| Raise concerns one-on-one rather than in a group meeting | Argue the point in front of the whole team |
| Keep copies of any improvement plan and any warnings | Ignore a first written warning because the tone was friendly |
| Check whether a 90-day trial or a high-income threshold applies to your contract | Assume dismissal protections are identical for every employee |

## Chile

The official literature on Chilean performance reviews is thin, so one relies on the specialists who write for the business press. An October 2025 Emol article, quoting consultants from BDO Chile and Talana, says reviews generate anxiety because employees perceive them as a "judgement or punishment" linked to pay and promotion. It lists unclear criteria, perceived unfairness and little feedback during the year as recurring problems. The advice offered is the standard repertoire: continuous feedback, advance criteria, self-assessment and a separation of development from pay. A recommendation list of this kind is usually a description of the problem written in the imperative.

Hierarchy is the supporting structure. Hofstede Insights scores Chile at 63 for power distance, 49 for individualism, 28 for motivation towards achievement and success, and 86 for uncertainty avoidance, which describes a country that respects rank and prefers rules to surprises. A 2019 study by DNA Human Capital, reported by Chilevision, found that 40% of employees who leave voluntarily do so because of a bad boss, and its director described Chilean bosses as authoritarian in very hierarchical structures. A 2012 opinion piece on the Gestiopolis site argues that direct criticism is regarded as rude and that colleagues will explain it away by saying the critic is "going through problems". It is a single author's view and I treat it as such, but it matches what the other sources imply.

The legal backdrop shapes how little anyone needs to say. The Meganoticias summary of the Labour Code describes termination for the needs of the company under article 161 as carrying severance of 30 days of the last monthly pay per year of service, capped at 11 years, on a base limited to 90 UF, for employees with indefinite contracts and at least a year of service. That is a defined price for ending a job. A manager who can end a contract at a known cost has less need for an elaborate paper trail of reviews, and an employee has less ability to demand one.

The practical consequence for a newcomer is that the review, where one exists, is a conversation about rank rather than a measurement of output. The Emol piece notes that the anxiety stems partly from the link to salary and promotion, and the Gallup data add a wrinkle: Chile's engagement of 33% is the highest in the series, yet only 43% of its employees report thriving. Staff who are committed to the job and unconvinced by the rest of their lives are the natural audience for an annual verdict delivered by someone they cannot contradict.

## New Zealand

New Zealand has the documentation, and the government publishes it. Employment New Zealand says there is no specific legal obligation to hold a performance review or give a pay rise unless an agreement or policy says so. It nonetheless recommends formal reviews every 3 or 6 months and informal catch-ups every 2 to 3 weeks, and warns that concerns not raised during the year may later be seen as accepted. For an underperformer it prescribes good faith, a documented performance improvement plan with a realistic timeframe, support, and a series of warnings, recommending at least a written warning and a final written warning before termination unless the agreement says otherwise. The state describes a process in which no one is surprised, which is more than most workplaces manage.

Delivery is where the process runs into the national temperament. Advisory writing on Kiwi workplaces says people tend to downplay praise and soften criticism, so that "not bad" may be generous and "we'll have a think about it" may be a refusal. A 2025 study by Gonzalez-Montero and Maydell in the New Zealand Journal of Employment Relations interviewed 17 managers and found they reported "high levels of stress and various negative emotions" when giving negative feedback. Managers in the process-heavy country dislike the process, and the employee, reading the cues, is expected to guess.

The protections are also changing. Employment Relations Amendment Act 2026 took effect on 21 February 2026, according to DLA Piper, and bars personal grievances over dismissals during 90-day trial periods. It also excludes employees above an initial threshold of NZD 200,000 a year from unjustified-dismissal grievances, unless they contract back in, applying to new agreements from that date and generally to existing ones from 2027. The Kiwi review process is, for most employees, still a real safeguard. For new hires and high earners it is now partly optional.

There is one more feature worth noting for an expatriate. Employment New Zealand advises managers to ask employees what could have gone better, which it says often yields a more useful development conversation than manager-led criticism, and to let the employee complete a self-assessment first. The result is a review in which you are invited to indict yourself, politely, before anyone else has to. Newcomers who treat this as a formality tend to learn, around the second quarter, that it was the main event.

## The Reckoning

The two countries pursue the same goal, which is saying something unpleasant without saying it, by opposite means. Chile uses rank: the criticism travels in one direction, from the boss, and the subordinate manages the response. New Zealand uses informality: first names, open-plan offices, and a manager who prefers to be one of the team, so that the criticism arrives in a form deniable by both parties. A settlement adviser writing in Multicultural Times describes Kiwis as indirect and managers as discomfited by titles. The Chilean employee knows who is in charge and what the boss is thinking. The New Zealand employee knows the boss's first name.

On paper the legal systems point the other way. Chile offers a severance schedule, and New Zealand a procedural fairness requirement that, for most staff, has teeth. A Chilean employee is protected by a payment, a New Zealander by a procedure, and the procedure is only as useful as the manager's ability to deliver bad news aloud. The Gallup figures fit this loosely: Chileans are more engaged but less likely to be thriving, New Zealanders the reverse. Whether the review system is cause or consequence I cannot determine from two percentages.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — A member who worked in Chile from 2013 to 2015 describes a workplace where meetings start late, personal matters mix freely with business and personal space is scarce, and advises staying in Europe. It is a single, dated and openly negative account, and I report it as such.*

> *Expat.com — A member with seven years in Chile describes employees as heavily dependent on their employers, with progress often reliant on personal connections, and says employers can be closed to outside ideas about improving processes. Integration took a long time, partly because of local Spanish.*

> *Expat.com — An American who has lived in Wellington for about a decade says offices are friendly and casual but still hierarchical, that being disliked can block advancement however capable you are, and that they are somewhat more formal and direct with management.*

> *Personal blog — An immigrant two years into life in New Zealand reports that politeness makes real reactions hard to read: a client's courteous reply preceded a cancelled project. Pushback, they conclude, usually means your input is valued, and past reputation does not travel.*

> *Potentia blog (2020) — A German migrant used to Swiss formality finds New Zealand work far more relaxed and credits volunteering, rewritten CVs and virtual coffees for getting her first role and her first successful interview.*

## Conclusion

If you want your performance judged against stated criteria and a defined process, New Zealand is the safer bet, provided you ask for the review cycle in writing, keep copies of warnings and check whether the 90-day trial or the NZD 200,000 threshold applies to you. If you are moving to Chile, assume feedback will be indirect and hierarchical, ask for criteria early, and learn the severance rules before you sign, because the review is less likely to be the thing that tells you where you stand.

In both countries the sentence that matters will be the one nobody says. In Chile it is withheld out of respect for rank, in New Zealand out of respect for your feelings, and in each case you are expected to work out the content from the tone.
