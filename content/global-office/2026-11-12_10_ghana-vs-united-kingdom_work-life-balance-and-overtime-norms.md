# Ghana Gives You Eight Hours and a Three-Hour Commute; the United Kingdom Gives You Contracted Hours and the Sense You Should Stay Anyway

🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Both countries have a legal working week and both tolerate its erosion. Ghana's Labour Act caps the standard week at 40 hours, and the UK's Working Time Regulations cap the average week at 48 unless you sign a form saying otherwise. What neither statute covers is the part that decides how tired you will be: the commute in Accra, and the unspoken expectation in a London office that a nine-to-six contract is a polite opening position. A reader considering either should understand where the hours actually go.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask the employer in writing what the overtime rate is before you accept a role | Assume the law fixes a rate for you; it does not |
| Choose where you live by commute, not by the size of the flat | Trust a "twenty minutes away" estimate in rush hour |
| Budget transport as a major line of your monthly spending | Assume the salary figure is what lands in your pocket after fares |
| Learn who actually holds authority, and show deference accordingly | Skip greetings and go straight to the agenda |
| Keep a record of hours worked beyond the standard day | Wait for the employer to volunteer a record |
| Know that the National Labour Commission exists if overtime goes unpaid | Treat a verbal promise as a contract |

### In the United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read your contract for the number of hours and whether you have opted out of the 48-hour limit | Sign an opt-out without understanding it |
| Take your full statutory holiday, which is 5.6 weeks for a five-day week | Treat unused leave as a badge of commitment |
| Ask whether overtime is paid, banked as time off in lieu, or simply expected | Assume that a salary buys unlimited evenings |
| Leave visibly on time, now and then, to establish that you can | Become the person who always replies at 10pm |
| Agree flexible arrangements in writing | Rely on a manager's good intentions surviving a reorganisation |
| Learn the understatement: "it would be great if" means "do it" | Take polite phrasing as optional |

## Ghana

On paper, Ghana is orderly. Act 651 sets a maximum of eight hours a day and 40 hours a week, and WageIndicator's summary adds a weekly rest period of at least 48 consecutive hours. Overtime is usually paid at 150 percent of the hourly wage, though, as Mondaq's legal summary notes, the Act sets no specific overtime rate; it is left to the employer's rules or a union agreement, and a worker cannot be forced to do overtime if rates have not been clearly set. A complaint about unpaid overtime can go to the National Labour Commission. The statistics are, at first glance, equally reassuring. The Ghana Statistical Service, reported by MyJoyOnline, estimated that about 7 percent of employed people worked more than 48 hours a week, falling to about 4 percent by the third quarter of 2023, with urban and male workers more likely to be among them.

Then comes the part the statute does not reach. A Graphic Online health feature from November 2024 observes that many Ghanaians regularly work beyond 40 hours and that enforcement of the limits is weak, particularly in the formal sector. Gallup's 2023 workplace report, carried by CitiNewsroom, ranked Ghana fourth among Sub-Saharan African countries for daily worker stress. And a May 2026 piece in Insight Ghana lists commutes of one to three hours each way as a hidden cost not reflected in salary, alongside rents in well-located Accra areas rising 8 to 14 percent year on year. A Graphic Online opinion piece from August 2026 describes the evening commute as another kind of work, with peak-hour fares well above the approved rates. The working day, in short, may be eight hours long; the working life around it is considerably longer.

Culturally, the office is hierarchical and relational. One Ghanaian academic thesis on Hofstede's indices describes power distance as relatively big, with managers not easily accessible, and a collectivist streak that makes workplace discipline harder to enforce. A Graphic Online piece on cultural influences in business adds that last-minute cancellations, long meeting delays and weekday funeral attendance are accepted as ordinary. For an expatriate on a local contract, this means that the number on the clock matters less than the number of people who can claim your time. Employer-provided housing and medical cover, according to an Expat.com poster, tend to attach to the foreign-employer jobs rather than the local ones. Pay is the other pressure. Insight Ghana notes that inflation fell to a four-year low of 5.4 percent in December 2025 but that everyday costs kept outrunning wages for many workers, and a YEN.com.gh report from November 2025 quotes a Ghanaian critic who argues that employers keep staff until the end of the day even when the tasks take a couple of hours, and that the nine-to-five was imported without its protections. That is one man's view and a sharp one, but it explains why overtime in Ghana is often less a payment question than a question of who controls your evening.

## United Kingdom

The British arrangement is the opposite: dense rules, mild enforcement and a great deal of social pressure. GOV.UK states that average working time cannot exceed 48 hours a week, normally averaged over 17 weeks, and that workers can opt out. Most workers are entitled to 5.6 weeks of paid holiday a year, which is 28 days for someone on a five-day week, though employers may count bank holidays within it. LeaveWizard, compiling ONS-based figures, puts the average full-time week at 36.7 hours in the fourth quarter of 2025, and says the UK ranks 29th of 38 OECD countries for the shortest working weeks. The average conceals the people at the edges.

Those people are numerous. The TUC, in analysis published in March 2026 and reported by HR Review, found that about 3.5 million workers, or 11.9 percent of the workforce, regularly do unpaid overtime, averaging 6.8 hours a week, with a combined value of £28.5 billion in 2025. That is roughly a free working day per week, donated by roughly one worker in eight. The Employment Rights Act 2025 is making the formal protections firmer: according to a summary from the Association of Optometrists, changes on flexible working and zero-hours contracts are expected during 2027, and the summary mentions no right to disconnect. In practice the protections reach the contract. They do not reach the culture, which asks whether you wish to be thought of as keen.

The culture, in practice, is one of understatement. Nobody will tell you to stay late; someone will remark that the deadline is tight and that they will probably be around. The British professional learns to read this the way a sailor reads weather. Pay level matters less than the type of job, as forum posters regularly note: a career with progression invites extra hours, while a job with none invites resentment. Public-sector staff in teaching and health are the most frequently cited examples of unpaid effort held together by goodwill. A separate Mumsnet thread from 2018 contains the classic complaint: a neighbour on a nine-to-six contract who rarely reaches home before half past eight because, as she put it, nobody only works their paid hours. Replies ranged from outrage to weary agreement, with bankers and accountants saying contracted hours are a formality.

## The Reckoning

Head to head, Ghana offers the weaker enforcement and the stronger family claim on your time, while the UK offers the stronger rules and the weaker excuse for ignoring them. Ghana's hours are mostly lost in transit and to a salary that does not stretch; the UK's are lost to a quiet expectation that is, in aggregate, worth £28.5 billion a year. In Ghana you can usually say what is wrong. In Britain you will be told that it is fine.

Hofstede Insights does not list Ghana separately in the compilation I could reach, which instead gives a West Africa estimate that includes it, so the comparison is a proxy. That composite scores 77 for power distance and 20 for individualism, against 35 and 89 for the UK. The gap explains much of the above: Ghana's office assumes that authority and community will make claims on you, while Britain's assumes that you are an individual who will be left alone, and that you will then offer the hours yourself.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Mumsnet — a 2023 thread asked at what salary unpaid overtime becomes acceptable. Replies split: some said extra hours are an investment in a career, others said any regular unpaid work is unfair, and one reported leaving a job that took 90 minutes of unpaid effort a day.*

> *Blind (adjacent: Europe versus the US, not Ghana) — commenters described European colleagues ending calls at 5pm and taking 25 to 30 days of leave, and one said Europeans also work hard but avoid weekend email, in contrast with the American expectation of constant availability.*

> *YEN.com.gh (press report built on a social-media video) — a young Accra corporate worker said his nominal nine-to-five stretched to 12 or 14 hours once early starts and late returns were counted, and that transport swallowed most of a GH¢3,000 net salary.*

> *Expat.com Ghana forum (adjacent: pay and permits, not hours) — a poster advised that high expatriate pay usually comes through UK employers, while local hires earn less and often lack housing or medical cover, and warned that employers often do not pay for work permits.*

## Conclusion

If you value legal clarity, a reliable holiday allowance and a weekly rhythm that mostly survives contact with the calendar, the UK is the better bet, provided you can resist the pull of the unpaid extra hour. If you take a Ghanaian posting, especially on a local contract, the hours worth fighting for are not the ones in the contract but the ones in transit. Ask about the commute before you ask about the salary.

Neither country has solved the problem of work expanding to fit the time available. One simply sends you home later, and the other sends you home on time and expects you to feel guilty about it.
