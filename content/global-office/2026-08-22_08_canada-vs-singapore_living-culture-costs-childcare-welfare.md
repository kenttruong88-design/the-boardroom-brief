# The Welfare State vs. the Savings Account: Pricing a Life in Canada and Singapore

🇨🇦 Canada · 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Canadian pays for healthcare gradually, through decades of income tax, and is still handed a bill at the dentist. A Singaporean pays for healthcare through a mandatory personal savings account that also, confusingly, helps buy an apartment. Both governments describe their arrangement as sensible. According to Numbeo's July 2026 index, a household needs roughly 31% more income in Singapore than in Toronto to sustain an identical lifestyle — and yet Singapore's fixed mortgage rates sit near 1.5% while Canada's prime rate holds at 4.45%, which means the two countries have found completely different ways to make housing feel unaffordable. This is the guide for people who want to know what it actually costs to live somewhere, not what it costs to visit.

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for provincial health coverage immediately and buy short-term private insurance for the gap — new arrivals can wait up to three months before public coverage kicks in | Assume "free healthcare" means free healthcare from day one |
| Get on a family doctor's waitlist the week you arrive, even if you feel fine | Wait until you're sick to start looking — some regions run multi-year GP waitlists |
| Check your specific province's progress toward $10-a-day child care before picking a city | Assume the national headline number applies to where you're actually moving |
| Budget separately for dental, vision, prescriptions and mental health care | Treat "universal healthcare" as a synonym for "all healthcare" |
| Compare rent control and tenant protection rules by province before signing a lease | Assume renter protections are the same in Alberta as in Ontario |
| Price in winter costs — tires, heating, a real coat — as part of relocation, not an afterthought | Underestimate what a Canadian winter does to a monthly budget |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget for private health insurance from day one — CPF and Medisave are closed to non-residents | Assume the city-state's famously efficient public healthcare system extends to your Employment Pass |
| Look at HDB flats, not just condos — they typically rent 20-40% below comparable condo units | Assume public housing is off-limits to foreigners; it isn't, with the right pass and landlord approval |
| Budget for a deposit, half a month's agent fee, and a month's rent in advance before you sign | Show up expecting the zero-deposit norms of some Western rental markets |
| Check childcare subsidy eligibility carefully — it's steeply means-tested and mostly built around citizens and PRs | Assume Employment Pass holders automatically get the subsidised rates citizens see |
| Confirm exactly what your employer's mandated S$60,000 minimum insurance policy covers | Assume "employer-provided insurance" means comprehensive insurance |
| Ask early about international school costs if you have school-age children | Discover the S$30,000–45,000-a-year price tag after you've already signed a lease |

### Canada: Universal, But Not All-Inclusive

Canada's healthcare system is genuinely free at the point of use for citizens and permanent residents, funded through general taxation rather than a fee-for-service model — a fact Canadians will mention unprompted, usually while also mentioning the wait. According to reporting on provincial systems, non-emergency specialist wait times in some provinces, such as Nova Scotia, can stretch past a year, and finding a family doctor at all has become its own small industry of waitlists and luck. Newcomers face a further wrinkle: provincial coverage can take up to three months to activate, leaving a gap that private insurers are only too happy to fill.

Beyond hospitals, the welfare architecture is broader but unevenly applied. Ottawa's flagship $10-a-day child care program, aimed at hitting its target by early 2026, has been met by only six of thirteen provinces and territories — Quebec, Manitoba, Saskatchewan, Nunavut, Prince Edward Island and Newfoundland and Labrador — while Ontario, British Columbia, Alberta, Nova Scotia and New Brunswick lag behind; families in Richmond, B.C., are reportedly still paying median infant fees near $39 a day, roughly four times the target. Canada's overall public social spending sits at about 19.3% of GDP, per OECD figures, comfortably mid-table among wealthy nations and well behind the roughly 30% seen in Austria, Finland and France. Housing hasn't helped the arithmetic: Statistics Canada's Q1 2026 rent data put average two-bedroom asking rents at $2,660 in Toronto and $3,100 in Vancouver, against a mortgage market where the prime rate has sat at 4.45% since late 2025.

### Singapore: The Compulsory Savings Account That Runs Your Life

Singapore doesn't really do welfare in the Canadian sense; it does compulsory self-funding, dressed up in acronyms. The Central Provident Fund pulls 7% to 9.5% of a citizen or permanent resident's monthly salary into personal accounts, one of which — Medisave — automatically covers most outpatient visits, surgeries and hospitalisations. It is efficient, and it is also entirely unavailable to foreigners, who cannot open a CPF account and must instead rely on private insurance; employers are legally required to provide a minimum of S$60,000 a year in inpatient coverage, which sounds generous until you've priced a serious illness.

Housing runs on a similarly two-tier logic. The HDB system — government-built flats housing the vast majority of citizens — keeps resale prices around a median of S$628,000 and mortgage rates as low as 1.45%–1.55%, an arrangement foreigners can rent into (a three-room flat runs roughly S$2,800–3,200 a month) but generally cannot buy into without permanent residency. Childcare follows the same script: fees at government-linked centres are capped around S$610–650 a month, with subsidies of up to S$710 a month for infant care stacked on top for lower-income households — a system that, per Singapore's Early Childhood Development Agency, tapers to zero above S$12,000 in household income and was never really built with an Employment Pass holder's children in mind. Add international school fees of S$30,000–45,000 a year, and the well-known headline of Singapore as the world's most expensive city, per the Economist Intelligence Unit, starts to look less like hyperbole and more like an invoice.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the pattern is one of timing rather than generosity: Canada spreads the bill across a lifetime of taxes and asks you to wait in a queue; Singapore hands you a lower sticker price up front — cheap mortgages, capped childcare fees — and then quietly excludes you from most of it unless you hold the right passport or PR card. Hofstede Insights scores Canada at 39 on power distance against Singapore's 74, and it shows up exactly where you'd expect: Canadians expect to be able to question a wait time, appeal a decision, write a strongly worded email to their MP; Singapore's systems are less interested in your feedback and more interested in your compliance, and — inconveniently for critics — they tend to run on time anyway.

The counterintuitive part is who actually saves money. A well-paid dual-income expat couple with no children often does better in Singapore, where low income tax (capped at 22%) offsets the rent. A family relying on the public system — healthcare, subsidised child care, tenant protections — is usually better served by Canada, even accounting for the wait times, simply because Canada's version of the safety net is designed to include you once you're a resident, while Singapore's is largely designed to exclude you until you're a citizen.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — An expat parent in Singapore described family housing running S$3,500–6,000 a month and international school fees from S$30,000 to S$45,000 a year, noting that an $8,000 monthly disposable income only feels comfortable when the employer covers housing.*

> *TeamBlind — A tech professional weighing a Singapore relocation offered a rule of thumb: roughly S$200,000 household income for a couple to live comfortably, with another S$100,000 needed per child.*

> *Quora — An American who relocated to Canada after decades on U.S. private insurance said they found a family doctor within weeks and received more preventive care in four years in Canada than in the previous twenty-five in the U.S. — a pleasant surprise after bracing for the horror stories.*

> *Quora — Another respondent pushed back on the "Canada has long healthcare waits" narrative as a cultural misread, pointing out that one person's family doctor wait was three days in one city and twenty-five in another — the variation is provincial and regional, not national.*

> *A Singapore expat rental forum thread warned newcomers that renting an HDB flat requires a landlord's subletting approval plus a deposit, half a month's agent fee, and a month's rent in advance — a detail that catches people who assumed public housing rentals would be simpler than condos, not more paperwork-heavy.*

### Conclusion

Neither system is more "generous" than the other; they've just decided to disguise the bill differently. Canada front-loads inclusion and back-loads the wait; Singapore front-loads the discount and back-loads the exclusion clause, unless you're prepared to eventually trade your passport for a red one. If you're moving with a family and expect to stay, run the numbers on Canada's public systems before you dismiss the wait times as a dealbreaker — they're real, but so is not getting a hospital bill. If you're moving solo or as a high-earning couple with no kids in the picture, Singapore's low taxes and cheap mortgages will likely outrun Canada's slower, steadier deal. Either way: read the fine print on your insurance before you need it, not after — the thing Priya would tell a friend over a drink is that "universal" and "everything's included" are not the same word, in either country.
