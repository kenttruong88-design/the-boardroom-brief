**Earned by the Year: Vacation Math in Taipei and Toronto**

🇹🇼 Taiwan · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Taiwan, a brand-new employee earns precisely zero paid leave for their first six months, three days after that, and doesn't cross into two full weeks until year three — a system, per Taiwan's Labor Standards Act, that treats vacation as something accrued through loyalty rather than granted on day one. In Canada, most provinces guarantee at least two weeks from the start, rising toward three after five years of service, and the country's own tourism industry runs an annual "Vacation Deprivation" report because Canadians, despite having the days, are famous for not using them. One country makes you wait to earn rest. The other hands it to you and then studies why you won't take it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇼 Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your exact tenure-based entitlement before assuming a flat "standard" vacation allowance | Expect two full weeks of leave in your first year — the statutory schedule starts much lower |
| Give plenty of advance notice and a clear handover plan before taking leave | Book time off last-minute, even if technically entitled — it disrupts team norms |
| Expect double pay if required to work a public holiday, per Taiwanese labor law | Assume a public holiday is automatically a day off — some sectors still require coverage |
| Watch senior colleagues' behavior around leave-taking as the real signal, not just the policy | Assume the written entitlement reflects what's culturally comfortable to actually use |
| Build tenure deliberately if long-term leave flexibility matters to you — entitlement compounds meaningfully after 5-10 years | Job-hop assuming your leave balance transfers — it resets with each new employer |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use your full statutory vacation allotment — it's yours by law, not a favor | Let unused vacation days quietly expire out of guilt or busyness |
| Check provincial variation — minimums differ (e.g., Saskatchewan mandates three weeks from year one) | Assume federal rules apply uniformly; employment law here is largely provincial |
| Give standard two-to-four-weeks' notice for extended leave requests | Assume last-minute time-off requests will be received the same way as planned ones |
| Expect genuine disconnection to be respected while you're away | Check email compulsively while on leave — colleagues will notice, and not favorably |
| Discuss unused-leave payout or carryover policy explicitly when you're hired | Assume unused days automatically roll over — policies vary by employer |

---

Taiwan's leave system, codified in the Labor Standards Act, is explicitly tenure-based: three days after six months, seven after one year, ten after two, fourteen after three, fifteen after five, and then one additional day per year of service up to a cap of thirty. Layered on top are fifteen paid public holidays, with double pay mandated if an employee is required to work one. The structure rewards loyalty in a very literal, arithmetic sense — but the more important cultural variable is usage, not entitlement. Taiwan's broader work culture, still shaped by long-hours norms common across East Asian corporate environments, means the written leave balance and the leave actually taken can diverge significantly; presenteeism (being visibly present regardless of productivity) remains a meaningful signal of commitment in many Taiwanese firms, and junior staff in particular often calibrate their vacation-taking to what senior colleagues visibly do, not just what policy allows.

Canada's system runs on the opposite logic: statutory minimums apply from day one (typically two weeks federally and in most provinces, with some provinces like Saskatchewan mandating three), and the entitlement isn't earned through years of service so much as guaranteed as a baseline right that grows modestly with tenure. And yet Canadians are chronically bad at using it — Expedia's own Vacation Deprivation research, run for years, consistently finds a meaningful share of Canadian workers leaving vacation days unused, driven less by cultural stigma than by workload anxiety and a nagging (often unfounded) sense that stepping away will create problems for colleagues. The entitlement is generous and untroubled by cultural guilt in principle; the follow-through is where the friction lives.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely interesting contrast isn't in what's written down — it's in *why* leave goes unused in each country. In Taiwan, underuse is largely structural and hierarchical: the entitlement itself starts low, and social pressure from a visibly present management culture discourages using even what's earned, especially early in a career. In Canada, underuse is largely psychological: the entitlement is generous from day one, legally protected, and yet workers self-police anyway, out of anxiety rather than any explicit hierarchical pressure. A Taiwanese employee moving to a Canadian company will likely be startled by how much leave is simply handed to them immediately — and equally startled by how many Canadian colleagues don't take it either, for reasons that have nothing to do with the boss watching.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A Taiwanese engineer who later worked at a Canadian subsidiary described his biggest shock as being explicitly asked by his new manager why he hadn't booked any vacation six months into the job — in his previous Taipei role, no manager had ever proactively raised the topic.*

> *r/AskACanadian — A worker described colleagues treating a coworker's fully-disconnected two-week vacation as completely unremarkable, while she personally still felt a low hum of guilt checking her phone anyway, something she chalked up to habit rather than any real workplace expectation.*

> *InterNations Taipei — A Canadian expat teaching in Taiwan noted that her contract technically granted paid leave, but that colleagues quietly signaled disapproval of anyone taking a full block during the school term, leading her to split her allotment into shorter, less conspicuous stretches instead.*

> *Blind — A tech worker who transferred from a Taipei office to a Canadian one wrote that the most disorienting adjustment wasn't the number of vacation days but the total absence of any implicit scorekeeping — no one tracked or commented on who took time off and when, a silence that took months to trust.*

> *r/expats — A Canadian who moved to Taiwan for a corporate role advised newcomers to ask directly, before accepting an offer, what the realistic (not contractual) vacation-taking norm was on their specific team, since the legal entitlement told her almost nothing about what was actually comfortable to use.*

### Conclusion

If your priority is maximum leave from day one, Canada's statutory floor is hard to beat — but be aware the culture won't force you to use it; that discipline is on you. If your priority is understanding how leave actually functions inside a hierarchy, Taiwan will teach you that the number on your contract is only the opening bid — the real entitlement is whatever your specific team culture will visibly tolerate. Either way, the practical advice is the same: ask a future colleague, not HR, how much vacation people actually take in their first year — the contract will tell you the law, but only a colleague will tell you the truth.
