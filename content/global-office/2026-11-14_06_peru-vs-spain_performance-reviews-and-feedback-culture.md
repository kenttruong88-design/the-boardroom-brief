# Peru Reviews You Once a Year and Tells You the Goals Beforehand; Spain Reviews You at Lunch, Loudly, and Then Buys You a Drink

🇵🇪 Peru · 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Spain, 54 percent of workers say they receive no performance evaluation at all, according to an InfoJobs survey; in Peru, 64 percent report a formal evaluation process, and 72 percent are assessed once a year, according to a Buk survey. The country with the process is not obviously the one with the feedback. Peruvian employees are formally reviewed and rarely consulted on their goals, while Spanish employees are frequently unreviewed and then criticised, to their face and at volume, over a coffee.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your manager early what your goals are and when the annual review falls | Wait to be consulted, since only 7 percent of surveyed employees help define their objectives |
| Address seniors by title and defer on formal decisions | Challenge a superior publicly in a meeting |
| Read indirect language carefully and ask a follow-up question in private | Treat a vague "we will see" as agreement or approval |
| Document your results ahead of the review cycle | Expect continuous coaching between reviews |
| Learn business Spanish well enough to catch tone | Rely on English to decode an evaluation |
| Ask what the rating means for pay and promotion | Assume a good rating is also a raise |

### In Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat blunt comments as being about the work, not about you | Take a direct critique of a slide as a verdict on your character |
| Acknowledge a point briefly, then propose a concrete fix | Argue defensively, which is read as weakness |
| Ask for a specific example when feedback is vague | Nod along to feedback you did not understand |
| Request a formal review yourself if none is scheduled | Assume silence means you are being quietly assessed |
| Revisit difficult feedback over a drink after work | Expect the discussion to stay private or to stay awkward |
| Ask your manager in advance how you will be assessed | Assume the criteria are written down, since only 36 percent of evaluated workers know them in advance |

## Peru

Peru has the paperwork. According to a January 2026 Buk survey of Peruvian employees, 64 percent work for an organisation with a formal evaluation process, 72 percent are assessed once a year, 10 percent every six months and 5 percent quarterly. Some 74 percent agree that evaluation helped them improve. Yet 62 percent receive their goals from the company or their manager without much consultation, only 7 percent help define them, and just 34 percent consider the system both fair and transparent. A further 34 percent find it clear but not objective enough, 13 percent call it confusing and irrelevant to their role, and 20 percent cannot see the process at all, either because none exists or because nobody told them.

The culture around the form is hierarchical. Expat Arrivals describes Peruvian workplaces as hierarchical, with respect based on age, status, education and title, and communication that is indirect and conflict-averse. Hofstede Insights scores Peru at 64 for power distance and 87 for uncertainty avoidance, which is the academic way of saying that written procedure is reassuring and that the boss decides. Cornerstone Talent Insights 2026, reported by Revista Economia, found 37 percent of Peruvian HR leaders naming poor communication as a main obstacle to results, and Cornerstone's Peru head observed that many companies still associate feedback mainly with the moment a rating is delivered. She added that firms treating feedback as culture, not a scheduled meeting, tend to use 360-degree input from peers and direct reports, and that good feedback should also name what the employee did well. The same report found 66 percent of Peruvian HR professionals had joined a leadership development programme in the past year, so the intention exists; the habit is still being installed.

The consequence, as Gestion reported in its coverage of Harvard Business School research, is that managers often soften negative reviews to avoid retaliation, which can be subtle: gossip, slower work, curt replies. In a country where the review arrives annually and criticism travels indirectly, the foreign employee should assume that the rating is a summary of conversations that did not happen. Gallup's engagement data, for context, put Peru at 25 percent engaged against a global average of 20 percent, with 12 percent actively disengaged.

## Spain

Spain has the opposite arrangement: a thin process and a thick tongue. The InfoJobs survey, reported by Corresponsables, found 54 percent of workers receive no performance evaluation, rising to 62 percent among those over 55. Of the 46 percent who are evaluated, 73 percent are assessed every six months to a year and a half, usually by their direct manager, 45 percent hear mainly about areas for improvement, and just 15 percent discuss pay. Only 36 percent know in advance what they will be assessed on. Even so, 82 percent say they feel professionally recognised, half of them by colleagues rather than managers, and only 21 percent say that recognition shows up in their salary. Younger workers are the most likely to be reviewed, at 55 percent for those aged 16 to 24, while only 41 percent of those with more than five years at a firm are evaluated at all. Tenure, it seems, is its own review.

Feedback, when it comes, is direct. Spain Handbook describes a culture that values sincerity over politeness, where wrapping criticism in compliments can look false, where criticism targets the work and not the person, and where silence from a manager can mean expectations are being met. The author recounts a Barcelona manager listing errors in front of three colleagues, after which the tension cleared and conversation turned to personal topics. Hofstede Insights puts Spain at 57 for power distance, 51 for individualism and 86 for uncertainty avoidance, a mix of hierarchy and personal relationships that explains why the bluntness is rarely meant as hostility.

Engagement is the awkward footnote. According to Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2026, only 10 percent of Spanish employees are engaged, against 12 percent in Europe and 20 percent worldwide, while 15 percent are actively disengaged and 24 percent report daily anger, against 15 percent across Europe. Gallup measures engagement, not review quality, so no causal claim follows. It does suggest that the warm tone around the bluntness has limits.

## The Reckoning

Peru reviews formally and criticises indirectly; Spain reviews sparsely and criticises directly. A newcomer to Lima should expect a calendar, a form, a rating and very little narrative, with the useful information delivered in private and in hints. A newcomer to Madrid or Barcelona should expect almost no form, ample narrative and a possible audience. Spain Handbook adds that short, blunt messages on WhatsApp are a standard work channel and are usually not meant as anger, a useful fact to hold at eleven at night. Peru punishes the employee who waits to be told; Spain punishes the one who takes it personally.

Hofstede Insights scores both countries near the top for uncertainty avoidance, 87 for Peru and 86 for Spain, yet they diverge on individualism, 16 against 51. That gap is the more instructive one. In Peru, feedback runs through the hierarchy and the group; in Spain, it runs between individuals who expect to argue and make up in the same hour. Neither system is warmer. They simply place the discomfort at different points in the year.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com (Peru forum, Spanish-language) — A Venezuelan poster described a 48-hour week, unpaid overtime and many bosses who treated staff as slaves rather than collaborators, with coworkers quitting worn down. The poster left jobs early and now works independently, and did not mention formal reviews at all.*

> *The Doe — A Cusco resident wrote that many expats avoid working for Peruvian companies and start their own businesses instead, and described light-skinned Peruvians barking orders at darker-skinned workers in restaurants and hotels, a picture of hierarchy that no appraisal form captures.*

> *Reddit r/Lima_Peru — The sole commenter in a thread on foreigners living in Peru advised learning Spanish first, joking that the advice could be skipped only by someone who never leaves Barranco and Miraflores.*

> *Expat.com (Spain forum) — A Dutch poster in Barcelona found too much hierarchy and bureaucracy in multinationals and felt that being liked impressed more than a CV, while a French poster said it depends, noting longer days and a slower pace but valuing the priority given to family and friends.*

> *Spain Handbook — A writer in a Barcelona office watched a manager list errors in front of three colleagues, then saw the matter dissolve into personal chat, and concluded that blunt feedback and warmth coexist without apology.*

## Conclusion

Before accepting a job in either country, ask for the review calendar, the criteria and the name of the person who will actually give you feedback. In Peru, build a relationship with your manager early and seek private conversations, because the annual rating will not carry the nuance. Gallup's data suggest neither market is awash in engaged staff, so a manager who bothers to give feedback of any kind is an asset worth cultivating, and a colleague who offers it informally may be worth more than the formal process. In Spain, volunteer for a direct conversation, hold your composure and treat the drink afterwards as part of the process, not a reward for surviving it.

The honest summary is that Peru will tell you everything once a year and Spain will tell you everything on a Tuesday. Choose whichever sort of surprise you handle better.
