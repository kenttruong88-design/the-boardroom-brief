**India Will Give You the Leave. Greece Will Give You the Entire Country, Closed.**

**🇮🇳 India · 🇬🇷 Greece**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In one country, the leave balance sits in your HR portal mostly untouched, like a gym membership. In the other, roughly a million people leave the capital at once every August, and the city itself seems to agree it's closed. Both approaches are, on paper, compliant with the idea of a vacation. Only one of them is actually taken.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Actually book your leave in advance and tell your manager why — a stated plan is harder to quietly erode than an open-ended "maybe" | Let unused leave accumulate as a point of quiet pride; most of it will simply expire or get paid out at a fraction of its value |
| Negotiate your leave policy explicitly when you join — comparing offers on PTO, not just salary, is increasingly normal | Assume every employer's "generous" leave is meaningful without asking whether weekends and holidays eat into it |
| Hand off your responsibilities clearly before you go, in writing, to someone specific | Go quiet without a handover and hope nobody notices you're gone — they will, and it won't read as trust |
| Say plainly that you're taking the day fully off | Answer "urgent" Slack messages from the beach; it resets everyone's expectation of what your leave actually means |
| Ask what the real norm is beyond the written policy — written and lived leave culture often diverge sharply | Take the offer letter's leave number at face value as a prediction of what you'll actually use |

#### 🇬🇷 Greece

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to be mostly alone in the office — or the city — for stretches of August | Schedule anything client-facing or decision-dependent for mid-August without a serious contingency plan |
| Plan around the Dormition of the Virgin Mary on August 15, the country's biggest holiday after Easter | Assume every business, especially family-run shops, will be open as normal that week |
| Use your statutory leave fully — 20 days minimum on a five-day week, rising with tenure — it's treated as a right, not a favor | Try to "save face" by skipping your full entitlement; colleagues will find it odd, not admirable |
| Check restaurant and shop hours ahead of a summer visit to any neighborhood outside the tourist core | Expect Plaka-level opening hours in a residential Athens suburb in the first half of August |
| Build real slack into project timelines that cross July–August | Promise an August delivery date without checking who, specifically, will still be in the building |

The numbers on India's side are almost comic in their consistency. A 2018 Expedia vacation-deprivation survey, cited repeatedly in Indian HR press, found 75% of Indian respondents felt vacation-deprived — the highest share of any country surveyed, ahead of South Korea (72%) and Hong Kong (69%). Separately, 53% said they take fewer vacation days than they're entitled to, and India ranked third globally for employees who feel guilty about taking time off at all. A Kimble Applications study found 14% of respondents genuinely believed that *not* using all their vacation days improved their chances of promotion — a belief that, whether or not it's actually true at any given company, is doing real work shaping behavior.

The reasons employees give for leaving leave unused read less like culture and more like structural strain: 35% say their schedule or understaffing doesn't allow it, a third struggle to coordinate time off with a travel companion, a quarter worry about missing important decisions while away, and nearly a fifth believe successful people simply don't take vacation days. None of this means Indian employers formally withhold leave — most policies look perfectly reasonable on paper. It means the gap between written entitlement and lived practice is where the real culture lives, and it's a wide gap.

Greece inverts that gap almost completely. Statutory annual leave starts at 20 days on a five-day week (24 on a six-day week), rising with tenure to 25 or 30 days for longer-serving employees — generous by most global standards and firmly protected by law. The lived practice matches the paper entitlement to a degree India's doesn't: every August, roughly a million people leave the greater Athens area, out of a metro population of about 3.6 million, in what's commonly called the "August exodus." Family-owned shops hang handwritten closed signs, often timed around August 15 — the Dormition of the Virgin Mary, the country's largest holiday after Orthodox Easter — while malls, hotels, and tourist-district businesses mostly carry on. It's worth noting the exodus has shrunk since the 2010s debt crisis; closures that once ran most of the month now often last a week or two. The instinct, though, clearly survived the economics.

### The Reckoning

Here's the genuinely strange part: India's leave policies are frequently more generous on paper than people assume, and Greece's are protected by law in a way few countries can match — yet the emotional experience of taking time off runs in opposite directions. An Indian employee with 24 paid days a year can feel, per the surveys above, guiltier using five of them than a Greek employee legally guaranteed 20 feels using all twenty. The difference isn't entitlement. It's whether the whole system — not just your own manager — backs you up when you're gone.

That's the real export opportunity for anyone managing across both markets: Greece's "everyone's basically out, so nothing moves" model gives individual leave-takers social cover that India's "leave exists, but good luck" model simply doesn't provide. A Greek employee skipping August isn't virtuous; they're just out of step with everyone else's absence. An Indian employee taking a full two-week block has to generate that cover alone, which is a much harder thing to do solo.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — On a thread comparing PTO across Indian tech and finance employers, one Amazon engineer, having fielded offers, found HDFC Bank's leave looked generous on paper but qualified it fast: "Otherwise this is not really meaningful," they wrote, once weekend overlap and combined leave categories were accounted for — a reminder that headline leave numbers in India routinely need unpacking before they mean anything.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — Another commenter on the same thread admitted they'd never benchmarked their own company's leave against the market at all: "just took it for granted" was how they put it, after realizing their package was unusually good only once colleagues elsewhere started comparing notes.*

> *Fishbowl (professional community app) — Asked point-blank whether anyone else feels guilty taking vacation days during a busy stretch, several professional commenters pushed back hard against the premise itself, with one flatly answering "No" and another noting the guilt only makes sense if you knew in advance the timing would clash with a crunch.*

> *tovima.com (Greek news outlet, reporting lived practice rather than law) — Its coverage of the August exodus notes that the tradition traces back to city dwellers with village roots returning home for the August 15 holiday, and that even now, "many Athenians still leave for islands or family villages" despite the shortened modern closures — a habit that outlasted the economic conditions that once made it near-universal.*

> *HR Katha / People Matters (Indian HR trade press, aggregating survey respondents) — Coverage of the underlying leave-deprivation research repeatedly surfaces the same quiet admission from Indian professionals: taking leave isn't read as a right being exercised, but as a risk being managed, which is why so much of it simply goes unused rather than formally denied.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing based on how recovery actually works, ask which failure mode you can live with: leave that exists on paper but requires real social courage to use, or leave that's compulsory enough that an entire capital city empties out around you. Greece will occasionally cost you a closed pharmacy. India will occasionally cost you the vacation itself.

What I'd tell a friend taking either job: in Greece, take the twenty days — nobody will think less of you, because everyone else is already gone. In India, take the leave you're owed and actually leave your phone in the other room; the system won't hand you the cover Greece gives for free, so you'll have to build it yourself, one firmly worded out-of-office at a time.
