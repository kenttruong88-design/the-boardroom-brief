**Sink or Swim vs. Sign Here: Getting Onboarded in America and Switzerland**

**🇺🇸 USA · 🇨🇭 Switzerland**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

American onboarding hands you a laptop, a Slack invite, and a vague sense that you're now expected to figure the rest out by Thursday. Swiss onboarding hands you a binder, a formally assigned mentor, and a multi-week integration plan that assumes competence is earned, not assumed. One system optimizes for speed. The other optimizes for not embarrassing anyone, including you.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask questions early and often — hesitation reads as low initiative, not humility | Wait for a structured training plan to appear; often there isn't one |
| Introduce yourself proactively to teams outside your immediate reporting line | Assume your manager will check in regularly without prompting |
| Treat your first 90 days as an audition, not a settling-in period | Expect detailed, written processes — a lot of institutional knowledge lives in people's heads |
| Use your onboarding buddy, if assigned, as your primary source of unwritten rules | Mistake friendliness in meetings for a long-term professional bond |
| Get comfortable being visibly, publicly new — nobody will hold your hand | Take a slow ramp-up personally; the system is built for people who self-start |

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a formally assigned mentor and a structured, multi-week integration plan | Rush relationship-building — trust in Swiss offices accrues slowly, not on day one |
| Master basic greeting etiquette — handshakes, titles, correct forms of address | Bring up politics, religion, salary, or other divisive topics in casual chat |
| Show up on time, every time — punctuality reads as basic competence | Interpret initial reserve as unfriendliness; it typically warms with consistency |
| Let your credentials and demonstrated competence do the talking | Show off or oversell your background — it reads as a red flag, not confidence |
| Learn the local language basics, even in English-speaking teams | Assume integration support is optional — many firms now build it in deliberately |

The American approach to onboarding is a case study in individualism dressed up as efficiency. Formal buddy programs exist and work — Microsoft's internal data found new hires who met with an assigned buddy eight or more times in their first 90 days ramped up productivity noticeably faster, and buddy-paired hires reported 23 percent higher onboarding satisfaction than those without one. But the buddy system is the exception dressed as the rule. In much of American tech in particular, structured on-the-job training is treated with mild suspicion — workers on the forum Blind have described a persistent industry norm where deliberate training gets dismissed internally as "handholding," leaving new hires to reverse-engineer process from context, Slack history, and trial and error.

Formal HR literature backs this pattern from the other direction: guidance for U.S. employers stresses that the real onboarding window extends through the first 90 days, and that new hires who report understanding "how we do things around here" are nearly five times more likely to rate their onboarding positively — implying that a lot of American companies still aren't reliably delivering that clarity up front. It is a high-trust, high-autonomy system that rewards people who ask good questions fast and quietly penalizes people who wait to be told.

Switzerland runs the opposite playbook, and it isn't cultural accident — it's downstream of an economy built on a formal apprenticeship tradition. Nearly two-thirds of Swiss teenagers combine school and paid workplace training through the country's vocational education system, which means most Swiss professionals were onboarded, in a formal sense, before they turned 20. That expectation of structured mentorship carries into white-collar hiring: new employees typically get a designated onboarding mentor for scheduled 1:1 check-ins specifically designed to de-stress the early weeks, on top of a broader integration plan. The Local Switzerland's own reporting on office culture notes that Swiss workplace chatter is markedly less laidback than in comparable countries, greetings are a small but real skill to master, and topics like politics, religion, and finances are best avoided in casual conversation — reserve isn't coldness, it's a default setting that takes time to earn past.

**The Reckoning.** The two systems are mirror images built from different assumptions about what new hires deserve to know versus what they should discover themselves. Switzerland's uncertainty avoidance score sits in the middle of the global range, but its onboarding behavior reads far more structured than that number alone suggests — a legacy of the apprenticeship system more than a Hofstede artifact. America, by contrast, scores among the most individualist cultures measured, and it shows precisely in the gap between its onboarding rhetoric (buddy systems, welcome packets, 90-day plans) and its onboarding reality, where a new hire's success is quietly treated as their own responsibility to engineer. The irony is that Swiss reserve, which reads as cold on arrival, is actually the more structurally supportive environment for a nervous new hire — while American warmth, all handshakes and "let's grab coffee," can mask a workplace that expects you to have already figured most of it out.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who relocated to Switzerland for work described the culture as formal and initially cold, noting that Swiss colleagues value mutual respect and privacy highly enough that casual encounters rarely turn into lasting friendships — a contrast to how quickly American coworkers seem to bond, even if that bond often doesn't outlast the job.*

> *The Local Switzerland — A guide to Swiss office etiquette for newcomers flagged something most arrivals miss entirely: getting the greeting right — handshake, correct title, right level of formality — functions as an unspoken competence test in the first weeks, well before anyone evaluates your actual work.*

> *TeamBlind — A widely discussed thread on why American tech companies skip formal on-the-job training argued that structured onboarding gets stigmatized internally as "handholding," leaving new hires to piece together process through Slack archaeology and trial and error rather than documentation.*

> *Substack (sethsivak.substack.com) — A piece on onboarding buddy systems in U.S. companies made the case that assigning a peer contact isn't just a nice-to-have but a measurable retention lever, given how much new-hire anxiety comes from not knowing who's safe to ask a "dumb" question.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving from the U.S. to Switzerland, the adjustment is mostly patience: let the structure work for you, don't rush intimacy, and treat the mentor relationship as genuinely useful rather than a formality to tolerate. If you're moving the other way, the adjustment is initiative: nobody is going to hand you a plan, so build your own, and don't wait for permission to ask the question that's actually bothering you.

The honest version, for a friend: America will make you feel welcome on day one and forget to teach you anything. Switzerland will barely smile at you on day one and then spend six weeks making sure you actually know what you're doing. Pick your discomfort.
