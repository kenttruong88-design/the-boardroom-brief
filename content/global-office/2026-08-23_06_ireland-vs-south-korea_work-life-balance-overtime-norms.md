# When Does the Workday End? Ireland Says Five O'Clock, South Korea Says It's Complicated

🇮🇪 Ireland · 🇰🇷 South Korea

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Q3 2025, the average Irish employee logged 32.9 paid hours a week, according to the Central Statistics Office — a figure a Korean office worker might file under "generous lunch break." South Korea, meanwhile, worked 1,833 hours across all of 2025 per Statistics Korea, a genuine improvement on the previous year and still comfortably ahead of the OECD average of roughly 1,736 hours, despite a national law explicitly designed to stop this from happening. Two countries, two governments that have each legislated a cap on working hours, and two entirely different relationships between the law on the books and the light still on in the office.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your contracted hours confirmed in writing during onboarding — the legal ceiling is 48 hours/week averaged over four months, but real life runs closer to 35-40 | Assume being last to leave signals commitment; it more often reads as an inability to manage your day |
| Book your full statutory leave — a minimum of four weeks plus 10 public holidays — early, before team calendars fill up | Skip the small talk before a meeting; launching straight into the agenda comes across as brusque |
| Treat hybrid and flexible-hours policies as genuinely operative, not decorative | Expect visible overtime to move the needle at review time — Irish managers weigh delivery, not desk time |
| Use tea/coffee breaks for the informal conversations where actual decisions often get made | Take "we should grab a pint sometime" as a scheduling commitment, but don't dismiss it as meaningless either |
| Ask directly about sick leave and remote-work norms in week one — entitlements are strong but underexplained | Panic if your manager is unreachable after 5:30pm; escalate the next morning, not by text that evening |

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clarify in your first week how overtime is tracked and paid — the legal cap is 52 hours (40 regular plus 12 overtime), but enforcement varies sharply by sector | Leave before your manager on your first few weeks, even with your own work finished — read the room before setting a precedent |
| Ask quietly what your team's actual working hours are, distinct from the official policy | Assume "you must be busy in the evenings since you leave so early" is a compliment — it usually isn't |
| Learn to decline a hoesik (company dinner) gracefully when you need to; attendance pressure is real but easing among younger staff | Discuss salary or overtime frustrations openly with Korean colleagues in your first months |
| Watch how senior colleagues frame requests before mirroring the behaviour yourself | Mistake the 52-hour cap for a description of reality rather than a ceiling still being tested |
| Budget social capital for nunchi — reading unspoken cues is treated as a professional skill, not an optional nicety | Treat a semiconductor-sector 64-hour exemption scheme as representative of office life generally — it isn't, yet |

## Ireland

Ireland's statutory framework is unglamorous but effective. The Organisation of Working Time Act, 1997 caps working hours at 48 per week, averaged over a four-month reference period, and guarantees a minimum of four weeks' paid annual leave on top of ten public holidays — entitlements an employer cannot simply pay out in lieu while someone remains employed. In practice, actual hours run well under the ceiling: CSO figures put average weekly paid hours at 32.9 in Q3 2025, a number that reflects both a services-heavy economy and a workforce with little patience for presenteeism.

The country's role as European headquarters for a large share of American technology and pharmaceutical firms has imported some intensity — expect longer hours and higher expectations at a US multinational's Dublin campus than at a domestic firm — but Irish workplace norms have proven sticky rather than porous. Multiple expat accounts describe managers who are, per one Dublin work-culture guide, "extremely protective of their time off" and routinely out of the office by 4pm, with tea breaks scattered through the day and lunch treated as an actual break rather than a desk-bound formality. The five minutes of small talk before any meeting is not filler; skipping it is read as a minor social offence.

None of this makes Ireland a work-life utopia. Research on hospital doctors, published via the National Institutes of Health's PMC archive, documents chronic work-life imbalance normalised within Irish hospital medicine — a reminder that statutory caps and cultural norms apply unevenly across sectors, and that healthcare, as usual, is where the exceptions live.

## South Korea

South Korea's 52-hour workweek law, introduced in 2018 and phased in across company sizes since, was a deliberate legislative attempt to break one of the OECD's most persistent working-hours habits. It caps the standard week at 40 hours plus a maximum of 12 hours' overtime — a hard ceiling, at least on paper. Statistics Korea's 2025 figures show the policy working, slowly: 1,833 annual hours, down from 1,865 in 2024, though still well above the OECD average and a long way from the roughly 2,124 hours Korea logged in the years before the law took effect. A new special exemption scheme, introduced in 2025 and aimed at semiconductor R&D workers facing surges in demand, permits up to 64 hours a week for three months and 60 for a further three — a carve-out already generating debate, per the Korea Herald, over how far it should extend.

The gap between the legal cap and the felt experience of office life is where things get interesting. Nunchi — the skill of reading a room's unspoken expectations — functions in Korean offices as something closer to a job requirement than a personality trait, and staying visibly late remains, for many employees, a low-risk hedge against being seen as uncommitted. Hoesik, the after-work company dinner often built around multiple rounds of drinking, has historically served the same signalling function socially that long hours serve professionally; attendance was rarely mandatory in writing and frequently mandatory in practice. The Korea Herald and others report this culture visibly loosening among younger workers, who increasingly decline both the dinners and the late nights, with mixed but real success.

## The Reckoning

Put side by side, the two countries invert the relationship between law and behaviour. Ireland's working-time law is a comparatively loose 48-hour ceiling, rarely approached, enforced mostly by a culture that has already internalised something stricter. South Korea's law is a tighter, more specific 52-hour cap, actively tested, negotiated around, and — per a presidential advisory body's 2026 recommendation to loosen it further for strategic sectors — currently under political pressure to expand rather than tighten. Ireland regulates lightly and behaves conservatively; Korea regulates tightly and behaves ambitiously. Neither pattern is obviously superior, but only one of them requires a law reminding people to stop working.

The more counterintuitive finding is where each country's outliers live. Ireland's overwork problem is concentrated and sector-specific — hospital medicine being the clearest example — rather than systemic. South Korea's is the reverse: comparatively mild at the legal margin, structurally embedded everywhere else, and dependent on generational turnover rather than legislation to actually shift the average. Anyone assuming Korea's 52-hour cap functions the way a 52-hour cap sounds like it should function is in for a recalibration.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — a tech worker comparing conditions across Germany, Austria, France, Switzerland, the UK, the Netherlands, and Ireland noted that Irish work-life balance for tech roles was genuinely strong by international standards, but flagged that base salaries lag the equivalent US or even Dutch roles enough to offset some of the lifestyle gain.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — a Meta veteran leaving the Dublin office described the pay as solid for an experienced IC but was explicit that the reason for moving on was better work-life balance and a less toxic team environment, not compensation.*

> *Quora — in a thread asking whether the "Koreans work until midnight" stereotype is true, several respondents said it varies enormously by company and generation: chaebol-adjacent and client-facing roles still run late regularly, while younger-skewing firms and public-sector jobs increasingly hold to something close to a real 9-to-6.*

> *Expat.com (Dublin work culture guide) — a contributor described Irish managers as initially reading as "lax and laid back" via informal email tone and low urgency, an impression that shifted once it became clear the flexibility was genuine rather than a sign of disorganisation.*

> *Dev Korea (nunchi guide for foreign professionals) — the advice given to incoming employees was to ask two separate questions in any new team: what the written policy says, and how the team actually operates day to day — treating the first as protection and the second as the real onboarding.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway for anyone weighing these two postings is less about which country works you harder on paper and more about which gap you'd rather manage — Ireland's between a loose law and a stricter culture, or Korea's between a strict law and a culture still catching up to it. Ireland rewards people who take its flexibility at face value; Korea rewards people who read the room before testing the cap. On Hofstede Insights' comparison, Ireland scores 24 on long-term orientation against South Korea's 100 — a fifty-year planning horizon versus a next-quarter one — and that gap alone explains more about overtime norms than any statute does.

Choose Ireland if you want the law to roughly describe your life; choose South Korea if you're prepared to treat the law as a floor negotiations happen above. Either way, read the actual team's hours before you read the employee handbook — the handbook, in both countries, is aspirational fiction with better formatting.
