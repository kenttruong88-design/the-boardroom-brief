**Two Hours for Lunch, Zero Minutes for Small Talk: An Office-Rituals Guide to Ecuador and Belgium**

**🇪🇨 Ecuador · 🇧🇪 Belgium**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Ecuador, lunch is a legally and culturally protected institution that can stretch toward two unhurried hours, often with family. In Belgium, a Stepstone survey of 5,000 workers found the average employee takes just 22 minutes and 20 seconds of a roughly 40-minute entitlement, and still manages to feel obligated about even that. Both countries will tell you, sincerely, that they value their colleagues. Only one of them will actually let you eat with them.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇪🇨 Ecuador

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat the midday meal as a real social and family event, not a quick refuel — it can run well over an hour | Schedule back-to-back calls over lunch; colleagues may genuinely be unreachable during this window |
| Greet everyone individually, with warmth, when entering a room — skipping this reads as cold | Expect punctuality to be treated with Belgian-grade rigidity — flexible, relationship-first timing is the norm |
| Invest time in small talk — family, weekend, local news — before any business agenda | Rush straight into negotiations or asks; relationship-building isn't a preamble, it's the actual work |
| Accept invitations to colleagues' homes or family gatherings when offered — they're genuine, not performative | Assume a lower-key or budget local business location means a company isn't serious — competition and positioning work differently here |
| Dress and present formally in business settings, even in casual-seeming workplaces | Underestimate how much decisions depend on personal trust built outside the meeting room |
| Expect hierarchy to matter — defer visibly to senior figures in group settings | Expect it to always translate into distance personally — many Ecuadorian bosses are warm, not just formal |

#### 🇧🇪 Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to the occasional "vrijmibo"-style Friday drink, especially early in a new job — about a quarter of attendees go partly because it's socially expected | Expect to be close with your manager outside work — only around 17% of Belgian employees say they're actually friends with theirs |
| Keep lunch efficient and often solo or desk-based — most colleagues genuinely do this too | Assume a short lunch is a personal failing; 58% of Belgian workers skip their full break, mostly citing workload |
| Separate French-speaking and Flemish-speaking office norms in your head — survey data shows Francophone offices report more pressure to skip lunch than Flemish ones | Treat a colleague's weekend time as automatically available; only about 4 in 10 Belgians see colleagues outside work at all |
| Expect formality and some real distance between managers and staff as the default, not a personal slight | Push for American-style instant workplace friendliness — trust here builds slowly and through repetition |
| Bring reasonably good food or drink if you host an office gathering — Belgian colleagues notice and judge the spread | Serve a predictable, uninspired snack table and expect no comment — Belgians will quietly note the room for improvement |
| Use the lunch-and-coffee-break rhythm that does exist, brief as it is, to build rapport incrementally | Mistake Belgian reserve for dislike — it's a cultural baseline, not a personal read on you |

### Ecuador

Hofstede Insights scores Ecuador at a striking 8 on individualism — one of the most collectivist scores recorded anywhere — paired with a power-distance score of 78, among the highest in Latin America. Together these numbers describe a workplace where decisions flow from clearly deferred-to authority, but where the social fabric around those decisions is dense, personal, and relationship-first rather than transactional. BizLatinHub's guidance for foreign businesses operating in Ecuador puts this plainly: meetings routinely open with extended personal conversation, punctuality is treated with real flexibility, and rushing straight to business is read as presumptuous rather than efficient.

The lunch ritual is where this becomes concrete. Ecuadorian labor norms and widely cited local custom treat the midday meal as a genuine, often family-oriented pause rather than a desk-bound formality, frequently extending well past an hour, especially outside the largest cities. One Expat.com forum contributor who settled in the small town of Vilcabamba, working as a graphic designer and music teacher, described local work culture as varying considerably person to person — some colleagues and clients warm, others notably cold, with most falling somewhere in between — and noted that in a market with less competition, some businesses visibly invest less effort in customer relationships than others. The takeaway for an incoming professional: Ecuador's social rituals are real and important, but like the hierarchy itself, they are personal and specific rather than uniform — the culture sets the stage, individual relationships still do the real work.

### Belgium

Belgium's Hofstede profile reads almost like a formality machine: 94 on uncertainty avoidance, among the highest scores Hofstede records globally, alongside 82 on long-term orientation and a comparatively high 65 on power distance for a Western European country. The result, per a PageGroup survey of 250 professionals, is a workplace where colleague relationships are valued in principle — 99% call them important — but bounded carefully in practice: only 56% of employees have any contact with colleagues outside office hours at all, and just 38% have any contact with their direct manager outside work. A PageGroup-cited finding notes plainly that "an amount of distance remains deeply rooted in the relationships between managers and their employees."

Lunch itself has become a casualty of this same restraint, oddly, despite — or because of — Belgium's formal entitlements. The Stepstone survey reported by VRT NWS found 58% of Belgian workers skip their full legal lunch break, averaging only 22 minutes and 20 seconds against a roughly 40-minute entitlement, costing an estimated €44,000 in lost value across a career if measured in forgone personal time; 74% blame workload, and 71% of those who shorten their break eat at their desk. A consulting-firm analysis from Haystack, comparing Belgian and Dutch after-work habits, found Belgian colleagues notably more likely to gather for a drink than their Dutch counterparts — genuinely "Burgundian," in the authors' words — even though, per PageGroup, roughly a quarter attend mainly because it's expected rather than purely wanted, and even the Belgian spread of snacks and drinks, the same authors noted dryly, usually leaves "room for improvement."

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't warmth versus coldness — both cultures report caring about their colleagues — it's where each culture chooses to spend its social capital. Ecuador spends it loudly and visibly, in the two-hour lunch, the personal greeting, the family invitation; Belgium spends it narrowly and specifically, in the occasional, semi-obligatory Friday drink, while guarding the actual lunch hour as something closer to a private, often skipped luxury. Hofstede's numbers suggest why: Ecuador's low individualism pushes relationship-building into the center of daily work life, while Belgium's extreme uncertainty avoidance pushes spontaneous socializing into a scheduled, bounded ritual that feels safer precisely because it's predictable.

For an incoming hire, the practical trap is assuming the two cultures' rituals are interchangeable. Treating Ecuadorian lunch as a quick American-style break will read as rude and rushed; treating Belgian after-work drinks as the main venue for relationship-building, the way Ecuador's lunch functions, will land you in the 25% who show up mostly out of obligation and leave having learned very little about anyone. The ritual that matters most is different in each place, and it's rarely the one a relocation guide mentions first.

There's also a quieter lesson in what each culture has chosen to protect versus sacrifice. Ecuador has kept the ritual that costs the most time — the long, unhurried lunch — precisely because the culture treats relationship-building as inseparable from getting anything done at all. Belgium, despite genuinely valuing colleague relationships by its own survey data, has let its lunch hour erode under workload pressure while keeping the comparatively cheap, scheduled Friday drink intact — a choice that looks, from outside, like prioritizing the appearance of sociability over the substance of it, even if that's not how any individual Belgian employee would describe their own Friday evening.

An incoming hire who actually wants to build real relationships, rather than simply observe correct etiquette, should take the hint embedded in each country's own trade-off. In Ecuador, the lunch table is where influence, trust, and information genuinely move, so skipping it to "save time" for emails is a false economy. In Belgium, the office itself is a less reliable venue for relationship-building than its own survey data on colleague-contact outside work hours would suggest, which means a newcomer who wants Belgian colleagues to actually warm up may need patience measured in months, not the single enthusiastic Friday drink a guidebook might promise.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com Ecuador forum — A contributor who relocated to the small town of Vilcabamba to work as a graphic designer and music teacher wrote that "the work culture varies a lot" from colleague to colleague and business to business, describing some as genuinely warm and others as notably cold, with most falling somewhere in between — a reminder that Ecuador's relationship-first norms don't guarantee uniform warmth.*

> *Haystack Consulting (workplace-culture blog) — Dutch consultants working with Belgian colleagues wrote that they expected Belgians to be more sociable after work than their Dutch counterparts — "more Burgundian," as they put it — and found their hunch confirmed, with Belgian after-work drinks significantly more common than equivalent Dutch gatherings, though they judged the actual food and drink on offer to have "room for improvement."*

### Conclusion

If office rituals are the thing you're trying to read correctly before you move, the honest shortcut is this: in Ecuador, show up for lunch, because that's where the real relationship-building happens, slowly and personally; in Belgium, show up for the occasional Friday drink, because that's the one ritual that's actually social, while lunch itself has quietly become something most Belgians sacrifice to their own workload. Neither country is colder than the other — they've just filed "caring about your colleagues" under completely different line items in the calendar.
