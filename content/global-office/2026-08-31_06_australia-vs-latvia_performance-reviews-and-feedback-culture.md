# Tall Poppies and Quiet Words: How Australia and Latvia Tell You You're Underperforming

🇦🇺 Australia · 🇱🇻 Latvia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Australia, your manager will tell you you've dropped the ball to your face, over a flat white, using your first name, possibly with a joke attached. In Latvia, your manager will also tell you — but privately, calmly, in writing if possible, and never where a colleague might overhear. Both countries consider themselves refreshingly honest. Only one of them thinks honesty and volume are the same thing. According to Gallup's 2025 Global Workplace report, just 20 percent of Australian employees are actively engaged at work, below the global average, which raises an uncomfortable question for a country so proud of saying what it thinks: saying it, apparently, is not the same as saying it usefully.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Speak up in the review — silence reads as disengagement, not humility | Assume a blunt comment about your work is a personal attack |
| Use first names with your manager, even in formal feedback sessions | Wait for a title or a memo before raising a concern informally |
| Take "no worries, mate" literally when it follows real criticism | Mistake informality for an absence of standards |
| Ask directly for a written summary if you need one for visa or HR records | Expect the annual review alone to carry your entire case for a raise |
| Bring achievements you can quantify — outcomes, not hours logged | Stand out too visibly without expecting some good-natured deflation |
| Treat "tall poppy syndrome" as real — excessive self-promotion draws needling | Skip the Friday debrief; a lot of real feedback happens off the record |

#### 🇱🇻 Latvia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect written follow-up after any verbal feedback — Latvians document | Ask for critical feedback to be given in front of the team |
| Read short, flat sentences as normal, not as anger | Pad every point with enthusiasm; it reads as insincere |
| Request a private one-on-one if a topic feels sensitive | Publicly praise one colleague over another in a group setting |
| Prepare specific, evidence-based points before raising an issue yourself | Expect small talk to soften the start of a formal review |
| Respect hierarchy in larger, older institutions even as newer firms flatten it | Assume a calm tone means the feedback is minor |
| Follow up in email to confirm what was agreed — it is expected, not distrustful | Interrupt; turn-taking in meetings is taken seriously |

## 🇦🇺 Australia

Australian workplace culture markets itself as egalitarian, and on paper it mostly is: Hofstede Insights scores the country 36 on power distance, low enough that a graduate hire can, in theory, tell the CEO he's wrong in a meeting and be thanked for it. In practice, feedback in Australia arrives fast, informally, and often in a hallway rather than a scheduled review. The formal annual appraisal exists, usually tied to the financial year, but according to Gallup, only 14 percent of employees globally say their performance reviews actually inspire them to improve — and Australian data suggests the country is not exempt. Gallup's Australian benchmarking puts the median employee Net Promoter Score at 13, with organisations scoring notably below average on recognition and on collaboration and communication, the very categories a good feedback culture is supposed to fix.

The paradox is that Australians describe their own communication style as direct almost as a point of national pride, yet the follow-through — documentation, structured coaching, consistent recognition — lags the reputation. The OECD Better Life Index notes Australians devote roughly 60 percent of the day to leisure and personal time, a work-life balance that colleagues elsewhere envy, but it also means work conversations, including the hard ones, get compressed into fewer, punchier exchanges. The result is a culture where you'll hear the criticism immediately and clearly, and then rarely hear it again in writing.

## 🇱🇻 Latvia

Latvia's feedback culture reads, on the surface, as the more reserved of the two, and Hofstede's numbers back that impression up: Latvia scores 44 on power distance against Australia's 36, and a still-high but comparatively lower 70 on individualism versus Australia's 90 — a workforce more attuned to group harmony and hierarchy than the Australian one, cited here once and not again. What that translates to in an open-plan Riga office is feedback that is direct in content but careful in delivery. Latvians will tell you precisely what went wrong, but calmly, privately, and usually with the paper trail intact. Public criticism, even mild, is treated as a genuine breach of professional courtesy rather than "just being real."

Eurostat's cultural employment data shows Latvia running one of the highest shares of permanent, stable positions in its cultural and public sectors — north of 95 percent — a labour market where job security is the norm rather than the exception, and where a bad review carries different stakes than in a hire-and-fire environment. That stability seems to encourage patience: managers write things down, employees expect a record, and confrontation is something to be engineered around rather than embraced. It makes for a workplace where nobody is surprised by their review, because nothing important was said for the first time in the room.

## The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the stereotypes invert almost immediately. Australia, the extrovert, delivers feedback that is loud, quick, and largely undocumented — a country that talks a big game about honesty but keeps surprisingly poor written records of it. Latvia, assumed to be the reserved one, turns out to be the more procedurally rigorous of the pair: quieter tone, harder paper trail. An Australian manager will tell you you've underperformed in the lift. A Latvian manager will schedule fifteen minutes, close the door, and follow up by email within the hour.

Neither approach solves the problem Gallup keeps finding everywhere: most people, in most countries, still don't think their formal review changes anything. Australia's informality means feedback is frequent but forgettable; Latvia's formality means feedback is memorable but rare. The country that talks the most about being blunt turns out to have the thinner file. The country assumed to be tight-lipped keeps the better records.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *British Expats forum — A British nurse who relocated to Australia wrote that she was startled to find colleagues who, unlike her old NHS ward, would report each other's small mistakes straight to the manager rather than sort it out among themselves; other posters in the same thread pointed her toward "tall poppy syndrome" as the underlying logic — stand out too much, good or bad, and someone will move to cut you back down.*

> *Quora — A poster describing Australian offices noted that what looks like a laid-back culture from outside comes with surprisingly heavy documentation and KPI tracking once you're inside a larger company, and that the shift from an informal small business to a corporate structure with formal check-ins felt, to some new arrivals, oddly infantilising rather than professionalising.*

> *Blind — A review of Australia Post left on the platform rated management at the bottom of the scale, with the complaint centring not on the harshness of feedback but on its inconsistency — praise and criticism seemingly handed out without a clear or repeatable process behind either.*

> *Expat.com (Latvia forum) — A contributor working in Riga advised newcomers that Latvian colleagues would give completely honest answers if asked directly, but volunteered almost nothing unprompted, and that the biggest adjustment was learning to actually ask the direct question rather than waiting for someone to raise a concern on their own.*

> *Quora — Someone listing disappointments about living in Latvia mentioned that criticism at work, when it came, was handled so calmly and privately that it was sometimes easy to underestimate how seriously the issue was being taken until a formal written warning appeared with none of the informal heads-up a newcomer might expect.*

### Conclusion

The practical advice is almost embarrassingly simple: in Australia, treat every offhand comment from a manager as real feedback and write it down yourself, because nobody else will. In Latvia, treat every calm, quiet comment as more serious than it sounds, because the paperwork is already being drafted. Neither system is more honest than the other — they've just outsourced honesty to different parts of the process, one to the tone of voice, the other to the follow-up email. Arrive expecting your national assumptions about "blunt" versus "reserved" to be roughly backwards, and you'll do fine in either country. Arrive expecting the annual review to actually change anything, and you'll be disappointed on both hemispheres of this particular map.
