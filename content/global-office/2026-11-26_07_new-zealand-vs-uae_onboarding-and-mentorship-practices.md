# New Zealand Greets You With Morning Tea, the UAE With a Six-Month Audition
🇳🇿 New Zealand · 🇦🇪 UAE

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A new hire in New Zealand can expect a morning tea, a health and safety briefing and a form to sign. A new hire in the United Arab Emirates can expect a contract that permits up to six months of close observation, a fourteen-day exit clause for the employer and a one-month notice requirement for themselves. Both countries call this onboarding. One of them mostly means the tea. The other means the observation. For anyone weighing a move, the difference matters less for the first morning than for the first half-year, which is when most of the real learning, and most of the real dismissals, tend to occur.

## Do's & Don'ts

### New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the 90-day trial clause before signing. It must be agreed and signed before your first day, or it is not valid. | Accept a trial clause slid across the desk on day one. |
| Attend the health and safety session and sign the acknowledgement. The law requires it. | Treat the safety briefing as the whole of your induction. |
| Prepare a few words in case the welcome includes a pōwhiri and someone asks you to speak. | Improvise a speech on the spot. |
| Book a general chat with your manager at the end of week one, as the government's careers service suggests. | Wait for feedback to arrive unprompted. |
| Go to the Thursday or Friday after-work gathering. It is where the team actually forms. | Skip it and then wonder why nobody knows your name. |
| Use first names, up to and including the general manager. | Lead with your credentials. Self-promotion invites the tall poppy treatment. |

### UAE

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat the first three to six months as an audition. Probation can run to six months. | Assume probation is a formality. The employer can end it on 14 days' written notice. |
| Ask your manager which key performance indicators will measure you, as Khaleej Times advises. | Rely on the company to define your role. 28% of junior staff say training and role clarity were lacking. |
| Use formal titles with senior colleagues. | Overhaul a system in your first weeks. Learn how things are done first. |
| Ask for feedback early and in writing. | Wait for a review that may arrive after the decision. |
| Check the notice terms before you accept any other offer. | Resign mid-probation casually. Moving to another UAE employer needs one month's notice and can trigger compensation. |
| Confirm your leave entitlements in the contract. | Assume paid sick leave during probation. It is at the employer's discretion. |

## New Zealand: Morning Tea Is Not a Mentorship Programme

New Zealand's approach to onboarding is, by the evidence of its own government pages, a matter of duties rather than destiny. According to Employment New Zealand, an employer's induction obligations run to a health and safety briefing, the tools and training needed for the job, an explanation of in-house policies, a tax code declaration and a personal file. The same page adds that induction need not happen entirely on day one and may include a follow-up once the employee has settled. It is a checklist, with a gentle suggestion that a human being might check in later.

The culture around the checklist is where New Zealand earns its reputation. Careers New Zealand tells newcomers to expect a morning tea welcome, possibly a pōwhiri, and an invitation to after-work drinks on a Thursday or Friday. It also suggests booking a chat with your manager at the end of the first week. What it does not describe is formal mentoring inside employers; the only mentoring it mentions is Work Connect, a free ten-hour coaching programme for skilled migrants. The guide at GoingNZ describes fewer management layers, first names used at every level, and a general manager spotted making coffee. It also notes that a 90-day trial period is available to employers of any size, provided it is signed before the first day, and that a February 2026 change closed a loophole around challenging trial-period dismissals.

The unspoken curriculum is independence. One immigrant blogger, writing after two years in the country, says to expect little oversight and to be trusted to deliver what you promised. Colleagues will not know your background, so reputation is built on the first visible piece of work. Claims of expertise get scrutinised, and bragging does not land well. The mentorship, in other words, is mostly the freedom to work it out, supplemented by a team that is friendly and that goes home at five. Whether that suits you depends on whether you wanted a mentor or merely wanted to be left alone with decent colleagues.

## UAE: The Probation Is the Onboarding

The UAE's onboarding is best understood through its probation clause. Under Federal Decree Law No. 33 of 2021, as reported by Khaleej Times, an employer may place a worker on probation for up to six months, may end the contract during that time on 14 days' written notice, and may not impose a second probation afterwards. An employee who resigns to join another UAE employer must give a month's notice, and the new employer may owe the old one compensation unless agreed otherwise. Khaleej Times also reports that a worker who breaches the probation rules can be barred from a new work permit for a year, with some categories exempt. That reporting dates from 2022 and concerns mainland employment, so check the current rules before relying on any of it.

The quality of what happens inside that window is uneven, according to Robert Walters research reported by Khaleej Times in July 2026. Among all professionals surveyed, 51% rated their onboarding good or excellent and 22% rated it poor or very poor. Among junior employees, 24% said their onboarding made them more likely to leave, only 31% felt very supported by their manager, and 21% needed more than six months to feel fully effective. The biggest gaps were in training and role clarity at 44%, and manager support at 37%. A separate Khaleej Times guide to the first 90 days frames probation as a dating stage and advises newcomers to be punctual, take notes, ask questions, learn how meetings and decisions work, and use formal titles with seniors.

Mobility is where the clock matters. A LinkedIn survey reported by Khaleej Times found 72% of UAE employees planning to switch jobs in 2026, while Korn Ferry data cited by the same paper put the UAE median turnover at 8% against a global median of 11.4%. Those two figures sit uneasily together, which is typical of the genre; intention is cheap and a notice period is not. The result is a system in which a new hire has strong incentives to stay quiet, deliver visibly and avoid being the person who needed more explaining.

## The Reckoning

On paper, New Zealand offers more explanation and the UAE offers more consequence. New Zealand's employers owe newcomers a defined set of induction steps and a human welcome, while the stakes of the early months are set by a trial clause that must be signed in advance. The UAE sets the stakes in statute: six months, 14 days, one month, and a possible permit penalty. In practice the quality of support in both places depends on the manager. The Robert Walters figures suggest that in the UAE about a third of juniors feel very supported, which is not obviously worse than the lottery anyone anywhere enters when they meet a new boss.

The cultural difference is in what silence means. In New Zealand, little oversight signals trust, and asking for a chat in week one is expected. In the UAE, advice to ask questions arrives alongside advice to defer to seniors and not to overhaul anything, so silence can mean caution or can mean nobody has noticed your confusion. No hard data on formal mentoring programmes turned up for either country, which is itself informative. Mentorship in both is mostly informal and personality-dependent. The tea is more pleasant in one, the paperwork more punishing in the other.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *ExpatExchange — an American woman in Wellington says her husband is struggling with the relaxed pace at his new workplace, having come from a culture where long hours prove your worth. She mentions "island time" and tall poppy syndrome as the things he keeps running into.*

> *Blind — a former New Zealand resident (2008 to 2012) says quality of life was good but career growth and salary progression were limited, and that anyone expecting high total compensation should adjust their expectations.*

> *Medium — contributors at a New Zealand business academy describe colleagues who are personally friendly, employers who are open to helping staff meet their goals, and career paths that move sideways more than straight up.*

> *Glassdoor — a product designer who left a local job for an Abu Dhabi employer says they were let go about a month into probation. They describe weak onboarding, unclear instructions, shifting priorities and no early feedback, and argue that a month is too short to judge anyone.*

> *Reddit (via Lovin) — a Dubai resident with strong credentials says she quit a local startup after three days, citing pay below the posted description, tracked breaks and no leave allowed for a year.*

## Conclusion

If you want to be left to work things out among friendly colleagues, New Zealand will oblige, and you should read the trial clause with the same care you give the flat lease. If you are heading to the UAE, treat the first six months as the real interview, ask for your key performance indicators in writing, and do not resign casually, because the notice and compensation rules make exits more expensive than entrances. In both countries, ask your future manager how the first month works before you sign. A good answer is a reasonably reliable predictor, and a long pause is also data.

Neither country promises a mentor, but only one of them reliably brings biscuits.
