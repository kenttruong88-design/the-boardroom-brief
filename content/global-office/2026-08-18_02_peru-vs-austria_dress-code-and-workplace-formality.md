# The Suit Never Comes Off: Dressing for Work in Lima and Vienna

**🇵🇪 Peru · 🇦🇹 Austria**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Lima, dressing sharply for the office is a form of social insurance — a dark suit signals that you take the meeting, the hierarchy, and the person across the table seriously, in a culture where first impressions are treated as close to permanent. In Vienna, dressing sharply for the office is a form of social maintenance — Austrians dress well not to impress anyone in particular, but because looking anything less than put-together, at any occasion, would suggest a small failure of discipline. Both cities will judge you by your clothes. Only one of them will tell you so directly.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a dark, well-tailored suit and polished shoes to any first meeting, regardless of your industry | Loosen up your attire just because a colleague seems informal in conversation — dress and warmth are separate registers here |
| Address people by their professional titles and surnames until explicitly invited to do otherwise | Assume coastal heat is an excuse for casual dress in Lima's business districts — lighter fabrics are fine, sloppy is not |
| Invest in visibly good shoes — they are read as a specific marker of professionalism | Skip the tie in a first meeting even in a "creative" industry — err formal until told otherwise |
| Expect hierarchy to be visible in how people dress, not just how they speak | Assume startup or tech roles mean the conservative dress norm doesn't apply — Lima's formality runs deep across sectors |
| Treat dressing well as part of relationship-building, not a box to check | Show up underdressed to build "authenticity" — it reads as disrespect, not humility |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn and use professional titles like "Mag.," "Dr.," or "Ing." — they are not optional flourishes | Treat business email like chat — Austrians write emails with the formality of a letter |
| Default to dark, conservative suits and white shirts unless your office has visibly signaled otherwise | Assume smart jeans are universally fine — acceptable in some offices, a misstep in client-facing ones |
| Keep your appearance neat even in "casual" Austrian offices — informal does not mean sloppy | Show up late — punctuality is treated as inseparable from professionalism, including how seriously your appearance is taken |
| Be ready to remove your shoes in some smaller offices — check before assuming | Push for first-name informality early — wait to be invited |
| Leave by 5pm without guilt if your work is done — formality doesn't mean long hours here | Eat lunch at your desk — Austrians treat the midday break as a proper, social, sit-down affair |

### Peru: A Dark Suit Is a Sentence, Not an Outfit

Peruvian business culture asks a great deal of a first impression, and clothing carries most of that weight. Men are expected to appear in dark suits and ties with polished shoes; women in tailored dresses or blouse-and-skirt combinations that read as modest and deliberate. According to BizLatin Hub, this formality holds even as Lima's corporate districts become increasingly internationally connected, layering a modern, globalized business environment on top of enduring local expectations around hierarchy, personal trust, and relationship-building. The dress code, in other words, hasn't loosened as the economy has opened up — it has simply had to coexist with more foreign counterparties who show up underdressed and have to recalibrate fast.

Formality in Peru extends well past clothing. Cultural Atlas and Commisceo Global both note that titles and surnames dominate initial interactions, and that hierarchy is expected to be visibly legible — in how people are addressed, where they sit, and yes, in what they wear. For a newcomer, the practical implication is straightforward: overdressing in Lima is essentially impossible in a professional context, while underdressing reads as a genuine misjudgment of the room, not a personality quirk.

### Austria: Formality as a Quiet, Constant Discipline

Austria's version of formality is less performative but arguably more totalizing — it runs through emails as much as it runs through wardrobes. Business correspondence in Austria is treated with the gravity of a written letter rather than the casualness of chat, a norm that surprises many newcomers used to firing off quick, informal messages. Dress mirrors this: dark, conservative suits and white shirts remain standard, and even in the more relaxed startup and coworking scenes emerging in Vienna, the wider cultural expectation of polish persists. As one 2021 overview from The Local Austria put it, Austrians "like to start the work day early" and prize a genuine boundary between work and personal time — but that early-to-leave rhythm does not translate into casualness during the hours people are actually at their desks.

Austria's formality has its own quirks that don't map onto typical "buttoned-up" stereotypes. Lunch is a proper, sit-down, social occasion — never eaten at the desk — and moderate lunchtime drinking isn't frowned upon. Some smaller offices even ask employees to remove their shoes at the door. The formality, in other words, isn't cold; it's structured. Titles like "Mag.," "Dr.," and "Ing." are used deliberately and often, a linguistic form of the same instinct that produces the dark suit: signaling that credentials and roles are known, respected, and worth stating plainly.

### The Reckoning

The two countries arrive at similarly conservative dress norms from very different cultural architectures. Hofstede Insights scores Peru at 64 on power distance against Austria's remarkably low 11 — meaning Peru's formality is substantially about visibly marking hierarchy, while Austria's formality persists despite one of the flattest, most consultative management cultures measured anywhere in Europe. Austria's high masculinity score of 79, reflecting a culture oriented toward achievement, decisiveness, and performance, helps explain why appearance still functions as a marker of competence even without steep hierarchy behind it.

The practical upshot for a newcomer: in Peru, dressing sharply is partly about respecting the person across the table and their place in the org chart. In Austria, dressing sharply is about respecting the occasion and yourself, regardless of who's in the room. Mistake one for the other and you'll dress correctly but read the room wrong — overplaying deference in Vienna, or underplaying it in Lima.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — An expat who moved to Lima from North America for a corporate role wrote that the biggest adjustment wasn't the suit itself but the shoes — a Peruvian colleague pointed out, gently but unmistakably, that scuffed shoes undercut an otherwise sharp outfit more than any other single detail, and that people really do notice.*

> *The Local Austria — A roundup of Austrian workplace quirks for foreigners noted that being asked to swap your shoes for slippers in a smaller Viennese office catches almost every newcomer off guard, since it clashes so directly with the buttoned-up, no-exceptions image of Austrian formality that most arrivals expect.*

> *Quora — Someone who spent time working and living in Lima described the local pace as initially frustrating for anyone used to fast-moving Western offices, but said the payoff was a business culture where showing genuine respect — through appearance, titles, and patience — opened doors that competence alone didn't.*

> *Quora — A foreigner working in an Austrian office described the email culture as the real formality trap: casual, dashed-off messages that would be normal in a US startup were read locally as slightly careless, and adjusting to letter-style formality in email took longer than adjusting to the dress code itself.*

> *The Local Austria — An explainer on Austrian office norms noted that lunch is treated as a genuine social ritual — colleagues head out to a café or restaurant together around noon rather than eating at their desks — and skipping it repeatedly to keep working was seen less as diligence than as mildly antisocial.*

### Conclusion

If you're packing for Lima, over-invest in the suit and the shoes — they are doing more social work than your résumé in the first meeting. If you're packing for Vienna, over-invest in your email voice — the wardrobe formality is easy to nail, but the written formality is the part that quietly signals whether you understand the culture at all.

Here's the honest version, over a drink: Peru will forgive an awkward sentence if you look the part; Austria will forgive slightly rumpled clothes if you write and speak like you belong. Know which currency you're spending before you land.
