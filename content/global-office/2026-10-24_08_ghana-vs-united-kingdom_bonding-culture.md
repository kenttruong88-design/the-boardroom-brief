# A Transfer Memo for the Socially Confused: Ghana vs. the United Kingdom

🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Accra, a colleague who skips your uncle's funeral is making a statement. In London, a colleague who shows up at your flat unannounced is making a different kind of statement, and not a good one. Both countries will tell you, sincerely, that they value community. They simply disagree, almost entirely, on what community is supposed to cost you in time, cash, and personal space — and nobody explains this before you sign the contract.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend a colleague's funeral or wedding if invited — these are treated as serious obligations, not optional social events | Assume a Friday invitation means dinner for two — expect extended family, neighbours, and at least one unannounced cousin |
| Learn the greeting ritual ("how is your family, how is work") before launching into business — skipping it reads as cold | Decline a request to contribute toward a colleague's funeral or wedding dues without a genuine, stated reason |
| Say yes if invited to join a susu (rotating savings group) — it is a trust exercise as much as a financial one | Treat "work friends" as a separate category from real friends — colleagues often function as family |
| Mark good news communally — a promotion or birthday is expected to come with something shared, even just drinks | Expect punctuality to be the main currency of respect — showing care and presence often matters more than the clock |
| Expect senior colleagues to be addressed and treated with visible deference | Challenge a superior's decision bluntly in front of the group — raise concerns privately instead |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the first pub invite you get — it is often the main bonding ritual on offer, and repeatedly declining reads as standoffish | Overshare about money, family troubles, or health in the office — reserve is the social default, not coldness |
| Join an early work-organised activity (quiz night, five-a-side football) — cliques tend to set within the first few months | Assume a warm work relationship implies weekend plans — colleague and friend are often kept as separate categories |
| Use self-deprecating humour to build rapport — sincerity about achievements can land as showing off | Expect colleagues to drop by your home uninvited — this is rare and can genuinely unsettle people |
| Treat "let's get a coffee" as a plan only once a calendar invite exists — otherwise it may be pure politeness | Take a declined invitation personally — it is frequently about a packed diary, not about you |
| Learn the specific etiquette of a round at the pub — it is a small test of social fluency | Skip every single team social on principle — occasional absence is fine, total absence gets noticed |

Ghana's bonding culture is built on obligation, and it is obligation in the literal, scheduled sense. The Ghana Statistical Service's Living Standards Surveys have long found that households rely on extended family and community networks — not formal insurance or savings products — as their primary buffer against financial shocks, which is a dry way of saying that your coworkers are also, functionally, your social safety net. The susu system, a rotating informal savings group historically organised by women excluded from formal banking, illustrates the mechanism neatly: members pool fixed contributions and take turns receiving the lump sum, with no interest and no profit, run on reputational trust rather than contracts. One Kumasi member interviewed by Nonprofit Quarterly described her susu group simply as "my family," having no blood relatives in the city. That is the operating logic of Ghanaian workplace bonding too — it extends outward from the office into funerals, weddings, and school-fee emergencies, and it is rarely optional once you are inside it.

This has a cost that the brochure skips. Gallup World Poll data compiled into the World Happiness Report has placed Ghana around 111th globally even while ranking it among the happier nations in Sub-Saharan Africa, a split that tracks with a culture rich in social support but thin on income security — communal obligation is partly a response to the absence of a state safety net, not a lifestyle preference layered on top of one. Hofstede Insights' cultural-dimensions framework, which scores societies on individualism versus collectivism, places Ghana and its West African neighbours firmly on the collectivist end, far below the UK — useful shorthand for why a Ghanaian team lead will treat your attendance at a colleague's funeral as a professionalism issue, not a personal-life boundary.

The United Kingdom runs the opposite experiment: extremely high trust in the existence of a support network, extremely low frequency of actually using it. The ONS's Community Life Survey for 2024/25 found that 94% of adults in England agreed that people would be there for them if they needed help, and 95% had at least one person they could really count on to listen — reassuring numbers that sit awkwardly beside the same survey's finding that 6.6% of adults felt lonely often or always, and ONS tracking shows that figure has risen from roughly 5% in 2016 to over 7% by 2021. The infrastructure of care, in other words, exists largely in theory; people believe help is available and don't go looking for it.

At work specifically, Reward Gateway's 2023 UK Workplace Connection Report, surveying 1,000 UK professionals, found that two in three employees feel no real sense of connection or belonging at their job — and the consequences are measurable: 37% of employees with a social network at work describe themselves as productive, against 17% without one, while 43% of disconnected employees report burnout versus 35% of connected ones. The British workplace has, in effect, formalised its own solution to this gap: the pub. It is cheap, time-boxed, deniable if you're tired, and does the job of bonding without anyone having to admit that bonding was the agenda.

**The Reckoning.** The irony is that Ghana's model looks exhausting and performs well, while the UK's model looks relaxed and quietly underperforms. Ghanaian communal obligation consumes weekends, salaries, and sometimes entire days off for a colleague's funeral — yet it reliably produces the "someone to count on" result that British respondents report having in theory but underusing in practice. The UK, by contrast, has engineered maximum personal space and minimum demands on your Saturday, and ends up with a third of employees privately unhappy about how disconnected they feel at work, resolved mostly by one more round at the pub.

The counterintuitive part is who actually integrates faster. A new hire in Accra becomes embedded almost immediately, simply by accepting the first set of obligations placed on them — attend this funeral, join that susu, greet properly. A new hire in London can go months technically well-liked and genuinely isolated, because British bonding runs on invitations that are easy to decline and easier to never receive twice. Ghana asks more of you up front and gives you belonging early; Britain asks almost nothing of you up front and makes belonging something you have to actively, repeatedly, slightly awkwardly pursue.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — a London-based commenter warned that "Londoners don't usually accept newbies that easily," since most locals already have tight friend groups formed years earlier, and suggested leaning on coworkers as the easiest entry point simply because you already see them daily.*

> *Blind — a different commenter in the same thread pushed back, calling London "very easy" to socialise in precisely because of how multicultural and transient it is, adding that it is "very social as long as you like to drink," which several others in the thread treated as the real unwritten rule of UK team bonding.*

> *Blind — one reply noted it generally "takes a good year at a minimum" to build genuinely close friendships after relocating abroad in the UK, a timeline offered as a practical expectation-setter rather than a complaint.*

> *Expat Exchange forum — a longtime Accra-based expat advised newcomers that the city runs on dense, interest-based expat associations covering everything from golf and bridge to swimming, and recommended joining an existing group immediately rather than waiting for organic bonding to happen on its own.*

> *Expat Exchange forum — another contributor pointed newly arrived women specifically toward NAWA and expataccra as the fastest route into a social circle, framing informal community referral networks as the real onboarding process no employer HR packet would mention.*

### Conclusion

The honest comparison is not warm versus cold, it is upfront versus deferred. Ghana will hand you a dense, demanding social contract on day one — funerals, susu dues, greetings you cannot skip — and in exchange you are rarely alone. The UK will hand you almost no obligations at all, and in exchange you may spend a surprisingly long time wondering why, a year in, you still don't quite have people. Neither is the brochure version: one is belonging as a tax, the other is belonging as a hobby you have to remember to keep up.

If a friend asked me which to pick purely for bonding, I'd tell them Ghana if they can handle being needed, and the UK if they can handle being fine on their own for longer than feels comfortable — and either way, to show up for the first pub round or the first funeral invite, because in both countries, that is the only test that actually counts.
