**Welcome Aboard, Now Figure It Out: Onboarding and Mentorship in Uzbekistan and Canada**

**🇺🇿 Uzbekistan · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Tashkent, a new hire's first real lesson is usually about seating arrangements, not software licenses; in Toronto, it is a 90-minute compliance video about seating arrangements, delivered by an HR portal that forgot to assign anyone to answer questions afterward. Both countries insist they take onboarding seriously. Both are telling the truth in ways that will surprise the newcomer standing in the doorway, unsure whether to sit down.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇿 Uzbekistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet the most senior person in the room first, by name, before anyone else | Assume an org chart on paper reflects who actually makes decisions |
| Accept tea, small talk, and family questions as the real onboarding agenda | Expect a written 30-60-90 day plan to appear unprompted |
| Watch how colleagues quietly step in to help a struggling new hire | Mistake informal mentorship for the absence of mentorship |
| Dress sharply and arrive early even if the meeting starts late | Push back on hierarchy publicly in your first month |
| Ask directly for feedback; silence is not automatically approval | Assume six-day-week norms apply at every employer, especially IT firms |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Rewrite your résumé to Canadian format before day one, not after | Keep a photo, age, or marital status on your CV |
| Ask HR directly whether a mentor or "buddy" is assigned | Assume one exists just because the handbook mentions "culture" |
| Treat the first six months as a probation both sides are quietly running | Interpret politeness and smiling as confirmation someone understood you |
| Ask the questions interviewers legally cannot ask themselves | Stay quiet about confusion to avoid seeming high-maintenance |
| Network directly with hiring managers, even cold | Wait for a formal program to introduce you to the team |

Uzbekistan's onboarding runs on relationship first, paperwork later. The country's growing IT outsourcing sector — revenue roughly tripled in a recent year to over $300 million, with the government's 2030 strategy targeting $5 billion and 300,000 workers, according to sector reporting compiled via Outsource.gov.uz — has imported some Western-style structure, but mostly at the margins. A Python tech lead from Samarkand, interviewed by Outsource.gov.uz, described no formal onboarding process at any employer he'd worked for; instead, colleagues simply stepped in with "let us do this together" when someone struggled. He contrasted this with the older, more hierarchical state-sector jobs he'd left, where bureaucracy and favoritism outweighed any mentorship at all. On an expat forum thread about Uzbek business etiquette, one poster's entire onboarding advice boiled down to a single sentence: make sure you greet the most important person in the room first. Gallup's 2026 State of the Global Workplace found Uzbek employees reporting notably higher engagement (46%) and lower daily stress (13%) than the regional and global averages — evidence that the lack of a laminated onboarding binder is not, in itself, making anyone miserable.

Canada formalizes what Uzbekistan leaves to etiquette, then frequently fails to deliver the formalization. SHRM-compiled industry research found that only 15% of companies continue onboarding past six months and just 2% beyond a year, even though roughly 90% of employees privately decide whether to stay within that first six-month window. The same body of research found that 56% of new hires consider a buddy or mentor very important, and that high performing organizations are roughly 2.5 times more likely to actually assign one — which, read the other way, means most organizations don't. Statistics Canada's 2023 Quality of Employment report adds an uncomfortable footnote for new arrivals specifically: employed immigrants participate in job-related training at a lower rate (27.4%) than Canadian-born workers (32.4%), despite holding more bachelor's degrees on average. The newcomer most likely to need structured mentorship is, statistically, the one least likely to get it.

The Reckoning is this: Uzbekistan tells you nothing on paper and then quietly catches you in practice, through tea, hierarchy, and colleagues who materialize when you're stuck. Canada hands you a polished onboarding deck, a Slack welcome message, and a mentorship "program" that may or may not have a second person attached to it. Hofstede Insights data (Uzbekistan's scores are estimated, given limited independent survey coverage of Central Asia, while Canada's are fully measured) still shows the expected gap — Uzbekistan clusters toward higher power distance and collectivism, Canada toward lower power distance and individualism — but the lived experience inverts the stereotype of which system actually produces a guiding hand on week one.

The irony sharpens further once politeness enters the picture. Canadian warmth is frequently mistaken, by newcomers from more hierarchical cultures, for actual comprehension — a nod and a smile can mean "I understand" or "I am too polite to admit I don't," and a manager untrained to notice the difference will not ask twice. Uzbek directness about seniority, meanwhile, reads to a visiting Canadian as intimidating formality, when it is often just an efficient shortcut to finding out who can actually unblock your work. Both systems, in other words, are solving the same onboarding problem — who do I ask, and will they tell me the truth — with opposite defaults: Tashkent assumes you'll work it out socially if you pay attention, Toronto assumes a policy document has already worked it out for you, whether or not anyone has read it since it was written.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — a poster advising newcomers to Uzbekistan said the single most useful etiquette rule was identifying the most senior person in any room and greeting them first, since no fixed protocol exists for titles or business cards.*

> *The Safety Magazine (Canada) — an Indian-born banking professional recalled assuming his new Canadian supervisor's casual question about vacation scheduling was secretly a loyalty test, and only stopped declining his own time off after Canadian colleagues convinced him the offer was genuine.*

> *The Safety Magazine (Canada) — a newcomer described nodding and saying yes throughout a work conversation she hadn't fully followed, which once led her to escort a colleague somewhere unintended; she noted that smiling doesn't mean understanding.*

> *Outsource.gov.uz — a Samarkand-based Python tech lead said no employer he'd worked for ran a formal onboarding process, but colleagues stepped in unprompted with "let us do this together" whenever someone got stuck, a habit he valued more than any written plan.*

> *CIC News — a newcomer to Canada credited a settlement-agency case manager, rather than any employer, with teaching them how to format a resume, explain overseas experience in local terms, and prepare for interview questions employers legally cannot ask.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway is to stop asking which country "has" onboarding and start asking who is actually responsible for it. In Uzbekistan, assume the responsibility is social and ask for it directly — find the senior person, ask for feedback explicitly, and treat colleagues' informal check-ins as the real curriculum. In Canada, assume the responsibility is institutional but underfunded — ask HR point-blank whether a mentor exists, and don't let politeness stand in for clarity when it doesn't. Neither country will hand you a map; one just admits it upfront.

If a friend asked me which was worse: Uzbekistan will make you figure out the hierarchy by week one, and Canada will make you figure out whether anyone's actually in charge of you at all.
