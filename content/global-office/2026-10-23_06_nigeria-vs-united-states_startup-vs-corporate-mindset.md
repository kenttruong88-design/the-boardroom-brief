**Startup Chaos, Corporate Chains: Nigeria and the United States Disagree on What Work Should Feel Like**

🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇺🇸 United States

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Lagos, a startup survives its first year through improvisation, a generator, and a founder who answers emails at 2 a.m. In the United States, a startup survives its first year because the Bureau of Labor Statistics says roughly four in five new establishments technically still exist after twelve months, which is a lower bar than it sounds. The two countries have built entirely different relationships with the word "startup," and an entirely different tolerance for the word "corporate," and a person weighing a move between them should know which mindset they are actually buying before they sign anything.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask upfront whether pay is naira-denominated or pegged to USD or a stablecoin; some startups peg wages specifically to outrun inflation | Assume a fixed naira salary will hold its purchasing power from one year to the next |
| Budget for a generator, inverter, or diesel as a real monthly line item | Assume remote work exempts you from Lagos's power and traffic realities just because you're not commuting |
| Get the notice period and termination clause in writing before you sign | Rely on a verbal understanding, however warm the founder seemed in the interview |
| Clarify your actual scope early, even if your title is one of three you'll informally hold | Expect a clean org chart or a dedicated HR function in your first year; many startups hire HR last |
| Ask directly about a founder's management track record and past staff turnover | Take a glowing pitch at face value without checking it against former employees |

#### 🇺🇸 United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the formal promotion and calibration cycle in month one | Expect a raise or title change simply because the work was good; document everything instead |
| Build visibility deliberately — speak up cross-team, find a sponsor above your manager | Assume good work is self-evidently visible across an organization of thousands |
| Map the real approval chain for budget, legal, or security requests | Expect a single manager's yes to be the end of the conversation |
| Price healthcare, 401(k) matching, and PTO into any offer comparison | Compare only base salary between a startup offer and a large-employer offer |
| Ask about at-will employment terms and state-specific labor law before accepting | Assume protections you're used to elsewhere apply automatically; at-will termination is fast and often cause-free |

---

Nigeria's labor market does not really have a "startup sector" set apart from everything else; it has an economy in which almost everyone is already running something. NBS data compiled for the Nigerian Economic Summit Group's 2025 report found that roughly 93 percent of the national workforce sits in informal employment, with formal jobs accounting for just 7.8 percent of total employment as of 2023. Only about 15 percent of working Nigerians draw a wage at all, and even that is unevenly distributed — Lagos state, the country's commercial engine, has a wage-earner share of 33.8 percent, against low single digits in states like Jigawa and Sokoto. Against that backdrop, joining a funded Lagos startup is already a privileged, formalized position, not the scrappy alternative the word "startup" implies elsewhere.

The ecosystem itself is real money now, if modest by global standards: Partech Africa's 2025 figures put total African startup funding at $4.1 billion, up 25 percent year on year, with Nigeria taking roughly 8 to 14 percent of that as one of the continent's "Big Four" markets alongside Kenya, South Africa, and Egypt. The World Bank has separately committed $500 million toward Nigerian innovation and entrepreneurship financing. What the funding rounds don't show up in is management training — TechCabal's reporting on workplace culture describes founders who are, in its phrase, "accidental people managers," promoted or self-appointed into authority with no preparation for it, which helps explain why roughly 65 percent of younger employees surveyed don't expect to stay in a role past a year.

None of this is static. Roughly 3.5 million young Nigerians enter the workforce every year, and labor productivity growth averaged only 1.5 percent between 1990 and 2018, against roughly 2.5 percent for Indonesia and Malaysia over the same stretch — a gap that has pushed a steady stream of developers toward remote contracts with foreign employers rather than local startup payrolls. TechCabal's own reporting found most Nigerian developers still can't actually land that remote arrangement, which keeps the domestic startup scene as the next-best option for formally employed tech talent rather than a stopgap on the way somewhere else. A newcomer joining a Lagos startup should expect to be one of the more fortunate people in the building, and to be reminded of that by nearly everything around the office.

The American side runs on the opposite assumption: that the economy has a stable corporate core and startups are the deliberate departure from it. BLS Business Employment Dynamics data shows about 80 percent of new establishments survive their first year, roughly half survive five years, and about 35 percent make it to ten — a failure rate high enough that "startup" still functions as a meaningful risk category rather than a synonym for "small company," the way it increasingly does in Lagos. Corporate America, meanwhile, had an unusually good 2025 by its own metrics: the Conference Board's Job Satisfaction report found worker satisfaction at its highest level since 1987, even as other surveys found a majority of workers calling it a bad time to be looking for a new job. Read together, those two findings describe people who are reasonably content largely because they are not moving — a structural stability startups are not built, or expected, to offer.

That stability is also contractual in a way it rarely is at a Nigerian startup. A corporate offer in the US typically comes with a formal offer letter, a benefits package with real dollar value, and an HR department whose entire job is administering the relationship — none of which should be assumed at a ten-person Lagos fintech still operating out of a shared office in Yaba. The trade-off is that US corporate employment is also at-will almost everywhere, meaning the same formality that protects you day to day offers comparatively little protection the day the company decides it doesn't need your role; large-employer layoffs have become so routine in American tech that they barely register as news anymore, which is its own kind of volatility hiding inside what looks, from Lagos, like total stability.

The Reckoning, then, is less about which system is better and more about what each one is honestly for. Hofstede Insights scores the United States at 91 on individualism against a regional West Africa estimate (the closest available proxy, since Nigeria has no standalone Hofstede country score) of just 20, and American corporate structure is built around that individual: promotion packets, personal performance reviews, visible credit. Nigerian work culture, by the same regional estimate, runs a power-distance score of 77 against America's 40 — hierarchy is assumed, not negotiated, whether you're at a three-person startup or a legacy bank. The practical upshot is that an American "startup mindset" of flat hierarchy and personal upside doesn't automatically travel to Lagos, where even the scrappiest fintech still runs on a founder's word being close to final.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — commenters following up on a reported story about the US startup Fast noted that it had paid contracted Lagos-based engineers around $800 a month, below the roughly $1,000 that Nigerian employers were said to pay entry-level developers for the same work; the engineers said they were later cut off from company Slack and left uncredited.*

> *Hacker News — a commenter on Nigeria's tech scene described local investors' "onerous investment terms," noting that funders often wanted a startup to already be a year or two old and profitable before they'd commit, inverting the venture-capital logic a US founder would expect.*

> *Blind — a Salesforce employee described the company's internal bureaucracy as "absolutely dismal," saying the delays in getting routine approvals through contributed directly to their burnout; their own manager reportedly confirmed the same bottlenecks exist at other large tech employers.*

> *Blind — in a different thread, an employee frustrated with a small company's "robot bureaucrat" founders was told plainly by another commenter that red tape at a FAANG-sized employer is categorically inescapable, and that the only real fix is moving to a much smaller company or a startup instead.*

> *Expat.com (used here in place of an unreachable Quora thread) — a contributor relocating for a corporate role in Nigeria was advised to get the termination notice period in writing before signing, to split pay between a local account and an offshore one to manage currency risk, and warned separately that finding employment for an accompanying spouse can be difficult, particularly in banking.*

---

### Conclusion

The honest comparison isn't "which mindset is better" but "which risk are you actually being asked to carry." In the United States, choosing a startup over a corporate job is a voluntary trade of safety for upside, made against a backdrop where the corporate default is comparatively durable and increasingly well-liked by the people inside it. In Nigeria, the entire formal labor market is small enough that working for a funded startup already is the safe, structured choice relative to the informal economy most of the country actually works in — the volatility you're importing isn't the absence of a safety net, it's a founder's personal management style standing in for one.

If you're moving for the autonomy, Lagos will give you more of it than you asked for, immediately, with no manual. If you're moving for the stability, the United States will give you plenty of structure and then make you fight your way through several layers of it to get noticed. Bring patience either way, and in Lagos, bring a backup generator for your patience too.
