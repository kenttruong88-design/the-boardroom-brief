# South Africa Treats the Performance Review as a Ritual; Australia Treats It as a Rumour

🇿🇦 South Africa · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Only 38 percent of Australian employees say their workplace has a consistent, formal review process with clear criteria, according to an ELMO and YouGov survey from 2025. In retail the figure drops to 11 percent. South Africa, by contrast, has a performance review that one local commentator describes as a ritual on the yearly to-do list, which implies that it at least happens. The question for an arriving professional is which is worse: a feedback meeting nobody enjoys, or a feedback system nobody has scheduled.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat the annual review as the moment pay, bonus and promotion are discussed | Assume a pleasant year means a good review; appraisals often feed pay decisions |
| Keep your own record of objectives and outcomes, because managerial discretion plays a part | Rely on your manager's memory of January in November |
| Ask for feedback between reviews instead of waiting for the formal meeting | Wait for an underperformance conversation to reach you early; managers often delay it |
| Show how your work contributes to company goals | Leave your contribution to be inferred; unrecognised high performers are the likeliest to leave |
| Query a rating through the proper channel, with evidence | Dispute a rating emotionally in the meeting itself |

### In Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask at interview what the review process actually is, because it varies by sector | Assume a formal annual review exists; in many sectors it does not |
| Be direct and ask questions when unsure; it is read as interest | Hedge so heavily that nobody can tell what you mean |
| Ask for feedback regularly, since nearly a quarter of Gen X and 29 percent of Baby Boomers rarely get helpful input | Treat silence as approval |
| Accept teasing as a sign of belonging, not of hostility | Mistake the first politeness for the whole relationship |
| Write your achievements down for yourself | Boast about them; one expat contributor links the local reaction to a tendency to cut down people who stand out |

## South Africa

South African performance management is formal in structure and uncertain in execution. According to the Remchannel October 2025 Salary and Wage Movement Survey, covering 66 organisations and more than 336,000 employees, over 70 percent of employers factor individual contribution into pay increases for non-bargaining-unit staff. CPI was cited by 89.4 percent and company performance by 75.8 percent, but 40.9 percent also said they partly rely on managerial discretion, and many lacked confidence that their appraisal systems supported well-informed pay decisions. Average salary increases over the previous year ran between 4.7 and 5.7 percent, and employee turnover was 20.7 percent. The survey identified high performers who feel unrecognised as the group most likely to leave.

The culture of the conversation is a more delicate matter. A 2014 study by The Human Edge, reported by Bizcommunity, found that 78 percent of South African managers took on extra work each week to cover for underperforming staff, and only 6 percent spoke up on the first or second occasion that targets were missed. Some 47 percent described themselves as anxious about performance conversations, and 32 percent thought their organisation's performance management had little value. The same research found that 78 percent of those managers rated themselves skilled at these conversations, that 70 percent said bad habits resurface within three months, and that 66 percent believed a concern must be raised two or more times before anything improves. Its author argued that formal reviews should summarise ongoing feedback and not serve as the main occasion for it. The study is old, and its conclusions should be treated with the appropriate caution, but it is consistent with the present-day commentary. A 2023 Sunday World piece quotes a recruitment executive calling the annual review time-consuming, subjective and often demotivating, while conceding that it remains the basis for pay and promotion decisions.

The more encouraging data comes from Culture Amp, whose July 2026 benchmark found management to be the area where South Africa scored most positively. Leadership scored well above average, though workplace engagement scored much lower than average, which is the sort of split that suggests managers are better liked than the workplaces they manage. Some 71 percent of employees are engaged, median eNPS is 16, and 91 percent understand how their work contributes to company goals. Twenty-five percent disagreed that compensation is fair, two points above the average, and 21 percent are thinking of or looking for work elsewhere. In Hofstede's dataset, South Africa scores 49 on power distance and 65 on individualism, a middle position that leaves the culture of feedback to the individual manager.

## Australia

Australia is nearly the opposite: informal, egalitarian and wary of ceremony. ELMO's survey of more than 1,000 employees found that 29 percent describe a partly structured process, 15 percent a mostly informal one, 9 percent no process at all, and 8 percent are unsure. The formal system is most common in healthcare and education, at 46 percent each, and rarest in retail, at 11 percent, and hospitality, at 17 percent. Despite this, 72 percent believe they are judged on fair and transparent criteria, though only 27 percent strongly agree, and 81 percent say their goals and expectations are clear.

Feedback is the soft spot. Some 42 percent say they receive helpful feedback often enough to improve, 35 percent occasionally and 18 percent rarely. The pattern is generational and inverted from what one might expect: 12 percent of Gen Z and 16 percent of Millennials rarely or never receive it, compared with 24 percent of Gen X and 29 percent of Baby Boomers. ELMO's chief executive warned that when structures are patchy, trust can erode. Culture Amp's July 2026 benchmark, from about 250 organisations, puts engagement at 73 percent and median eNPS at 25, and finds the most negative item to be whether anything is done when someone is not delivering, where 14 percent disagreed. Some 92 percent know how their work contributes to company goals, and 17 percent are thinking of or actively seeking jobs elsewhere, two points below the overall average. Australians, it seems, are broadly content and mildly unsure what happens to colleagues who are not.

Hofstede's dataset gives Australia a power distance of 36 and individualism of 90, which matches the anecdotal account of a workplace in which people treat each other as equals and expect directness. The expat accounts agree on the manners: be on time, ask when unsure, and expect politeness to turn into teasing once you have been accepted.

## The Reckoning

South Africa has the apparatus and Australia has the attitude. A South African employee is likely to receive a rating that matters, on a calendar that is known, from a manager who may have avoided the difficult conversation earlier in the year. An Australian employee is likely to be told things casually and quickly, or not at all, depending on the sector and the manager. The first system risks surprise at year end. The second risks a long, pleasant silence.

For pay, the South African route is more explicit: the Remchannel data shows individual contribution and discretion in the mix, so the appraisal has consequences. In Australia, 72 percent trusting the fairness of criteria is a respectable figure, but the strong-agreement figure of 27 percent suggests a trust that is real and faintly reserved. Both countries share a quiet strength, in that roughly nine in ten employees say they understand how their work contributes to company goals (91 percent in South Africa, 92 percent in Australia, according to Culture Amp). The disagreement is about what happens next: whether the answer arrives on a schedule, or only if somebody remembers to mention it over coffee. A newcomer from either direction should assume that the formal system tells only part of the story and ask for the informal one.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — One contributor to a thread on Australian business etiquette says workplaces are relaxed and people treat each other as equals, that Australians are direct and expect the same, and that asking questions when unsure is welcomed.*

> *Expat.com — Another contributor, a senior American businesswoman, says initial politeness gives way to teasing once you are accepted, which she links to a tendency to cut down people who stand out and says should not be mistaken for bullying.*

> *Wereldwyd — A South African who emigrated says her experience at New Zealand employers depended heavily on her supervisor, and that she and her husband find the work culture in Australia much more welcoming.*

> *DEV Community — A migrant engineer describes an Australian company that welcomed feedback: when the author shared onboarding gaps with platform engineers, the documentation was substantially revamped within months.*

> *Sunday World — A South African recruitment executive calls the annual review a yearly ritual that leaders and staff would both rather skip, and urges continuous feedback and manager training in its place.*

## Conclusion

Arriving in South Africa, assume the annual review is real, keep your own evidence and seek feedback long before it. Arriving in Australia, ask what the process is at the interview, request feedback regularly, and read an absence of comment as an absence of data, not a compliment.

In either country the useful habit is the same: do not wait for the system to notice you. It will eventually, and the timing is rarely in your favour.
