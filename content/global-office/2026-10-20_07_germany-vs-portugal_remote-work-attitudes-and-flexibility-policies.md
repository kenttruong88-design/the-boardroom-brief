# Germany Will Let You Work From Home. Portugal Will Fine Your Boss for Calling You There.

🇩🇪 Germany · 🇵🇹 Portugal

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In 2025, 13.3 percent of employed Germans usually worked from home, the fourth-highest share in the EU and more than triple the country's 2006 baseline, according to Eurostat's Labour Force Survey. Portugal, which has spent the last five years marketing itself to the world's laptop class as a sun-drenched remote-work utopia, logged a share of just 8.9 percent among its own residents — below the EU average, and twelfth in the bloc, despite starting from almost nothing in 2006. The country with the works council, the "Feierabend" and a labour minister who once floated a legal right to the home office has more people actually working from one than the country that fines employers for texting staff after six. Neither fact is quite what the respective national brochures lead with.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your home-office days written into your contract or works-council agreement | Assume "hybrid" is yours to schedule; many teams fix specific office days centrally |
| Ask the Betriebsrat (works council), if one exists, what's already been negotiated | Try to negotiate flexibility solo if a council already covers your role |
| Log on and off at consistent, predictable hours | Message colleagues after 6pm or on weekends expecting a reply |
| Confirm the company's current office-attendance policy before signing | Trust a two-year-old job ad's "generous home office" line; many firms rolled this back after 2023 |
| Keep careful records for the Homeoffice-Pauschale tax allowance | Skip registering your setup, since reimbursement and insurance both depend on paperwork |
| Be on time to video calls, camera on | Treat a Zoom call as more casual than an in-person meeting; punctuality norms carry over |

#### 🇵🇹 Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get "teletrabalho" status written into your contract, not just promised verbally | Assume informal flexibility carries the same legal protections as formal remote status |
| Expect your employer to cover home internet and electricity under Law 83/2021 | Assume "remote" means no one checks; many firms still require occasional office days |
| Build buffer time into meetings with Portuguese colleagues | Schedule back-to-back international calls assuming everyone joins exactly on time |
| Use the D8 visa route if you work for a non-Portuguese employer | Assume the visa exempts you from filing taxes in two countries |
| Budget for a 9-to-18 day with a full lunch hour if you join a local team | Mistake the relaxed pace for short hours; actual hours worked run above the EU average |
| Ask whether a four-day-week pilot applies to your specific employer | Expect it company-wide; it remains a pilot, not policy |

### 🇩🇪 Germany: Structure Flexes, Reluctantly

Germany's home-office share has risen steadily since Eurostat started tracking it, and the pandemic-era debate briefly looked like it would end in law. Labour minister Hubertus Heil pushed a Mobile Work Act that would have guaranteed roughly 24 days of home office per year, arguing the pandemic had proven "much more mobile working is possible than we thought." It never passed in that form. What Germany has instead is a patchwork: no general statutory right to work from home, but a strong tradition of works councils (Betriebsräte) negotiating remote-work terms company by company, which means your actual entitlement depends less on national law than on which firm, and which union, you land with.

That patchwork is now flexing back the other way. A 2024 Robert Walters Germany survey found 42 percent of companies had increased in-office days over the prior year, and remote job listings fell from 1,235 to 873 between November 2022 and September 2024 alone. Yet the same survey found over 70 percent of employees still rate hybrid arrangements as most appealing, prompting recruiter Thomas Hoffmann to call flexibility "not a passing trend but a permanent shift" — a verdict employers are only partially acting on.

Hofstede's data adds useful texture: Germany scores a low 35 on Power Distance, meaning decision-making is formally decentralised and subordinates are expected to challenge upward rather than simply defer. In practice this coexists with rigid process: the office is frequently written into employment contracts as the legally defined workplace, so "working from home" in Germany is less a cultural vibe than a specific, negotiated, and increasingly contested entitlement. It also helps explain why Germany, despite the stereotype of rule-bound inflexibility, posts some of the lowest average annual working hours among OECD economies: structure, once negotiated, tends to be honoured on both sides of the desk.

### 🇵🇹 Portugal: Generous Law, Traditional Office

Portugal's legal framework is, on paper, more protective than Germany's. Law 83/2021 obliges employers to reimburse costs like internet and electricity for remote staff and allows fines for contacting workers outside agreed hours — though lawmakers explicitly rejected a formal, codified "right to disconnect," so the protection is narrower than headlines suggested at the time. Parents of children under eight gained a standing right to request remote work, and the country has piloted four-day work-weeks at a handful of firms, with labour minister Ana Mendes Godinho positioning Portugal as "one of the best places in the world" for digital nomads and remote workers.

That marketing is aimed largely outward. Eurostat's numbers show the domestic telework share climbing from a mere 1.2 percent in 2006 to 8.9 percent in 2025 — a large proportional jump, but still below the EU-27 average and well behind leaders like Finland (20.9 percent) or Ireland (19.8 percent). Day-to-day office culture for Portuguese employees still runs a conventional 9-to-18 schedule with a full lunch hour, and actual hours worked remain above the EU average despite the country's laid-back reputation.

Hofstede's Power Distance score for Portugal sits at 63, nearly twice Germany's, reflecting a workplace culture where decisions concentrate at the top and deference to hierarchy is the norm rather than the exception. That hierarchy shows up in the hours, too: OECD figures consistently place Portugal's average annual hours worked well above Germany's, undercutting the idea that a culture friendly to digital nomads is automatically a culture of short days. The generous legal scaffolding around remote work, in other words, was built more for the incoming D8-visa holder answering to a foreign employer abroad than for the local employee answering to a Portuguese manager down the hall.

### The Reckoning

Put plainly: Germany has more remote workers and weaker remote-work law; Portugal has fewer remote workers and stronger remote-work law. The explanation is structural rather than contradictory. Germany's flexibility is earned through collective bargaining and company-level negotiation, which is durable where it exists but patchy and currently retreating under return-to-office pressure. Portugal's flexibility is legislated top-down and aimed squarely at attracting outside talent and investment, which makes it generous for the newly arrived remote employee of a foreign company, and largely irrelevant to the Portuguese colleague sitting beside them in the office at 9am sharp.

For the person actually relocating, the practical distinction is who you'll be answering to, and on whose terms. Join a German company's domestic headcount and your remote-work reality depends largely on your specific employer's current mood about office attendance, which the 2024 data suggests is souring faster than the 70-percent employee preference for hybrid would predict. Move to Portugal as a visa-holder on a foreign payroll instead, and the national law works almost entirely in your favour — cost reimbursement, after-hours protection and a minister actively courting your arrival — while your Portuguese neighbours quietly clock a longer, more hierarchical in-office week than the beach-laptop branding ever advertised to you.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — a commenter who worked inside a German company before the pandemic recalled that management treated anyone not physically present at their desk as suspect, regardless of what they actually produced that day.*

> *The Local (Germany) — journalist Barbara Woolsey, writing from her own kitchen table during a lockdown, admitted she wasn't getting "too used to" working from home, since German colleagues and workplace culture still prize a firm line between office and living room.*

> *Portugal Resident — Kelly Murtagh, who moved her work to the Algarve, said the change gave her roughly four extra hours a day and noticeably less stress, crediting dependable local infrastructure rather than any change in the job itself.*

> *Hacker News — a remote worker based in Portugal but invoicing a UK employer described the part nobody mentions in the brochure: filing two separate national tax returns every year, since the admin load gets heavier, not lighter, once the scenery is sorted.*

> *Hacker News — another contributor who has worked across several remote-friendly jurisdictions warned newcomers specifically not to let an employer control their legal or visa status, advising people to secure their own standing independent of whichever company sponsored the move.*

### Conclusion

Neither country is lying, exactly; they are simply advertising different halves of the same transaction. Germany offers remote work as something you negotiate, company by company, backed by genuinely strong worker protections that are currently trending back toward the office. Portugal offers remote work as something you're granted by statute, generously, provided you arrived from somewhere else to take advantage of it. Choose Germany if you want institutional weight behind whatever arrangement you land, and can tolerate renegotiating it as policy shifts. Choose Portugal if you want the legal insulation of Law 83/2021 and don't mind that the welcome was designed for you specifically, not for the colleague who was already there. Either way, read the actual office-attendance line in your contract before you read anything about the beach.
