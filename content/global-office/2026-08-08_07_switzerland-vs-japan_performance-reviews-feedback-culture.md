**The Feedback Nobody Says Out Loud: Reviews in Zurich and Tokyo**

🇨🇭 Switzerland · 🇯🇵 Japan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Both Switzerland and Japan score high on Hofstede's uncertainty-avoidance dimension — Switzerland around 58, Japan considerably higher — meaning both cultures crave structure, documentation, and clearly defined process. You would think that shared discomfort with ambiguity would make feedback similarly clear in both places. It does not. Swiss managers, per widely echoed cross-cultural management research, will tell a junior employee precisely what isn't working and expect a thank-you in return; the same blunt correction delivered in a Tokyo office, per the same body of research, risks humiliating the recipient and quietly damaging the working relationship for months. Structure, it turns out, is not the same thing as directness.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect direct, specific criticism delivered without much cushioning | Read directness as personal hostility — it's simply the professional register |
| Come to review meetings with documentation and precise examples | Show up with vague self-assessments — precision is expected and respected |
| Treat punctuality and reliability as core, non-negotiable performance markers | Arrive late to a review meeting, even by a few minutes |
| Ask clarifying questions if feedback feels underspecified — it's welcomed | Assume silence after feedback means the conversation is over — follow-up is normal |
| Expect quality and process to be scrutinized equally, not just outcomes | Assume a good result excuses a sloppy process — both are reviewed |

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read between the lines — criticism is often implied, not stated outright | Expect explicit negative feedback to be said plainly, especially upward in the hierarchy |
| Pay attention to formal, structured feedback moments (scheduled reviews) rather than informal cues | Rely on ad hoc feedback moments — they're often deliberately avoided |
| Treat a lack of praise as neutral, not necessarily bad | Assume no news is bad news — silence is often simply silence |
| Prepare thoroughly for formal review sessions; they follow expected structure and etiquette | Improvise or treat the review as casual — structure is part of the respect shown |
| Watch for feedback delivered through a third party or in writing rather than face-to-face | Confront a colleague directly to demand blunt feedback — it can backfire socially |

---

Switzerland's feedback culture pairs high uncertainty avoidance with low power distance and a strong individualist streak, producing a workplace where clarity is treated as a form of respect rather than a risk. A Swiss manager telling a direct report exactly why a proposal isn't working, complete with specific critique, is functioning inside a well-understood professional norm — and the expected response is gratitude for the clarity, not defensiveness. This directness is backed by process: Swiss performance reviews tend to be thoroughly documented, scheduled, and precise, reflecting a broader cultural emphasis (frequently cited in comparative workplace research) on quality and reliability as baseline expectations rather than aspirational goals. The system rewards employees who can absorb direct correction without reading it as a relationship threat.

Japan's high uncertainty avoidance manifests completely differently, layered onto a culture where preserving group harmony and hierarchical face outranks the value of blunt clarity. Academic and cross-cultural management research on Japanese workplaces consistently notes that explicit constructive criticism is rarely voiced aloud — and almost never directed upward in the hierarchy, regardless of how badly a decision might be going. Effective feedback systems in Japanese organizations, per workplace research, tend to rely not on informal in-the-moment correction but on formal, structured review events with clear agendas and preparation — the structure itself becomes the vehicle that makes feedback deliverable at all, because it provides social cover that an off-the-cuff comment wouldn't. The result is a system that is, in its own way, just as rigorous as Switzerland's — it simply routes almost all of its critical information through indirect, face-preserving channels instead of direct statements.

### The Reckoning

The real trap for a newcomer is assuming that "high uncertainty avoidance" predicts directness, when in fact it predicts *structure* — and structure can be built around either bluntness (Switzerland) or diplomatic indirection (Japan). A Swiss employee who receives a lukewarm, vaguely worded review in Tokyo may walk away believing their performance is fine, when the review was actually a carefully coded warning. A Japanese employee who receives a blunt, itemized critique in Zurich may interpret it as an unusually harsh personal attack, when it was simply standard professional register. Both misreadings are dangerous precisely because both cultures value process and documentation highly — the paperwork looks similarly serious in both places, but what it's actually saying requires two entirely different decoding skills.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *TeamBlind — An engineer questioned in a thread whether they were "too naive" after repeatedly missing indirect signals from a Japanese manager, describing feedback that arrived wrapped in phrases like "it might be worth considering" that turned out, months later, to have been a serious performance warning.*

> *Quora — Someone describing Swiss workplace norms wrote that colleagues expected a genuine "thank you for the honesty" after pointed criticism, and that visibly sulking or getting defensive — even briefly — was noted as a mark against your professionalism, not a sympathetic reaction.*

> *r/AskEurope — A Japanese professional who relocated to Zurich described the first performance review there as physically uncomfortable — not because the criticism was unfair, but because nothing about it was softened, and it took months to stop personalizing directness that colleagues clearly didn't intend as an attack.*

> *Substack (Hello World Japan) — A European intern's account described being told by a Japanese colleague, after a mistake, only that "we might think about a different approach next time" — a phrase the intern initially read as a mild suggestion and only later understood had been a formal correction.*

> *InterNations Zurich — A Japanese expat manager noted that she had to consciously train herself to give feedback more bluntly to Swiss subordinates, since her natural instinct to soften criticism was being read by her new team as evasive or unclear rather than polite.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Switzerland from a high-context culture, treat direct criticism as information, not confrontation — the bluntness is the respect. If you're moving to Japan from a low-context culture, learn to read the formal review structure itself as the message — the softness of the language is not an accident, it's a system, and the real content is often one layer beneath the words. The line I'd give a friend before either move: in Zurich, ask "what specifically should I change," and expect a precise answer; in Tokyo, ask the same question of a trusted colleague afterward, in private — because the review itself may not have said it out loud the first time.
