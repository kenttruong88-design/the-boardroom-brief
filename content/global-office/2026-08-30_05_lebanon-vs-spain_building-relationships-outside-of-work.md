# Lebanon vs. Spain: Two Ways to Build a Career by Not Talking About Work

## 🇱🇧 Lebanon · 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Lebanon, interpersonal trust sits at roughly 10 percent, one of the lowest readings Gallup records anywhere, and yet almost nothing of consequence gets done there without knowing somebody's cousin. In Spain, a country that ranks fourth in the OECD for work-life balance, employees devote nearly two hours after a business lunch to conversation that is, on paper, unrelated to business. Neither country trusts its institutions much. Both trust the table.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇱🇧 Lebanon

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the coffee, every time it's offered | Rush a first meeting straight to the agenda |
| Say yes to a spontaneous invitation, even a same-day one | Assume a warm welcome means the deal is done |
| Ask after someone's family before their job title | Decline hospitality outright — offer resistance, then accept |
| Expect the "real" negotiation to happen over a second or third meeting | Mistake generosity for an absence of shrewdness |
| Let a personal introduction (wasta) open doors before LinkedIn does | Treat a home dinner invitation as unusually intimate — it isn't |
| Bring a small gift if invited to a home | Split the bill without a token protest first |

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to the mid-morning coffee break (desayuno) | Schedule a serious meeting for 2 to 4 p.m. |
| Linger for the sobremesa after a work lunch | Leave the table the moment the plates are cleared |
| Let a group absorb you gradually through a shared friend | Expect instant inclusion in an existing friend circle |
| Use bars and terraces, not the office, as the real forum | Assume async or remote habits substitute for showing up |
| Learn Spanish beyond survival level | Stay inside the English-speaking expat bubble |
| Treat August as functionally over for business | Push for decisions during the summer lull |

## Lebanon

Hofstede's individualism score for Lebanon is 39, well into collectivist territory, and it shows up less in survey data than in scheduling. Lebanese business culture treats the personal relationship as a prerequisite for the professional one rather than a pleasant byproduct of it: meetings routinely open with coffee, family inquiries, and general conversation before anyone reaches for an agenda. Academic work on *wasta* — the informal networks of family, tribe, and friendship that substitute for weak formal institutions — describes it as simultaneously how business gets done and a driver of exactly the institutional weakness that makes it necessary in the first place, per a 2023 study in *Thunderbird International Business Review*.

That paradox has only sharpened since Lebanon's economic collapse. Gallup found roughly 76 percent of the population struggling to get by as the currency lost most of its value, and confidence in banks cratered to single digits. What survived, notably, wasn't institutional trust — it was the informal kind. Karam, the cultural imperative of hospitality, remained a "real pillar of daily life," according to accounts collected from newcomers, with home invitations arriving well before a Western visitor would expect them. The math is almost backwards: the less people trust the system, the more elaborately they invest in the people around it.

## Spain

Spain's individualism score of 51 puts it in the middle of the pack — more self-directed than Lebanon, considerably less atomized than the United States — and its relationship-building runs on a rhythm rather than an obligation. The split workday, with a long midday break historically built around siesta, has left institutional room for the sobremesa, the extended post-meal conversation that can run two hours past the last plate and routinely includes colleagues and clients rather than just family. The OECD's Better Life Index puts Spain fourth among member countries for work-life balance, with full-time workers logging 15.7 hours a day on leisure and personal care against an OECD average of 15 — modest on paper, structurally significant in practice, because so much of that time is scheduled socially rather than left to chance.

Spain also scores 57 on Hofstede's power-distance measure, which sits awkwardly next to all that conviviality: hierarchies are real and companies skew vertical, but the coffee break functions as the informal channel where hierarchy gets temporarily suspended and office politics actually get navigated. Skipping the desayuno isn't neutral — it's read as a mild refusal to participate in the part of the job that isn't written down.

## The Reckoning

Put side by side, the two systems look like mirror images built to solve the same problem — low confidence in formal structures — with opposite tools. Lebanon compresses relationship-building into intensity: the invitation comes fast, the hospitality is immediate and slightly competitive, and the emotional investment happens up front, before either party knows if there's a deal at all. Spain stretches relationship-building into duration: the invitation is implicit (just show up to the bar), the pace is unhurried, and trust accumulates through repeated, low-stakes exposure — coffee break by coffee break, sobremesa by sobremesa — rather than a single generous gesture.

The counterintuitive part is which one is harder to break into. Lebanese hospitality, precisely because it's so immediate, gives outsiders an early foothold — you're inside someone's home before you've earned it in any conventional sense. Spanish sociability, precisely because it's so unhurried, can leave outsiders circling the outside of a friend group for months, waiting for the one shared connection that finally pulls them in. Fast trust, it turns out, isn't the same as fast belonging.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A newcomer to Spain described existing friend groups as very hard to crack from the outside, but noted that once you're accepted by a single member, you're gradually introduced to the whole circle rather than left to network your own way in.*

> *Quora — A remote worker living in Madrid for a year, self-described as introverted, observed that Spain isn't a particularly introvert-friendly culture and that bars, not any organized activity, are simply where socializing defaults to.*

> *The Local Spain (thelocal.es) — An explainer on sobremesa noted it routinely stretches two hours past the end of a meal and, unlike in some cultures, explicitly extends to colleagues and business associates, not just family.*

> *Personal expat blog (Beirutista, an American expat's account of life in Beirut) — The writer described the warmth of Lebanese hospitality as a genuine surprise on arrival, with home invitations arriving from acquaintances far sooner than would be typical elsewhere.*

> *Personal expat blog (Jarnias Cyril's expat guide to Lebanon) — The advice given to newcomers was to actively say yes to spontaneous coffee invitations and not habitually decline dinner invites, framed as the single most useful adjustment for integrating into daily life.*

## Conclusion

Neither country's approach to relationship-building is really optional overhead bolted onto the job — in both, it functions as the actual infrastructure that low-trust or slow-moving formal systems can't provide. In Lebanon, showing up for the coffee and the home visit isn't networking adjacent to the work; for many purposes, it is the work. In Spain, skipping the desayuno or bolting after lunch doesn't just cost a few friendships — it quietly caps how far someone gets, since so much real coordination happens at the table rather than in the inbox.

The practical synthesis, unglamorous as it is: budget actual calendar time for the parts of the job that don't look like the job. In Lebanon, that means treating the second coffee as non-negotiable. In Spain, it means treating the two-hour lunch as a meeting you weren't told about. Anyone hoping to network efficiently in either country should recalibrate expectations accordingly — efficiency was never really the point.
