**[HEADLINE] The Leave Is Owed. The Flight Off the Island Is Not: India vs. the Maldives**

**🇮🇳 India · 🇲🇻 Maldives**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

An Indian software engineer with fifteen years of state-mandated earned leave will apologize for using four of them. A resort employee in the Maldives, entitled by law to thirty paid days a year, may spend some of that leave on the same sixty-hectare island where they work, because the only way off is a seaplane the resort books, not one you do. Both countries technically guarantee you time off. Only one of them guarantees you somewhere to spend it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask HR for your specific state's leave rules before signing — entitlements run from roughly 12 days to 21-plus depending on whether you're in Gujarat, Andhra Pradesh, or West Bengal | Assume "unlimited PTO" on a term sheet means anything; it is frequently a euphemism for no tracked entitlement and no cultural permission to use it |
| Negotiate leave encashment at hiring — many employers let you cash out unused earned leave annually or on exit | Expect silence from your phone while on approved leave; WhatsApp-based "urgent" pings routinely cross into vacation time |
| Check your city's public holiday count before planning travel — it ranges from about 14 days in Delhi to nearly 39 in Sikkim | Take undocumented mental-health days; most sick-leave policies require a medical certificate after one or two days |
| Pre-clear long leave informally with your manager before filing it in the HR system | Book non-refundable travel around festivals without confirming your specific employer's festival-holiday list, which is not standardized nationally |
| Budget time for the incoming Labour Codes to change entitlements again — they are passed but not yet uniformly in force | Treat a verbal "take as much time as you need" from a founder or manager as binding; get it in the offer letter |

#### 🇲🇻 Maldives

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm in writing whether your statutory 30 days of annual leave includes travel time home, since resort contracts often bundle them | Assume you can leave the resort island on a day off; about 89% of resort staff live on-site, per the Maldives Bureau of Statistics |
| Ask specifically about "R&R" rotations in your offer letter — it is a defined hospitality rostering term, not a vague perk | Expect your leave calendar to match Western holidays; Maldivian public holidays follow the Islamic lunar calendar and shift every year |
| Factor employer-covered food, housing, and often medical care into your real net pay, not just the salary line | Sign before clarifying how service charge is split and whether it is guaranteed or occupancy-dependent |
| Clarify your sick-leave allowance (statutorily up to 30 paid days) and what documentation triggers it | Count on quick repatriation in an emergency; inter-island and international transfers from a resort atoll take real planning |
| Ask how many of your colleagues are foreign nationals on the same visa track — about 58% of the resort workforce is, which shapes how leave requests get prioritized | Assume "island life" means leisure; the island you live on is also the island you clean, cook in, or manage |

---

Start with the paperwork, because in India it is genuinely confusing by design. There is no single national annual-leave number. The Factories Act of 1948 sets a baseline of earned leave accrued by days worked, but implementation runs through each state's own Shops and Establishments Act, and the results diverge sharply: Andhra Pradesh offers 15 days accumulable up to 60, Gujarat stacks up to 5 days per 60 worked plus a further 21 after 240 days, and West Bengal grants a flat 14 days of privilege leave, according to the WageIndicator labour law database. Public holiday counts are just as scattered, ranging from about 14 days in Delhi to nearly 39 in Sikkim. India's long-awaited Labour Codes, meant to simplify this into one framework, have been passed but are still not uniformly in force, so for now the patchwork holds.

What makes this more than a filing-cabinet problem is that Indian professionals barely use the leave they do have. Expedia's Vacation Deprivation research, reported by WION News, found 75% of Indians feel vacation-deprived — the highest share among surveyed countries — with 41% having skipped a vacation entirely in the prior six months and 17% taking zero leave across a full year, ranking India fifth globally for unused entitlement. InterNations' Expat Insider survey placed India 58th out of the countries ranked for work-life balance. None of this is explained by law; it is explained by workplace hierarchy. Hofstede Insights scores India among the more hierarchical societies measured on its power-distance dimension, which tracks tidily with a culture where "self-service" leave portals exist, but the real approval still runs informally through a manager who will remember who asked. A newly arrived expatriate hire, used to a workplace where booking leave is an administrative transaction, will usually discover this the first time a one-line approval request turns into a week of hallway diplomacy.

The Maldives inverts the problem entirely. Its Employment Act sets one of the more generous statutory baselines in the region: 30 paid annual leave days after a year of service, accruing at 2.5 days a month, alongside up to 30 paid sick days and 60 days of maternity leave, per Rivermate's employment guide. Public holidays follow both fixed dates — Independence Day on 26–27 July, Republic Day on 11 November — and the Islamic lunar calendar, so Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha move every year and must be tracked, not assumed. On paper, nobody in the Maldivian tourism sector is leave-starved.

The catch is geography. The Maldives Bureau of Statistics' Resort Employee Survey found that 89% of resort staff live on the resort island itself, and foreign nationals made up 58% of the tourism workforce as of the last count. Indian workers are a substantial part of that population — diaspora estimates put roughly 29,000 Indians living and working across the Maldives, concentrated in medicine, skilled trades, business roles, and tourism. A day off, in this context, is not automatically a day away. "Leave" in resort hospitality frequently means an R&R rotation off the atoll on a fixed schedule set by the employer, not a personal decision made the way it would be onshore, and getting from a one-island resort back to Malé or home to India is a logistics exercise, not a walk to the car.

**The Reckoning**

Here is the part that should unsettle anyone comparing the two purely by entitlement: the Maldives' legal leave allowance is nearly double what most Indian states offer, and yet Indian professionals, paradoxically, often have an easier time actually leaving. A software engineer in Bengaluru with 15 contractual days can, in theory, book a weekend getaway with two hours' notice. A resort employee in the Maldives with 30 legally guaranteed days may need a seaplane seat, a rostering slot, and a manager's sign-off weeks in advance just to use them, because the workplace and the only exit route are the same company.

The two cultures also fail in opposite directions. India's dysfunction is behavioral — adequate leave on the books, undermined by guilt, hierarchy, and an always-on messaging culture that makes using it feel like a confession. The Maldives' dysfunction is structural — generous leave in the statute books, bottlenecked by physical isolation, visa-tied contracts, and an industry where your home and your workplace share a shoreline. One is a culture that won't let you leave your desk. The other is a geography that won't let you leave the island. Neither failure shows up on the offer letter, which is precisely why both deserve more scrutiny than either country's tourism board or recruiter will volunteer.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — a longtime resort hospitality manager noted that newcomers rarely ask about service-charge splits before signing, then are frustrated months later when a third of their "salary" turns out to be a variable tip pool tied to the resort's monthly occupancy.*

> *Expat.com forum — another poster on the same thread pointed out that accommodation, food, and utilities are covered outright at most resorts, so a modest listed wage can still be livable once set against mainland rent and grocery costs.*

> *Blind — one commenter who relocated from the US to India described the work pace as dramatically lighter than expected, only to be corrected by a colleague who said the workload was identical, just conducted through meetings scheduled for US Pacific hours after dark.*

> *Blind — a separate commenter familiar with offshore banking units in Gurgaon and Bangalore argued that the relaxed midday rhythm visitors often praise reflects lower pay for the same output, not genuine slack in the job.*

> *LinkedIn, via Business Today — a talent-acquisition professional recounted asking for two days off in India and being questioned first about what was wrong and second about whether he'd still be reachable, and argued the fix isn't a new HR policy but a workplace that stops treating rest as a favor owed to good behavior.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to India for work, the leave fight you'll have is mostly psychological: the days exist in your contract, and your job is to actually take them without apologizing, scheduling around festival-season guilt, or answering your phone anyway. If you're moving to the Maldives for resort or tourism work, the leave fight is logistical: the days are statutorily generous, but whether they translate into a flight home, a rotation off the atoll, or genuine time away from your employer depends on contract terms most new arrivals don't think to ask about until they're already living on the island in question.

Either way, read the leave clause before the salary line, not after, and ask the question recruiters least want to answer in detail: what does a typical week off actually look like for someone already in this role, not for the policy itself. Leave policies are written by HR; leave culture is enforced by everyone else in the building, or on the island.

And if a friend asked me over a drink which one to pick: in India, the paperwork says you're allowed to leave your desk; in the Maldives, you're already on an island, and the paperwork decides whether you're allowed to leave it.
