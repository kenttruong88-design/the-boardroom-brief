**Japan Tried Remote Work and Went Back to the Office. Turkey Tried It and Found a New Workforce.**

**🇯🇵 Japan · 🇹🇷 Turkey**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Japan, the share of employees teleworking actually fell after the pandemic peak — from 27% in 2021 to 24.8% in 2023 — and at least one prominent tech company that went fully remote in 2020 has since pulled everyone back to the office five days a week. In Turkey, 74% of business leaders surveyed say they support remote work long-term, and the shift to flexible arrangements has helped push female workforce participation in remote-eligible roles from roughly 50% to 76% in a single year. Same global disruption, same starting shock, two workforces that drew opposite lessons from it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly, before joining, how many office days a week are actually expected now vs. during peak-pandemic policy | Assume a company's 2020-era remote policy still applies today |
| Expect foreign-owned or multinational employers to offer more remote flexibility than domestic Japanese firms | Assume all Japanese employers treat remote work the same way |
| Treat in-person presence as tied to trust-building, especially in your first year | Assume remote output alone will be judged purely on merit early on |
| Learn Japanese if you want full access to remote-eligible, higher-flexibility roles | Expect strong remote opportunities without local language skills |
| Watch how manufacturing- and retail-adjacent industries differ sharply from office/tech roles | Generalize one industry's remote policy across the whole economy |
| Note four-day workweek pilots (Microsoft Japan, Tokyo civil service) as a sign of where things may head | Assume current low telework rates are the permanent ceiling |

#### 🇹🇷 Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect real hybrid or fully remote options in tech, finance, and digital-marketing roles | Assume remote flexibility extends to manufacturing, logistics, or retail work |
| Treat flexibility as a genuine competitive factor employers use to attract talent | Assume salary alone determines whether a strong candidate accepts an offer |
| Check your visa and tax status carefully if working remotely from Turkey for a foreign employer | Assume remote work from Turkey is automatically legal on a tourist visa |
| Expect major local employers (Turkcell, Koç Holding) to have established hybrid frameworks | Assume only foreign or startup employers offer flexible arrangements |
| Recognize remote work as a meaningful driver of women's labour-force participation | Treat flexible policy as a minor perk rather than a structural opportunity |
| Prioritize Istanbul- and Ankara-based tech hubs if flexibility is a top priority | Expect the same flexibility norms in every Turkish city or sector |

**Japan**

Japan's relationship with remote work is a case of adoption without full acceptance. The share of firms offering work-from-home options jumped from 20% pre-pandemic to 48% in 2020, but usage has since retreated: nationwide telework participation dropped from 27.0% in 2021 to 24.8% by 2023, with regional variation stark — 42.1% in the Tokyo metropolitan area against just 17.7% in regional urban centers. Academic research on Japan's telework "digital divide" points to deep cultural factors, not just technology gaps: a strong preference for in-person presence and direct supervision, tied to Hofstede Insights' characterization of Japan as a high-context, hierarchy-sensitive, collectivist culture (46 on Individualism, a borderline-hierarchical 54 on Power Distance) where trust and evaluation have traditionally run through physical co-presence.

The retreat is visible at the company level too — one cloud-software firm that went fully remote in 2020 has since required five-day in-office attendance — even as counter-signals emerge: Microsoft Japan's widely cited 2019 four-day-workweek trial boosted productivity by roughly 40%, and Tokyo's municipal government is piloting a four-day week for civil servants starting in 2025. Measured WFH productivity in Japan also lags in-office productivity by around 20% in some studies, a gap researchers partly attribute to the kind of employees who opt into remote roles rather than remote work itself being inherently less productive — but it's a statistic Japanese management cites often enough to matter for policy.

**Turkey**

Turkey's trajectory point in almost the opposite direction, treating pandemic-era remote work less as an emergency measure to unwind and more as infrastructure worth keeping. Per surveys of Turkish business leaders, 74% support continuing remote work long-term, against just 15% opposed outright and 11% who tried it and reverted. Major domestic employers — Turkcell and Koç Holding among them — have built out lasting hybrid frameworks rather than one-off pandemic policies, and adoption concentrates heavily in tech, digital marketing, e-commerce, finance, and shared-services roles, the same knowledge-work sectors that lead remote adoption globally.

The clearest social effect shows up in the labor-force data: remote work access is credited with pushing women's participation in remote-eligible roles from about 50% to 76% within roughly a year, a striking shift given Turkey's overall female labour-force participation rate hovers closer to 35% nationally — implying flexible work is disproportionately unlocking participation that more rigid office structures were suppressing. Hofstede Insights scores Turkey notably high on Uncertainty Avoidance (85), which typically predicts a preference for clear rules and structure; Turkish employers appear to have squared that instinct with remote flexibility by building explicit hybrid frameworks and formal remote-work legal provisions (Labor Law Article 14) rather than leaving arrangements informal, satisfying the cultural preference for defined rules while still extending real flexibility.

**The Reckoning**

The counterintuitive pairing here is that Turkey's higher Uncertainty Avoidance — a trait that should, in theory, make change harder to sustain — actually produced more durable remote-work adoption than Japan's comparatively more flexible cultural profile, because Turkish employers formalized remote work into explicit rules and legal frameworks rather than treating it as an ambiguous, trust-dependent exception. Japan's hesitance isn't really about rules at all; it's about a workplace trust system built on physical presence that remote arrangements structurally undermine, regardless of how clearly a policy is written down.

The other divergence is who benefits most. In Turkey, remote flexibility functions as a labor-market equalizer, pulling in workers — especially women — who were previously locked out by rigid office structures. In Japan, remote work remains disproportionately available to those already advantaged: Tokyo-based, multinational-employed, and often foreign or foreign-adjacent workers, while regional and traditional-sector employees see comparatively little of the flexibility dividend.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A response to whether work-from-home jobs exist in Japan noted that they do, but flagged the visa requirement as the real gatekeeper — legal work authorization, not employer willingness, is often the first barrier a foreign remote worker hits.*

> *Quora — Someone answering how work culture in Japan actually feels described a sharp divide between foreign-owned multinationals, which tend to offer genuinely easier, more flexible working environments, and fully domestic Japanese companies, where the experience remains distinctly more traditional regardless of stated remote policy.*

> *Quora — A Pakistan-based remote software engineer asking whether relocating to Turkey made sense specifically framed the country as attractive precisely because of its remote-work-friendly reputation and lower cost of living relative to income earned abroad — a calculation increasingly common among international remote workers scouting Turkey as a base.*

> *Quora — A separate question about working remotely from Turkey on a tourist visa, with no local banking or company ties, pointed to a real gray area in enforcement — a reminder that Turkey's welcoming remote-work reputation coexists with a legal framework that foreign remote workers still need to navigate carefully rather than assume is settled.*

### Conclusion

If flexibility is a top priority, Turkey currently offers more of it more reliably — codified into law, embraced by major employers, and treated as a genuine talent-retention tool rather than a grudging concession. Japan can still offer real remote flexibility, but you'll need to select carefully: multinational employer, Tokyo-based role, language skills that open the widest set of options — because the national trend line is currently pointing back toward the office, not away from it. The honest version for a friend deciding between them: in Turkey, ask what the remote policy is; in Japan, ask what it actually is this year, because it may not be what it was in 2021.
