# Taiwan and Japan Trust You to Work From Home, From the Office

🇹🇼 Taiwan · 🇯🇵 Japan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

According to the Daiwa Institute of Research, 24.6 percent of Japan's workforce were employed teleworkers in fiscal 2024, and the rate has been stable since fiscal 2023. In Taiwan, a 2022 poll by 104 Job Bank found that 71 percent of employers wanted staff back in the office and 0.3 percent favoured full-time remote work. The two economies are often filed together under East Asian presenteeism, which is fair, but the filing system hides some differences that anyone relocating should know before asking about a laptop.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask a prospective employer in writing whether work-from-home days exist and who approves them | Assume a startup or tech firm's flexibility extends to the whole company |
| Follow a senior colleague's instructions closely and build a relationship with your boss | Suggest improvements to your line manager in your first weeks |
| Offer feedback privately and face to face where possible | Criticise a colleague in a team meeting |
| Show output in terms the company can count, since managers value revenue and KPIs | Rely on goodwill to prove you are working at home |
| Show interest in colleagues' lives and bring back a small gift after trips | Skip lunches and the personal chat that builds guanxi |
| Expect to be reachable and, if asked, to confirm your location | Treat a remote day as a day without supervision |

### Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get remote-work terms written into the employment contract | Rely on a verbal promise, which is harder to prove |
| Check how many office days a hybrid role requires, since many are expected four or five days | Assume hybrid means the same thing at every firm |
| Propose a compromise such as video calls plus occasional office visits | Open with an ultimatum about your rights |
| Ask whether the company has laptops, secure access and paid collaboration software | Assume the paperwork has gone digital |
| Raise remote work in regular one-to-ones, ideally with a trial | Raise it for the first time in a performance review |
| Ask whether the firm covers commuting costs for required office visits | Take the commute allowance for granted |

## Taiwan: Hybrid in Spirit, Visible in Practice

The Taiwan Gold Card portal, run by the government, describes a work culture that is more conservative and slower to change, in which managers often do not trust employees to work from home and performance is judged largely by physical presence and supervised hours. Al Jazeera described workplaces in 2021 as hierarchical, with long hours and an emphasis on face time, and noted that 97.5 percent of Taiwanese businesses are small or medium enterprises, which are less likely to adopt remote work. A Taipei employment lawyer told the broadcaster that attitudes vary by industry, with tech workers used to remote work and local businesses focused on whether a style of working generates revenue.

The survey record is dated but instructive. The 104 Job Bank poll, reported by Outsource Accelerator, found 65 percent of employees preferring hybrid arrangements, 30 percent preferring the office and 5 percent preferring home, against employers of whom 29 percent were open to hybrid at all. Focus Taiwan reported separately in April 2022 that 63.3 percent of companies surveyed by a job site planned to bring back work-from-home policies during a Covid wave, which suggests flexibility in Taiwan has mostly been something that arrives with an emergency and leaves with it. The government, according to Al Jazeera, offered no financial incentives to firms that allowed remote work.

Hofstede Insights is, as ever, the cheerful background hum. In the secondary summary seen here, Taiwan scores 93 for long-term orientation, which is consistent with an employer culture that remembers a manufacturing past: one Taipei consultancy head told Al Jazeera that employees clock in and out, tardiness is sometimes shamed, and most of Taiwan's GDP is export-related, which keeps factories staffed on site and on time. The consultancy head also wants staff in the office because it aids communication, a view with many sympathisers. The Gold Card portal adds that feedback in Taiwan tends to be non-confrontational and that seniority carries significant weight, so asking a new manager to rethink attendance is a delicate matter. Exceptions exist. A sales director at Tern Bicycles Taiwan said his focus was always on output and capability, not physical presence, which suggests flexibility depends heavily on who the employer is.

## Japan: The Hanko Is Still Warm

Japan's headline figure is higher than the stereotype implies, and the gap is revealing. Daiwa reports that teleworkers in fiscal 2024 averaged two or more remote days a week, that the rate was 31.2 percent for men and 16.9 percent for women, and that more than 60 percent of teleworkers want to continue, most often at two days a week. Hybrid, in other words, has quietly become an established arrangement for a quarter of the workforce. The other three quarters, who include most service, health care and blue-collar workers, were never going to see a laptop.

For those who could work remotely, the direction is mixed. TokyoDev's 2024 survey found that respondents able to choose fully remote work fell from 43 percent in 2023 to 38 percent in 2024, and cites Morgan McKinley's finding that 62 percent of Japanese companies require employees in the office at least some days. Morgan McKinley's own figures show 29 percent of employees expected in the office four or five days a week, while 30 percent prefer three days. Among those surveyed, 55 percent said mandatory office presence increases stress and burnout and 38 percent said it increases their desire to leave.

The structural explanation is older. The Japan Times reported in 2021 that small and midsize businesses make up 99.7 percent of Japanese businesses and employ about 70 percent of the working population, and that the government had not mandated remote work. Researcher Ryusuke Ishii attributed the reluctance partly to older managers, with the average age of corporate presidents at a record 60.1. TokyoDev adds a useful legal note: Japanese law has no specific provision on return-to-office mandates, and the contract wording is central. The Hofstede summary seen here gives Japan 95 for masculinity, which fits a country that treats long hours as a character reference.

Paper still matters. A 2020 article on the Izanau site reported that Japan had 93.3 fax machines per 1,000 people against 55.3 in the United States, that company seals cannot leave the office, and that in a March 2020 survey of 21,448 people, 71 percent said their company had not addressed telework. The Japan Times found full-time remote work at just 2.4 percent in July 2021. TokyoDev's survey adds a corporate cost: 39 percent of full-time office workers were job hunting, against 10 percent of those who could choose where to work. Employers who insist on attendance may be paying for it in recruitment.

## The Reckoning

Both countries confirm that employees want hybrid and employers want attendance, but Japan has the larger installed base of flexibility and the more explicit legal conversation about contracts. Taiwan's evidence is thinner and older, and what it shows is a sharp split between tech firms that measure output and local businesses that measure presence. A foreigner in Japan is more likely to find a policy; in Taiwan, more likely to find a manager.

The practical difference is where to negotiate. In Japan the contract is the instrument, and TokyoDev's contributors suggest that small firms may find an employee hard to replace, which gives some leverage. In Taiwan the relationship with the supervisor is the instrument, and the guidance points to building trust before asking for flexibility. In both places, flexibility offered informally is flexibility that can be withdrawn informally.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Japan Today — Commenters on a telework piece said managers cite security to resist home working, that a bank retirement form reverted to paper, and that cramped housing makes home offices unappealing.*

> *inkl — A tech worker in Taiwan said her company ordered half of staff home with no flexibility for parents, and that there was not a lot of trust; others reported managers asking for GPS tracking or snap polls on whether staff were at their desks.*

> *TokyoDev — Developers in Japan trading advice recommended offering to cover office visits with video calls, and noted that verbal promises of remote work are difficult to enforce.*

> *Japan Times — An HR worker at a manufacturer said the people at the top were against remote work, and a marketing employee called her firm very traditional and top-down.*

> *Al Jazeera — Taiwanese employees posted that supervisors refused remote work because they doubted productivity, and some were told they could work from home but would not be paid.*

## Conclusion

For a prospective mover, the sensible approach in both countries is to treat flexibility as a contract term, not a culture. In Japan, ask for the arrangement in writing and learn how many days are truly required. In Taiwan, ask who approves home days, what output is measured, and whether the arrangement survives the end of whatever emergency created it.

Neither country is hostile to hybrid in principle, since employees in both want it and many already have it. The difficulty lies in the managers who approve it, and they will approve it exactly as soon as they can see it working.
