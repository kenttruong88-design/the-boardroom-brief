# The Review Nobody Argues With: Feedback in Nigeria and Kazakhstan

**🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇰🇿 Kazakhstan**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Ask a junior employee in Lagos to formally rate their manager, and the responses that come back are, by every account, diplomatically inaccurate — a system asking for candor from people whose culture has correctly taught them candor upward can cost them something. Ask the same question in Almaty, and you'll run into a related but distinct problem: a post-Soviet administrative legacy where the feedback loop, going in either direction, was never really built to begin with. Both countries have hierarchical workplaces. Both have performance review systems that quietly struggle against that hierarchy rather than working with it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Deliver feedback to junior staff clearly, constructively, and with real empathy — Nigerian HR guidance explicitly stresses managing bias and navigating sensitive conversations carefully | Don't expect candid upward feedback in a formal 360-style system; junior employees rating a manager honestly is documented as a genuine cultural friction point, not a hypothetical one |
| Recognize and reward achievement visibly and specifically — a strong performance culture is described as one where contributions are explicitly connected to broader organizational goals | Don't assume silence from a subordinate after a critical review means agreement or acceptance; it may simply reflect deference to hierarchy rather than genuine buy-in |
| Build regular, ongoing feedback moments rather than relying solely on an annual formal review | Don't treat a Nigerian team's warm, "family"-style workplace culture (frequently cited in employee reviews) as evidence that direct critical feedback will land easily; warmth and appetite for blunt critique are not the same thing |
| Frame reviews around growth and development explicitly, since job satisfaction is described as heavily dependent on a direct manager's individual leadership style | Don't assume review quality is consistent across a large organization; anecdotal accounts suggest it varies enormously by individual manager and department |

#### 🇰🇿 Kazakhstan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect formality and status-awareness in how reviews are delivered, especially in government-linked or state-owned organizations, where authoritarian management styles remain common | Don't expect a well-developed, consistent constructive-feedback loop by default; research on Kazakhstani organizations specifically flags "the absence of efficient methods for employee development" as an ongoing structural issue |
| Treat the end-of-probation review as a genuinely consequential checkpoint — it formally determines whether an employee's permanent status gets confirmed | Don't assume Soviet-legacy hierarchy has fully faded in newer or private-sector firms; even growing professional ambition sits layered on top of, not in place of, older status-aware norms |
| Show visible respect for age and seniority when receiving or giving feedback, consistent with the broader hierarchical culture | Don't push for open, egalitarian-style feedback exchange as a default expectation; leadership in Kazakhstan is described as feeling notably more top-down than in highly egalitarian cultures |
| Build relationships with a trusted local colleague or go-between who can help you read what's genuinely being communicated in a review, beyond its formal language | Don't rely purely on the formal review document as a complete picture of your standing; much of the real signal travels through informal, relationship-mediated channels instead |

### Nigeria: Hierarchy That Muffles the Upward Half of the Conversation

Nigerian performance-review culture has a well-documented asymmetry: downward feedback — from manager to employee — functions reasonably normally, with HR guidance from firms like Matog Consulting and ICS Outsourcing pushing for clarity, empathy, and connection to organizational goals. Upward feedback is where the system breaks down. Mondaq's coverage of employee-productivity evaluation in Nigeria describes the core problem directly: when junior employees are asked to formally rate a manager in a new system, the responses tend to be "diplomatically inaccurate" — a polite way of saying the hierarchy makes honest upward criticism functionally unsafe, whatever the org chart says about anonymity.

Employee reviews aggregated on Indeed paint a workplace culture that is genuinely warm in daily texture — "team spirit and family," "fun and energetic" being common descriptors — which makes the upward-feedback gap easy to miss on the surface. The warmth is real; it just doesn't automatically translate into psychological safety for criticizing a superior, and a newcomer instituting a Western-style 360-review process without accounting for that gap will get data that looks clean and is, in practice, close to useless.

### Kazakhstan: A Feedback Loop Still Being Built

Kazakhstan's challenge is structurally different and, in some ways, more foundational. Commisceo Global's management guide describes a workplace culture still visibly shaped by "Soviet legacy" alongside nomadic and tribal traditions — formal, status-aware, with leadership that "can feel more top down than in highly egalitarian cultures," especially inside government and state-owned organizations where authoritarian management styles persist most strongly. An academic study on organizational learning in Kazakhstan, pointedly titled "No Room for Mistakes," found that Kazakhstani organizations frequently lack "efficient methods for employee development, such as a constructive feedback loop" at all — not a cultural preference for indirection, but a genuine gap in the infrastructure of feedback itself.

Where a formal review process does exist and carries real weight, it tends to cluster around specific structural checkpoints — most notably the end-of-probation review, which directly determines permanent employment status — rather than functioning as an ongoing developmental rhythm. Expat accounts of working in Kazakhstan, per Expat Arrivals, consistently note that a great deal of the real signal about how you're doing travels through informal, relationship-based channels rather than the documented review process, echoing a pattern seen elsewhere in this pillar's coverage of post-Soviet workplace cultures.

### The Reckoning

Both countries share a structural symptom — hierarchy suppressing honest feedback — but the underlying mechanism differs in a way that matters for anyone trying to fix it. Nigeria's downward-feedback machinery works reasonably well; it's specifically the upward channel that hierarchy chokes off, meaning a foreign manager's fix should focus on building genuinely safe, indirect, or anonymized ways for junior staff to flag concerns upward. Kazakhstan's gap is more symmetrical and more foundational — the entire feedback loop, in many organizations, is underdeveloped rather than selectively suppressed, meaning the fix looks less like "make upward feedback safer" and more like "build feedback infrastructure that doesn't yet fully exist."

The ironic overlap: in both places, the formal review document is a genuinely incomplete picture of where you stand, and the real information travels through relationships, tone, and informal cues that no performance-management software captures.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Indeed.com, First Bank of Nigeria employee reviews — Multiple reviewers described the workplace atmosphere as "fun and energetic" with "cordial relationships between colleagues," but separately noted that overall job satisfaction depended heavily and specifically on which line manager or departmental head an employee reported to — a variance reviewers attributed to leadership style rather than any company-wide policy.*

> *Indeed.com, aggregate Nigeria workplace reviews — Several reviewers characterized workplace culture as capable of turning "toxic depending on your boss," a pattern consistent with a system where downward feedback flows relatively freely but upward accountability for how that feedback gets delivered has few real checks.*

> *Expat.com, Almaty forum — A contributor working at a Kazakhstani university described navigating employment contracts written across three languages (English, Russian, and Kazakh) and noted that understanding one's actual standing at the institution required cultivating a trusted local colleague, since formal correspondence alone left real ambiguity about where things stood.*

> *Expat Arrivals, "Working in Kazakhstan" — The guide specifically advises securing a job before arrival and warns that negotiating contracts and understanding organizational expectations "without a trusted go-between" is genuinely difficult — advice that extends naturally to reading performance feedback once employed, not just landing the initial offer.*

### Conclusion

If you're managing in either country, the honest fix isn't a better review template — it's building trust through a channel the formal system doesn't reach. In Nigeria, that means creating deliberately safer, less exposed ways for junior staff to actually tell you the truth, since the standard upward-feedback mechanism is documented as unreliable by design, not by accident. In Kazakhstan, it means recognizing that the feedback loop you're used to may simply not exist yet in the infrastructure sense, and investing in the relationship-based channels that currently do the job informally. Either way, the review document you get at the end of the cycle is a floor, not a ceiling, on what you actually need to know about how you're doing.
