# Never Wrong, Never Off Duty: How China and India Do Hierarchy

## 🇨🇳 China · 🇮🇳 India

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Hofstede Insights puts China's Power Distance score at 80 and India's at 77, which is statistical shorthand for "your boss's word is close to final in both places, so don't get any ideas." What the number does not capture is the mood in the room while that word is being delivered: in Beijing, in near-total silence; in Bengaluru, over a very long phone call that started as a work update and ended as a discussion of your cousin's wedding. Same hierarchy, radically different soundtrack.

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address your boss by title plus surname (Wang Zong, Li Jingli), not first name | Default to first-name informality on day one because it felt friendly |
| Raise disagreement privately, after the meeting, framed as a question | Contradict a decision in front of colleagues, even when you're demonstrably right |
| Invest in guanxi outside the office — dinners, toasts, showing up | Assume competence alone will fast-track trust or promotion |
| Confirm who actually holds final sign-off before you act on "approval" | Treat a mid-level yes as the end of the approval chain |
| Let the most senior person speak first and signal when a meal or meeting ends | Leave a dinner, or a discussion, before your superior indicates it's over |
| Give credit upward, generously and publicly | Point out a superior's mistake in front of the team, however gently |

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use "Sir" or "Ma'am" or a title until explicitly told otherwise | Assume first-name terms mean the hierarchy has actually flattened |
| Raise concerns privately and frame them as questions, not corrections | Challenge a decision publicly, in a meeting, in front of the team |
| Expect — and accept — your manager asking about your family and personal life | Read personal interest from a boss as overreach; it's relationship management |
| Verify that a "yes" or a nod means agreement, not just acknowledgment | Take silence, a head-wobble, or "we'll see" as a firm commitment |
| Clarify who signs off if your manager says "let me check with someone" | Assume the person you report to has full autonomy to decide |
| Set explicit boundaries on after-hours availability in your first week | Be surprised when a 9pm call is treated as an unremarkable Tuesday |

### China: The Silence Is the Message

Chinese workplace hierarchy is not merely respected, it is engineered — a legacy of Confucian ordering that assigns everyone a fixed position relative to everyone else, and expects the arrangement to be honored without commentary. According to CKGSB Knowledge, that structure is loosening at the edges as younger professionals push for flatter reporting lines and two-way feedback, but the shift is generational and slow, not a wholesale renovation. In the meantime, an OECD-affiliated management survey spanning twenty countries found Chinese firms clustered toward the bottom of global management-quality rankings, citing weak internal accountability and a reliance on top-down instruction rather than distributed decision-making — a finding that reads less like an indictment of competence and more like a description of how the system was built to run.

The practical result is a workplace where dissent is expressed through omission. A Chinese manager rarely says no; a proposal is more often met with "we'll think about it," which functions as a full stop dressed as a comma. Subordinates, in turn, rarely correct a superior in the room, preferring silence to the alternative of causing someone to lose face — a concept so load-bearing in Chinese professional life that entire consulting careers exist to explain it to foreigners who keep tripping over it.

### India: The Hierarchy That Calls You at Night

India's workplace hierarchy is just as pronounced, per Hofstede, but it wears a warmer face: bosses are expected to be directive and protective at once, tracking not just deliverables but the well-being of the people producing them. Deloitte's India Culture Sensing Report found a Culture Index of 82 out of 100 among the country's Global Capability Centres, with organizations scoring especially well on empowerment and inclusion — evidence that the paternalism is, on balance, experienced as care rather than control. The trouble is what that care demands in return.

Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2026 report found Indian employee engagement at a four-year low, with manager engagement in South Asia dropping eight points in a single year, the steepest regional decline measured — a slump Gallup ties partly to shrinking management layers and rising pressure on whoever remains. The everyday texture of that pressure shows up less in survey data than in the expectation that a "yes" from a junior colleague signals understanding rather than agreement, that meetings exist to transmit decisions rather than debate them, and that being reachable at 11pm is simply part of showing commitment, not a violation of anything.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the near-identical Hofstede scores start to look like the least interesting fact about either country. China enforces distance through restraint — the boss who never socializes with the shop floor, the meeting where nobody but the senior-most voice is heard, the org chart that functions like scripture. India enforces distance through intimacy — the boss who knows your family, asks after your health, and will absolutely call you at dinner time, precisely because the relationship is understood to run both directions, all the time.

The counterintuitive part is which system produces more reported friction for foreign employees. China's version is easier to misread as cold and harder to actually violate, since the rules, once learned, are consistent. India's version feels more welcoming on arrival and is, by Gallup's numbers, quietly grinding its own workforce down — the warmth is real, but so is the unlimited availability it's quietly priced into the relationship.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — One respondent described watching a colleague get promoted into a management role almost entirely on the strength of his personal network, despite having neither the education nor the technical grounding the job required, and noted that competence was treated as a secondary qualification at best.*

> *Blind — A poster new to a Chinese-led team described colleagues greeting every instruction from their manager with exaggerated smiles and nervous laughter, and admitted it took months to realize the behavior wasn't warmth but risk management — decisions were being shaped to please the boss rather than to serve the product.*

> *Quora — A different respondent, asked what it was like working for an Indian boss, described a manager who tracked birthdays, family milestones, and personal setbacks in as much detail as project deadlines, and said the directiveness stopped feeling controlling once it became clear how much the same manager was quietly advocating for the team upward.*

> *Blind — A commenter working in an India-based team described being expected to remain reachable for calls until nearly 11pm most weeknights, framing it not as an occasional crunch but as an ordinary baseline of visibility that nobody above them questioned.*

> *Reported via Deccan Herald, from a Russian professional's widely shared social media post about her twelve years working in Bengaluru — she praised the genuine warmth of colleagues who checked in on each other daily, while noting the flip side: nobody on her team left the office before the boss did, regardless of what time that turned out to be.*

### Conclusion

Neither country is going to hand a newcomer a flatter org chart, and trying to import one is the fastest way to be quietly filed under "doesn't understand how things work here." The more durable move is to learn each hierarchy's actual operating language — silence and indirection in China, warmth and availability in India — rather than assuming that "respecting the boss" means the same behavior in both places. The Hofstede numbers said these two systems were nearly identical. The 11pm phone call and the meeting held entirely in nods suggest otherwise, and only one of the two will ever text you back.
