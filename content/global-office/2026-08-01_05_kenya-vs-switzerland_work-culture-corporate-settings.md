# Stone Tablets and Silent Pauses: How Kenya and Switzerland Run a Meeting

**🇰🇪 Kenya · 🇨🇭 Switzerland**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In a Nairobi office, the org chart isn't a suggestion — it's closer to scripture, and a junior employee who tries to casually mingle with the boss at lunch may make everyone in the room uncomfortable rather than earning points for approachability. In a Zurich office, a nine o'clock meeting starts at nine o'clock, silence during discussion is thoughtful rather than awkward, and the org chart matters far less than whether you actually know what you're talking about. Both cultures are, in their own way, deeply serious about order. They've just built entirely different machines to produce it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇪 Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use professional and academic titles when addressing colleagues and superiors | Skip the small talk at the start of a meeting — it's not a delay, it's the warm-up |
| Let senior staff speak and decide first in group settings | Casually mingle with senior leadership as if hierarchy were optional — it can unsettle people |
| Deliver criticism privately, diplomatically, and indirectly | Give blunt public feedback, even when it's accurate — it reads as disrespectful |
| Build a personal relationship with colleagues and clients before pushing business | Assume decisions get made by consulting junior staff — deference to seniority is expected |
| Expect meeting start times to flex somewhat — build in patience | Interpret a slow start as disorganization — it's a different relationship with time |

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive at or before the stated time — five minutes late reads as disrespect | Assume all of Switzerland works the same way — German, French, and Italian-speaking regions differ |
| Prepare thoroughly before meetings — "good enough" is a genuine professional liability | Fill every silence — pauses in discussion are thinking time, not discomfort |
| Speak directly and expect direct feedback in return, without excessive cushioning | Confuse an accessible manager for a peer — hierarchy is real even when it's understated |
| Let competence and expertise carry more weight than seniority in most workplaces | Push for fast, informal decisions — process and planning are valued over speed |
| Learn which region's style you're working in before assuming a national default | Treat punctuality as a soft norm — it's closer to a hard rule |

### Kenya: Hierarchy as Load-Bearing Structure

Kenyan corporate culture runs on a clearly legible hierarchy, and multiple business guides describe it in almost identical terms: Commisceo Global and Cultural Atlas both note that senior staff are rarely consulted by juniors before decisions are made, not because junior input is unwelcome in principle, but because the culture assumes seniority reflects earned experience that doesn't need real-time validation from below. Hofstede Insights scores Kenya at 70 on power distance — a clearly hierarchical culture — and the practical texture of that number shows up in small, specific ways: titles used in address, feedback delivered privately and diplomatically rather than publicly and bluntly, and a documented expat account describing hierarchy lines as drawn "very clearly on stone," to the point that casual cross-rank mingling at lunch can visibly unsettle a room rather than put people at ease.

None of this makes Kenyan offices cold. Business guides consistently emphasize that Kenyans don't draw a hard line between professional and personal relationships — people do business with those they know, and warmth, patient relationship-building, and unhurried meeting openings (small talk before business, elastic starting times) coexist comfortably with the formal hierarchy rather than undercutting it. The two things that look, from outside, like they should be in tension — deep hierarchy and genuine personal warmth — are simply operating on different axes.

### Switzerland: Precision as the Actual Hierarchy

Swiss corporate culture inverts the relationship between formality and hierarchy that a newcomer might expect. Hofstede scores Switzerland at just 34 on power distance — genuinely flat by global standards — and multiple guides, including Upreer's workplace-culture breakdown, describe competence and expertise, not rank, as the thing that actually earns deference in a Swiss office. What replaces hierarchy as the organizing force is precision: Expatica and Cyborlink's etiquette guides describe five-minutes-late as a real breach of respect, "good enough" work as a professional liability, and thorough preparation as the baseline expectation rather than a differentiator.

The regional variation matters more than most newcomers expect. German-speaking Switzerland (Zurich, Bern, Basel) runs formal, structured, and direct; French-speaking Switzerland (Geneva, Lausanne) is comparatively more relaxed and relationship-driven; Italian-speaking Ticino leans warmer and more Mediterranean still. A Blind post on "Work Life in Switzerland" captures the texture well: managers are generally accessible, but accessibility isn't the same as a peer relationship, meetings involve real silences that function as thinking time rather than awkward gaps, and — perhaps the sharpest warning for a newcomer — genuinely surviving a Swiss office long-term requires real language and cultural integration, not just professional competence.

### The Reckoning

The paradox is that Kenya's visible, titled, deference-driven hierarchy and Switzerland's flat, competence-based structure can produce oddly similar outcomes: both cultures make it genuinely hard for a newcomer to shortcut their way to influence. In Kenya, you can't bypass hierarchy through friendliness or flattening gestures — the structure is the structure, and respecting it is the fastest way to be taken seriously. In Switzerland, you can't bypass the flat structure through charm or seniority elsewhere — you have to actually demonstrate competence, prepare relentlessly, and match the culture's relationship with time and silence. Kenya asks you to respect a visible order. Switzerland asks you to earn an invisible one. Assuming either is optional is the single fastest way to be quietly written off in the first month.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — A tech worker posting about "Work Life in Switzerland" described Swiss managers as genuinely accessible day-to-day, but warned that mistaking that accessibility for a peer relationship was a common and costly misread for people arriving from flatter US tech cultures.*

> *The Filipino Expat — A newcomer to Nairobi wrote candidly about the adjustment to workplace hierarchy, describing an early instinct to treat everyone the same way as something that had to be deliberately unlearned once colleagues began quietly signaling discomfort.*

> *Expat.com Nairobi forum — A poster asking about Kenyan office culture was told by a longtime resident that meetings routinely open with unhurried personal conversation before any business is discussed, and that treating this as wasted time rather than relationship-building was a reliable way to be seen as cold.*

> *Quora — Someone reflecting on hierarchical workplace cultures more broadly described the specific discomfort of watching feedback get relayed privately and diplomatically through indirect channels rather than stated directly in a meeting, and having to learn to read tone rather than words for the real message.*

> *iamExpat Switzerland — A professional comparing Swiss and American work culture noted that the biggest surprise wasn't the punctuality, which was expected, but how much unfilled silence a Swiss meeting could comfortably hold before anyone felt obligated to speak.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Kenya, learn the titles, respect the order of who speaks first, and don't mistake warmth for an invitation to flatten the hierarchy — the two coexist without canceling each other out. If you're moving to Switzerland, show up early, prepare more than feels necessary, and get comfortable letting a silence sit in a meeting without rushing to fill it. The honest version, if a friend asked: Kenya will tell you exactly where you stand, and Switzerland will make you demonstrate it — pick the one that matches how you'd rather earn your place in a room.
