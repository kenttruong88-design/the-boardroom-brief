**Poland Pays You to Have the Baby. Korea Is Still Trying to Convince Anyone To.**

🇵🇱 Poland · 🇰🇷 South Korea

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Polish mothers get 20 weeks of maternity leave at full pay through the state social insurance system, followed by up to 41 weeks of shared parental leave, plus a monthly PLN 800 (Rodzina 800 plus) benefit per child regardless of income (Bizky, RemoFirst, Wikipedia). South Korea's total fertility rate fell to 0.72 children per woman in 2023 — the lowest recorded anywhere — despite a government that has spent years throwing increasingly generous parental leave benefits at the problem (OECD). One country built its family policy around the assumption that people will keep having children if the system doesn't punish them for it. The other is trying to reverse a fertility collapse that its own workplace culture arguably caused.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand your leave entitlement fully: 20 weeks maternity + up to 41 weeks parental | Assume the leave is unpaid — Polish maternity leave runs at 100% of salary |
| Apply for Rodzina 800 plus — it's universal, not means-tested | Assume you need to prove financial need to qualify |
| Request flexible hours or remote work if your child is under 8 — it's a legal right | Assume employers can penalize you for requesting this accommodation |
| Note fathers get 14 fully-paid days, usable within the child's first year | Assume paternity leave is symbolic — take it seriously as an entitlement |
| Expect your job or an equivalent role to be legally protected during leave | Worry that returning from leave means demotion — it's not legally permitted |

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research your specific employer's programs — banks and chaebols vary widely | Assume government minimums are the whole story; company culture matters more |
| Consider the "6+6" scheme if your partner will also take leave — near-full pay for 6 months each | Assume leave-taking is career-neutral just because it's legally protected |
| Ask directly, before accepting an offer, how the company treats returning parents | Assume promotion timelines will be unaffected by a leave of absence |
| Note South Korea has the OECD's widest gender pay gap — women earn about 33.7% less | Assume this gap is primarily about qualifications rather than career interruption |
| Expect real reliance on grandparents for childcare — it's the cultural norm, not a backup plan | Assume formal daycare alone will cover a typical working schedule |

---

Poland's approach treats parenthood as a policy priority to be actively subsidized, not merely tolerated. Beyond the paid leave structure, the Rodzina 800 plus program pays PLN 800 monthly per child with no income test, a universal benefit that has become a fixture of Polish family life since its 500-zloty predecessor launched years earlier (Wikipedia). March 2025 reforms added extended leave for parents of premature or hospitalized newborns — up to 15 weeks for the most extreme cases — closing a gap that had previously left such families with inadequate support (Bizky). Crucially, the legal protections have teeth: employers cannot terminate employment during leave, cannot discriminate in promotions or training because of it, and must reinstate the employee to their previous role or an equivalent one (RemoFirst, KORDECKI). None of this makes Poland's system flawless — a World Bank paper on whether "mothers can afford to work in Poland" flagged real tension between childcare access and female labor force participation — but the direction of policy travel is unambiguous: toward more support, not less.

South Korea's problem is not a lack of policy effort — it's that policy hasn't been able to outrun workplace culture. The government's 2025 "6+6 Parental Leave Scheme" offers near-full wage replacement if a second parent, typically the father, takes at least six months of leave, an explicit attempt to normalize paternal caregiving and rebalance the burden (Lockton, AtlasHXM). Childcare leave benefit payments were raised substantially from January 2025, up to KRW 2.5 million monthly for the first three months (AtlasHXM). And yet the OECD's own analysis is blunt: these reforms haven't been sufficient to make work and motherhood genuinely compatible, and whatever fertility gains have resulted have come largely at the expense of women's continued employment, not alongside it (OECD). South Korea also carries the OECD's widest gender pay gap, with women earning roughly 33.7% less than men — a figure that reflects career interruption and the informal "mommy track" as much as any direct wage discrimination (Fortune). Some employers are stepping in where policy hasn't closed the gap: KB Kookmin Bank offers up to three years of unpaid child leave with a guaranteed return to the same rank, and Woori Financial Group offers a similar 2.5-year program (Fortune).

### The Reckoning

The real divergence isn't generosity on paper — both countries now offer leave policies that would look progressive by American standards. It's whether the surrounding workplace culture actually absorbs the disruption without penalizing the parent. Poland's legal protections against demotion and discrimination are backed by a culture where the state, not the individual employer, is understood to be underwriting the cost of parenthood, which diffuses the stigma. South Korea's protections exist on paper but collide with a workplace culture built around continuous presence and seniority-linked advancement, where a multi-month absence — even a legally protected one — still reads as a career interruption in ways the policy alone can't fully neutralize, which is precisely why individual employers like the major banks have had to invent their own supplementary guarantees.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *TeamBlind — A tech employee in Women in Tech described being placed on what she called the "mommy track" after four months of maternity leave, receiving no raise at her next performance review and only a small bonus in its place — a pattern several other commenters recognized from their own companies.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a question on Warsaw life as a foreign PhD candidate with a family noted the healthcare and daily cost of living were noticeably more manageable than comparable Western European capitals, making the practical logistics of raising children there easier than expected.*

> *Fortune (reported feature) — South Korean banks have begun offering "baby bonuses" and multi-year guaranteed-return programs specifically because standard government leave wasn't enough to stop women from quietly leaving the workforce after having children — an admission, in effect, that policy alone hadn't solved the retention problem.*

> *InterNations — A relocating parent in Warsaw described being surprised that requesting flexible hours for childcare was treated as a routine, expected request by their employer rather than something to be justified or negotiated, a contrast to more adversarial experiences elsewhere.*

> *A recurring theme in accounts of Korean corporate life: heavy reliance on grandparents for childcare isn't a stopgap but a structural expectation, and families without nearby extended relatives often describe feeling that the system quietly assumes a support network most transplant families won't have.*

### Conclusion

If you're planning a family and want a system where the legal protections are backed by a culture that treats parental leave as unremarkable, Poland's combination of paid leave, universal benefits, and anti-discrimination enforcement is a genuinely strong deal. If you're weighing South Korea, look past the headline leave policy and interrogate your specific employer's actual track record with returning parents — the government has done its part on paper, but whether your career survives intact depends far more on your company than the law. The honest tip: in Korea, ask to speak to someone who actually took the leave and came back, not just HR reciting the policy.
