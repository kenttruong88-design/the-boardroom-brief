# Twenty Points Apart: What "Working While Female" Means in Dublin vs. Tokyo

**🇮🇪 Ireland · 🇯🇵 Japan**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Ireland's mean gender pay gap fell to 3.5% in 2025, down from 6.9% the year before, according to the Central Statistics Office, and nearly one in five Irish CEOs is now a woman. Japan's gender pay gap sits at roughly 22%, the second worst in the OECD, and only 15% of Japanese managers are women, with 42% of companies having no female managers at all. Two advanced economies, two labor markets, and a nearly twenty-point gap in how far equality has actually traveled from the office org chart down to the paycheck.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect gender pay gap figures to be published — check your employer's if they have 50+ staff | Assume the improved national averages mean every individual company or sector has closed its gap |
| Negotiate directly — assertiveness in salary talks is broadly normalized | Assume maternity leave automatically means a smooth return; plan the transition explicitly with HR |
| Use available parental leave; uptake among fathers is rising and increasingly normal | Treat requesting flexible hours post-parenthood as a career-limiting move — ask anyway |
| Look for genuine representation at senior level, not just policy statements | Take a company's stated "family-friendly" branding at face value without checking retention data |
| Speak up in mixed-gender meetings; norms favor direct participation from everyone | Assume seniority alone earns airtime — it doesn't, for anyone |

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly, before accepting an offer, whether you'd be on the "career track" or "general track" | Assume "employee" status means equal access to promotion; dual-track systems still operate widely |
| Research a specific company's female management ratio, not just national averages | Assume a large or foreign-facing company has automatically solved this — verify per employer |
| Expect childcare leave to functionally derail promotion timelines in many firms | Assume returning from leave means returning to the same track or trajectory |
| Connect with other foreign or Japanese women already at the company before joining | Rely solely on official diversity statements; ask about actual outcomes |
| Recognize this is shifting — new 2026 disclosure rules require firms with 101+ staff to report gaps | Assume individual advocacy alone will shift a structural, decades-old dual-track system |

### Ireland: Closing, Not Closed

Ireland's numbers tell a genuine story of movement. The CSO's 2025 Gender Pay Gap Report shows the mean gap nearly halved year over year, and its "Women in the Labour Market" data shows female board representation rising from 19.6% to 27.9% between 2019 and 2025, with female senior executives up to 32.3%. This isn't cosmetic — Ireland has mandatory gender pay gap reporting for larger employers, a legal lever that's forcing companies to actually track and publish the numbers they'd otherwise be able to ignore.

That said, the improvement sits on top of an older, unresolved structural issue: research on Irish women's career progression has repeatedly found that the deeper driver of the remaining gap isn't the maternity leave period itself but the assumption that follows it — that women will remain the default primary caregiver, with all the scheduling, travel, and promotion consequences that assumption carries. The numbers are trending the right way, but the caregiving penalty hasn't disappeared, just narrowed.

### Japan: A Structural Fork in the Road

Japan's gap is not a matter of degree so much as a matter of separate systems. Many Japanese firms still operate a formal or informal dual-track hiring structure — sogoshoku (career track, historically male-dominated) versus ippanshoku (general/clerical track, historically where women were funneled) — a legacy structure that the International Bar Association and multiple academic reviews identify as a primary driver of the persistent 22% pay gap. RIETI's analysis is blunt about this: much of the wage gap isn't explained by identical roles paying differently, it's explained by women and men being sorted into different roles from the start.

Momentum exists — from April 2026, firms with 101 or more regular employees must disclose gender pay gaps and female management shares, an expansion from the previous 301-employee threshold, following "Womenomics" reforms that have been underway for over a decade with mixed results. But a 2023 survey found nearly a third of recent job seekers reported experiencing gender-based discrimination during hiring, with roughly 40% steered toward different job types by gender before they'd even been hired.

### The Reckoning

The sharpest contrast isn't the size of the gap, it's what closing it actually requires. Ireland's remaining gap is largely a caregiving-penalty problem layered on top of a system that already treats men and women as interchangeable on paper — meaning the fix is mostly about follow-through: enforcement, retention support, normalizing paternal leave. Japan's gap is a structural-sorting problem — the system itself, at the point of hiring, still often assigns different trajectories by gender, which means no amount of individual negotiation fixes it; the track itself has to change.

For a woman deciding where to build a career, this reframes the real question. In Dublin, ask "will I hit the same caregiving penalty everyone still hits, and how well does this specific employer buffer it?" In Tokyo, ask a much more foundational question before signing anything: "which track am I actually being hired onto, and can I get off it later if I need to?"

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Japan Today reader account — A woman working at a mid-sized Japanese firm described being required to wear a uniform that male colleagues in the same role didn't have to wear, and said the deeper frustration wasn't the uniform itself but what it symbolized: an assumption that she was there to be looked at as much as to work.*

> *r/AskWomen — An American who took a job in Tokyo described the culture shock of realizing, a year in, that she'd been quietly placed on what colleagues later explained was the "general" track rather than the management track — a distinction no one had explained clearly during hiring.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A foreign engineer at a Japanese tech firm noted that even in an ostensibly modern, international office, female colleagues were still routinely asked to pour tea in client meetings, a small ritual that turned out to be a fairly reliable predictor of who got taken seriously in the room afterward.*

> *Internations Dublin — A financial services professional who relocated from Southern Europe said Ireland's gap felt smaller day to day but not absent, and that the real tell was watching who took parental leave without visible career consequences — increasingly other women, but still rarely men at her firm.*

> *Quora — Someone comparing offers between an Irish and a Japanese employer wrote that the Irish company's published gender pay gap report, warts and all, made her trust it more than the Japanese company's vaguer "we value diversity" language, since one was measurable and the other wasn't.*

### Conclusion

The practical advice for anyone weighing these two moves: in Ireland, ask for the numbers — they're increasingly public, and a company that won't discuss its gap candidly is telling you something. In Japan, ask about the track, not the title, because the title can look identical while the trajectory is entirely different.

The honest version, over a drink: Ireland's gap is a problem being actively fixed with real tools, however imperfectly. Japan's gap is a problem still being defined, company by company, before it can even be fixed — so if you're negotiating a Japan offer, negotiate the structure, not just the salary, because the salary is downstream of a decision that may already have been made about you before you walked in.
