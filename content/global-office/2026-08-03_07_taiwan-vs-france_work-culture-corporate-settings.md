# The Legal Workweek Is a Suggestion in Both Taipei and Paris

**🇹🇼 Taiwan · 🇫🇷 France**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Taiwan's legal workweek caps out at 40 hours, and its overtime rules are precise down to the third hour of extra work. France's legal workweek is a famous 35 hours, protected by a literal right to disconnect from your inbox after hours. Neither number describes what a white-collar employee in Taipei or Paris actually experiences. Both countries wrote a workweek into law, and both produced a professional class that quietly ignores it in almost identical ways.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇼 Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a solid personal relationship with your direct boss — it's treated as essential to long-term standing, not just politics | Don't assume the legal 40-hour week and overtime caps (46 hours/month) reflect what senior staff actually work |
| Expect decisions to move through consensus-building among stakeholders rather than a single authority figure | Don't push for fast, unilateral decisions — it can read as bypassing a process everyone else respects |
| Notice that overtime expectations generally rise with seniority — the more senior you are, the more hours are assumed | Don't assume every company has an "overwork" culture — expat accounts genuinely vary by employer, especially at multinationals |
| Ask specifically about your company's actual norms around leaving on time versus staying late | Don't ignore Taiwan's documented overwork controversies — some notable cases have made national news |
| Value the collaborative, information-flows-through-channels structure — it's a feature, not just bureaucracy | Don't skip the informal relationship-building that determines how much real trust and autonomy you're given |

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clarify whether your contract is "cadre" (forfait jours, ~218 days/year, no clocked hours) or non-cadre (35-hour based, clocked) | Don't assume the 35-hour week applies to you if you're hired into a management, engineering, or senior professional role |
| Use your legal right to disconnect — companies over 50 employees must let you actually ignore after-hours emails | Don't send non-urgent emails late at night expecting it to read as dedication — it's more likely read as poor time management |
| Respect hierarchy signals in meetings — who speaks first, who proposes changes, and who uses "tu" all carry real weight | Don't use first-name casual greetings in emails before hierarchy is established — it can read as not understanding the structure |
| Expect real vacation culture — five weeks minimum plus RTT days for cadres working beyond standard hours | Don't leave before roughly 6-6:30pm as a cadre without checking whether that's genuinely normal at your specific office |
| Take meetings and formal processes seriously — logical, thorough reasoning is valued over fast improvisation | Don't expect much appetite for fast, disruptive innovation — process and precedent carry real institutional weight |

Taiwan's legal framework is unusually specific: an 8-hour day, 40-hour week, a 48-hour absolute ceiling, and overtime pay that steps up in defined increments — one-third extra for the first two overtime hours, two-thirds for the next two. In practice, that precision coexists with a workplace culture built on hierarchy and consensus, where, as several guides to Taiwanese work culture note, overtime tends to scale with seniority rather than disappear with it, and junior staff are more likely to actually leave on time than the managers above them. Taiwan's Hofstede power distance score sits at 68 — coincidentally identical to France's — reflecting a workplace where authority is respected and decisions flow through recognized channels, even as consensus-building softens the edges of that hierarchy day to day. Overwork remains a live public issue rather than a solved one: Taiwan has seen documented cases significant enough to make international news, even as recent efforts push toward more flexible scheduling and remote options.

France's legal framework is just as specific and just as unevenly applied. The 35-hour week is real law, but it functions mainly as an overtime trigger for non-cadre employees who clock their hours — anyone promoted into cadre status, roughly management, engineering, or senior professional tracks, moves to a "forfait jours" arrangement of about 218 working days a year with no hourly count at all. Cadres are described across multiple guides as routinely staying until 6:30pm or later, with leaving earlier — even after finishing core tasks — carrying a mild social cost. The compensations are real: a legal right to disconnect from work email outside business hours at companies over 50 employees, a minimum five weeks of vacation, and RTT days that convert extra hours worked into additional time off. France's Hofstede power distance score of 68 shows up daily in office life — in meeting protocol, in who initiates a "tu," in whose opinion carries default weight.

**The Reckoning:** Both countries built strong legal protections around working hours, and both produced a professional class — Taiwanese senior staff, French cadres — who function largely outside those protections by design, not by loophole. The irony is almost exact: Taiwan's overtime math gets more generous the more senior you become, while France's forfait jours removes hourly protection altogether once you're promoted into it. In both places, hierarchy and long hours travel together, just via different legal mechanisms — Taiwan's is an overtime scale that senior staff work extra within, France's is an entire separate contract category that opts senior staff out of hourly protection altogether.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A response to "What is the overall work culture/hours like in Taiwan?" noted that the honest answer depended entirely on the specific employer: some multinationals ran close to the legal cap with real work-life balance, while others quietly expected far more, especially the higher up the hierarchy an employee climbed.*

> *Blind — A thread comparing hours at TSMC's Taiwan operations to expat expectations found that the gap between "legal maximum" and "actual senior-staff hours" was real and openly discussed among engineers, though several noted the compensation and benefits made the trade-off feel more negotiated than imposed.*

> *The Local France — Coverage of France's droit à la déconnexion explained that businesses over 50 employees are legally required to let staff disconnect after hours, but noted the law works best when middle management actually models the behavior — a boss who emails at 10pm still shapes what "normal" looks like regardless of what's written in the handbook.*

> *Medium (Blake Anderson) — An account comparing jobs held in Taiwan described the consensus-driven decision process as initially frustrating for anyone used to faster, individually-authorized calls, but noted it produced fewer reversed decisions once everyone had actually bought in.*

> *Press account (AOL) — An American who took a corporate job in Paris described being surprised that French colleagues worked late and answered weekend emails at rates comparable to what they'd left behind in the US, directly contradicting the 35-hour-week reputation before ever setting foot in the office.*

### Conclusion

Ask about the specific employer, not the country, before you assume either legal workweek applies to you. In Taiwan, ask where you'll sit in the hierarchy, because that answer predicts your real hours better than the labor code does. In France, ask whether you're being hired as cadre or non-cadre, because that single word determines whether the 35-hour week is your reality or someone else's. Priya's honest read: in both countries, read the org chart before you read the labor law — it's the more accurate document.
