# The Intern Who Argues With the CEO vs. The Manager Who Expects a Suit: Israel and Nigeria at Work

**🇮🇱 Israel · 🇳🇬 Nigeria**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Israel scores roughly 13 on Hofstede's Power Distance Index, one of the lowest readings anywhere in the world — low enough that a 22-year-old engineer fresh out of military service will openly tell a company founder their roadmap is wrong, in a meeting, without anyone treating it as insubordination. Nigeria scores around 80 on the same measure, high enough that showing up without a jacket to a client meeting can quietly cost you credibility before you've said a word. Neither country is wrong about how to run an office. They are simply answering a completely different question about what authority is for.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇱 Israel

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Speak up immediately if you disagree with a plan, even in front of leadership | Wait for a formal invitation to contribute — it won't come, and silence reads as low engagement |
| Treat a manager's request as a starting point for discussion, not a fixed order | Take blunt, direct feedback personally — it's a sign you're being taken seriously |
| Expect the org chart to matter less than who has the best idea in the room | Assume a casual dress code and first-name culture mean the pace is relaxed — it isn't |
| Come prepared for rapid pivots and last-minute changes in direction | Expect long onboarding — you'll be given real responsibility almost immediately |
| Ask questions bluntly; hedging your point is read as uncertainty, not politeness | Mistake informality for a lack of ambition — hours are long and intensity is high |

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress formally and conservatively for meetings, even in creative or tech roles | Address senior colleagues by first name until explicitly invited to |
| Acknowledge seniority and age directly — deference is a sign of professionalism | Skip the greeting and pleasantries before getting into business topics |
| Follow explicit instructions closely — managers expect execution, not reinterpretation | Assume input from junior staff will be solicited without being asked for |
| Invest time in personal relationships with colleagues and clients — trust is built slowly | Push back publicly on a superior's decision in front of the team |
| Expect visible time at the office to be read as a signal of commitment | Leave promptly at closing time if others are staying late — it's noticed |

### Israel: Authority Is a Suggestion, Not a Structure

Israel's tech sector has earned its "Start-Up Nation" reputation honestly: the ecosystem produces one of the highest concentrations of startups per capita in the world, and the culture inside those companies reflects a deliberate allergy to bureaucracy. A significant part of this traces back to mandatory military service, where young Israelis lead small teams and improvise under real pressure well before they enter the civilian workforce — by the time they're hired at 22 or 23, taking initiative without waiting for permission is already second nature. Power Distance sits around 13 on Hofstede's index, among the flattest in the world, and in practice that means an intern's technical objection in a planning meeting gets debated on its merits, not dismissed on the basis of rank.

This flatness comes at a cost that's easy to underestimate from the outside: Israeli startups commonly run 50–60 hour work weeks, and the same directness that makes feedback fast and useful can feel confrontational to newcomers from more diplomatically-coded cultures. Requests from managers function more like strong recommendations than binding orders, which is empowering right up until the moment a new hire realizes nobody is going to hand them a checklist — initiative is not optional, it's the entire operating system.

### Nigeria: Authority Is the Structure

Nigerian corporate culture runs on an almost inverse logic. Companies remain firmly hierarchical, with decision-making concentrated at the top and managers who expect explicit instructions to be followed precisely rather than reinterpreted by initiative. Hofstede scores Nigeria at roughly 80 on Power Distance, reflecting a business culture where age and rank are openly, visibly respected — formal titles, conservative dress, and a structured greeting before any substantive discussion are not optional politeness, they're the actual mechanism by which trust and credibility get established.

None of this means Nigerian offices are cold. The culture is simultaneously described, in workplace accounts, as both formally hierarchical and warmly relationship-driven — colleagues collaborate closely across hierarchical lines, and personal connection remains central to how business actually gets done, particularly in Lagos, where "team spirit and family" language shows up frequently in how employees describe their workplaces. What's notably absent, compared to Israel, is the expectation that a junior employee's disagreement with a decision belongs in the room at all — that disagreement, if it exists, typically travels through more private or indirect channels.

### The Reckoning

Both cultures produce hard-working, committed employees, but they've built entirely different machinery for getting there. Israel bets that speed and innovation come from removing friction between idea and action — flattening the hierarchy so the best argument wins regardless of who's making it. Nigeria bets that trust and coordination come from clarity about who decides what — a structure that, done well, protects junior staff from being blamed for decisions they didn't actually have the standing to make.

The genuinely surprising overlap: both systems are, in their own way, deeply relationship-based. Israeli directness isn't coldness — Israelis frequently describe their blunt feedback culture as a form of respect, a belief that you don't waste someone's time softening a critique they need to hear. Nigerian formality isn't distance — it's a slower, more deliberate way of building the same trust Israelis build through argument. Someone arriving from a "polite but vague" corporate culture, like much of the Anglophone West, might find both extremes disorienting for the same underlying reason: the ambiguity they're used to simply doesn't exist in either country.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — An American engineer who took a role at an Israeli startup wrote that the hardest adjustment wasn't the long hours but realizing that a manager's "can you look into X" was genuinely optional, and that nobody was going to notice — or forgive — you for not reading between the lines.*

> *Indeed reviews (Nigeria) — A former employee at a Lagos firm described the culture as "team and family," but noted that closing late became an unspoken requirement after repeated instances of staff being quietly penalized for leaving at the official closing time.*

> *InterNations Tel Aviv — A relocating consultant said the single most disorienting moment of her first month was being asked, directly and by name, why she hadn't objected to a plan she privately thought was flawed — silence, she learned, was read as either agreement or disengagement, never as diplomacy.*

> *Quora — A respondent comparing Israeli and American work culture noted that Israeli colleagues treat direct disagreement as basic professional courtesy, while the same bluntness delivered to an American colleague without warning had previously been mistaken for hostility.*

> *Quora — Someone reflecting on Nigerian workplace norms explained that showing up without acknowledging a senior colleague's greeting first, before discussing business, was interpreted as a serious breach of respect — not a matter of communication style but of visible disrespect for age and rank.*

### Conclusion

If you're deciding between these two moves, the real diagnostic question is how you personally handle ambiguity about your own authority. Israel will hand you real responsibility on day one and expect you to argue for what you believe, loudly, regardless of your title — which is liberating for people who thrive without a script and exhausting for people who want to be told what "good" looks like. Nigeria will tell you exactly what's expected, exactly who to defer to, and exactly how to earn trust — which is reassuring for people who want clarity and frustrating for anyone hoping their ideas will be solicited before they're senior enough to be asked.

Priya's honest read: Israel will make you a better arguer. Nigeria will make you a better listener. Pick the skill you're missing.
