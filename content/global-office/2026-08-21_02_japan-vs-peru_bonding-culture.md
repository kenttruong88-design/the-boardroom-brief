**One Country Bonds You Over Beer After the Meeting Ends. The Other Bonds You Before It Starts.**

**🇯🇵 Japan · 🇵🇪 Peru**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Tokyo, the real meeting often happens at the izakaya afterward, where rank is theoretically suspended and everyone drinks toward a shared understanding they'll pretend not to fully remember on Monday. In Lima, the meeting barely starts until the small talk, the coffee, and the personal check-in have run their course — because in Peru, you don't bond after doing business, you bond before you're trusted to do it at all. Both cultures will tell you relationships matter more than the org chart. They just disagree on when the relationship-building is supposed to happen.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend at least half of your team's nomikai, especially in your first year | Skip every single one and expect full inclusion in informal decisions |
| Stay for one or two rounds even if you don't drink alcohol | Feel obligated to match everyone's pace — non-drinkers are increasingly normal |
| Treat bureikō (rank suspension) as real but temporary | Bring up what was said at nomikai once you're back in the office |
| Let junior colleagues pour senior colleagues' drinks, and vice versa when offered | Pour your own drink first — it reads as impatient |
| Use nomikai to build the informal rapport that formal meetings won't give you | Assume declining has zero career cost — it usually has some |
| Notice that younger colleagues attend less than they used to, and calibrate accordingly | Assume 2019-era mandatory attendance norms still fully apply |

#### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept coffee, lunch, or dinner invitations before discussing business specifics | Push straight into the agenda at a first meeting |
| Ask about family and show genuine interest — it's not small talk, it's the actual work | Treat relationship-building as a formality to rush through |
| Expect meetings to open with warm, unhurried conversation | Check your watch or visibly redirect to "the point" |
| Show up to team lunches and outings consistently | Opt out repeatedly — confianza erodes quickly with absence |
| Communicate important matters face-to-face or by video call | Rely on email alone for anything that requires trust |
| Be patient — a real social network typically takes 2–6 months to build | Expect instant inclusion just because people are friendly on day one |

**Japan**

Japan's approach to workplace bonding is built around a single loaded ritual: nomikai, the after-work drinking gathering, and its cousin bounenkai, the year-end party. According to industry guides for foreign workers, these are functionally semi-mandatory in traditional Japanese companies — not written into any contract, but tracked socially, especially during an employee's first six to twelve months. The mechanism at work is bureikō, roughly "no formalities tonight" — a temporary, sanctioned suspension of the hierarchy that otherwise structures Japanese offices, itself a reflection of Hofstede Insights' data showing Japan scoring a striking 95 on Masculinity, one of the highest in the world, alongside a collectivist baseline (46 on Individualism) that prizes group harmony over individual comfort.

The bet nomikai makes is that people who can't speak freely under hierarchy during the day will speak freely once the hierarchy is nominally paused at night — and that the resulting candor builds the trust formal meetings can't. But the culture is visibly shifting: company-hosted bounenkai attendance dropped from 78.4% of firms in 2019 to 59.6% in 2024, and multinational employers in Japan increasingly treat nomikai as optional rather than expected. Advice circulating for foreign employees is pragmatic rather than romantic: attend roughly half, stay for a round or two, then leave — full absence carries a real if unofficial cost to relationship-building and project inclusion, but full immersion is no longer required to belong.

**Peru**

Peru runs the same instinct — that real trust requires informal contact — but front-loads it before business gets serious rather than after. Peruvian business culture, per Cultural Atlas and management guides for foreign employers, centers on confianza, a concept of trust built through sustained personal relationship rather than assumed from a job title or contract. Meetings typically open with unhurried small talk; skipping straight to the agenda reads as brusque at best, untrustworthy at worst. Social invitations — coffee, lunch, a shared meal — are the actual mechanism by which colleagues decide whether to extend real cooperation, not a courtesy layered on top of it.

This shows up starkly for newcomers: expat guides on building a professional network in Peru put the typical timeline for establishing a workable social circle at two to six months, depending on Spanish fluency and proactivity, and describe Peruvian friendships as more interdependent and quicker to form once underway than many Western equivalents — the line between a colleague's family gathering and a friend's is often blurry in a way that would feel intense in a more individualist office culture. Peru's blend of hierarchy — deference tends to track age, authority, and economic status — with high relational warmth means bonding is less an event you attend than a continuous background obligation you maintain, meeting after meeting, lunch after lunch.

**The Reckoning**

The counterintuitive part is that Japan, the culture with the more intense single bonding ritual, actually asks for less sustained emotional investment: show up to enough nomikai, and the informal trust mostly renews itself in concentrated bursts. Peru asks for less of any single event and more of everything, continuously — confianza isn't won at one dinner, it's a running balance that depletes if you stop showing up. A newcomer who nails one legendary nomikai in Tokyo can coast on that goodwill for months; a newcomer in Lima who acts warm once and then goes quiet will find the goodwill quietly draining away.

There's also a hierarchy paradox running in opposite directions. Japan's nomikai exists precisely because the workplace hierarchy is otherwise so rigid that an artificial rank-suspension has to be manufactured to allow honesty. Peru's hierarchy, while real, is porous enough day-to-day — softened by constant relational contact — that no special ritual needs to be invented to access it; the warmth is just baseline, all the time, not switched on for one sanctioned evening.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone answering what surprised them most about working in Japan described an English-speaking colleague who was technically excellent but kept getting passed over for cross-team projects, until they started attending occasional nomikai and picking up more Japanese — after which, they said, the same work suddenly started getting noticed.*

> *Quora — A contributor addressing whether nomikai attendance is truly mandatory explained that no one will ever formally require it, but a Japanese company man who repeatedly declines after-work socializing will feel the effects on how much he's looped into decisions — described as a slow, deniable kind of exclusion rather than any overt penalty.*

> *Quora — One answer on Japan's drinking-heavy social scene noted that international employers, like a well-known American entertainment company's Tokyo office, deliberately did not run on the stereotypical model of new hires being pressured to drink with superiors nightly — a contrast the poster clearly found notable enough to volunteer unprompted.*

> *jarniascyril.com (expat network guide) — A foreign professional building a network in Peru wrote that language exchanges and mixed local-foreigner social events did more for their career integration than any formal networking effort, describing the professional and personal spheres in Lima as functionally the same circle.*

### Conclusion

If your instinct is to get straight to work and socialize later as a reward, Japan will at least let you compress that obligation into a few well-chosen evenings a year — show up, drink a beer you don't have to finish, and the trust mostly takes care of itself. If you try that instinct in Peru, you'll spend months wondering why nobody quite lets you in, because there the socializing isn't a reward for good work, it's the precondition for anyone believing your work is good. The line I'd give a friend packing for either: in Tokyo, bond hard for one night and coast; in Lima, bond a little every single day and never stop.
