# Indonesia's Startups Run So Hot That Employees Built Entire Meme Accounts Just to Cope. Singapore's Corporate World Runs So Smooth That the Main Complaint Is Boredom.

**🇮🇩 Indonesia · 🇸🇬 Singapore**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

An Indonesian tech CEO snapped at an employee during a company town hall over a question about career progression, and within weeks, satirical Instagram accounts mocking the entire startup ecosystem's hustle-and-burn culture had tens of thousands of followers. Meanwhile, a worker on the corporate-heavy career forum Blind summed up seven months in Singapore with a single flat sentence: "I feel boring." One country's work culture produces open revolt through memes. The other's produces mild existential ennui and a very clean apartment.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇩 Indonesia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to wear multiple hats with limited formal training or structure | Expect clearly defined job boundaries from day one |
| Build resilience — long hours and founder intensity are treated as normal | Assume a startup's growth metrics reflect sustainable internal operations |
| Follow the startup meme accounts — they're a genuine pulse-check on industry sentiment | Dismiss meme culture as just jokes; it reflects real, widely shared frustration |
| Expect to sit in on high-level strategic conversations even as a junior hire | Expect the structured mentorship and training common at larger, older companies |
| Adapt quickly and improvise — problem-solving is valued over following procedure | Expect formal escalation paths for workplace conflict to be well established |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Weigh an MNC's structured career ladder seriously if stability matters to you | Assume a startup role offers the same job security as a multinational |
| Consider a startup specifically for broader, faster skill development | Choose purely on starting salary — long-term competency-building matters more |
| Budget seriously for housing — even subsidized HDB units involve decades of debt | Assume "government housing" means cheap or quick to acquire |
| Build a social life deliberately — it won't happen passively through work alone | Expect work itself to fill the social and leisure gap many newcomers notice |
| Take work-life balance claims with a grain of salt and verify them directly | Assume every company's stated "good WLB" reflects the actual day-to-day reality |

### Indonesia

Indonesian startup culture runs hot enough that employees have built an entire satirical media ecosystem just to process it. The Ken's reporting on the phenomenon traces it back to a specific flashpoint: Kevin Aluwi, then-CEO of ride-hailing giant Gojek, reportedly "snapped at someone for asking a 'hostile' question" about career progression during an internal town hall — a moment that triggered anonymous venting across social media and the rapid growth of meme accounts like @Ridehaluing, which crossed 13,000 followers within months of its January 2021 launch satirizing "the glorification of hustle culture and excessive working hours." Separate accounts like @Ecommurz and @Taktekbum built similar audiences around the same targets: bragging about long hours, aggressive discounting, and inflated valuations across virtually every major Indonesian tech company, from Gojek and Tokopedia to Grab and Shopee.

What the memes capture in satire, a Seamplified internship essay captures in earnest. Sofia, a chemical engineering student who expected Indonesian startup life to feel more relaxed than Singapore's reputation for corporate intensity, instead found her department operating as "the eye of the tornado" — sitting in on major strategic decisions with minimal formal training, expected to "adapt quickly, pick up new skills on the go, and work with limited resources" from day one. The intensity is real, but so, by her account, is the genuine upside: exposure to decision-making far beyond what a typical internship would offer anywhere more structured.

### Singapore

Singapore's corporate world offers almost the inverse trade: less chaos, more structure, and a startup scene that exists as a deliberate alternative rather than the default. TheSmartLocal's career guide for young Singaporeans frames the choice plainly — startups are "dynamic places to work, where everyone works closely together, even the CEO," demanding employees "wear multiple hats," while multinationals can feel like being "a small fish in a big pond" but compensate with defined departments and clearer advancement pathways. Crucially, the guide treats the startup path as increasingly legitimate rather than risky, citing homegrown unicorns like Carousell, Lazada, and Ninja Van as proof that the ecosystem has matured well past its scrappy origins.

The complaint that surfaces most often about Singapore's corporate mainstream isn't intensity — it's monotony. A Blind thread titled "working in Singapore is the nightmare" features a poster summing up seven months in the city-state with the flat admission "I feel boring," while another commenter's dry rejoinder doubled as unintentional cultural diagnosis: "Singapore is boring so not much else to do but work and workout." Even cost of living compounds the sense of a tightly managed life — one contributor noted that securing even a subsidized government HDB flat can mean going "in debt over 20 years."

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't hustle versus laziness — both cultures work hard — it's where the friction actually shows up. Indonesia's startup intensity produces visible, vocal backlash, aired publicly through memes and open venting because the culture around confrontation and dissent, however uncomfortable, has some tolerance for public processing. Singapore's corporate intensity produces almost no public friction at all; the discontent that does surface reads as quiet resignation rather than open revolt. Hofstede Insights data offers a clean explanation: Singapore's uncertainty-avoidance score of 8 is among the lowest ever recorded anywhere, reflecting extraordinary comfort with ambiguity and change at the institutional level — yet that same comfort with structural ambiguity coexists with a famously risk-averse, security-conscious individual career culture, producing workers who tolerate systemic uncertainty but crave personal stability. Indonesia's uncertainty-avoidance score of 48 sits closer to the middle, consistent with a startup culture willing to operate with far less structure, far more improvisation, and, evidently, far more room for a meme account to vent about it.

### Conclusion

If you're moving into Indonesia's startup scene, go in expecting genuine chaos and genuine opportunity in roughly equal measure, and don't mistake the meme culture around it for exaggeration — it's closer to an accurate, if darkly funny, read on how intense the pace actually gets. If you're moving into Singapore's corporate mainstream, trade that chaos for structure and stability, but budget emotionally for a slower, quieter kind of dissatisfaction that shows up less as crisis and more as a long, well-organized stretch of mild boredom. Indonesia will burn you out loudly. Singapore will bore you out quietly. Pick the friction you'd rather manage.
