# The Shop Is Closed: Leave Culture in France vs. Australia

**🇫🇷 France · 🇦🇺 Australia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

An Australian who takes four straight weeks off is making a statement. A French employee who does the same in August is simply following the national script, alongside the bakery, the pharmacy, and quite possibly their own employer, who has posted a handwritten sign reading "fermé pour congés annuels" and gone to the coast. Both countries protect generous leave by law. Only one of them has built an entire national rhythm around actually using it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan your major vacation for July or August if you want colleagues and clients around to hand off to before you leave | Schedule an important client meeting or product launch in the first three weeks of August — there may be nobody left to attend it |
| Track your RTT days separately from your 25 statutory leave days — private-sector employees can bank up to 27 extra, public-sector up to 34 | Assume RTT applies to you automatically; management and executive-track roles are frequently excluded from RTT entitlement |
| Expect stores, banks, and even some public services to close for stretches in August, especially outside Paris | Try to get anything administrative done — apartment leases, bank appointments, medical referrals — in the back half of August |
| Use your full 5 weeks; unused leave is a mild source of manager concern, not pride | Brag about working through your vacation; it reads as poor planning, not dedication, to French colleagues |
| Say goodbye properly before a long break — an out-of-office alone is considered abrupt | Expect instant replies to work messages while a colleague is on congés; it is genuinely disconnected time |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bank your 4 weeks of annual leave for a proper block — a 2–3 week trip is the cultural norm, especially over the Christmas–January stretch | Expect a company-wide shutdown outside the Christmas period; leave is staggered and individually scheduled |
| Track long service leave if you're staying put — most states grant roughly 8.67 weeks after 7–10 years with one employer | Forget that long service leave accrues separately from annual leave; the two entitlements don't offset each other |
| Use personal/carer's leave (10 days, paid) for your own illness or to care for an immediate family member without guilt | Show up sick to "prove commitment" — carer's leave exists precisely so you don't have to |
| Plan around the Australian summer (December–February) if you want to take leave when the rest of the country is also taking it | Assume European-style multi-week August absences are normal; most Australian leave is shorter and more dispersed across the year |
| Negotiate purchased annual leave (buying extra weeks against salary) if your employer offers it — increasingly common in white-collar roles | Assume unlimited PTO policies, where offered, function as more generous than the 4-week statutory floor — in practice usage often ends up lower |

### 🇫🇷 France

French law guarantees 25 days of paid leave (five weeks) for every full-time employee, on top of 11 public holidays, and that floor is treated as a floor rather than an aspiration. Layer on RTT — additional days off granted to compensate for working beyond the statutory 35-hour week — and private-sector employees can accumulate up to 27 more days, public-sector workers up to 34, according to reporting compiled by The Local France. Combined, a French employee working a standard 39-hour week can realistically bank somewhere between 35 and 45-plus paid days off annually, excluding public holidays.

What makes France distinctive isn't just the number, it's the synchronization. August functions as an unofficial national leave month: government data and multiple workforce guides note that entire sectors slow to a crawl, with small businesses shutting their doors outright. This is not employees sneaking away — it's collectively sanctioned absence, embedded closely enough into the calendar that scheduling a serious business initiative for mid-August is considered a rookie planning error. Hofstede Insights scores France comparatively high on uncertainty avoidance, and the rigid, calendar-bound structure of French leave-taking — codified, collective, rarely improvised — reflects that preference for predictability over ad hoc flexibility.

### 🇦🇺 Australia

Australia's Fair Work Act guarantees a statutory floor of 4 weeks (20 days) of annual leave for full-time employees, plus roughly 10 to 13 public holidays depending on state, and a separate 10 days of paid personal/carer's leave that covers both illness and caring for family members, according to the Fair Work Ombudsman. Long service leave adds a distinctly Australian wrinkle: after seven to ten years with one employer, depending on the state, workers typically accrue an additional 8.67 weeks — a loyalty-rewarding entitlement with no real French or American equivalent.

Unlike France's synchronized August exodus, Australian leave-taking is dispersed and individually negotiated, clustering loosely around the December–January summer but otherwise scattered across the year based on personal preference and team rostering. There's no cultural expectation that the whole office empties out at once; instead, employees quietly work out coverage with a manager, often well in advance. The entitlement is generous by global standards but the culture around using it is closer to the pragmatic, individually-scheduled American model than to France's collectively enforced shutdown.

### The Reckoning

On paper, France offers more days. In practice, the deeper difference is who decides when the leave happens. French leave culture is collective and calendar-driven — the country effectively agrees, informally but firmly, on when everyone stops. Australian leave culture is generous but atomized — the entitlement is real and well-protected, but the timing is a private negotiation between employee and manager, closer in spirit to workplace scheduling than to national ritual.

The counterintuitive part is what this does to guilt. French employees rarely report anxiety about taking their full allotment — it's structurally normalized, almost mandatory in spirit even where not in law. Australians, despite comparable legal protection, more frequently describe letting leave balances build up, particularly in competitive white-collar sectors, because there's no equivalent social permission structure telling them August (or any month) is safe to disappear.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A respondent who worked in both French and Australian offices explained that the French 5-week baseline sounds generous until you realize RTT is often excluded for management roles, meaning the most senior — and most stressed — employees frequently get the least real time off.*

> *r/AskEurope — One contributor originally from Australia described the disorientation of arriving in Paris in early August for a new job and finding half the office, and half the neighborhood, simply gone, with no one available to properly onboard them for nearly three weeks.*

> *Internations — A member relocating from Sydney to Lyon noted that the hardest mental shift wasn't the extra leave itself but learning that replying to work email during congés was viewed by French colleagues as a mild character flaw rather than diligence.*

> *thelocal.fr — A long-term foreign resident described the "reverse culture shock" of returning to a non-French job market and realizing how much of their annual rhythm — expecting government offices, banks, and clients to simply pause every August — did not travel with them.*

> *Quora — Someone who moved from Melbourne to Paris for work observed that Australian long service leave rewards staying with one employer for a decade, while nothing in the French system rewards tenure the same way — French generosity is front-loaded and universal rather than earned over time.*

### Conclusion

The practical question for anyone relocating isn't which country gives more leave on paper — France wins that comparison outright — it's whether your role and seniority actually qualify for the full entitlement, and whether the surrounding culture will let you take it without cost. In France, ask specifically whether RTT applies to your contract level before you count on it. In Australia, ask how leave is rostered on your specific team, since the generous legal floor means little if nobody schedules around you using it. If I'm being honest with a friend: move to France for the calendar, move to Australia for the flexibility, and don't expect either country's HR department to tell you which one you're actually getting until you're three months in.
