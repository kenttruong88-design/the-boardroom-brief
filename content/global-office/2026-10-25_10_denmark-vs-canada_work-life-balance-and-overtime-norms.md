# Denmark Logs Off at 1,369 Hours a Year. Canada Is Still Checking Slack.

**🇩🇰 Denmark · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

According to the OECD, the average worker in Denmark clocked 1,369 hours last year, against 1,687 for the average Canadian — a gap of 318 hours, or just under eight additional 40-hour workweeks, vanished somewhere into Canadian inboxes. Both countries market themselves internationally as havens of humane employment, heavy on cycling infrastructure and craft coffee, light on burnout. Only one of them, however, has reached the point of near-total cultural consensus that replying to a work email after four in the afternoon is not commitment, but a mild character flaw.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇰 Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect the office to empty out by 4 or 5 p.m., your boss included | Schedule a 3:30 p.m. meeting and expect full attendance |
| Use "flextid" to shift your own start and end times | Assume unpaid overtime is normal — in the private sector, it's only owed if your contract says so |
| Take your full five weeks of statutory leave, ideally three in a row each summer | Skip vacation days to look dedicated — colleagues will read it as poor planning, not loyalty |
| Negotiate formal "plus time" if you genuinely want more hours and more pay | Stay late "to be seen" — Danes tend to read this as inefficiency |
| Leave in time for daycare pickup without apologizing for it | Treat a Friday afternoon email as urgent; it almost never is |
| Ask HR what your collective agreement actually says about overtime | Rely on general labor law for an answer — Denmark largely doesn't have one |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get written clarity on whether you're "exempt" (salaried) or covered by overtime rules | Assume your overtime entitlement matches a colleague's in another province |
| Track your actual hours yourself, informally | Trust that someone else is tracking them for you |
| Ask if the role is unionized before you ask about salary | Assume a manager title protects you from overtime — managers report working it at nearly double the general rate |
| Use every vacation day you're entitled to | Read "we're not really a 9-to-5 place" as a compliment |
| Ask directly what the unwritten after-hours norms are at that specific employer | Expect a legal "right to disconnect" everywhere — only Ontario requires a written policy, and even that doesn't ban after-hours messages |

Denmark's advantage shows up less in legislation than in its conspicuous absence. According to IDA, the union representing Danish engineers, the country has no general statute governing working hours at all; the 37-hour week most people cite is a product of collective bargaining, not parliament. What law does exist is mostly a ceiling — average weekly hours, including overtime, cannot exceed 48 over any four-month stretch, and everyone is guaranteed at least 11 consecutive hours of rest in a day. Overtime pay in the private sector is contractual rather than automatic, which sounds precarious until you notice the OECD Better Life Index figure behind it: only about 1 percent of Danish employees work very long hours, a tenth of the OECD average of 10 percent. Nobody needed a law to stop working late because almost nobody expects anyone to.

Part of this is cultural architecture rather than policy. Hofstede Insights' country-comparison data scores Denmark at just 18 on power distance and 16 on "masculinity" — a dimension Hofstede uses to measure competitive, achievement-driven norms versus cooperative, quality-of-life-oriented ones. Translated out of consultant-speak: flat hierarchies and a weak cultural premium on visible striving mean that staying late to impress a boss mostly just signals that you're bad at your job. Surveys back the self-image: InterNations' Expat Insider data, reported by The Local Denmark, found 76 percent of international workers satisfied with their work-life balance and a 39.7-hour average week, the shortest of any country in that survey. The caveat, buried deeper in the same dataset, is that satisfaction and hours worked aren't tightly coupled everywhere — expats in the Czech Republic logged over 44 hours a week and still reported high satisfaction. Denmark's case is unusually clean, but the brochure's claim that fewer hours automatically equal more contentment is, elsewhere, not quite proven.

Denmark's flexibility also cuts both ways. IDA's own guidance on "jobløn," the fixed-salary contract common among specialists, notes that this arrangement carries no maximum hours and no automatic overtime compensation at all, unless the contract specifically grants it — precisely the kind of fine print a new arrival signs without reading closely. For salaried professionals, the famous Danish "work less" reputation holds only as long as an employer actually treats them as a standard 37-hour employee, rather than as someone whose fixed pay has quietly absorbed every evening handled "because it had to be done."

Canada's numbers tell a messier story, mostly because its machinery for enforcing balance is legal rather than cultural, and legal machinery varies by jurisdiction. The OECD Better Life Index puts 3 percent of Canadian employees in the "very long hours" category — still comfortably under the OECD average, but triple Denmark's share. The Angus Reid Institute's survey of working Canadians found 23 percent work overtime "regularly," with managers and executives doing so at nearly double the baseline rate of 43 percent. Whether that overtime is compensated depends heavily on bargaining power: 68 percent of unionized workers say they're always paid or given time off for it, against just 37 percent of non-union employees, who are more than twice as likely to say they're never compensated at all.

Ask Canadians why they're putting in the extra hours and the answers skew voluntary, at least on paper. The Angus Reid Institute found deadlines (34 percent) and simply having more work than fits into a normal day (32 percent) as the leading reasons, with seasonal crunches accounting for roughly another quarter. Nearly half, 47 percent, described the overtime as "very much" their own choice. But a quarter called it a flat requirement of the job, and almost one in five said they did it because declining would read as a failure to impress the boss — which is less a culture of genuine choice than a culture that has outsourced the pressure onto the employee's own judgment.

The legal backstop that might otherwise standardize this is still being assembled piecemeal. Ontario is the only province requiring employers of a certain size to maintain a written disconnecting-from-work policy — and even that policy, legal summaries note, "does not itself create a general right to stop answering work messages after hours." Alberta and British Columbia have no equivalent requirement. A federal right-to-disconnect provision has technically passed but, as of this writing, remains officially "not in force." The practical result is that a Canadian's actual overtime experience depends less on national identity than on which province, which employer, and which union card they happen to be holding.

The irony, set side by side, runs backward from what each country's reputation implies. Denmark, the one with almost no hard statute on working hours, achieves near-total compliance through cultural consensus and collective bargaining coverage so broad it functions like law. Canada, the one with actual provincial employment standards acts, overtime thresholds, and now a federal statute on the books, achieves much more uneven compliance, because the law is real but the enforcement and the culture around it are not uniformly real. Denmark proves that strong norms can substitute for legislation. Canada is quietly demonstrating that legislation without matching norms mostly produces excellent paperwork.

Both countries, confusingly, turn up near the top of international "best work-life balance" rankings — just in different rankings, built on different methodologies for different audiences. Immigration consultancies tend to place Canada in their global top ten; expat-satisfaction surveys crown Denmark outright. Neither list is dishonest, exactly; they are simply measuring different things, one a basket of statutory entitlements and public holidays, the other lived daily experience. A newcomer who reads only the rankings, rather than the underlying hours data, could reasonably end up booking a one-way flight on the strength of a list that never once mentions the 318-hour gap between them.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — a Canadian software worker who relocated to Germany recalled that logging unpaid extra hours had been an unremarkable, expected part of life among peers back home, and named escaping that always-on culture as one reason for the move.*

> *Hacker News — a commenter describing an early-stage Vancouver startup recounted colleagues routinely staying well past the end of the workday, with a quiet but unmistakable expectation that newer employees match the pace, formal overtime policy notwithstanding.*

> *Hacker News — another contributor, comparing Canadian and American tech employers, argued Canadian workers generally log off earlier and see more of their families than their American counterparts, but acknowledged the trade-off shows up directly in lower pay.*

> *The Copenhagen Post — an American who had lived in Denmark for seven years contrasted his old routine of arriving before eight, leaving after six, and sometimes working weekends, with a Danish norm where a full month off each summer is unremarkable and the office, he found, does not collapse in your absence.*

> *Work in Denmark expat profiles — an Italian engineer who left a project-management role in Milan for a technical position in Denmark described his new days as shorter and less dramatic, summarizing the shift simply as working less but more focused, with newly reclaimed time for travel and sport.*

### Conclusion

If the single number that matters most to someone actually packing boxes is hours spent at a desk per year, Denmark wins decisively, and wins it through something closer to social agreement than statute. Canada's protections, by contrast, exist mostly on paper, province by province, and the real experience of overtime there will depend far more on your specific employer and whether you carry a union card than on anything printed in a government brochure about "Canadian values." Treat a Canadian job offer the way you'd treat a lease: read the actual terms, not the neighborhood's reputation. Ask, specifically, whether the role is unionized, what the collective agreement actually says about overtime pay, and whether the province you're landing in has gotten around to a disconnect policy yet — the honest answer, this year, is probably not.

Priya's honest answer, over drinks: move to Denmark if you want the culture to do the disconnecting for you, and move to Canada if you're prepared to negotiate it yourself, in writing, before you need it.
