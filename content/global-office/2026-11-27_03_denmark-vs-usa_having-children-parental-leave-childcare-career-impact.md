# The Stork Files a Claim: Parenthood in Denmark and the United States

🇩🇰 Denmark · 🇺🇸 USA

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The United States was, as of the data in a widely cited Pew Research Center comparison, the only one of 41 OECD countries that did not mandate any paid leave for new parents. The other forty required at least about two months. Denmark, meanwhile, will pay you to stay home with the baby and then charge you a fraction of the true cost of the crèche. For the professional weighing a move, this is less a cultural difference than a difference in what the word "benefit" means.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your leave eligibility with Udbetaling Danmark before the birth, since work-history thresholds apply | Assume a non-EU partner is covered automatically, a question that stumps even forum regulars on Expat.com |
| Put your child on the municipal daycare list early, because some areas have waitlists | Expect the daycare to cover evening shifts or concerts; opening hours follow the office, not the gig |
| Take the leave you are entitled to and treat it as a legal right, not a favour | Treat a long absence as a confession of low ambition |
| Leave at a civilised hour; even corporate parents tend to collect children by about 4 p.m. | Schedule a 4.30 p.m. meeting and call it flexibility |
| Learn the unwritten daycare rules, such as eating from a plate and sleeping outdoors | Bring American ideas about individual lunchboxes without asking first |
| Check your household income, since the income-based subsidy phases out well before the top salary bands | Assume the state subsidy is universal |

### USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the employer leave policy before signing the offer, because there is no federal paid leave | Assume that leave means paid leave |
| Check whether your state runs its own programme; California, New Jersey, New York, Rhode Island and Washington D.C. did, as of 2019 | Assume that your neighbour's state works like yours |
| Price childcare before you price the house | Budget on last year's childcare rates |
| Ask HR in writing how health insurance continues during any leave | Take unpaid leave without understanding the insurance consequences |
| Join daycare waitlists as early as the policy allows | Plan to improvise a care arrangement in month five |
| Keep a record of your leave arrangements | Treat a verbal promise from a manager as a policy |

## Denmark: The State as Co-Parent

Denmark's leave system is described by the national portal Borger.dk in three parts: 14 weeks of maternity leave for the mother, 2 weeks for the father or co-parent, and 32 weeks of parental leave that the parents can divide between them. Salaried employees on full leave receive a weekly maximum of DKK 4,865 before tax, according to the same portal's 2025 figure. The structure was reformed for births from August 2022, so a reader with a due date should check the current rules before booking anything, including holidays.

Childcare is where the arithmetic turns dramatic. Fortune, reporting on a University of Michigan study of Danish administrative data, notes that subsidised care is available from six months and that parents pay no more than a quarter of the cost. Scandinavia Standard, in a 2022 piece, puts the out-of-pocket share at about 30 percent and quotes Copenhagen fees of roughly DKK 3,950 a month for a crèche place, lunch included. The subsidy is income-based: Borger.dk says households above DKK 677,500 a year receive no income-based subsidy, so the system is generous but not naive. Siblings help further: Borger.dk says parents pay half the fee for their cheapest place, applied automatically.

The working day is arranged around the same assumptions. The American teacher interviewed by The International in 2025 took about 16 months of leave after her first child, partly to match the school calendar, and expects her husband to use his full earmarked share this time. Her union helped her claim her entitlements, which is a useful reminder that a right on paper still needs someone who has read the paper. A second American, writing in an essay on AOL, reports that even parents in corporate roles usually collect their children by about four in the afternoon. For a foreigner raised on the visible-presence school of management, this is either liberation or a mild existential threat.

The career consequences are real but cushioned. The Danish study, by Alexandra Killewald and Therese Christensen, found that mothers lost roughly 12 percent of what they would otherwise have earned over twenty years, but benefits offset about 80 percent of that loss. Hofstede's dimensions offer a cultural explanation. Wikipedia's summary of them gives Denmark a power distance score of 18 against 40 for the United States, and the United States an individualism score of 91. The first suggests a society where a manager is unlikely to be impressed by an employee who never sees daylight. The second explains why American families are expected to run their own childcare supply chain.

## The United States: Choose Your Own Adventure

The American position is that leave is something an employer may offer, not something the state provides. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics benefits page puts access to paid family and medical leave at 49 percent of private-industry workers, which means the other half are negotiating from first principles. A handful of states have filled the gap with their own programmes, but Pew's list dates from 2019 and has no doubt moved since.

Childcare supplies the rest of the drama. The Department of Labor's Women's Bureau reported in November 2024 that full-day care for one child cost between $6,552 and $15,600 a year in 2022, equal to 8.9 to 16 percent of median family income. Pew adds that day care and preschool prices rose about 22 percent between January 2020 and September 2024. The World Economic Forum, citing OECD figures, puts net childcare costs at 32 percent of an average couple's income in the United States, the highest of the countries it listed, against 5 percent in Sweden.

The strain is not confined to the poor, although it is worse there. In a fall 2022 survey cited by Pew, about two in ten parents who needed child care said there were times in the previous year when they lacked the money to pay for it, rising to 38 percent among lower-income parents. Among employed parents, 19 percent were extremely or very worried about losing pay for time off over child care problems, and 45 percent of lower-income working parents felt the same. Pew also reports that 36 percent of childless adults under 50 who say they are unlikely ever to have children name affordability as a major reason. A pro-natalist government might wish to read that last figure twice.

## The Reckoning

On paper this is not close. Denmark offers a structured period of paid leave, subsidised care from six months and a measurable cushion against the earnings penalty of motherhood. The United States offers a patchwork, a childcare market priced like a mortgage and a labour force in which roughly half have no paid family leave through work. The Danish system is not free, since the tax bill is how it is funded, and it still penalises mothers by about 12 percent over twenty years. It simply declines to let the penalty be total.

For the American considering the move, the trade is lower take-home pay and higher predictability. For the Dane considering the opposite move, it is higher salary and a spreadsheet. The quality argument deserves a mention too. Liza Gibbs Fox, the American musician interviewed by Scandinavia Standard, says Danish care is consistent across the country, whereas American quality varies widely. That is one parent's view, not a national audit, but it matches the logic of a system run by municipalities to a common standard. Anyone relocating with children should model both countries' costs for the first five years, not the first five months, because the difference compounds, and so does the sleep deprivation.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Scandinavia Standard, an American musician who has lived in Denmark since 2009 says childcare is far cheaper than at home and the staff are consistently well trained, but that unwritten expectations about routines take time to learn, and that having no family nearby makes irregular working hours hard to cover.*

> *The International, an American teacher married to a Dane describes taking roughly a year of leave after her first child and calls that nearly unheard of at home; she values the family-friendly public spaces, but notes that some areas have long daycare waitlists and that the system can be harder for foreigners.*

> *AOL essay, a self-described workaholic American parent says Danish corporate parents usually collect their children by 4 p.m., that family comes first, and that this took adjusting to, though she adds that expat parenting has its own challenges and that risky outdoor play was initially uncomfortable.*

> *Expat.com forum, a Danish citizen asks whether his American partner must be married to receive maternity benefits; the thread yields only links to official pages and one unverified comment about work-history thresholds, a fair preview of the paperwork.*

> *Hacker News, 2010 thread, commenters on a Swedish leave story argue that in the United States health insurance is tied to employment, which makes a long unpaid absence risky, and that career gaps are harder to manage there than in Europe.*

## Conclusion

If you want children and a conventional career in the same decade, Denmark is the more forgiving venue, and the evidence from OECD-linked comparisons, Danish administrative data and parents who have actually done it points the same way. The costs are tax, bureaucracy, a social code with unwritten rules and, for non-citizens, some uncertainty about eligibility. None of these is trivial, and none of them arrives with a four-figure monthly invoice from a nursery.

The United States remains the better venue for a high salary and a high tolerance for improvisation. Check the leave policy and the insurance terms in writing, price childcare before accepting the offer, and treat any manager's warm words as non-binding. In Denmark the state is a co-parent. In America your employer might be one, if you read the contract carefully.
