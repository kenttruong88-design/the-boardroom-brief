# Why an Italian Will Ghost You After One Dinner and a Mexican Will Adopt You After One Coffee

**🇮🇹 Italy · 🇲🇽 Mexico**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Mexico, a coworker who has known you for eleven days may invite you to their mother's birthday party, hand you a plate before you've asked, and introduce you to four cousins as "mi amigo." In Italy, a coworker who has known you for eleven years may still greet you with a formal handshake at the coffee machine and nothing more, because the friend groups that matter were assembled in primary school and the roster closed decades ago. Both countries will tell you, sincerely, that they are warm people. Both are correct. The warmth simply arrives on very different lease terms.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇹 Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn Italian past a survival level — it is the single biggest unlock for social access | Expect colleagues to "network" purely for career reasons; Italians rarely socialize instrumentally |
| Accept the second and third invitation, not just the first — persistence signals seriousness | Assume a friendly lunch means you're now in the friend group; that takes months, sometimes years |
| Find a regular bar or café and become a recognized face — proximity builds trust slowly | Try to rush intimacy by oversharing early; Italians are private until they aren't |
| Join a language exchange or hobby class outside the expat bubble | Stick exclusively to English-speaking expat circles — it's comfortable but a social dead end |
| Expect long, unhurried lunches to double as relationship-building time | Skip the aperitivo or team lunch to save time — that's where trust actually gets built |

#### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say yes to the first invitation to a family gathering — it's a genuine door, not a formality | Show up empty-handed to a home invite; bring something small, always |
| Expect physical warmth — cheek kisses, hugs, close conversational distance — as the norm, not an overstep | Mistake fast warmth for fast intimacy; being liked quickly doesn't mean you're trusted with everything quickly |
| Learn a few names of coworkers' kids and partners — Mexicans track this and notice when you do too | Turn down a meal invitation without a real reason — declining reads as rejection, not busyness |
| Participate in office birthday and holiday rituals fully | Assume friendliness at work translates automatically into being invited into someone's private circle |
| Be reachable outside strict work hours for a social plan | Be overly transactional in early friendships — Mexicans read that as cold |

---

Italy's social architecture is built on what sociologists call strong, closed ties. According to World Values Survey data, only around 26 percent of Italians say most people can be trusted — a modest figure that reflects not hostility but a cultural default toward small, deep circles over broad, shallow ones. The OECD's Better Life Index has repeatedly ranked Italy near the top globally for work-life balance, with just 3.3 percent of Italian employees regularly logging very long hours, versus an OECD average of around 10 percent. That free time, though, is largely pre-allocated to family and the friendships formed in one's own hometown. Quora threads from long-term foreign residents describe a familiar arc: genuine kindness from neighbors and colleagues, followed by the slow realization that Italians "already have their friends" and structurally don't network the way transplants expect. The Local Italy has run the same warning for over a decade — Italians tend to view English-speaking newcomers as temporary guests and don't always invest early, because why build a friendship with someone who may leave in eighteen months.

Mexico runs the opposite operating system. InterNations' Expat Insider surveys have consistently ranked Mexico first worldwide for Culture & Welcome and among the top three for Finding Friends, with 75 percent of American residents reporting that building relationships was easier there than anywhere else they'd lived, against a 42 percent global average. Hofstede's cultural dimension data shows Mexico scoring 81 on Power Distance against Italy's 50, and it also scores high on collectivism in practice — family and in-group loyalty extend outward faster to include new arrivals, especially once they're vouched for by a colleague or neighbor. The catch, per OECD figures, is that this warmth coexists with the longest average working hours in the OECD, at roughly 2,207 hours per worker per year, often across a six-day week. Socializing in Mexico frequently happens inside the seams of a demanding schedule rather than in generous blocks of leisure time.

The reckoning is this: Italy makes you wait for depth but eventually offers something durable and low-maintenance, once earned. Mexico offers immediate inclusion but expects ongoing reciprocity — showing up, remembering names, accepting invitations — to keep that inclusion warm. An American transplant used to "let's grab coffee sometime" as a polite non-commitment will misread both countries. In Italy, that same vague overture usually means nothing will happen unless you propose a specific time, twice. In Mexico, it usually means something will happen soon, and if you don't follow through, that will be quietly noted.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/expats — A longtime American resident of Italy estimated it took the psychological equivalent of the "34 hours to casual friend, 200 hours to close friend" research to crack an actual Italian friend group, and noted that joining a language school did more for their social life than any work happy hour ever did.*

> *Quora — One foreigner living in Italy for three years wrote that colleagues were unfailingly polite and helpful, yet after three years they still didn't feel part of the society — describing it less as rejection and more like being permanently filed under "guest."*

> *The Local Italy — A contributor's most-repeated tip for cracking Italian social circles was almost anti-climactic: befriend your neighborhood bartender first, since being introduced by someone locally trusted works faster than any effort made solo.*

> *InterNations — A relocating manager in Mexico City described being invited to a colleague's family Sunday lunch in her second week, and said no amount of research had prepared her for how quickly "coworker" became "honorary family member," nor for the mild guilt she felt the one time she had to say no.*

> *r/expats — Someone who moved from Mexico City to Milan for a corporate transfer said the hardest recalibration wasn't the language, it was accepting that a warm "ci vediamo" (see you around) from an Italian colleague was social small talk, not an actual plan — something that would have been a firm commitment back home.*

### Conclusion

If you need friendship to happen on a predictable timeline, Mexico will reward you faster and with less effort — the door opens early, and the main job is simply walking through it and coming back. If you can tolerate a longer runway and don't mind being "the foreigner" for a year or two, Italy offers something that, once built, tends to be sturdier and less performative than most expat friendships elsewhere. The mistake in both places is applying the other's rulebook: don't wait for Mexicans to build up to intimacy in the Italian sense, and don't expect Italians to speed-run it in the Mexican one. If I'm being honest with a friend over a drink: move to Mexico if you want to feel at home by December; move to Italy if you want, by year three, a friend who'd actually show up at 3 a.m. if your car broke down.
