# France Gives You 25 Vacation Days by Law. The US Gives You a Standing Desk.

**🇺🇸 USA · 🇫🇷 France**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

An American new hire in Paris will spend their first two weeks wondering why the office empties out at noon and doesn't fully return until 2pm. A French new hire in New York will spend theirs wondering why nobody eats lunch at all. According to OECD data, the average French worker logs around 1,490 hours a year against roughly 1,721 for the average American — a gap of nearly six standard US workweeks, achieved not through laziness but through law.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to negotiate your own vacation days — there's no legal floor | Assume unused PTO rolls over or gets paid out; check your specific employer's policy |
| Answer urgent emails outside work hours if your role expects it | Be surprised when "unlimited PTO" quietly means less time off than a fixed allowance |
| Eat lunch at your desk if that's the office norm — it usually is | Take a two-hour lunch on your first week without checking if that's normal there |
| Treat visible availability as a career signal, fair or not | Assume a manager's "take the time you need" is a binding guarantee |
| Build your own boundaries — nobody else will build them for you | Expect labor law to backstop your work-life balance the way it might elsewhere |

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use your full 25 days of statutory leave — not using it can seem strange, not virtuous | Schedule important meetings in August; much of the country is functionally closed |
| Expect a real lunch break, often 1–2 hours, away from your desk | Eat at your desk if it's discouraged at your workplace — some offices still frown on it |
| Respect the "droit à la déconnexion" — don't expect after-hours replies | Email colleagues late at night expecting a same-night response, even from ambitious ones |
| Learn the 35-hour week's real mechanics — many salaried roles use RTT (extra days off) instead of shorter days | Assume 35 hours means everyone leaves at hour 35 sharp every week |
| Greet colleagues individually each morning, often with a handshake or bisous | Wave generally to the room instead of acknowledging each person |

The American system is built on Hofstede's high-indulgence, low-uncertainty-avoidance profile — Americans score higher on indulgence than the French and dramatically lower on uncertainty avoidance, 46 versus France's 86. That combination produces a workplace that prizes flexibility and spontaneity over structure, but offers almost no legal floor beneath it: the US is the only OECD country with a statutory minimum of zero paid vacation days, according to comparative labor data compiled from OECD and Bureau of Labor Statistics sources. Whatever time off you get is a negotiated benefit, not a right, and unlimited-PTO policies — increasingly common at US employers — frequently correlate with employees taking less time off than a fixed allowance would guarantee, since nothing marks the boundary of "enough."

France runs on the opposite instinct. French labor law guarantees 25 days of paid annual leave plus 11 public holidays, and the 35-hour workweek — while frequently misunderstood abroad as a hard cap — mostly operates through RTT days (Réduction du Temps de Travail), additional days off that compensate salaried employees who work beyond 35 hours in a given week. The 2017 "right to disconnect" law, born out of union pressure over the way smartphones were quietly destroying the 35-hour week's intent, requires companies with 50+ employees to formally negotiate after-hours email policies. According to The Local France, French offices also tend to run later in the calendar day — closer to a 10-to-7 schedule than 9-to-5 — with the missing hour absorbed almost entirely by a lunch break that OECD time-use data shows the French spend nearly twice as long on as Americans do.

The reckoning here isn't really about hours; it's about where the boundary lives. In America, the boundary between work and life is a personal project — you build it, defend it, and are judged by how well you manage it, with almost no institutional backup. In France, the boundary is a legal fact, enforced from outside the individual, which means less personal negotiation but also less personal control: you don't get to trade your lunch break or your August for a bigger bonus even if you wanted to. An ambitious American will find the French system's uniformity mildly infantilizing. A French professional dropped into an American office will likely find the total absence of guardrails alarming — not because Americans work more hours in a literal sense necessarily, but because nothing stops them from working more if the culture around them expects it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — An American who relocated to Paris for work wrote that the truest thing anyone told her about balance was that there's no such thing — some weeks life takes over, some weeks work does — but the difference in France was that colleagues actually expected the pendulum to swing back toward life, and didn't read a quiet week as a lack of commitment.*

> *Quora — An American who has worked in France for three decades described the daily ritual of shaking hands or exchanging la bise with every single colleague each morning as more than politeness — skipping it, even once, registered as a small social event that people noticed and mentioned.*

> *Quora — Someone researching how to get hired in France as an American noted that the legal hurdles — visa sponsorship, having non-French qualifications ratified, required language proficiency — were a bigger practical barrier to actually experiencing the vaunted work-life balance than any cultural adjustment once inside.*

> *Hacker News — A commenter discussing the 35-hour week's rollout recalled that the law's original design didn't account for salaried managers, who ended up working the same total hours as before but compressed and often unpaid for the difference — a reminder that legal caps on hours don't automatically cap expectations.*

> *The Local France — The publication's culture-shock coverage noted that Americans are consistently thrown by the "closed 12–2" signs on offices and shops, and that English-speaking transplants often try to shorten their lunch and leave earlier, only to find the rest of the office still there well past dark.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving from the US to France, the adjustment isn't just getting used to more time off — it's getting used to time off you're not allowed to quietly skip. If you're moving the other way, the adjustment is realizing that the flexibility Americans prize as a feature is also the reason nobody's watching the clock for you. Neither system is more relaxed than the other so much as differently supervised: one trusts the law, the other trusts you — and you should decide honestly which one you trust less.
