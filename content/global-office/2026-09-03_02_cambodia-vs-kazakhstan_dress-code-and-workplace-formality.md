**Blazers in the Tropics, Furs on the Steppe: Cambodia and Kazakhstan on the Art of Looking Employed**

**🇰🇭 Cambodia · 🇰🇿 Kazakhstan**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Phnom Penh, a man in a full suit at 2 p.m. is making a statement about his relationship with air conditioning, not his employer. In Astana, a man without a coat at the same hour is making a statement about his relationship with hypothermia. Both countries take office formality seriously; they have simply organized their formality around opposite weather systems, and the newcomer who packs for one and lands in the other will spend the first month looking either overdressed and damp or underdressed and blue.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇭 Cambodia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a full suit to the first meeting, then read the room before shedding the jacket | Assume the tropical heat excuses shorts or flip-flops in a client-facing role |
| Keep a long-sleeved layer at your desk — offices run aggressively over-cooled | Criticize a colleague's work in front of others; it costs everyone face, not just them |
| Default to conservative coverage for women (shoulders, knees) until the office's norms are clear | Loosen up faster than your most senior local colleague does |
| Greet with the sampeah (hands together, slight bow) when locals initiate it, otherwise a handshake is fine | Show up in visibly worn or ill-fitting clothing — neatness reads as respect, cost does not |
| Treat the first few weeks as an observation period before matching casual dress to peers | Push for radical transparency or blunt feedback in your first month |

#### 🇰🇿 Kazakhstan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to a suit and tie (men) or a tailored suit/dress (women) in corporate, finance, and government jobs | Show up underdressed relative to local colleagues — Kazakhs, per multiple expat accounts, dress up, not down |
| Invest in real winter outerwear; a good coat is a professional asset from November to March | Wear the same wardrobe you'd wear in a Gulf posting or a Bangkok office — the climate logic is inverted |
| Use full name plus patronymic, or title and surname, until told otherwise | Assume startup-casual dress extends beyond the tech sector into finance or ministries |
| Expect ties to loosen slightly after a long lunch, not before it | Skip the small talk and relationship-building before getting down to business |
| In smaller tech or startup shops, take a cue from the room — smart-casual is increasingly normal | Wear synthetic fabrics that fail badly across a 60-degree annual temperature swing |

Cambodia's dress code is best understood as a negotiation between two forces that agree on almost nothing: colonial-era formality, which still governs the first impression, and a climate that makes wool a hostile act. According to guidance compiled by Commisceo Global's Cambodia country profile, businesspeople are expected to arrive at initial meetings in full suits and dresses, with the concession that "given the tropical climate more relaxed attire without a jacket and tie can often be worn once initial meetings are over." Expat.com's guide to corporate culture in Phnom Penh describes the underlying logic as hierarchical and face-conscious: punctuality and deference to seniority are treated as non-negotiable, while criticism delivered in front of colleagues can cause a loss of face that outlasts the meeting itself.

The garment industry adds a wrinkle Priya finds hard to resist: Cambodia dresses much of the world for work while frequently underpaying the people who sew the clothes. The ILO's Better Factories Cambodia programme has documented a raw gender pay gap of roughly 4.5 percent in the sector and continuing irregularities in overtime and wage payment, even as exports grew 7.2 percent in one recent reporting year while the registered factory workforce shrank (International Labour Organization). The World Bank's labor data put Cambodia's total labor force above 9.9 million as of 2024, a scale that makes the country's garment-export machine one of the more consequential dress-code footnotes in Southeast Asia — it is, after all, manufacturing the blazers other countries wear to work.

Kazakhstan's formality runs on a different fuel entirely: Soviet administrative habit, oil-and-gas corporate money, and a genuine winter. Business guides converge on a picture of conservative, status-conscious dressing — suits and ties for men, tailored suits or dresses for women — with qualifications and titles that "carry status," per Commisceo Global's Kazakhstan profile. The formality is procedural as much as sartorial: colleagues are expected to be addressed by first name and patronymic in formal settings, decisions flow top-down, and open challenges to a manager's authority are rare (ClickUp's Kazakhstan business-etiquette guide; Rivermate's cultural considerations guide). None of this comes from nowhere. Kazakhstan's public administration inherited a Soviet model that was, in the words of one recent academic study, "highly centralized, hierarchical, and bureaucratic" (Public Administration Review, 2025), and thirty-plus years of independence have modernized the economy considerably faster than they have flattened the org chart.

**The Reckoning**

Put the two side by side and the counterintuitive part isn't the formality gap — both countries expect you to dress up, not down, for a first meeting — it's the direction of the risk. In Cambodia, the professional failure mode is overdressing into visible discomfort: sweating through a jacket that nobody else is still wearing by eleven in the morning. In Kazakhstan, the failure mode is underdressing into visible unseriousness: showing up in a puffer jacket and trainers to a meeting where local colleagues have already worked out exactly how to look sharp at minus twenty. Hofstede Insights' comparative framework, where usable data exists for the region, places Kazakhstan at a middling-to-high power-distance score around 58, indicating a moderate acceptance of hierarchy, while academic treatments of Cambodia describe an even steeper hierarchy with strong collectivist leanings — though Cambodia sits outside Hofstede's original core dataset entirely, itself a small joke about how much research attention the country's workplaces have historically received relative to its garment exports.

The other asymmetry is who does the adjusting. In Cambodia, the foreign employee is usually granted a grace period to relax into local informality. In Kazakhstan, expatriates report a steep but rewarding adjustment curve: the InterNations Expat Insider survey, as reported by the Astana Times, showed Kazakhstan jumping 27 places in its 2019 career-satisfaction rankings, with 58 percent of respondents happy with their career prospects and a quarter finding it "very easy" to settle into the local culture. Translated: Phnom Penh will forgive you for staying formal a beat too long. Astana will not forgive you for staying casual at all.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Tripadvisor Cambodia forum — a longtime visitor described Phnom Penh's "better establishments" as smart casual with no jacket or tie required, but warned that the good restaurants and offices are kept "semi-refrigerated" rather than merely air-conditioned, making a long-sleeved shirt a practical necessity rather than a formality — the opposite problem everyone expects from a tropical posting.*

> *Cambodia Expats Online forum — in a thread about menswear culture, longtime members joked that Phnom Penh's backpacker strip has "not too many Savile Row shoppers," a running bit that captured a real relief among new arrivals: nobody in Cambodia is quietly judging the cut of your suit, only whether you own one at all.*

> *Tales From a Small Planet (Foreign Service community, Real Post Reports — Astana) — one contributor described Kazakh professional dress as more formal than American norms but with ties loosening and top buttons undone "after lunch," while another warned that surviving deep winter means learning to "suit up" before stepping outside, like preparing to leave a space station.*

> *Tales From a Small Planet (Real Post Reports — Astana) — a separate contributor noted that local Kazakh colleagues consistently "dress up more" than visiting Americans, with fur coats treated as genuinely posh rather than costume-like, and spiked heels a normal sight on icy pavement in deep winter — a cultural misread that catches plenty of newcomers underdressed relative to their local peers.*

> *Quora — a thread offering advice to people moving to Almaty described it as a comfortable, walkable, visibly Euro-Asian city where most business contacts speak workable English, with the tolerant, foreigner-friendly atmosphere flagged as the thing that made the professional adjustment easier than expected.*

### Conclusion

The practical decision points in different directions depending on what you are optimizing for. If you want a workplace that forgives sartorial mistakes and cares more about whether you show up on time and defer appropriately than what fabric you are wearing, Cambodia is the more forgiving posting — provided you can survive an office thermostat set for a different climate than the one outside. If you want a workplace where the formality is legible, consistent, and status carries real institutional weight, Kazakhstan rewards the investment in a good suit and a better coat, though it will not warn you twice about underdressing.

Priya's honest read, the one she would give a friend weighing both offers over a drink: pack the blazer for Phnom Penh and wear it once, for the photo; pack the coat for Astana and wear it every single day you're not indoors, because the steppe does not do irony.
