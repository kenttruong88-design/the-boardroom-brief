# Singapore and Hong Kong Offer the Same Seven Days, and the Same Quiet Fear of Using Them

🇸🇬 Singapore · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

On paper, Singapore and Hong Kong treat the worker identically: seven days of paid annual leave in the first year, rising by one day a year to a ceiling of fourteen. In practice, the two cities have built quite different relationships with that ceiling. A June 2026 Deel analysis of workers across Asia-Pacific found that the median Singaporean took 19 days of leave in 2025 and the median Hong Konger took 16.5, with Singapore ahead of the entire region. This is a notable result for a city-state whose workers regularly describe themselves, in surveys and on forums, as short of time. Whether the leave is taken, and with what expression on the manager's face, is the more useful question for anyone planning a move.

## Singapore: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your contract; the legal floor is 7 days and most employers go well beyond it | Assume the statutory minimum is what your employer offers |
| Ask early about carry-forward and cash-out; some firms allow neither except on resignation | Plan a two-week break in December without confirming whether unused days lapse |
| Book leave well ahead and use the 11 public holidays to build long weekends | Expect last-minute leave to be welcomed in every team |
| Offer a staycation or short regional trip when colleagues cannot take much time off | Expect friends to burn annual leave on a catch-up dinner |
| Ask about hours as well as days; heavy workloads shrink real time off | Expect Western overtime norms or paid overtime to travel with you |

## Hong Kong: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Count both the 17 general holidays and the annual leave scale when comparing offers | Assume every employer exceeds the statute; some 2017 forum accounts describe firms that stuck to seven days |
| Read the leave policy for caps on consecutive days and blackout periods | Assume you can attach annual leave to a weekend without asking |
| Check whether sick days are deducted from annual leave | Assume sick days are kept separate from your annual allowance |
| Plan for short breaks; Hong Kong staff favour them over long absences | Expect your first-year seven days to stretch to a long trip |
| Ask whether leave must be used at Lunar New Year | Assume leave dates are yours to pick |

## Singapore: The Land of Fourteen and a Half Days

Singapore's Ministry of Manpower states the rule plainly: seven days of annual leave in the first year, rising by one day per year of service to a maximum of fourteen. In a written parliamentary answer in January 2026 the ministry added that in 2024 more than 90 percent of full-time resident employees aged 25 to 64 were entitled to more than seven days, and 68.2 percent to more than fourteen. The floor, in other words, is a footnote for most professionals, though it is the number that is always quoted. A candidate who is told that Singapore offers only seven days is being told about the law, not about the market. The ministry's own figures show that more than 90 percent of comparable employees are entitled to more, and the useful conversation is therefore about the contract in front of you. The Ministry has said in recent parliamentary replies that it has no plans to raise it.

The harder question is whether the entitlements are used. The Deel analysis puts Singapore at the top of the region, with 77.5 percent of workers taking at least 80 percent of their allowance, against 68.6 percent in Hong Kong. Expedia's 2023 Vacation Deprivation Study, which surveyed 500 Singaporeans, reached a different answer: 58 percent described themselves as vacation deprived, and respondents took 14.5 days against 17 allotted. The two surveys use different methods and populations, and the sensible reading is that Singaporeans take most of their leave and feel they should be taking more.

The cultural weight of the unused days shows up in the forums. A People Matters report in December 2025, built on a Reddit post, described an employee whose boss criticised her for "maximising" a 21-day allowance with no carry-forward, while mentioning that he would forfeit some of his own. Commenters concluded that leave which exists on paper but is discouraged in practice is a management problem rather than an employee one. It is also a reminder that Singapore, with 11 public holidays in 2026 and six long weekends, is a city where the calendar is generous and the office occasionally less so.

## Hong Kong: Seventeen Holidays and Fewer Illusions

Hong Kong's Labour Department publishes the same seven-to-fourteen scale under the Employment Ordinance. What differs is the holiday calendar. Human Resources Online counts 17 general holidays in Hong Kong in 2026, of which 15 are statutory, the total having risen with the addition of Easter Monday. That is six more than Singapore's public holidays, and it explains a good deal of the apparent generosity.

Hong Kong staff use what they have. Expedia's 2024 study, as reported by TTR Weekly, found Hong Kong to be the only market surveyed where respondents left no vacation days unused, and its 2023 edition recorded 61 percent of respondents as feeling vacation deprived despite taking more days than their allotment as Expedia counted them. The Deel figures, taken in 2025, show a median of 16.5 days used and a habit of short breaks: 37.5 percent of multi-day requests were for two days, against 31.9 percent in Singapore. Hofstede Insights scores Hong Kong at 93 for long-term orientation and 17 for indulgence, which is the profile of a culture that defers pleasure and takes its breaks in small, efficient portions.

The darker side is anecdotal and old, but instructive. The Deel data hints at the remedy: employees on flexible leave arrangements took more time off than those on fixed contracts, which suggests that the more rigidly leave is administered, the less of it gets used. A 2017 Human Resources Online compilation of posts on the Hong Kong Discussion Group forum recorded employers capping leave at three consecutive days, banning leave adjacent to weekends, deducting sick days from annual leave and requiring staff to burn their statutory seven days at Lunar New Year. The article noted that some firms, including large banks and multinationals, offered family-friendly leave beyond the legal minimum. The article's own summary was that taking leave was "mission impossible" for many, a verdict that deserves to be treated as a warning about contracts, not a description of every office.

## The Reckoning

Singapore and Hong Kong are close enough that a candidate might reasonably regard them as interchangeable. On leave, they are not. Singapore has the higher take-up and the larger median in days actually taken, but the fewer public holidays, which makes its time off feel more like an individual negotiation with a manager. Hong Kong has the bigger public calendar, which supplies collective rest independent of anyone's mood, and a workforce that uses its annual leave in small pieces.

The numbers are close enough that the gap is better read as a difference in habit than in law. Nine percentage points separate the share of Singaporeans and Hong Kongers who use at least four-fifths of their entitlement, and the Hong Kong workforce compensates with shorter, more frequent absences. A visitor from Europe, accustomed to a fortnight on a beach, will find the Hong Kong rhythm of two-day escapes slightly bleak and the Singapore rhythm of one big trip and a few long weekends only marginally better.

The deciding factor is therefore the employer, not the city. Both places offer the same legal floor, and both have examples of firms that treat it as a ceiling. In Singapore, the risk is the colleague who reads your full use of 21 days as a comment on theirs. In Hong Kong, the risk is the policy: caps, blackout periods and sick days deducted from annual leave can narrow what the entitlement means in practice, and the 2017 forum accounts suggest that some employers did exactly that.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (r/singapore, via The Independent Singapore) — paraphrased experience: A Singaporean returning home after nearly ten years found that none of his close friends would take annual leave to see him. Commenters explained that people with 14 to 18 days save them for overseas trips and family, that last-minute leave is hard at some firms, and that evenings and weekends work better.*

> *Reddit (via People Matters SEA) — paraphrased experience: A woman back from maternity leave, with 21 days and no carry-forward, said her boss disparaged her for using them. An HR commenter said the boss was likely irritated that the company would not hire cover; a manager said their team ran short-staffed as "malicious compliance."*

> *Hong Kong Discussion Group forum (via Human Resources Online, 2017) — paraphrased experience: Anonymous posters described employers that capped leave at three consecutive days, barred leave next to weekends, deducted sick days from annual leave and made staff take the statutory seven days over Lunar New Year.*

> *Expat.com (Singapore forum) — paraphrased experience: A reply to a prospective hire at a large technology firm warned that Western work-life norms do not apply across Asia, with overtime often expected and unpaid and leave limited. Another said Singapore is not a "996" country, though the job remains demanding.*

## Conclusion

Do not compare the two cities on the statutory number; it is the same. Compare the employer's written policy on carry-forward, cash-out, consecutive-day caps and whether sick days come out of annual leave. Then add the public holidays, 11 in Singapore against 17 in Hong Kong, and ask how many days your future manager took last year.

If you are an expatriate used to thirty days and a culture of using them, either city will feel brisk. Hong Kong will feel briskest on paper and Singapore, perhaps, in the office. In both, the entitlements are real, the conventions are quieter than the contract, and the person who most needs to be convinced that you may take a holiday is, as ever, you.
