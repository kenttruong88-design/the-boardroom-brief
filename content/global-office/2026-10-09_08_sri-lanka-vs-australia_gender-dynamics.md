**The Country That Scores "Feminine" and the Country That Scores "Equal" Are Both Lying to You a Little**

**🇱🇰 Sri Lanka · 🇦🇺 Australia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Hofstede Insights measures Sri Lanka as one of the most "feminine" cultures on earth, scoring just 10 on masculinity, where competitiveness and assertiveness are culturally low-status traits. It also has one of the worst female labor force participation rates in the region, ranked 165th out of 185 countries, with women earning 63% of what men earn. Australia, by contrast, prizes individual assertiveness and scores among the highest in the world on individualism — and still can't close a national gender pay gap that sat at 21.1% in 2025. Neither country's cultural self-image predicts its own workplace numbers. That gap is the actual story.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇱🇰 Sri Lanka

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly about flexible hours and transport safety before accepting a role; both are cited as major, documented reasons women leave or avoid jobs | Assume a lower salary offer to a woman reflects lower ability; a male manager quoted in one investigation said women are "more responsible and harder working" but "generally satisfied with lower salaries" |
| Expect strong family input into career decisions, for better and worse — it can mean real support or real pressure to stop working | Assume harassment complaints get handled smoothly; one architect described leaving a job after being physically struck by an employer in front of colleagues |
| Look specifically at the industry's gender composition before joining; arts fields run roughly 80% women while IT and engineering skew heavily male | Assume "progress" is linear; women held 75% of migrant labor jobs in 1996 and under 40% by 2019 — the trend has moved backward on several measures |
| Budget real time for unpaid care work if you're a working mother; women in western Sri Lanka report spending 5-7 hours a day on childcare on top of a job | Travel without factoring in harassment risk; 90% of Sri Lankan women report experiencing harassment on public transport, a real driver of workforce exit |
| Seek out companies with documented family-friendly policies; the World Bank and others have specifically flagged their absence as a solvable gap | Assume a STEM degree guarantees a STEM job; women make up a small share of engineering and IT graduates despite real demand |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate total remuneration, not just base pay; the all-in gender pay gap (26.2%) runs wider than the headline base-salary figure (21.1%) | Assume a progressive reputation equals progressive outcomes; the national pay gap narrowed only 0.7 points in a year |
| Ask specifically about board and executive gender composition if leadership matters to you; 24% of boards still have zero female members | Assume men and women perceive the workplace the same way; a national survey found 71% of men say they're treated equally versus 61% of women |

### Sri Lanka

Sri Lanka's low Hofstede masculinity score reflects real cultural values — modesty, consensus, caring over competing — but those values haven't translated into workplace access. Female labor force participation has fallen more than 10 percentage points over three decades, tracking a shift away from agriculture that happened to coincide with women losing ground rather than gaining it (ft.lk). Over 40% of working-age women cite family responsibilities as the reason they're not employed, and the obstacles are structural as much as personal: 90% of women report harassment on public transport, which alone accounts for more than 15% of the participation gap, and employer-side bias compounds it — a UN Women study found roughly three-quarters of male employers surveyed showed measurable unconscious prejudice against hiring women at all (unwomen.org, ft.lk).

The individual stories sharpen the statistics. Shanika, a 32-year-old architect, left her last job after an employer she described as "a pervert" struck her with a plank in front of colleagues. Nojitha Sukumaran, 21, said her mother stopped speaking to her for a month in an attempt to talk her out of working at all. Ashmini J. Udhyani, a 26-year-old engineering graduate, put the structural bias in one line: "Society believes that engineering is not for women" (ft.lk). A male manager interviewed in the same reporting, Ruwantha Hettiarachchi, offered perhaps the most revealing line in the entire piece, intended as praise: women are "more responsible and harder working than men but are generally satisfied with lower salaries."

### Australia

Australia's 2025 Gender Equality Scorecard put the national pay gap at 21.1%, narrowed slightly from 21.8% — meaning women earn roughly 79 cents for every dollar men earn, a gap worth $28,356 a year on average (moneymag.com.au). The number gets starker at the top: women chief executives earn $83,493 less in base salary than their male counterparts, a gap that widens to $185,335 once super, bonuses, and other payments are included. Representation tells a similar story of slow, partial progress — women hold 43% of management roles and 33% of board seats, but 24% of boards still have no female member at all.

What the raw numbers miss is what a University of Sydney survey of 2,000 young professionals found underneath them: a persistent perception gap, where 71% of men believe men and women are treated equally at work against only 61% of women who agree, and strikingly little movement on that gap since 2017, despite #MeToo and the pandemic in between (sydney.edu.au). Researcher Rae Cooper's reading of the data was blunt: "Women are telling us they are sick of being spoken down to and passed over for opportunities." Her colleague Elizabeth Hill found something that should reframe the whole conversation: respect, not pay, is what women rank as their single top workplace priority.

### The Reckoning

The real reckoning here isn't Sri Lanka versus Australia — it's Hofstede's framework versus outcomes in both places at once. A "feminine," consensus-valuing culture (Sri Lanka, scoring 10 on masculinity) produced some of the worst female workforce access numbers in the region. A highly individualist culture (Australia, scoring 90) that nominally rewards personal merit regardless of gender still runs a 21% pay gap and a well-documented "boys' club" perception problem among its own employees. Cultural values about gender and actual institutional outcomes for women are, it turns out, two almost entirely separate systems that happen to share a vocabulary.

What connects them is a shared, specific failure: both countries've made real progress on access (getting women hired, counted, surveyed) while making much less progress on power (who gets promoted, who sits on the board, who gets listened to in the room). Sri Lanka's gap shows up as women simply not entering certain fields. Australia's gap shows up as women entering every field and still, on average, ending the year $28,356 poorer for it.

### Conclusion

If you're a woman weighing these two postings, the honest comparison isn't which culture "respects women more" in the abstract — neither one, by its own data, fully does. It's which specific failure you're better positioned to navigate. In Sri Lanka, the barriers are concentrated and visible: transport safety, family pressure, occupational segregation, each addressable with the right employer and the right precautions. In Australia, the barriers are diffuse and procedural: a pay gap that persists despite formal equality, a culture of nominal merit that still runs a boys'-club undercurrent underneath it. The thing I'd tell a friend: ask for the specific numbers — the real pay gap, the real board composition, the real harassment policy — in both places, because in neither country does the national reputation tell you what you're actually walking into.
