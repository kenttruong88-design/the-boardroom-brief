# Kenya Assigns You a Mentor by Osmosis, the UK Assigns You a Laptop and Hopes for the Best

🇰🇪 Kenya · 🇬🇧 UK

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In a 2018 survey of 1,000 UK office workers, commissioned by an HR software firm, 41.1% had quit a job within six months, and 23% reported turning up on day one to find no desk. The UK has produced a thriving industry of induction checklists and a workforce that still sometimes meets its first Monday standing near a printer. Kenya, by contrast, runs onboarding on hierarchy, relationships and a probation clause, which is a different kind of system with a different kind of surprise.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use job titles and full names when introducing seniors | Open with a first-name nickname for the managing director |
| Accept invitations to lunch or dinner with colleagues | Treat small talk before a meeting as wasted time |
| Have your contract reviewed by an advocate before signing | Assume the probation terms match what was said at interview |
| Ask a senior colleague outright to mentor you | Wait for an official programme to announce itself |
| Be on time; meetings in Nairobi tend to start when stated | Judge smaller towns' pace by the capital's clock |
| Defer to age and rank in the room, then raise ideas privately | Contradict the most senior person in front of the team |

### UK

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Chase HR politely if you have heard nothing before your start date | Assume silence means everything is in hand |
| Ask who your buddy is on day one | Wait to be told, because nobody may have decided |
| Read feedback for what is not said ('interesting idea' can mean no) | Take the absence of criticism as approval |
| Book your own check-ins with your manager | Expect the manager to schedule them unprompted |
| Complete the mandatory health and safety and data training promptly | Skip it because it looks like a formality |
| Ask a colleague to explain the unwritten office rules | Guess, and learn them by breaking them |

## Kenya

Kenyan onboarding is rarely a programme and frequently a set of relationships. Expat.com's guide to working in Kenya says hierarchy is very important in business, that junior staff generally defer to senior managers and elders, and that getting to know colleagues is expected, with small talk before meetings and lunches or dinners as a normal part of the working week. A Business Today Kenya opinion piece from 2018 describes informal onboarding, through everyday conversation with managers and peers, as better suited to small firms or companies with a strong culture, and the same author admitted he knew of no measures of employee turnover in East Africa. Data, in other words, is thin.

What the guide does supply is the rhythm of the job: standard hours of 8am to 5pm with a one-hour lunch, a performance-focused culture in many private firms, at least 21 days of annual leave and about 12 public holidays. It also says that many workers report weaker work-life balance than they would like, while expats often report generous salaries and satisfaction despite long hours. It does not discuss probation or onboarding processes at all, which tells you something about where the preparation is expected to come from.

The legal frame is firmer. Under Kenya's Employment Act, as summarised by CM Advocates, a probationary contract must be in writing, may run for six months and can be extended by up to six more only with the employee's consent, for a total of no more than twelve months. Either side can end it with seven days' written notice unless the contract says otherwise, though a 2021 High Court ruling cited by the firm extended fair-hearing protections to probationary employees. Mentorship, meanwhile, is increasingly formalised in sectors that hire at scale. A Generation Kenya impact study, presented in Nairobi in May 2026, found that 63.6% of digital customer service mentees who exceeded workplace expectations credited mentorship directly, and a Teleperformance executive cited more than 318 employee mentors across Kenya, Nigeria and Rwanda since 2022. The article did not state the study's sample size, which should temper the enthusiasm. At the individual level, a Nation Africa piece quotes a radio director who says he has mentored eight former interns and argues that supported juniors stay longer, and a theatre veteran who recalls having to travel to Nairobi to find production mentorship at all. Mentorship in Kenya is real, but it is unevenly distributed by sector and by postcode.

## UK

British onboarding is formally well-documented and informally erratic. Acas advises employers to consider assigning a mentor or buddy and to avoid giving too much information on the first day, with paperwork such as National Insurance numbers and health and safety covered early. In practice the 2018 Cezanne HR survey found 45.6% of workers heard nothing from their new employer between accepting and starting, 20% felt frustrated or ignored on day one, and 28.6% had no computer. Of those who left within six months, 50.61% said the job was not what they expected. A far older survey from Begbies Traynor, reported by Personnel Today in 2003, found that only 32% had been given a buddy while 71% thought they would have benefited from one, and 46% received no training in their first week. The dates matter, but the shape of the problem has aged well. Asked what would have helped, 38.1% of the 2018 respondents wanted more training, 32.4% wanted clearer goals and 24.6% wanted more regular manager check-ins, which is a respectable description of mentorship by people who were not offered the word. Only 55.5% had received data security training, so the one thing the UK reliably formalises is not even reliably formalised.

For the expat there is a second layer in the communication style. Hofstede's scores, as reproduced by GVSU Pressbooks, put the UK at 35 on power distance and 89 on individualism. The East Africa regional figures on a business etiquette site put the equivalent at 64 and 27. That translates into a UK manager who expects you to take initiative and tell them what you need, and a Kenyan manager who expects you to defer and wait for direction. Both are comfortable. Each finds the other faintly alarming.

## The Reckoning

On the evidence, Kenya offers closeness and the UK offers structure, and neither delivers both. A Kenyan newcomer is expected to read the room, find a senior ally and earn trust, which works well if the room has a senior ally in it and badly if it does not. A UK newcomer gets paperwork, a policy handbook and a manager whose feedback arrives wrapped in so much courtesy that it needs decoding.

The practical difference lies in who owns the relationship. In Kenya, mentorship tends to be granted from above and rewards the person who shows respect and persistence. In the UK, it is something you are meant to request, and silence is read as contentment. If you are moving from Nairobi to London, ask for your buddy on the first day. If you are moving the other way, find your senior person by the second week.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Mumsnet — paraphrased experience: A new UK starter, contract signed and equipment delivered, had no start time, contact names or induction details a few days before Monday and feared chasing would look needy. Replies were calm, predicting an invite by Friday and advising them to be ready for 9am regardless.*

> *Expat.com England forum — paraphrased experience: A Spanish resident of about five years feels settled in England but still finds indirect communication at work tiring. She misses straight feedback and spends time softening her own criticism and guessing what colleagues mean.*

> *Expat.com England forum — paraphrased experience: A German resident said colleagues called him confrontational for speaking openly. He no longer minds, but suspected a younger person might find the reticence unbearable and go home.*

> *The Filipino Expat — paraphrased experience: A writer who headed a college in Nairobi described rigid roles, superiors expected to keep their distance, and an owner whose decisions were final. Her hire turned out to be partly symbolic, and she left after about three months.*

> *Nation Africa, Baptism by fire — paraphrased experience: Two Kenyans recounted first jobs. One receptionist was left running everything alone with no support. A young presenter was mentored by veteran journalists and given real control by his supervisor, and says it launched his career.*

## Conclusion

Neither country has solved the first ninety days. Kenya relies on the human network and a legal probation window, and the UK relies on a checklist that works about as well as the person holding it. The sensible expat treats both systems as incomplete and supplies the missing piece personally: a senior ally in Nairobi, a documented buddy in London.

Either way, arrive on time, learn the names of the people who matter, and remember that in both countries the real induction begins the moment someone forgets to give you one.
