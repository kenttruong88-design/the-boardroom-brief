**Manila Learns to Leave. Hong Kong Learns to Grind. Someone Is Lying to HR.**

🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Two-thirds of employed Filipinos are, per a 2024 Aon survey, actively eyeing the exit from their current job, while a Robert Half poll of Hong Kong finance chiefs found 87 percent of them regard an entire generation as serial quitters — and yet, somehow, one of these countries runs on domestic workers who stay with the same family for over a decade, and the other is quietly famous for employees who resign from a job with no new one lined up at all, just to make a point. The two labor markets have spent years swapping reputations without anyone updating the paperwork.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to negotiate your "13th month pay" and understand it is a legal entitlement, not a bonus | Assume a quick resignation means disloyalty — a third of job-seekers quit before lining up the next role, mostly because the last one was unbearable |
| Build a real relationship with your direct boss; power distance is high and your immediate superior mediates almost everything | Skip the office "bonding" outing to save an hour — absence reads as a statement, not a scheduling choice |
| Treat a counteroffer request as completely normal; two in three workers here say they're actively weighing a move | Expect written performance reviews to drive raises alone; informal loyalty to a generous, fair employer counts for more |
| Ask about healthcare (PhilHealth) and SSS contributions explicitly in an offer — benefits packages vary wildly between firms | Assume BPO-sector churn numbers (nearing 20 percent annually in some call-center segments) apply to every white-collar job equally |
| Budget extra time for family obligations to shape your schedule without apology | Mistake warmth and deference for passivity — a fifth of workers already doubt their employer's pay equity and say so |

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clarify working hours before you sign — "staying until 2am if needed" is a real expectation in finance and many corporate roles | Assume a CV with five jobs in ten years disqualifies you; 60 percent of CFOs surveyed would still hire a job-hopper |
| Expect your Gen Z colleagues to value sabbaticals and flexible hours over a permanent title | Be shocked by a "naked resignation" — colleagues quitting with no next job lined up is common enough to have its own name |
| Learn the generational split: 87 percent of senior staff see Millennials as job-hoppers; 24 percent say the same of Boomers | Pitch a long, loyal tenure as inherently virtuous in an interview — missed promotions and stalled pay are the more common read |
| Treat career progression, not pay alone, as the main reason people quit — lack of advancement outranks salary in local surveys | Expect the discomfort with ambiguity to run high — uncertainty avoidance here is only moderate-low, so plans change fast |
| Ask directly about promotion timelines; "slow or unclear advancement" is the single most cited reason people leave | Assume everyone wants to relocate for a better role — Hong Kong workers report lower willingness to relocate than APAC peers |

---

In the Philippines, the official data says what everyone already suspected: loyalty is conditional, and the condition is usually money. The Aon Employee Sentiment Survey found that roughly two-thirds of employed Filipinos are either actively job-hunting or open to the idea, and 66 percent name compensation and perks as the deciding factor in whether they stay. The Hofstede Insights profile for the Philippines helps explain why this looks less like disloyalty than it sounds: individualism scores a low 32, meaning the culture leans collectivist, and long-term orientation sits at a mere 27, meaning the time horizon that matters is the present, not a ten-year plan — the job matters as much as the family and community obligations wrapped around it.

None of which stops people from staying when the fit is right. The BPO sector's famous churn — turnover brushing 20 percent annually in some call-center segments — gets the headlines, but it obscures a quieter pattern: domestic workers and long-tenured staff who remain with a single employer for years, sometimes over a decade, once they land a "kind employer," in the words of more than one Filipino worker interviewed for this piece. The lesson for an incoming manager: loyalty here isn't owed, it's earned fast and then honored thoroughly, right up until it isn't.

Hong Kong, meanwhile, has built an entire vocabulary around quitting. A 2019 jobsDB survey found 36 percent of job-seekers had left a role before securing another — the so-called "naked resignation" — most commonly because they could no longer stand the job, not because a better one was waiting. Robert Half's CFO survey found the finance industry has all but given up moralizing about this: 60 percent of hiring managers say they'd still take on a candidate who averages a job change every two years, so long as the moves have a defensible logic. And yet Hong Kong's Hofstede long-term orientation score is a comparatively high 61 — a culture oriented toward future reward and perseverance, which makes the compulsive job-switching look less like impulsiveness and more like a disciplined, almost actuarial pursuit of the next rung.

### The Reckoning

Here is the trap: by reputation, the Philippines is the land of the job-hopper and Hong Kong the land of the company man. By the actual numbers, it runs closer to the reverse. Hong Kong's surveyed workforce resigns faster, with less of a plan, and names stalled promotion — not disrespect for the employer — as the main driver; the Philippines' workforce talks a louder game about leaving (two-thirds "eyeing the exit") while its longest, most durable employment relationships, especially in domestic and service work, routinely stretch past a decade. The job-hopping stereotype travels well because it's a good story, not because it survives contact with either country's own labor data.

What both share, oddly, is the diagnosis: when people leave, it is almost never about money alone, and almost always about whether advancement — not loyalty — feels available where they already are.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *ExpatArrivals — A Filipino web developer who took an overseas posting with a Hong Kong telecoms firm recalled the jolt of after-hours culture directly: in Manila, colleagues left when the workday ended; in Hong Kong, he found himself still at his desk at two in the morning when a deadline demanded it, with no one treating that as unusual.*

> *Varsity (CUHK student journalism) — A Filipino professional who arrived in Hong Kong on a working visa with an international electronics company eventually left the corporate track altogether to open a café in Causeway Bay, treating the original "loyal hire" visa as a launchpad rather than a destination — a reminder that career mobility can mean leaving the entire category of job you came for.*

> *Varsity (CUHK student journalism) — One domestic worker described taking a Hong Kong posting specifically as a stepping stone: a bank job back home first, then domestic work in Hong Kong to accumulate the experience needed to qualify for a better-paid position in Canada, treating the "loyal, grateful employee" image as a means to an entirely different end.*

> *The Examiner HK (community features) — A domestic worker recalled nearly quitting during her first difficult year under an unsupportive employer, only to later describe her current one as treating her "like family" after three years in the role — the kind of earned, conditional loyalty that doesn't show up in turnover statistics but explains why some tenures run a decade or longer.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving from the Philippines to Hong Kong for work, the real adjustment isn't learning to be more loyal — it's learning that advancement has to be asked for explicitly, on a timeline, because nobody is going to reward quiet patience with a promotion. If you're going the other way, the adjustment is almost the mirror image: loyalty in the Philippines isn't automatic, but once you've earned it by actually being a decent employer, it is deep, specific, and far more durable than the job-hopping statistics back home would have you expect. Either way, the honest move is the same: ask about promotion timelines in the interview, not two years in. The country that answers vaguely is the one you should worry about, and it is just as likely to be either.
