**The Forty-Minute Sandwich vs. the Two-Hour Negotiation: How the Netherlands and France Turned Lunch Into a Referendum on National Character**

**🇳🇱 Netherlands · 🇫🇷 France**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In OECD time-use comparisons, French respondents post some of the longest daily meal durations recorded anywhere in the survey — well over two hours a day spent at the table, lunch included. Dutch respondents, sharing a border, a currency, and a train line with them, treat lunch as a scheduling inconvenience to be resolved in under half an hour with cheese on bread. Neither country considers its own approach remarkable. Both consider the other's slightly unhinged. This is, as far as workplace culture shock goes, one of the more reliable ones.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring your own lunch to the office — bread, cheese, maybe fruit. It's standard practice, not stinginess. | Expect a subsidized hot-meal canteen; many offices have a fridge and a toaster, not a kitchen. |
| Treat lunch as a 30-to-45-minute refuel, then get back to your desk. | Book a two-hour client lunch and expect anyone to clear their calendar for it. |
| Show up to the Friday-afternoon *borrel* (drinks); it's where relationships actually get built. | Assume work bonding happens over a meal; it mostly happens over beer, standing up. |
| Split the bill exactly, even with close colleagues. | Insist on paying for the group — it reads as strange, not generous. |
| Expect dinner early, often at home around 6pm. | Propose an 8:30pm dinner meeting; most colleagues will already have eaten. |
| Say plainly if you don't like what's served. | Fake enthusiasm for raw herring — nobody will be offended either way. |

#### 🇫🇷 France
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Block out a real lunch hour, even on a deadline day. | Eat a sandwich alone at your desk — colleagues will quietly worry about you. |
| Accept a lunch invitation from a client or boss as a legitimate work venue. | Try to pitch the deal before the cheese course arrives. |
| Use your *titre-restaurant* vouchers at an actual sit-down place. | Treat lunch as "just food" — it's where trust gets negotiated. |
| Let the senior person at the table set the pace and order the wine. | Flag down the waiter for the bill mid-meal; it will not go well. |
| Expect dinner around 8pm, and take a home dinner invitation seriously. | Arrive exactly on time to a French dinner invite — ten minutes late is the norm. |
| Compliment the meal specifically. | Call the food "fine" — mild praise lands as a quiet insult. |

The Dutch relationship with lunch is best understood as an extension of a broader national allergy to fuss. Eurostat's harmonised time-use surveys have long placed the Netherlands among the countries spending the least time on meals as a discrete activity, and DutchNews.nl has reported, more than once and without apparent shame, that a large share of Dutch office workers eat lunch at their desks, often alone, often in under twenty minutes. The FAO's food balance data tells a complementary story: the Netherlands is one of Europe's largest dairy and agri-food exporters, yet domestic food spending as a share of household budgets is among the lowest on the continent — a country that is extremely good at producing food and only moderately interested in dwelling on it.

This is not indifference so much as prioritisation. Hofstede Insights' country-comparison data shows the Netherlands scoring low on Power Distance and markedly high on Individualism relative to most of Europe, which tracks neatly onto a culture where nobody's lunch is anybody else's business, where splitting the bill to the cent is a form of respect rather than meanness, and where the real currency of workplace bonding is the after-hours *borrel*, not the midday meal. Gallup World Poll data on daily wellbeing consistently shows Northern Europeans reporting fewer meals shared with others than their Southern and Western neighbours — and the Dutch wear that statistic with something close to pride.

The practical upshot for an incoming expat: lunch in a Dutch office is logistics, not theatre. Pack something. Eat it quickly. Save your social capital for the drinks trolley.

France runs the opposite experiment. The country's labour code sets only a modest legal minimum break, but the social contract around lunch has always demanded far more than the law requires — and recent coverage in the Economist and the Financial Times on the slow creep of desk-sandwich culture into Parisian offices reads, to French traditionalists, as a minor act of civilisational decline. Eurostat and OECD figures converge on the same point from different angles: French respondents report some of the longest total daily eating time of any surveyed population, and the subsidized *titre-restaurant* voucher system — still used by millions of employees — exists precisely to keep lunch a sit-down, paid-for, hour-plus event rather than something consumed standing up.

FAO consumption data adds the texture: France remains near the top of Europe for per-capita wine and cheese consumption, even as younger, urban workers quietly trim both from the weekday ritual. HBR and MIT Sloan case material on doing business in France consistently flags the same lesson for newcomers — the lunch or dinner invitation is not a pleasantry layered on top of the negotiation, it is the negotiation, or at least its necessary rehearsal. Hofstede's data on France shows notably higher Uncertainty Avoidance than the Netherlands, which maps onto a culture that prefers to build trust slowly, over courses, before getting to the point — the opposite of the Dutch instinct to get to the point and skip the courses.

The practical upshot: resist the urge to speed-run lunch. In France, slow is not a sign of inefficiency. It is the mechanism by which colleagues decide whether they trust you.

**The Reckoning.** Put the two side by side and the irony sharpens: the country that eats fastest, the Netherlands, also posts some of the shortest average working hours in the OECD, while France's languorous lunches coexist with hourly productivity figures that regularly rival or beat countries that eat standing up. Long lunches, it turns out, are not obviously the productivity drain the sandwich-at-the-desk crowd assumes — and the forty-minute Dutch lunch is no guarantee of a longer workday either, since the whole point of finishing fast is finishing the day fast too.

The more useful contrast is about where the actual work gets done. In Amsterdam, it happens in meetings and over beer afterward; lunch is neutral territory nobody weaponises. In Paris, lunch is not neutral at all — it is one of the primary venues where hierarchy, trust, and deals get quietly arranged, which means skipping it isn't just antisocial, it can be strategically costly.

### Conclusion

The single fact worth carrying into a relocation decision is this: in the Netherlands, lunch is logistics and the real relationship-building happens elsewhere, mostly standing up with a drink in hand; in France, lunch is the relationship-building, and treating it as an interruption to the workday is itself the cultural mistake. Neither approach is more "serious" about work than the other — the Dutch simply decided food wasn't where seriousness lives, and the French decided it was.

If you're choosing between the two for a career move, choose based on what kind of professional intimacy you can tolerate: fast, transactional, and resolved over beer later, or slow, loaded, and resolved over the cheese course. Either way, over drinks with a friend, I'd put it like this: in Amsterdam they'll tell you exactly what they think of your work by 5pm; in Paris you'll find out over lunch, three courses in, right after they've decided whether they like you.
