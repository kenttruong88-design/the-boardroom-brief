# In the UAE, Your Performance Review Might Be Tied to Whether You Get to Stay in the Country. In Poland, It's Just Someone Telling You the Truth, Privately.

**🇦🇪 UAE · 🇵🇱 Poland**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In the UAE, HR research on performance management is explicit about the stakes: with an overwhelmingly expat workforce, structured and transparent feedback is described as directly tied to retention "which is expensive to manage" given visa and recruitment costs — meaning your review isn't just about your output, it's implicitly about the cost of replacing you as a sponsored worker. In Poland, feedback tends to be blunt and specific, delivered person to person, but a 2026 Statista survey found that 90 percent of Polish workers reported never being criticized, humiliated, or ridiculed in front of colleagues or customers in the past five years. The lesson in both places: directness and cruelty are not the same thing, and neither is politeness the same as softness.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇪 UAE

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand that performance reviews are increasingly tied to structured retention strategies, not just goodwill | Assume feedback culture is uniform — it varies enormously between multinational firms and local employers |
| Seek out 360-degree or peer feedback channels where offered — many firms are actively building these | Expect a single annual sit-down to be the whole picture; some companies now use continuous, multi-source feedback |
| Take manager coaching and mentorship elements of appraisal seriously — it's an intentional retention tool | Assume a lack of feedback means a lack of concern about your performance; visa economics make retention data-driven |
| Ask directly how review outcomes connect to compensation and contract renewal | Assume review outcomes are disconnected from your sponsorship and visa status — in practice, they can be linked |
| Research your specific employer's review cadence and rigor before accepting an offer | Extrapolate one company's laid-back review culture onto the whole country — variance is very high |

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect direct, specific, and honest feedback delivered privately, one to one | Assume bluntness means hostility — most critical feedback happens without any public element at all |
| Treat criticism as a sign you're taken seriously, not a signal you're disliked | Take direct feedback personally or assume it reflects a breakdown in the relationship |
| Expect structured, procedure-driven review processes, consistent with high uncertainty avoidance norms | Expect a loose, improvisational review process; Poland's workplace culture favors defined structure |
| Give feedback back the same way — direct and specific — once trust is established | Soften your own feedback so much that it reads as evasive or insincere to Polish colleagues |
| Note that public criticism in front of others is rare and generally considered inappropriate | Assume that because feedback is direct, it will also happen publicly — the two are separate norms |

---

The UAE's performance review landscape is shaped less by a single national norm than by the country's uniquely expat-heavy labor structure. HR industry research from firms like peopleHum and Bayzat frames modern UAE appraisal systems around structured transparency specifically because turnover is costly in a market defined by visa sponsorship and recruitment overhead — retention isn't just a management nicety, it's a line item. This has pushed a growing number of employers toward 360-degree feedback, peer review, and manager-as-coach models, according to industry guidance, even as Quora threads from workers describe wide variance in practice: one respondent characterized Dubai workplace culture as ranging from "highly professional in global firms to more hierarchical" at some local employers, with bullying and power harassment occurring at some but by no means all workplaces. Hofstede data placing the UAE high on both power distance and collectivism suggests that even where structured feedback processes exist, their delivery is likely to be filtered through relationship and hierarchy rather than treated as a purely procedural exchange.

Poland's feedback culture runs on a different axis entirely: high uncertainty avoidance translates into structured, procedure-driven review processes, but the delivery style within that structure is notably direct. Research into Polish workplace communication consistently frames this as a low-context, task-oriented style — critical feedback is specific and unambiguous rather than softened or buried in diplomatic language. The Statista figure is the important corrective here: directness does not mean public humiliation. Ninety percent of Polish workers report never having been criticized or ridiculed in front of others in the past five years, suggesting a workplace norm where the blunt part of Polish feedback culture is reserved for private, one-to-one delivery — a nuance that Blind (teamblind.com) discussions of Polish tech offices echo, describing frank internal critique without describing public callouts as a norm.

The reckoning: the UAE's feedback culture is shaped primarily by structural economics — the visa-tied, high-turnover-cost dynamics of an expat labor market — more than by any single cultural disposition toward bluntness or diplomacy, which is why it varies so widely company to company. Poland's feedback culture is shaped by a genuine cultural preference for direct, honest communication, but bounded by an equally genuine norm against public embarrassment. A UAE-based manager transplanted to Warsaw might expect the same variance they saw at home and instead find remarkably consistent bluntness, delivered privately, everywhere they go. A Polish employee moving to Dubai might expect that consistency and instead find that the entire tenor of their review depends almost entirely on which specific company they've landed at.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone asking how to handle a performance review under a "really mean boss" received advice acknowledging that workplace bullying does happen, particularly at less internationally-managed employers in markets like Dubai, but that documenting specific incidents and understanding your visa-linked leverage mattered more than the review conversation itself.*

> *Quora — A respondent describing Dubai's working culture broadly said it ranged from highly professional at global multinational offices to considerably more hierarchical and occasionally harsh at some local employers, making blanket assumptions about "UAE feedback culture" unreliable without knowing the specific company.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A discussion of a Polish tech office (CD Projekt RED) described a culture of frank, risk-taking internal critique and improved compensation in recent years, while separately rating work-life balance as the company's weakest metric — direct feedback and demanding workload showing up as genuinely separate axes.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — An employee comparing an offshore Krakow engineering team's management to headquarters expectations described very low accountability at the local leadership level, illustrating that even within Poland's generally structured, direct feedback norms, individual team culture can diverge sharply from the national pattern.*

> *Quora — A poster asking whether it's appropriate to offer constructive criticism about a manager during a formal review was advised, generally across contexts, that unsolicited upward criticism is risky regardless of country — the structural power gap in a review conversation rarely disappears just because the culture prizes directness.*

### Conclusion

If you want feedback that's predictable in style but unpredictable in structural stakes, the UAE requires you to research your specific employer closely, since national generalizations will mislead you as often as they help. If you want feedback that's blunt but reliably private and procedurally consistent, Poland delivers that with unusual regularity — the bluntness is real, but so is the norm against making anyone feel humiliated over it. The practical move in both places is the same: find out what actually happens at your specific company before you assume the national stereotype applies. If a friend asked me over a drink: in Poland, trust that direct means direct, not cruel; in the UAE, trust nothing about feedback culture until you've asked someone who already works there.
