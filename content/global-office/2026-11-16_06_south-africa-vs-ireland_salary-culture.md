# South Africa Pays You in Rand and Resentment; Ireland Pays You in Euro and Silence

🇿🇦 South Africa · 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Ireland, asking a colleague what they earn invites the reply that a priest would not ask that. In South Africa, the average formal worker earns R29,490 a month, and nearly half of working professionals say they are grossly underpaid anyway. Two countries have independently concluded that salary is a sensitive subject. They differ chiefly in what they do about it.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate everything before you sign, including relocation help and any sign-on payment | Expect to win concessions after you have arrived |
| Treat pension and medical aid as non-negotiable parts of the package | Accept an offer without them and assume you can sort it out later |
| Benchmark against salary surveys and developer communities before you answer the offer | Quote a figure without checking the city; Johannesburg typically pays more than Cape Town |
| Ask whether the company has an expat policy; local hires usually get none | Assume a local contract carries expat perks |
| Ask about pay equity and gender pay audits | Assume the pay gap is someone else's problem |

### In Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Look for a stated salary range in the advertisement; the draft law goes beyond the EU directive on this | Be offended when a private employer lists no figure; only 39 percent of Irish ads did in March 2026 |
| Use published public-sector pay scales as a benchmark | Assume private employers publish equivalents |
| Expect questions about your pay history to become unlawful under the incoming rules | Volunteer your current salary unprompted |
| Ask younger colleagues what they earn; they are likelier to answer | Ask your manager's generation over lunch |
| Compare weekly and hourly figures, not headline salaries | Forget that average weekly earnings are measured against a roughly 32-hour paid week |

## South Africa

South Africa's salary picture is a series of records accompanied by a mood. According to Statistics South Africa's Quarterly Employment Survey for the third quarter of 2025, the average salary for formal, non-agricultural workers reached R29,490, up 0.3 percent on the quarter and 4.3 percent on the year. BusinessTech called it a record high. The survey excludes agriculture and the informal sector, so it describes the people with payslips, not the country.

Those people are not delighted. A recruitment platform cited by TimesLIVE found that 48 percent of working professionals say their salaries do not reflect their work, and that many live from paycheque to paycheque. The same report notes that salaries in Johannesburg run more than 10 percent above Cape Town's, and that women earn roughly 24 percent less than men on average, according to a 2025 Discovery Corporate report, with the gap widest at mid and senior levels. OfferZen, a developer marketplace, reports that more than half of developers feel they are not paid fairly and that about one in four avoid salary discussions altogether.

None of the sources reviewed for this piece describes a South African requirement to print a number in the advertisement. What one finds instead is a market in which the offer is the opening move, and in which benefits such as pension and medical aid carry weight that foreign salaries often do not need to. The negotiation takes place on the employer's schedule, and the useful moment to conduct it is before you land. The recruitment platform's chief executive attributes the gender gap to structural issues, such as male-dominated leadership, rather than direct discrimination, and recommends regular pay audits with results published internally. Whether any given employer does this is a question worth asking in the interview, where the answer is as informative as the policy.

## Ireland

Ireland's pay culture is a case of high earnings and low disclosure. The Central Statistics Office reported that average weekly earnings passed 1,000 euro for the first time, reaching 1,026.20 euro in the first quarter of 2025, with average hourly earnings of 31.72 euro and a paid week of 32.4 hours. Public-sector earnings were higher, at 1,224.44 euro a week, and the civil service publishes pay scales for the vast majority of its posts, per TheJournal.ie. Very few private employers do.

The Irish Times describes asking someone's pay as a long-standing social taboo, and notes that secrecy sometimes extends to spouses. Surveys quoted by TheJournal.ie found that only one in four people think their workplace has full pay transparency, over half consider discussing salaries taboo, and only 16 percent would tell colleagues their salary if asked. Robert Walters research reported by Fleet.ie found a generational split: 54 percent of Irish Gen Z professionals discuss pay openly with colleagues, against 29 percent of those aged 45 and over, 41 percent of whom call salary too personal a topic.

The law is about to disagree with the habit. The EU Pay Transparency Directive set a transposition deadline of 7 June 2026, and Ireland's bill, published in January 2025, would require salary information in the advertisement itself and bar employers from asking about pay history. Indeed's March 2026 data show 39 percent of Irish ads carrying a salary, against 56 percent in the UK and 12 percent in Germany, and 82 percent of Irish jobseekers surveyed said a listed range would make them likelier to apply. The advertised ranges are fairly tight: Indeed puts the median at 19 percent wide, against about 35 percent in the Netherlands and roughly 50 percent in Italy. The Irish Examiner adds that the directive would require employers with more than 50 staff to explain how pay and raises are decided, and would let workers ask for average pay among colleagues doing similar work. The Irish Congress of Trade Unions says women earn about 89 cent for each euro men earn per hour, and points out that Sweden and Finland, where pay is largely public, perform well on equality. Employers, for their part, warn that openness may breed resentment. Check the current statute, since the implementation status after the deadline was not confirmed in the sources reviewed here.

## The Reckoning

Head to head, South Africa offers a market where the number is negotiable, the benefits are the real story, and the grievance is widely shared and openly discussed. Ireland offers a market where the number is higher, the paid week shorter, and the conversation about it is still being conducted in euphemism, with legislation arriving to supply the vocabulary. A South African can usually find out what the market pays by asking loudly. An Irish worker, for now, finds out by waiting for a statute.

Hofstede Insights scores Ireland at 68 on motivation towards achievement and success, formerly masculinity, and 28 for power distance. South Africa scores 63 and 49 respectively, with individualism at 65. The numbers suggest two competitive cultures, one marginally more deferential. They do not predict that one of them would regard a pay question as an offence against manners, which is an Irish particularity. Both countries also share a quieter complaint: pay is a proxy for worth, and nearly everyone believes theirs is understated. A University of Limerick professor, writing for RTE, argues that most people overestimate their own contribution and so feel underpaid even at market rate, which may explain why a record-high average and a majority sense of grievance can coexist. For a newcomer, the practical difference is who volunteers numbers: in South Africa, recruiters and peers will; in Ireland, the government will, eventually.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — a poster advised that packages vary enormously by level and employer, that relocated staff often receive an allowance for about three months while local hires receive nothing special, that pension and medical aid are essential given the cost of living and the rand's instability, and that anything not agreed before signing is unlikely to be agreed afterwards. The thread is several years old.*

> *Hacker News — a Cape Town developer, in a 2015 thread, offered rough monthly ranges of about 18,000 to 22,000 for juniors, 30,000 to 40,000 for mid-level and around 50,000 for experienced engineers, called 50,000 a really good salary against local living costs, and noted that private school for children is expensive. The currency was not stated and the figures are dated.*

> *Irish Examiner — a woman using a pseudonym said that in a private-sector job all staff negotiated their own rates, that those who accepted the first offer, often the more qualified, earned less and resented it, and that she later learned she had accepted half of a television appearance fee that was available. She now works in the public sector and prefers its published scales.*

> *Image.ie — an Irish writer argues that she was brought up to treat asking about money as rude, that privacy about pay mostly protects employers, and that telling a colleague your salary can help either of you ask for a raise.*

## Conclusion

If you value a number you can read in advance, Ireland is moving towards you and its public sector already delivers it. If you value room to negotiate and are prepared to do your own benchmarking, South Africa will leave the door open and the work to you. In both countries the sensible default is to treat the first offer as an opening position and to ask what the package includes rather than only what it totals.

One more practical point: compare like with like. South Africa's headline figure is a monthly salary in rand from an employer survey, and Ireland's is weekly earnings in euro from the national statistics office. Converting one into the other without adjusting for local costs, tax and benefits is how people arrive at either excessive optimism or unwarranted despair. Ask for the whole package, in writing, and do the arithmetic after.

In Ireland, wait to see whether the new rules have taken effect before building a negotiating strategy on them. In South Africa, build one regardless. Either way, your pay will eventually become public, in the first country by law and in the second by group chat.
