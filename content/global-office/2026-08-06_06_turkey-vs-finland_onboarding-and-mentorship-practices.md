## Two Ways to Learn Where the Coffee Machine Is (and Who's Allowed to Move It)

🇹🇷 Turkey · 🇫🇮 Finland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Helsinki, a new hire's manager will hand them a laptop, a login, and roughly four sentences of instruction, then vanish into what appears to be productive silence. In Istanbul, a new hire's manager will introduce them personally to every person on the floor, insist on lunch together for the first week, and ask about their family before their inbox. Both approaches are functioning perfectly as designed. Neither would survive being transplanted into the other country, and the sooner a transferee accepts that, the sooner they stop mistaking a cultural default for a personal referendum on their competence.

#### 🇹🇷 Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the tea. Repeatedly. It's how relationships open, not a distraction from work. | Decline the first offer of çay and expect to be taken seriously an hour later. |
| Address your manager and senior colleagues with proper titles until invited to drop them. | Assume first-name informality reads as friendliness rather than disrespect. |
| Expect your onboarding to run through people, not a portal — ask who, not just what. | Rely solely on the HR handbook; much of the real orientation happens over lunch. |
| Show up in person for the first weeks, even if the role is nominally flexible. | Push for remote-first status before you've built any in-office rapport. |
| Let a mentor or senior colleague vouch for you before proposing changes to how things run. | Suggest a "better way to do this" in your second week, however obviously correct. |
| Budget real time for small talk before meetings actually start. | Walk in and open with the agenda; it reads as cold, even transactional. |

#### 🇫🇮 Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the onboarding documentation closely — it's often thorough and meant to replace hand-holding. | Wait for someone to walk you through it verbally; that person may not be coming. |
| Say what you actually think in meetings, plainly, when asked. | Hedge everything diplomatically; it can read as evasive rather than polite. |
| Take the silence in conversations at face value — it isn't a hint to fill it. | Interpret a quiet colleague as an unfriendly one. |
| Use the flexibility you're given; managers assume competent adults don't need supervision. | Ask your manager for approval on decisions that are clearly yours to make. |
| Treat the informal "how's it going" check-ins as genuine, not performative. | Expect a formal 30/60/90-day mentorship program; ask directly if you want more structure. |
| Take your full annual leave — colleagues will notice if you don't. | Treat unused vacation days as a sign of dedication. |

### Turkey: Onboarding as an Extended Introduction

Turkish workplace culture runs on relationships that are established before the actual job begins, and onboarding reflects that. According to the OECD's vocational education and training research, work-based learning in Turkey is heavily mediated through structured relationships — trainee-master arrangements, apprenticeship contracts, and direct workplace pairing — rather than standardized digital modules. A newer data point reinforces the same pattern from the employee side: industry surveys of Turkey's younger workforce find that 86 percent actively seek mentors and 88 percent rate on-the-job learning as central to how they want to be trained, a preference that tracks with Hofstede Insights' finding that Turkey scores 66 on power distance against a global norm closer to 55 — hierarchy is expected, and a mentor figure sits comfortably inside that structure rather than undermining it.

The economic backdrop makes the mentorship instinct almost load-bearing. TurkStat's Household Labour Force Survey shows youth labor force participation running well below the OECD average, with a meaningful share of new entrants lacking prior formal work experience — which means the informal apprenticeship a new hire receives from a senior colleague is often filling gaps that the education system left open. The dry read: Turkish companies haven't built elaborate onboarding software because they've never needed to. A person who vouches for you does the job a portal cannot.

### Finland: Onboarding as an Act of Trust

Finland's approach starts from the opposite assumption: that a competent adult, given clear documentation and left alone, will figure most of it out. Statistics Finland's 2022 survey on adult education found that 54 percent of all wage and salary earners received employer-provided personnel training that year — a genuinely high figure — but the delivery skews toward structured courses and self-directed materials rather than paired mentorship. Gallup's State of the Global Workplace research, which finds that managers account for roughly 70 percent of the variance in team engagement worldwide, maps onto Finland in an unusual way: Finnish managers score well on trust precisely by doing less overt managing, not more.

This is consistent with Hofstede Insights' power distance score of 33 for Finland, roughly half of Turkey's, and it explains why so many arriving professionals describe the first weeks as disorienting in a specific way — not unwelcoming, just quiet. Feedback, when it comes, tends to be short, factual, and rare, which Finnish colleagues intend as respect for your competence and which everyone else initially reads as indifference.

### The Reckoning

Put the two next to each other and the counterintuitive part isn't the hierarchy gap, which everyone half-expects — it's that the country with the flatter, more egalitarian structure is actually the harder one to get useful feedback out of. Turkey's steeper hierarchy produces more contact, more instruction, and more correction, precisely because a senior person checking in on a junior one is the whole point of the relationship. Finland's flatter structure produces more autonomy and less contact, because checking in too often would imply the new hire can't be trusted to handle it.

The practical upshot for a mover: in Turkey, silence from your manager is a bad sign — go find the person and ask. In Finland, silence from your manager is the default setting — assume competence unless told otherwise, and stop waiting for a debrief that isn't coming.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/expats — Described the first month in a Finnish office as unnervingly calm: no one asked how the move was going beyond a single polite question on day one, no small talk before meetings, and it took weeks to realize that the silence in the open-plan office wasn't tension, just how people worked.*

> *Quora — A respondent working in Istanbul noted that meetings routinely start ten to fifteen minutes late with food and family talk first, and that trying to skip straight to the agenda in the first few weeks got read as brusque rather than efficient, a habit they had to consciously unlearn.*

> *r/Turkey — A new hire recalled being quietly corrected by a colleague for using informal address with a department head, and realizing only afterward that half the "orientation" they'd received had actually been social coaching disguised as friendliness.*

> *teamblind.com — An engineer who relocated to Helsinki posted about spending two weeks unsure whether their work was any good, since no one had said so either way, before a teammate explained that in Finland "no news is good news" is taken almost literally.*

> *Quora — Someone who'd worked in both countries advised that in Finland you should ask directly for feedback rather than wait for it, since Finnish managers won't volunteer praise, while in Turkey you should let a senior colleague introduce you around before doing anything else, since arriving without an introduction makes every later interaction harder.*

### Conclusion

The single fact worth carrying into either move is this: Turkey onboards you into a network, Finland onboards you into a system. In Turkey, your first weeks are a test of whether people like and trust you, and the org chart is almost secondary to who took you to lunch. In Finland, your first weeks are a test of whether you can be handed documentation and left alone, and the warmth arrives later, once you've proven you don't need to be managed. Mistake one for the other and you'll spend a month either offending people in Istanbul with your efficiency or unsettling people in Helsinki with your need for reassurance.

If a friend asked me over a drink, I'd say: in Turkey, show up to the tea. In Finland, read the document twice before you ask the question — and when you finally get an answer, trust that it was complete the first time.
