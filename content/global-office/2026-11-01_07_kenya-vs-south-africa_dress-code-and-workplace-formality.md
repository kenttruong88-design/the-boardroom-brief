**Kenya to South Africa: The Suit Comes Off Somewhere Between Nairobi and Cape Town**

🇰🇪 Kenya · 🇿🇦 South Africa

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In November 2023, Kenya's parliament banned the Kaunda suit — a collarless, short-sleeved jacket named for Zambia's first president and a favourite of President William Ruto — from its own debating chamber, reversing a three-year-old ruling that had explicitly permitted it. Around the same time, in Cape Town, a visiting engineer could walk into an Amazon Web Services office and reportedly find a workforce more relaxed than its Seattle counterpart, no tie in sight. Between those two data points sits one of the more revealing culture gaps on this continent: not whether to dress up for work, but what "dressed up" is even supposed to signal.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇪 Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to a full suit in banking, finance, and government roles until you've confirmed otherwise. | Assume "business casual" travels the same meaning across sectors — image consultants note banks enforce far stricter codes than IT firms. |
| Expect written dress guidebooks at some employers to specify neckline count, sock colour, and trouser cut, not just "smart." | Treat a vague verbal dress policy as the norm everywhere; some large employers spell it out in granular detail precisely to avoid disputes. |
| Research whether traditional or national dress is welcomed or restricted at your specific employer before wearing it to a formal meeting. | Assume African traditional attire is automatically acceptable in every formal or government-adjacent setting — Kenya's own parliament banned it in 2023. |
| Expect county and some employer policies to regulate women's hemlines and necklines more strictly than men's attire. | Assume dress rules fall equally on men and women; several reported disputes involve women specifically being pulled up over skirt length or sleeves. |
| Notice that senior executives increasingly favour open collars, rolled sleeves, and groomed facial hair over a full traditional suit-and-tie look. | Assume a loosened executive dress code at the top has filtered down to junior or client-facing roles — it often hasn't yet. |
| Budget for appropriate formal wear before your first week if your sector leans corporate — several young professionals cite cost as the real barrier to compliance. | Assume employers factor clothing costs into entry-level pay; several interns report they don't. |

#### 🇿🇦 South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect the dress culture to vary sharply by city — Cape Town runs far more casual than Johannesburg or Durban. | Pack a Cape Town tech-startup wardrobe for a Johannesburg corporate role, or vice versa; the two cities read formality very differently. |
| Expect jeans, sneakers, and hoodies to be genuinely normal in Cape Town's tech and creative sectors, even client-facing. | Assume the same casualness applies at a Johannesburg bank or law firm, where tailored blazers and "power" dressing remain closer to the norm. |
| Keep one blazer on hand even in a casual office — several Cape Town workers still throw one over a t-shirt for client meetings. | Assume zero formal wear is ever needed just because daily office dress looks relaxed. |
| Expect employers increasingly to offer curated clothing ranges or colour-coordinated collections rather than one rigid uniform. | Assume a uniform policy, where one exists, means identical outfits for everyone; many now favour shared colour palettes with individual fit and cut. |
| Expect comfort and practicality to be taken seriously as a design factor, especially post-pandemic. | Assume "comfort" signals low effort; several employers now treat fit and breathability as part of looking professional, not opposed to it. |
| Dress for Durban's climate with breathable fabrics while still keeping the look reasonably polished. | Assume Durban's more relaxed, linen-and-sandals climate style means no effort is expected at all — workers there reportedly still aim to look put together. |

### Kenya

Kenyan workplace dress sits at an unusually visible intersection of colonial inheritance, religious and cultural identity, and generational pushback, and 2023's Kaunda-suit saga captured all three at once. Parliament's speaker justified banning the garment — along with other African traditional attire — by citing "emerging fashion trends" that seemed to challenge the chamber's existing dress code, a code that still requires male MPs to wear coats, collars, ties, and long trousers, with separate rules governing women's sleeves and skirt length. Critics on social media called the reversal a retreat toward "colonial norms," noting the irony of an African parliament restricting African dress; one UDA member of parliament had reportedly already been expelled from the chamber for wearing a Kaunda suit before the ban made it official policy.

That tension between Western-style formality and local identity plays out well beyond parliament. Equity Bank's internal dress guidebook, as reported in a 2026 feature, runs to specifics most Western employees would find startling: women's necklines limited to six approved styles, skirts barred from rising above the knee, men required to cut dreadlocks and steer clear of "happy socks" patterned with cartoons in favour of long, dark ones. The bank frames this as reflecting "professionalism, attention to detail, and respect for customers," and image consultant Nthenya Macharia argues the specificity is deliberate — vague terms like "short" invite disputes, so spelling it out removes the argument. Younger employees aren't uniformly convinced: one Gen Z intern, Ian Momanyi, pushed back after being questioned over casual clothing, arguing his intellect mattered more than his outfit and that employers should pay enough to make formal dress affordable in the first place, while 30-year-old business developer Trevor Ojiro takes the opposite view, crediting his suit-and-tie habit with being taken more seriously at work. County-level politics add a further, gendered layer: West Pokot's governor banned miniskirts, halter tops, and trousers for female county employees while requiring neckties for men, and a Nyeri assembly member was ejected from a session for wearing a sleeveless dress — incidents HR practitioners and feminists in the same reporting characterised as patriarchal overreach dressed up as professionalism. Hofstede's data, which groups Kenya into a broader "East Africa" cluster rather than scoring it individually, puts power distance in that cluster at 64, well above South Africa's 49 — a gap consistent with dress codes that, in Kenya, are more often handed down from above than negotiated.

### South Africa

South African dress culture resists a single national description because it genuinely isn't one — it fractures by city in a way few countries this guide has covered do so cleanly. A 2025 industry feature put it bluntly: Cape Town runs "comfort first, style second," with jeans, t-shirts, sneakers, and hoodies as standard even in client-facing roles, suit jackets reserved for presentations and sometimes thrown on over a t-shirt rather than a shirt underneath. Johannesburg sits at the other end, where professionals reportedly treat office wear as a personal brand statement — crisp blazers, tailored trousers, "power" heels — while Durban occupies a breathable middle ground of linen shirts and flowy dresses that still aims for polish despite the humidity. One Blind commenter visiting an Amazon Web Services office in Cape Town described the engineering culture there as unusually well-adjusted compared with Seattle, for whatever that's worth as an informal data point on the city's relaxed register more broadly.

The broader trajectory, independent of city, has been toward loosening rather than tightening. An industry analysis citing the shift "over the past few years (and especially after Covid)" describes companies moving away from issuing one rigid, identical uniform toward curated ranges — shared colours and styling cues rather than visual sameness — with named internal product lines like "Corporate Chic" and "Boardroom Brilliant" built explicitly around fit, movement, and all-day comfort rather than formality for its own sake. Staff, per the same reporting, increasingly suggest new pieces to management rather than simply receiving a fixed kit. Hofstede's individually scored South African data shows individualism at 65 against Kenya's clustered 27 — a gap that tracks reasonably well with a workplace culture where personal style choice, city by city and even outfit by outfit, has become something closer to an expected form of self-expression than a risk to be minimised.

### The Reckoning

The real split here isn't formal-versus-casual so much as centralised-versus-fragmented. Kenya's dress rules, where they exist, tend to be explicit, written, and handed down — a bank's neckline count, a county governor's skirt-length ban, a parliamentary chamber's coat-and-tie requirement — which makes them easy to violate accidentally but also, at least, easy to look up. South Africa's rules are looser almost everywhere except in specific sectors, but they vary so much by city and industry that there's no single "South African office dress" to learn in the first place; the real research task is learning your specific city and company, not the country.

Both systems share a quieter throughline: the loosening, where it's happening, is happening from the top down and the young up. Kenyan executives dropping ties for open collars and Gen Z interns questioning why formality should cost money both push in the same direction as Cape Town's casual creative-sector norms — even as more conservative institutions, a county government here, a bank's guidebook there, hold the older line, often enforcing it more strictly against women than men in both countries.

### Conclusion

Pack the suit for Nairobi and the sneakers for Cape Town, but hold both loosely — the honest lesson from a Kaunda-suit ban in one capital and a hoodie-friendly tech office in another is that neither country's dress code is really about clothing. It's about who gets to decide what "professional" means, and in both places, right now, that fight is still very much in progress.
