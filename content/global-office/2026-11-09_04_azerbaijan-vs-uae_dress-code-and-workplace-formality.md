# Suit Up, Cover Up: What Baku and Dubai Actually Expect You to Wear to the Office

🇦🇿 Azerbaijan · 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Baku, the dress code is a suit you did not know you were required to own until your first client meeting. In Dubai, it is a suit, plus a working theory of exactly how many centimeters of shoulder, knee, and ambition you are permitted to show in a given building. Both cities will judge you instantly on your tailoring, and both will deny, with a straight face, that they care.

## Do's and Don'ts: Azerbaijan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a dark, well-pressed suit to any first meeting, regardless of industry | Assume "business casual" means anything less than a blazer |
| Keep accessories minimal and leather goods visibly good | Wear visibly worn or budget shoes — Bakuvians notice footwear first |
| Dress up, not down, for government and banking offices | Show up in sneakers to a ministry building, even "fashion" ones |
| Let seniority dress more conservatively and copy it | Out-dress your boss on your first week |
| Keep women's office wear tailored but modest at the neckline | Treat Baku's cosmopolitan nightlife wardrobe as office-transferable |

## Do's and Don'ts: United Arab Emirates

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to conservative business formal — full suit for client-facing roles | Wear sleeveless tops or above-the-knee skirts in government buildings |
| Carry a light jacket or abaya-adjacent cover-up for mixed settings | Assume beach-adjacent Dubai means beach-adjacent office wardrobe |
| Ask HR for the written dress policy — most companies have one | Guess the policy from Instagram influencers who do not have your job |
| Dress more formally in Abu Dhabi than you would in freewheeling Dubai media offices | Treat free-zone startup casualness as the UAE-wide default |
| Keep collars and hemlines higher during Ramadan, even in casual industries | Wear overtly branded, logo-heavy clothing to formal negotiations |

## Azerbaijan: Suits as Social Contract

Azerbaijan's office culture inherited its formality from two directions at once — Soviet-era bureaucratic conservatism and a post-independence business elite eager to look, quite literally, like it belonged at the table with European and Gulf investors. The result, as expats on Expat.com's Baku forum describe it, is a city where a blazer is not optional professional theater but closer to a basic entry fee for being taken seriously (Expat.com, "Baku: A City of Extremes for the Expat"). Government ministries, banks, and the state oil sector — still the gravitational center of the Azerbaijani economy — dress with a formality that would read as old-fashioned in Berlin and entirely normal in Baku.

The ILO's Labour Force Survey data on Azerbaijan point to an economy still heavily weighted toward public-sector and large-formal-enterprise employment, the exact kind of structure that enforces, rather than negotiates, dress norms (International Labour Organization, Labour Force Survey in Azerbaijan). The European Training Foundation's research on Azerbaijan's labor market similarly notes a workforce concentrated in traditionally structured institutions where informal dress simply has not had room to take root the way it has in, say, Western tech scenes (European Training Foundation, Platform Work in Azerbaijan). Hofstede Insights scores Azerbaijan high on power distance relative to most of Western Europe, and nowhere is that more visible than in a junior employee's reluctance to dress more casually — or more expensively — than their manager.

The practical consequence is that Baku's formality is largely non-negotiable rather than aesthetic. A foreign manager who shows up in chinos to signal approachability is, more often than not, read as either unprepared or subtly disrespectful of the meeting's importance, not as relaxed. Local colleagues tend to read sartorial effort as a proxy for how seriously you take the relationship itself — a client meeting, a ministry visit, even an internal review with a department head above your own, all call for the same baseline: dark suit, polished shoes, minimal jewelry. Where the rules loosen is almost entirely generational and sector-specific — a handful of Baku's newer IT and startup offices, often staffed by returnees from European universities, have begun experimenting with smart-casual norms, but even there, client-facing staff revert to full formality the moment an external meeting is on the calendar. The safest long-term strategy for an incoming expat is to dress for the most formal version of any given day, since downgrading later costs nothing and upgrading on short notice reliably does.

## United Arab Emirates: Formality, Negotiated by Neighborhood

The UAE's dress code operates less like a single national rule and more like a set of overlapping jurisdictions. Abu Dhabi's government-adjacent formality sits at one end; a Dubai Internet City startup's "smart casual, mostly casual" sits at the other; and in between is a genuinely confusing middle layer that Khaleej Times has reported can affect hiring decisions outright — recruiters surveyed described turning away otherwise-qualified candidates over clothing choices perceived as unprofessional or culturally tone-deaf (Khaleej Times, "Can how you dress affect your employability in UAE?"). Mercer's 2025 UAE workforce outlook, largely about pay, nonetheless captures the broader point: the UAE's labor market is unusually segmented by sector and free-zone status, and dress norms track that segmentation closely (Mercer, UAE salary and workforce outlook survey, 2025).

What trips up newcomers most consistently, according to a FlyerTalk thread on Dubai workplace dress codes and a Fishbowl post from a New York transplant asking what to pack for a Dubai finance job, is the gap between the UAE's famously liberal retail and leisure image and its considerably more conservative office reality — modesty requirements that tighten noticeably around government buildings, during Ramadan, and in any client meeting involving a national partner (FlyerTalk Forums, "Dubai workplace dress code"; Fishbowl, women in the Middle East thread). The Talesmag Real Post Report for Abu Dhabi, written by a diplomatic spouse, puts it more bluntly: pack for Washington, not for the beach you can see from your balcony (Talesmag Real Post Report, Abu Dhabi).

This segmentation is the UAE's defining feature, and it is almost entirely absent in Azerbaijan's more uniform formality. A free-zone media company in Dubai Internet City may run on jeans and branded hoodies five days a week; a bank two highway exits away may require a full suit for anyone client-facing; and a government-linked entity in Abu Dhabi may expect a standard closer to a foreign ministry than either. Expat.com's UAE forum repeatedly surfaces the same confusion from newcomers: nobody hands you a written rulebook, and the unwritten one changes depending on whether you are in a free zone or mainland company, in Dubai or Abu Dhabi, and whether Ramadan has started (Expat.com, "Business Etiquette in the United Arab Emirates"). Female professionals report navigating an especially narrow band — tailored and modern enough to read as competent in an international firm, modest enough in neckline and hemline to avoid friction in government-adjacent settings — a balance Khaleej Times' reporting suggests many recruiters quietly factor into hiring decisions even when no written policy says so.

## The Reckoning

Put side by side, Azerbaijan and the UAE are both far more formal than their international reputations suggest, but for opposite reasons. Baku dresses up because status is scarce and must be visibly claimed; Dubai dresses up because status is everywhere and must be visibly matched. Azerbaijan's formality is inherited and institutional — you dress like the ministry because the ministry has always dressed that way. The UAE's formality is negotiated and situational — you dress like whichever version of the country you happen to be standing in that day, government tower or startup loft.

The practical difference for a relocating professional is predictability. Get a blazer right in Baku and it will serve you in nearly every formal setting the country has. Get a blazer right in Dubai and you will still need a second wardrobe for Abu Dhabi, a third mental model for Ramadan, and a fourth for whichever free zone your employer happens to be registered in.

## Conclusion

Neither Baku nor Dubai is the freewheeling, visa-run, anything-goes destination that travel content sometimes implies, and both will cost a newcomer real professional capital for guessing wrong early. The honest advice for either city is the same: buy fewer, better pieces, dress one notch more formal than you think you need to for the first month, and watch what the most senior person in the room is wearing — not the most online one. If the brochure promised you linen and ease, the brochure was describing the beach, not the building where you will actually be paid.
