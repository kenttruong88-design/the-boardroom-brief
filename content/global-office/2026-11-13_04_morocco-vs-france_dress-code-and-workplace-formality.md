# Morocco Wears the Suit, France Wears the Opinion

🇲🇦 Morocco · 🇫🇷 France

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

According to a 2015 OpinionWay survey for Monster, 65 percent of French employees follow no dress code at all, which sounds like liberty until you notice that every Frenchman at the meeting somehow arrived wearing the same unspoken one. In Morocco the code is more often spoken, by the employer, the client or the recruiter, and the question is rarely what you wish to say with your clothes but how much of it the customer will tolerate.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress for the client: suit and tie for men and a tailored suit or dress for women are the safe default in customer-facing and senior roles | Assume that a relaxed pace means a relaxed wardrobe |
| Keep shoulders and knees covered, particularly outside the big coastal cities | Wear very short skirts or sleeveless tops to a first meeting |
| Iron everything; wrinkled clothes look improper however much they cost | Arrive in sportswear, which most firms ban |
| Keep jewellery modest | Wear large, flashy earrings or beads to the office |
| Greet colleagues properly and allow time for it | Treat the morning greeting as a delay to the actual work |
| If you wear a hijab, research the employer before applying | Take for granted that every international employer will welcome it |

### France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use "vous" with superiors and clients until a colleague moves to "tu" | Switch to "tu" first, particularly with a manager |
| Ask about the dress code at the interview; it varies enormously by sector and by company | Assume that Paris means black suits everywhere, or that startups mean hoodies everywhere |
| Say bonjour and shake hands (or brush cheeks) every morning, and say goodbye in the evening | Walk to your desk without greeting anyone |
| Dress neatly and understatedly; fit counts for more than labels | Turn up in beach sandals; flip-flops are not illegal but they will be noticed |
| Keep small talk to culture, food and the weather | Open with personal questions or complaints about the firm |
| Remove the tie in a heatwave if others have | Be the only person in shorts |

## Morocco

Moroccan business dress is formal and visibly European in inspiration. Cyborlink's business-etiquette guide describes suits and ties for men and professional suits or dresses for women, with shoulders and knees covered, and notes that expectations are less strict than in the Gulf. Expat.com adds the practical detail: dress depends on the profession and the amount of customer contact, many distribution firms require a blouse in the company colours, sportswear is banned almost everywhere, and large flashy jewellery is out of place.

The formality is hierarchical as well as sartorial. In the Hofstede Insights data Morocco scores 70 for power distance against France's 68, a gap too small to settle an argument, and 46 for individualism against France's 74. Morocco World News describes a society in which everybody has a place and the ideal boss is a benevolent autocrat. The practical effect is that appearance signals your place in that order, and a junior who dresses above it will be noticed more than one who dresses carefully within it. In practice the formality is less rigid than it sounds. Expat.com notes that colleagues spend time on greetings and breaks and that the pace can be relaxed, and respondents to the Rabat Post Report described ordinary business attire, with one calling it less formal than Washington and the embassy setting as the exception.

Then there is the awkward question of the headscarf. In April 2025, according to Bladi.net, a recruiter advertised a Casablanca international school's vacancy for a non-veiled occupational nurse, prompting a wave of criticism on LinkedIn, with users arguing that the Labor Code bans discrimination on the basis of appearance, religious belief or clothing. Expat.com's work-culture guide separately notes that many recruiters regard the hijab as an obstacle to hiring women. The World Bank, in a country framework reported by Le360 in July 2026, puts female labour force participation at only 19 percent and lists social norms among the causes. Dress, in Morocco, is occasionally a labour-market policy.

## France

France runs on an unwritten law. Connexion France reports that the labour code does not forbid flip-flops, shorts or tank tops and bars employers from imposing clothing restrictions, yet a former head of the HR directors' association told the paper that in a hot office people remove their ties but rarely adopt shorts, because they moderate their dress to avoid rejection by colleagues. The rule is enforced by glances. The OpinionWay survey found that just 8 percent wear formal clothes such as a suit and tie, 14 percent wear a uniform or something very formal, and 13 percent follow an informal code. Companies with 250 to 999 employees were far more likely to have a code (49 percent) than firms under ten staff (22 percent).

The code also has a gender and a geography. In the same survey 41 percent of men followed a dress code against 28 percent of women, and 32 percent of workers in the Paris region did, a share well below the north-east of the country, at 42 percent. Employment lawyers quoted by Connexion add that flip-flops could be a problem for receptionists or anyone dealing with the public, even if dismissal over clothing would be exceptional. A 2026 YouGov survey for Galaxus found that only 37 percent of French male workers wear closed-toe shoes, the lowest share in the comparison, and that around 20 percent of French respondents said spaghetti-strap tops had no place at work, against roughly half elsewhere. The French are, in other words, relaxed about clothing and exacting about everything else.

The everything else is language. According to The Local, the standard advice is to use "vous" with superiors and clients, and a 2019 study by sociologist Alex Alber found that about seven in ten men use "tu" with their immediate boss, while women very rarely do. Expat.com's Paris guide advises switching only when a colleague has done so first. Uncertainty avoidance in the same data, 86 for France against 68 for Morocco, is as good an explanation as any: written rules are few, but everyone knows what they are. Expat.com's Paris guide adds that classic companies expect women in simple, elegant clothing and men in a dark suit or at least a shirt, while startups can be far more casual, and it recommends asking about the dress code at the interview, where it sometimes turns out to be in the internal rules after all.

## The Reckoning

Morocco regulates the surface and relaxes the substance: wear the suit, then take your time over the greeting. France does the opposite. It tolerates a wide range of clothing and then punishes a misjudged pronoun. For a newcomer, the Moroccan test is visible and answerable in advance, since the dress code can be seen on the first day. The French test is a pop quiz on a syllabus nobody will show you.

The sensible adaptation is a modest one. In Morocco, dress slightly above the room and learn what your role signals. In France, dress to the room, defer on the vous, and watch what your colleagues do for two weeks before you commit to a look. Neither country rewards the outfit that tries hardest, and both reward noticing. The practical gap is that Morocco will tell you what is expected if you ask the recruiter, whereas France expects you to have worked it out, preferably without appearing to have tried. Budget for a second round of shopping either way, and do not buy the full wardrobe before your first week.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com (Morocco forum) — a poster answering a newcomer explained that dress norms depend on the city: in Rabat, Tangier and Casablanca women wear what they like, while smaller villages are more conservative. Her own advice was to stay aware of local attitudes and avoid very short skirts, since revealing clothing draws unwanted attention anywhere.*

> *Tales Magazine Post Report (Rabat) — respondents from the diplomatic community described office dress from business casual to suits, with one calling it less formal than Washington, another finding most local staff in suits, and the embassy as formal. One noted that guests in Moroccan homes can feel underdressed.*

> *Tales Magazine Post Report (Paris) — respondents between 2008 and 2020 charted the slow loosening of the capital: suits and ties in the early years, then smart dress with some jeans by 2015, and one 2018 respondent still calling it quite formal. By 2020 the answer was business smart, depending on the office.*

> *The Local France — workers interviewed about tu and vous gave answers that no handbook could unify: a waitress uses tu with everyone including her supervisors, a caterer never used it with her boss, and a trader still says vous to his after ten years. Some supervisors, it appeared, simply care more about hierarchy than others.*

> *Global Voices (comments on hijab in Morocco) — commenters argued over whether employers may judge a woman by her head covering. One said refusing to hire a veiled woman reflects the employer's prejudice rather than the customer's displeasure, another that how a scarf is worn matters more than the scarf, and a third that freedom must include the choice to remove it.*

## Conclusion

If your wardrobe is formal and you want to be understood quickly, Morocco's code is legible and slightly old-fashioned: dress to the client, cover the shoulders and knees, and read the employer carefully if you wear a hijab. If you prefer rules to be implicit and you can read a room, France offers latitude on clothing and none on the vous. Check the company, not the country, because a Casablanca call centre and a Rabat ministry have little in common, and neither has a Paris startup.

The honest summary is that both countries judge you on how you look and on how you greet, and each is entirely certain it does neither.
