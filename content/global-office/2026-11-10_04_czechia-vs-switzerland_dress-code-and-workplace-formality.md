# Czechia and Switzerland Agree the Shirt Must Be Ironed, and Differ on Almost Everything Else

🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In a YouGov survey of 2,616 office workers across five countries, commissioned by the retailer Galaxus and published in July 2026, 72 percent of Swiss respondents called flip-flops unsuitable for the office, and only 27 percent of Swiss men said they wear shorts to work. In Prague, by contrast, a good many colleagues keep a pair of indoor slippers under the desk and think nothing of it. Both countries expect the shirt to be ironed. Beyond that, the Czech office and the Swiss office are governed by different theories of what formality is for, and the newcomer who mistakes one for the other will be corrected, usually without anyone saying a word.

## Czechia: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep clothes ironed and neat, and follow your colleagues when unsure | Wear bright colours or anything flashy to a formal meeting |
| Use formal address and professional titles until invited to drop them | Move to first names with older colleagues on your own initiative |
| Keep your suit jacket on until the most senior Czech present removes his | Be the first person in the room to take your jacket off |
| Check whether your employer has a written code; large organisations often do | Assume a small or mid-sized firm has none, or that it has no unwritten one |
| Leave the desk for lunch, which often starts around 11:30 | Schedule anything important for a Friday afternoon in July or August |
| Treat desk slippers as unremarkable | Remark on them, or on a beer ordered with lunch |

## Switzerland: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet each colleague every morning in the local language, with their name | Walk in silently or offer a casual wave |
| Arrive on time or early | Blame your watch or the tram |
| Default to long trousers and closed shoes | Wear shorts or flip-flops, even in a heatwave |
| Match your dress to your sector: banks formal, IT and creative firms freer | Wear anything mismatched or flashy, in any sector |
| Take lunch away from your desk, preferably with colleagues | Eat at your keyboard |
| Acknowledge rank and reporting lines, even where first names are used | Read a flat structure as permission to be casual |

## Czechia: Formal Address, Informal Footwear

Czech formality is mostly vertical. Hofstede Insights scores the Czech Republic at 57 on power distance and 74 on uncertainty avoidance, against 34 and 58 for Switzerland, which is a statistical way of saying that rank matters and that people prefer a rule to an improvisation. The business-etiquette site businessculture.org reports that large Czech organisations often set a dress code while small and medium-sized firms usually do not, so business casual is the default unless an important meeting is on the calendar. Its advice for that meeting is conservative: a dark suit, or a jacket and tie, no bright colours, and the jacket stays on until the most senior Czech present takes his off.

The detail lives in the small print. According to Expats.cz, many Czechs keep slippers under the desk, older colleagues often retain the formal form of address for years, professional titles matter, and lunch begins around 11:30 with a beer regarded as unremarkable. The same publication notes a legal workplace temperature limit of 27 degrees Celsius, at which employees must be sent home, which turns the summer dress question into a matter of statute rather than taste. Radio Prague once reported on a Ministry of Environment code that required long-sleeved shirts, no sandals and no clothing declaring political or environmental allegiance. A fashion editor called it a courteous way of setting expectations without pointing fingers, while a former minister called it somewhat nonsensical in a building with poor ventilation. Both views appeared in the same report, which is about as close to consensus as the subject gets.

Context matters. Expat.com's guide to Prague describes corporate roles as structured and startups and tech firms as more relaxed, with hierarchies less rigid than newcomers expect. A recruiter quoted in a 2011 Expats.cz feature said the trend ran toward more formal office dress, with neatness the thing Czechs are fussiest about, and advised following your superiors when in doubt. It is a rule that works in any country, which may be why it is offered in all of them. Formality in Czech offices also governs conversation. Expats.cz reports that colleagues tend to keep work and home separate, that weekend small talk is less common than in Anglo-American offices, and that friendships often form after hours rather than at the coffee machine. Expat.com adds that Fridays are usually more relaxed and teams often go out together afterwards. The Czech office, in other words, is formal at the desk and sociable at the pub, which is a defensible division of labour.

## Switzerland: Egalitarian Rules, Strictly Applied

Switzerland presents an apparent paradox. Its power distance score from Hofstede Insights, 34, describes a flat culture, and yet the YouGov survey for Galaxus, as reported by SWI swissinfo.ch, found 60 percent of Swiss men preferring long trousers and 56 percent preferring closed shoes. Some 73 percent of Swiss respondents called crop tops inappropriate when dealing with customers, against 37 percent in Italy, and 61 percent said the same of sweatpants. Swissinfo concluded that Switzerland has the strictest dress code of the five countries surveyed. Among women the differences were small: trousers and T-shirts were the norm everywhere, even in extreme heat. Only about a third of women chose skirts or dresses. Germany, the control case, posted the most casual figures in the survey: 38 percent of German men wearing shorts and 69 percent wearing T-shirts to work.

The resolution is that Swiss formality is horizontal. Everyone is held to the same standard, and it is enforced by the group rather than by rank. The Local describes a code that runs from formal to smart casual depending on role and client contact, in which mismatched or flashy clothing is out of place, colleagues are greeted each morning in the local language with their name, and lunch is taken away from the desk, since many businesses close from noon until about 13:30. Expat.com's Zurich guide adds the sectoral split familiar everywhere: banks formal, creative and IT staff freer, with respect for hierarchy observed regardless.

None of this is driven by overwork. Eurostat figures reported by IamExpat put the average Swiss employee at 36 hours a week in 2024, against a European average of 37, so the polish is not a symptom of exhaustion. It is simply the house style. The Local adds that punctuality is treated almost as a moral category, with illness, a doctor's appointment or a death in the family accepted as excuses and, in one reported case, a herd of goats on a village road. The Local also notes that Swiss casual dress is more restrained than casual elsewhere, Italy being its example, which is worth remembering before assuming that a smart-casual instruction permits anything resembling a weekend.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, the two countries are formal in different dimensions. Czechia asks who you are: your title, your seniority, whether your jacket may come off before the boss removes his. Switzerland asks whether you have made an effort: the same neat, unflashy, closed-shoe standard applies to the intern and the director. The Czech office will forgive slippers and a lunchtime beer, because those are private comforts inside a public hierarchy. The Swiss office will forgive neither, because there is no private zone in a workplace governed by consensus.

For the newcomer, the cost of error differs too. In Prague, misjudging the dress code is likely to earn a raised eyebrow from someone senior and a gentle suggestion to follow colleagues. In Zurich, an offence against the code is less likely to be mentioned and more likely to be remembered. Neither country is hostile to outsiders, but both reward the person who watches for a week before choosing a wardrobe, and the Swiss version of that week lasts somewhat longer.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — paraphrased experience: A Swiss software engineer who has worked at several small companies says engineers are in high demand and well paid, the standard week is 42 hours with five weeks of holiday, and flexible or remote arrangements were rare before the pandemic.*

> *Fishbowl — paraphrased experience: In an anonymous thread, a traveller asked what to wear to offices in Germany, Switzerland and France. Replies agreed that European offices dress more formally than American ones, recommended dark blue or grey suits often worn without a tie, leather lace-ups and muted colours, and advised against hoodies, polos and baggy fits. The consensus was that the aim is to show you are making an effort to fit in.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report — paraphrased experience: A woman who lived in Zurich in 2008, writing in 2016, says business attire is expected and that smart casual with a little extra is acceptable at the office. Dress outside work is less strict, she adds, but still more polished than in much of Europe, a blend of German rule-following and French or Italian flair.*

> *Expats.cz — paraphrased experience: An American researcher in an all-Czech office in Prague, interviewed in 2011, says colleagues were reserved at first and attributes the difference to being the lone foreigner rather than to anything else. She found business casual interpreted far more loosely than she expected. A former English instructor was told by her director that her style was too independent, and was not asked to teach that course again.*

> *ExpatExchange — paraphrased experience: One expat living in Czechia says being too casual in dress is considered rude, as is being late to an appointment, and recommends avoiding English unless it is necessary. A second recalls mixing up yes and no in conversation with a taxi driver.*

## Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two on the basis of what to wear, you will need roughly the same wardrobe: dark trousers, a pressed shirt, closed shoes and a jacket in reserve. The difference lies in behaviour. In Czechia, learn the titles, keep the formal form of address until invited otherwise, and expect the dress rules to bend for a firm's size and sector. In Switzerland, learn the greetings, be early, and eat lunch like a person with nowhere better to be.

The harder adjustment is conceptual. In one country the dress code is a negotiation conducted through rank and context; in the other it is a settled matter held in place by everyone at once. Pack the same suit for both, and bring slippers for only one. In the other, the shoes are expected to stay on and the opinions to stay in.
