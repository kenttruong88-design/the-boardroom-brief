# Lifetime Employment Meets 131 Unicorns: Choosing a Risk Profile in Japan and India

**🇯🇵 Japan · 🇮🇳 India**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Japan now counts over 10,000 startups pulling in roughly $6 billion a year in venture capital, according to Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry — and yet shūshin koyō, lifetime employment at a single large firm, remains a career ideal that shapes hiring even at companies that no longer formally promise it. India, meanwhile, has minted 131 unicorns as of mid-2026 and recognized more than 164,000 startups nationally, per DPIIT, in an ecosystem where jumping from a five-person startup to a mid-sized company and back again barely raises an eyebrow. Both countries are building serious startup sectors. Only one of them has actually let go of the corporate ideal that startups were supposed to be an alternative to.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clarify explicitly whether a "startup" job is structured like a Silicon Valley startup or a smaller version of a traditional Japanese firm | Assume startup culture automatically means flat hierarchy or fast decisions — many Japanese startups retain consensus-based process |
| Expect a large corporate job to still carry real social status and family approval | Underestimate how much "which company" still matters more than "what role" in Japan |
| Treat job-hopping into a startup as increasingly normal for workers under 35 | Expect the same tolerance for job-hopping if you're older or at a more traditional firm |
| Research whether a startup offers "daini shinsotsu" (second new graduate) reentry paths back into corporate tracks | Burn the bridge back to corporate life assuming the startup will definitely work out |
| Ask directly about equity, since understanding of stock options is still maturing in Japan's market | Assume Japanese startup compensation packages mirror US or European equity norms |

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect real ownership and responsibility fast, even at junior levels, in a startup | Expect the same structure, escalation paths, or defined role boundaries as an MNC |
| Weigh MNC brand-name value on your resume against the faster skill compression of startup life | Dismiss "Lala company" (family-run Indian business) roles without checking specific culture — they vary enormously |
| Negotiate pay directly; startup compensation is far more variable and negotiable than MNC bands | Assume a lower startup salary means lower total upside — factor in equity and title inflation |
| Ask about founder turnover and funding runway before joining a growth-stage startup | Join purely for the "unicorn" name without checking burn rate and recent funding history |
| Treat frequent job changes as a normal, even expected, career strategy | Assume MNC tenure signals more competence than startup hopping — recruiters increasingly don't |

### Japan: Risk Inside a Stable Frame

Japan's startup boom is real, but it's happening inside a labor market still culturally organized around long tenure. GLOBIS Insights notes that large firms like Toyota and Mitsubishi still practice a version of lifetime employment for core staff, while a growing tier of startups and tech companies hire purely for skill and don't weight tenure the same way — meaning Japan increasingly has two labor markets running in parallel rather than one that's fully transformed. A 2025 Recruit survey found "personal growth" is now the top priority for new employees at 35.1%, evidence that younger workers are pulling toward the startup model even as older institutions hold their shape.

The safety valve that makes this work is "daini shinsotsu" — a formal-enough concept that a graduate who takes a first job and later wants a do-over gets treated less harshly by the market than in most countries. That's a distinctly Japanese hedge: it lets ambitious young workers take a startup risk without fully burning their bridge back to the corporate track that's still, for many families, the more prestigious outcome.

### India: Risk as the Default Setting

India's version of this choice looks almost inverted. With 55,200 new startups recognized in FY 2025-26 alone — the largest single-year figure since Startup India launched a decade ago — the startup path isn't a countercultural alternative to corporate life, it's simply one of two mainstream options, and Bengaluru alone hosts 53 unicorns. Quora threads comparing MNC and startup life describe a real trade: MNCs offer brand-name stability and structured pay bands, but "you will be a star among billions of galaxies" — individual contribution gets diluted at scale. Startups offer faster responsibility and skill compression, with the tradeoff being lower initial pay and far less structure.

What distinguishes India's dynamic from Japan's is the direction of prestige. There's no strong cultural penalty for having jumped between three startups by 30; if anything, that trajectory can read as more impressive to a hiring manager than five static years at a single MNC, since it signals adaptability in a market moving as fast as India's is.

### The Reckoning

The real difference isn't risk tolerance — both countries clearly have workers willing to take startup risk. It's what a career "return trip" costs. In Japan, the system has quietly built an off-ramp back to corporate stability, so the risk of trying a startup is genuinely lower than it looks, because failure doesn't permanently damage your standing. In India, there's no real "off-ramp" concept because there's increasingly no single track to return to — the whole market already assumes fluid movement, so there's nothing to be rehabilitated back into.

Ironically, this means a foreign worker moving to Japan for a startup role should actually feel more free to take the leap than instinct suggests, given the safety net; and a foreign worker moving to India for an MNC role should recognize that the "safe" choice isn't actually safer in a market where startup experience is increasingly viewed as the more dynamic credential.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who moved from a five-person Indian startup to a large MNC described feeling like "a star among billions of galaxies" — better pay, far less visibility, and a strange sense of having become replaceable in a way the startup never made them feel.*

> *r/cscareerquestions — A software engineer who left a stable Japanese corporate track for a Tokyo startup said the hardest adjustment wasn't the work itself but explaining the decision to family, since a known company name still carried more social weight at dinner conversations than the startup's funding round ever would.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — One poster comparing offers wrote candidly about weighing "high pay, no-name startup vs. elite" corporate brand, noting that in Japan specifically, the corporate name mattered more for future job prospects than the startup's actual growth trajectory.*

> *Internations Tokyo — A European product manager who joined a Japanese startup noted that despite the "startup" label, decision-making still moved through layers of quiet consensus-building rather than the fast, founder-led calls she'd expected, and adjusting her pace took months.*

> *r/developersIndia — A developer who'd worked at both a Bengaluru unicorn and a multinational bank advised that the real question to ask before joining any Indian startup isn't the mission statement, it's the runway — funding history and burn rate tell you more about your next two years than anything in the offer letter.*

---

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between a startup and a corporate job in either country, the honest calculation isn't "which is more exciting," it's "what happens to my career if this specific bet doesn't pay off." In Japan, the system still offers a real path back to stability, even if that path is culturally underappreciated by outsiders. In India, there's less of a "back" to go to, because the market itself has already normalized moving forward through risk rather than around it.

What I'd tell a friend deciding between the two: take the Japanese startup risk more seriously than your gut says to, because the downside is softer than it looks — and take the Indian MNC "safe choice" less seriously than your gut says to, because in a market this fluid, safety is relative, and it isn't free.
