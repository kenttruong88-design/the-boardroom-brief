# On Time in Kigali, Endless in Paris: A Practical Guide to Meetings in Rwanda and France

🇷🇼 Rwanda · 🇫🇷 France

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

According to Gallup's 2026 State of the Global Workplace, a mere 8 percent of French employees describe themselves as engaged at work, well under the European average of 12 percent and roughly a third of the global figure. One theory, offered here without peer review, is that a large share of French professional life is spent in meetings that produce another meeting. In Rwanda, meanwhile, the World Bank has watched the country climb from 149th to 38th in the world for ease of doing business over the past decade or so, a rise built partly on the unglamorous discipline of punctuality and follow-through. Anyone weighing a posting between Kigali and Paris should understand that booking the meeting is the easy part. What happens once everyone is, eventually, in the room is where the two postings part ways entirely.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇷🇼 Rwanda

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive at or before the stated time, even if the room fills slowly | Assume a soft "we shall see" is agreement — it usually isn't |
| Let the most senior person present open the discussion | Raise a disagreement loudly or in front of the group |
| Budget time at the start for greetings and rapport-building | Rush past the handshake to get to the agenda |
| Route concerns through your direct supervisor, not around them | Skip the chain of command to reach someone more senior |
| Follow up verbal agreements with a written note | Assume a nod in the room is a signed commitment |

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Come ready to defend your position with logic and context | Say "let's just get to the point" — it reads as an insult |
| Expect the meeting to run late and overtime; pad your calendar | Book anything across the 12:00–14:00 lunch window |
| Address senior colleagues formally (Madame/Monsieur plus surname) at first | Switch to first names before you're invited to |
| Treat vigorous debate as normal, even a sign of respect | Mistake a sharp counterargument for a personal attack |
| Confirm decisions in writing afterward, since the room rarely closes one | Expect the meeting itself to end in a clear decision |

### Rwanda

Rwanda's meeting culture reads, at first, like a corrective to every complaint ever lodged about African bureaucracy: punctuality is close to a civic virtue. Government sessions in particular start on schedule, and foreign arrivals quickly learn that being late marks them as the outlier, not the local hosts (World Bank, Doing Business rankings). That discipline sits alongside an unambiguous workplace hierarchy. Age, title, and tenure determine who speaks first and who defers, and an objection to a superior's plan is typically raised quietly and later, not across the table (Cultural Atlas; Cross Culture Academy). Hofstede Insights maintains no official profile for Rwanda, but the country's own workplace research points the same direction as its meeting etiquette: high deference to authority, low tolerance for public confrontation.

The government's Vision 2050 strategy, which aims to more than double tertiary enrollment and build a "globally competitive workforce," is already reshaping how younger, internationally trained employees behave in the room, even as the older hierarchical instinct persists (Rwanda Ministry of Finance and Economic Planning). The practical effect for a new arrival is a communication style that is genuinely high-context: a great deal is conveyed through tone, pause, and what's left unsaid, and a flat "no" is rare enough that its absence should never be mistaken for agreement. Meetings also open with unhurried greetings and small talk before the agenda begins, which can feel like padding to someone used to a five-minute stand-up and is, in fact, the actual work of the meeting.

### France

French meetings run on a different fuel: argument. Business culture here treats vigorous debate not as a breakdown of process but as the process itself, an inheritance of an education system built around dissertation and dialectic (Commisceo Global; Cultural Atlas). Decisions, by contrast, tend to be made afterward and elsewhere, often by whoever sits highest in a notably centralized hierarchy — a structure Hofstede's framework captures in France's comparatively high Power Distance score, among the highest in Western Europe. The result is a paradox familiar to every expatriate manager: meetings that are intellectually combative and procedurally hierarchical at once, everyone arguing passionately about a plan that one person will ultimately approve alone.

Time behaves differently too. The average French working week runs to about 35.5 hours, among the shortest in the OECD, and full-time employees are typically entitled to five weeks of paid leave plus compensatory rest days, pushing many toward six or more weeks off a year (Eurostat, Actual and usual hours of work). None of that generosity extends to meeting length: they routinely start late, run over, and treat "let's get to the point" as close to a diplomatic incident, since skipping the context is read as skipping the thinking (TheLocal.fr). Whether the shortened workweek and the engagement number Gallup reports are connected is a question for another department, but the correlation is hard to ignore.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the irony sharpens. Rwanda, a country still building out its institutions, runs meetings with more procedural discipline — punctuality, clear deference, deliberate pacing — than France, a country with centuries of administrative tradition behind it. France treats the meeting as intellectual theatre: a place to perform rigor rather than produce a decision, which then gets made quietly afterward by whoever outranks the room. Rwanda's hierarchy is enforced through restraint; France's is enforced through who gets the last, most eloquent word.

The counterintuitive lesson for a transplant is that "hierarchical" doesn't mean "efficient," and "egalitarian-sounding debate" doesn't mean "flat." A Rwandan meeting that looks slow and deferential from the outside is often moving toward a decision faster than a French one that looks lively and combative. Anyone who assumes vigorous argument signals dysfunction, or that quiet agreement signals consensus, will misread both rooms badly in the first month.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A commenter who worked alongside French colleagues recalled being quietly corrected for switching to first names too soon, learning that formal titles often persist for months after two people have started grabbing lunch together.*

> *TheLocal.fr — A journalist covering French office life cited a study finding French managers spend roughly sixteen years of their working lives in meetings, and reported that saying "let's get to the point" is treated as an outright insult, since skipping the background is read as skipping the thinking.*

> *Hacker News — An engineer who had worked at both a traditional French company and a Silicon-Valley-style outfit in Paris described the former as ticket-driven, with product managers pre-deciding the details and little expectation that developers would question the plan, versus far more autonomy in the latter.*

> *Expat.com — A Nigerian-Turkish professional who relocated to Kigali for a university faculty role said the city's "quiet energy" took real adjustment after a fast-paced background, and that the slower, more passive rhythm of meetings and daily life was frustrating before it became something she came to value.*

> *Theo Is Back (expat blog) — A foreign professional working in Kigali noted that official start times were more a loose target than a rule, with colleagues drifting in anywhere from eight to ten in the morning, even as everyone still treated the stated meeting time as firm.*

### Conclusion

The practical advice is almost boringly simple: in Rwanda, show up on time, let seniority lead, and listen for what isn't being said; in France, come prepared to argue your case in full, expect the clock to be treated as a suggestion, and don't mistake a heated debate for a fight. Both cultures reward patience, just in opposite directions — Rwanda asks for patience with restraint, France asks for patience with noise. Anyone who can do both without flinching will do well in either capital; anyone who can't should probably stick to email.
