# Silence Is a Sentence, and Other Things Nobody Tells You Before You Move for Work

## 🇨🇱 Chile · 🇫🇮 Finland

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Santiago, a business meeting can open with fifteen minutes of unhurried conversation about your flight, your family, and possibly the national football team before anyone mentions the agenda. In Helsinki, the agenda has already been circulated, read, and silently agreed upon before the meeting starts, and the meeting itself may end four minutes early because nobody has anything left to say. Both cultures consider this normal. Both cultures are, from the other's vantage point, faintly alarming. The distance between the two isn't measured in kilometers, of which there are roughly 12,700 separating Santiago from Helsinki, but in what each culture is trying to accomplish with a room full of people: in one, trust; in the other, closure.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇱 Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget real time for small talk before business begins | Pull out the agenda in the first five minutes |
| Address senior colleagues by title and surname until told otherwise | Assume a first-name email means informality applies everywhere |
| Expect the real decision-maker to be the most senior person in the room | Push a mid-level manager for a final yes on the spot |
| Build the relationship before the deal, not during it | Treat a lunch invitation as optional networking fluff |
| Show up on time yourself, even if others arrive later | Read a late start as a personal slight |
| Follow up in person or by phone after a big meeting | Assume a warm meeting equals a signed agreement |

#### 🇫🇮 Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive at the exact start time, not "around" it | Show up five minutes late and call it culturally acceptable |
| Send a clear agenda in advance and stick to it | Wing it and expect the room to fill dead air for you |
| Let silence sit; it usually means someone is thinking | Interpret a pause as disagreement or discomfort |
| Say what you mean plainly, without much cushioning | Bury a "no" in three paragraphs of polite qualifiers |
| Speak up if you actually have something to add | Perform enthusiasm in meetings to seem engaged |
| Expect decisions to stick once made, without re-litigating | Keep raising a topic after the group has moved on |

### Chile: The Room Is Warming Up Before It Gets to Work

Chilean business culture scores 63 on Hofstede Insights' power distance index, meaning hierarchy is not just tolerated but expected: decisions flow from the top, and knowing who actually holds authority in the room matters more than what's printed on the agenda (Hofstede Insights). That hierarchy coexists with a strong relational streak — Hofstede's individualism score for Chile sits at a low 23, reflecting a culture organized around extended networks of trust rather than individual initiative. In practice, that means meetings routinely open with unhurried personal conversation, and rushing straight to business can read as brusque or even faintly suspicious, according to expat business guides covering Chilean etiquette (Expat Arrivals, JustLanded).

None of this is inefficiency for its own sake. Chileans also work some of the longest hours in the OECD, clocking in at roughly 1,953 hours annually, well above the OECD average, even as productivity growth has lagged (OECD Employment Database). The math is almost poetic: a culture that spends real time building trust in the room is also a culture that spends a great deal of time in the room, period. A 2023 labor reform trimmed the legal work week from 45 to fewer hours, suggesting even Chilean policymakers noticed the imbalance. One Quora thread on Chilean etiquette describes the culture as simultaneously formal and role-oriented, with a certain reserve that outsiders can misread as coldness — a reminder that "relationship-driven" doesn't mean "instantly familiar." The two truths, warmth and formality, coexist without much apparent friction, which is precisely what visitors tend to find disorienting.

### Finland: The Meeting Is a Formality for the Decision You Already Made

Finland's Hofstede power distance score is 33, less than half Chile's, and its individualism score is 63 — the inverted mirror image (Hofstede Insights). Structurally, that produces workplaces where junior staff address the CEO by first name and where, per accounts of Finnish organizational life circulated on Hacker News, hierarchies are flat enough that authority has to be exercised through argument rather than title. Meetings start on the minute and often end early, because the point of the meeting is to confirm a decision that has effectively already been reached through written agendas and quiet pre-work, not to perform deliberation live.

The most disorienting feature for newcomers is silence, which Finns treat as a natural conversational unit rather than a gap to be filled. Silence signals that someone is thinking, not that something has gone wrong, and interrupting it is the actual faux pas (Expat Focus). This tracks with Finland's high scores on generalized social trust: World Values Survey data puts trust in others among Finns at roughly 68 percent, near the top globally, a level of institutional and interpersonal confidence that makes elaborate meeting choreography unnecessary — you don't need forty minutes of rapport-building with someone you already structurally trust (World Values Survey). A Quora thread asking whether Finns are quiet drew consistent agreement: yes, deliberately, and no, it does not mean disengagement.

### The Reckoning

Here is the counterintuitive part: the culture that spends the most time in meetings, Chile, is not necessarily the one where the most gets decided in the room. Real authority in Chilean meetings often sits with a senior figure who may not even be present, and the visible warmth of the discussion can mask the fact that the actual call gets made afterward, upstairs. Finland inverts this entirely — the meeting looks sparse and brief precisely because the deciding has already happened asynchronously, and the room exists mainly to ratify it. An American or Brazilian manager parachuted into Santiago might mistake conviviality for consensus; parachuted into Helsinki, they might mistake brevity for a rubber stamp, when in fact it's the most binding format either culture offers.

The other inversion is emotional rather than structural. Chile's high score on Hofstede's uncertainty avoidance dimension (86) suggests a culture that actually prefers more structure and predictability than its freewheeling meeting style implies — the informality is social, not procedural. Finland, despite its reputation for cold efficiency, scores closer to the middle on the same measure, tolerating more ambiguity in outcomes than in etiquette. The two cultures have simply chosen different places to be strict: Chile is strict about respect and sequence, Finland about time and truth-telling.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A commenter describing Chilean etiquette noted that despite the culture's reputation for warmth, day-to-day interactions can feel formal and reserved until real personal trust is established, which surprised newcomers expecting instant friendliness.*

> *Expat Arrivals — A guide to doing business in Chile warned that opening straight into agenda items, without acknowledging family or weekend plans first, can come across as cold and even damage a working relationship before it starts.*

> *Hacker News — A thread on flat organizational structures included a poster who'd worked in a Finnish-style flat hierarchy describing the adjustment: no one was going to assign you visibility, so staying quiet in meetings out of politeness just meant getting overlooked.*

> *Expat Focus — An advice piece for new arrivals in Finland flagged that Finnish silence and lack of small talk are frequently misread by foreigners as rudeness or disinterest, when locals experience it as ordinary respect for other people's time and space.*

> *Quora — Responding to a question on whether Finns are quiet, several answers converged on the same advice: don't fill every pause, since a Finnish colleague's silence in a meeting is far more likely to mean "processing" than "problem."*

### Conclusion

The practical upshot for anyone weighing a move: in Chile, invest in the unstructured minutes before the meeting starts, because that is where the actual negotiating happens, and expect that the person nodding along may not be the one who signs off. In Finland, invest in the written agenda and your own preparation, because the meeting will not wait for you to catch up, and a well-timed silence will do more relationship work than a well-timed joke. Neither approach is more "professional" than the other; they're optimized for different scarce resources — trust in one case, time in the other. The honest advice is to stop bringing your home country's meeting instincts as a carry-on, because in both places, they will be the first thing confiscated at customs.
