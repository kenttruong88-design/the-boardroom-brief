# China Gives You 5 Vacation Days by Law. The Real Question Is Whether Anyone Will Let You Take Them.

**🇺🇸 USA · 🇨🇳 China**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Neither country is going to hand you a European-style vacation allowance, but they get to their stinginess through opposite mechanisms. America simply mandates nothing — zero statutory paid vacation days, a policy vacuum filled entirely by employer discretion. China mandates something specific and small — five days a year for anyone with under ten years of total work experience — and then layers a work culture on top of it that makes using even those five days feel like a career risk.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate PTO explicitly when you take a job offer — it's a line item, not a given | Assume "unlimited PTO" means more time off than a fixed policy; often it means less |
| Check your state — some, like California, treat accrued PTO as earned wages that must be paid out | Assume vacation policy is federally standardized; it varies enormously by state and employer |
| Use your vacation days; unused time is rarely rewarded and often just disappears | Expect a public holiday calendar as generous as most of the rest of the world — the US federal list is short |
| Plan around company blackout periods for high-demand seasons | Book vacation without checking your team's crunch schedule first |
| Treat visible boundary-setting around time off as a skill to build, not a given right | Wait for permission that isn't coming — no law is going to grant you rest here |

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Know your legal entitlement by tenure: 5 days under 10 years, 10 days at 10–20, 15 days after 20 | Assume your five statutory days are guaranteed to be honored without pushback in practice |
| Plan around China's public holidays, especially Spring Festival and National Day — they shape the whole year | Book personal travel that conflicts with mandatory "make-up workdays" surrounding long holidays |
| Read the room on 996-adjacent norms (9am–9pm, six days) at more traditional or startup-heavy employers | Assume every Chinese employer runs on 996 — increasingly regulated and pushed back on, but still common |
| Ask directly and early about how leave requests are actually handled at your specific employer | Assume law enforcement matches the letter of the law — enforcement of the 8-hour cap is famously weak |
| Watch how senior colleagues use their leave, and calibrate accordingly | Take unused leave as evidence nobody wants it — it's more often evidence nobody feels safe taking it |

---

America's approach is a pure market outcome: with zero federally mandated leave, the US is the only OECD country with that distinction, and whatever vacation exists is a benefit companies compete on, not a floor the law provides. Actual practice varies wildly — many white-collar employers offer 10 to 15 days as a norm, some tech firms advertise "unlimited" policies that in practice see employees take less time off than a fixed allotment would guarantee, since there's no clear number to hit. The result is a system where your vacation days are a function of your specific employer's generosity and your own willingness to actually use what you're offered.

China's approach is a legal minimum layered onto a workplace culture that frequently overrides it in practice. The statutory structure — 5/10/15 days scaled by total years of work experience, plus around 11 to 15 public holidays depending on the year — looks reasonably generous next to America's zero. But China also carries the well-documented "996" culture (9am to 9pm, six days a week), which the Supreme People's Court has explicitly ruled illegal in recent years amid mounting worker pushback, even as enforcement of the underlying 8-hour-day cap remains weak. The gap between the legal entitlement and the lived reality is the whole story here: workers technically have leave rights that plenty of offices make socially costly to exercise.

The reckoning is that both systems produce workers who under-use whatever time off they nominally have, for almost opposite reasons. Americans skip vacation because no law tells them not to, and hustle culture fills the vacuum the law leaves behind. Chinese workers, particularly in tech and startup environments, skip vacation because taking legally guaranteed leave can read as a lack of commitment in an environment where visible hours still function as the main proxy for dedication — even as courts and regulators increasingly say that shouldn't be true. An American landing in a Chinese office will be startled that "legal" and "normal" are such different things. A Chinese professional landing in an American one will be startled that there's no floor at all to be startled by.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A respondent explaining China's public holiday calendar noted the ironic contrast that China has more public holidays than the US on paper, but that many of those holidays come attached to mandatory "make-up" workdays on the surrounding weekends, so the net time off is smaller than the headline number suggests.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a question about Chinese work hours described a standard day running from 8:30am to 6pm with a lunch break, and noted that the schedule looked reasonable in writing but that staying past 6pm was the unspoken norm at many private employers regardless of what the offer letter said.*

> *teamblind.com — A thread reacting to the Chinese government's crackdown on 996 culture noted that legal rulings against excessive overtime hadn't yet changed daily practice at the poster's own company, where staying late remained the clearest signal of dedication to leadership.*

> *Quora — A commenter pushed back on the assumption that Chinese workers have no labor protections, pointing out the legal 8-hour cap and mandated overtime pay exist on paper, but conceded that weak enforcement meant individual employees often had little practical recourse against a manager who ignored them.*

> *teamblind.com — An American tech worker comparing PTO policies noted that "unlimited" vacation at their company functioned, in practice, as roughly two weeks — nobody explicitly capped it, but nobody took more either, because there was no number to point to as justification.*

### Conclusion

If you're negotiating time off in either country, negotiate the actual number, not the policy's name — "unlimited" in America and "legally entitled" in China both mean less than they sound like on paper. The real predictor of how much rest you'll get isn't the statute or the stated policy; it's what your specific manager and specific team actually do when someone takes it. Ask that question in the interview, not after you've accepted the offer.
