# Israel Calls It a Meeting When Everyone Interrupts at Once; America Calls It Alignment and Sends a Calendar Invite

🇮🇱 Israel · 🇺🇸 United States

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Israeli startups raised about $10.6 billion in 2024, according to the Israel Innovation Authority, making the country the fifth-largest startup funding hub in the world, even though only about 500 new startups were founded that year against more than 1,000 a decade earlier. More money, fewer companies: the Startup Nation is evidently maturing into the Corporate Nation, which is a transition the United States completed some time ago and has been refining with slide decks since.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Israel

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say what you think, and expect others to say what they think of it | Wait for a polite invitation to disagree |
| Treat blunt feedback as information and not as hostility | Read directness as a verdict on your character |
| Expect to be handed a problem and left alone with it | Wait for a manual, a mentor or a formal onboarding plan |
| Improvise, and find the right person to solve the problem | Insist that the process be followed to the letter |
| Interrupt back, once you understand the rhythm | Sit silently and assume your silence will be read as agreement |
| Earn loyalty through results and honesty | Expect titles or pedigree to do the work for you |

### In the United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan, document and secure alignment before you move | Improvise in front of the stakeholders |
| Wait for a pause before speaking | Interrupt as a sign of engagement |
| Build your network in person and by introduction | Count on cold emails to open doors |
| Soften critical feedback and frame it constructively | Deliver your verdict in one blunt sentence |
| Expect a formal process for hiring, review and exit | Assume things are settled because everyone nodded |
| Understand that military pedigree counts for little with investors | Open with your unit's reputation |

## Israel

Israel's startup mindset is better documented than its corporate one, in part because the country has built much of its economy on the former. The Innovation Authority's State of High Tech 2025 report says high-tech accounted for about NIS 317 billion, or 17 percent of GDP, in 2024, that high-tech exports reached $78 billion and 57 percent of all exports in the first half of 2025, and that 403,000 people, or 11.5 percent of the workforce, worked in the sector. Funding reportedly rose to nearly $16 billion in 2025, according to the Algemeiner's coverage of the Authority's report, though that figure comes from a headline only.

What this means in the office is a culture that prefers improvisation to procedure. A sociologist quoted in Ynet News, Gad Yair, observed that Israelis tend to look for the right person to solve a problem while Americans follow the manual; the article itself is candid that this rests on anecdote and generalization and not on data. A Jweekly piece about Israeli startups in Silicon Valley described Israelis interrupting and debating at once and regarding that as efficient, and noted that elite military-unit pedigree counts for little with American investors.

Hofstede's older scores, reproduced by Expert Program Management, give Israel a power distance of about 13 and an uncertainty avoidance of about 81. This is the profile of a workplace where nobody is quite the boss and everybody is slightly anxious, and anyone who has attended an Israeli planning meeting will recognize it. The positive reading is that a junior employee can challenge a director. The negative reading is that the director will challenge back.

## United States

American corporate culture is built around process, and the data suggest that process is winning the competition for talent. A study by Boston University's Technology Policy Research Initiative, using job ads from more than 140,000 US startups founded since 2010, found that when large firms take a bigger share of local hiring, startups must pay up to 10 percent more for key roles and their expected growth falls by more than a third. The effect is strongest in management, STEM and sales. The corporate employer, in other words, does not need to be better than the startup. It needs only to be larger.

The Gallup figure for onboarding suggests the corporate machine is less polished than its reputation: only 12 percent of US employees say their company does a good job of onboarding. The process exists. It is not always good. The Hofstede figures, again from the older tables, put the United States at a power distance of about 40, individualism of 91 and uncertainty avoidance of 46, which is the profile of a culture that likes autonomy but expects it to be exercised within the plan.

The new arrival should expect meetings that are shorter on interruption and longer on preparation, feedback that arrives wrapped in encouragement, and an unspoken rule that the way to be taken seriously is to have documented your intention before acting on it. An Israeli engineer will find this slow. An American manager will find the Israeli engineer unpredictable. Both will be right.

A few details explain why each side finds the other puzzling. The Blind thread suggests that the Israeli habit of firing quickly is paired with a loyalty that follows once trust has been earned, which is a different bargain from the American one, in which procedure protects the employee and the relationship develops more slowly. The Jweekly article makes a similar point from the other direction: Israeli founders in the Valley found that Silicon Valley business runs on in-person networking and warm introductions, not on cold emails or on the credentials earned in an elite military unit. Both observations point to the same conclusion, which is that each system has a currency of trust, and that the currency does not convert at par. An Israeli in California must learn to earn introductions, and an American in Tel Aviv must learn to earn arguments.

## The Reckoning

The contrast is that Israel optimizes for speed of argument and the United States optimizes for clarity of agreement. In Israel the discussion is the decision-making process, and it is expected to be loud. In the United States the discussion is a preliminary to the decision, which is recorded and circulated. The Israeli approach produces fast pivots and occasional chaos. The American approach produces consistent execution and occasional paralysis.

For a mover, the useful distinction is who bears the cost of misunderstanding. In Israel the cost falls on the person who is too polite to speak. In the United States it falls on the person who is too direct to be heard. The Israeli moving to America must learn to wait their turn, and the American moving to Israel must learn to take theirs.

There is also a difference in what each country expects a newcomer to bring. In Israel the expectation is initiative: the sector's scale, with 403,000 people working in high-tech, means that a capable engineer is expected to find the problem and propose a fix without waiting for a brief. In the United States the expectation is fit: the large-employer share of hiring that the Boston University study describes implies a labour market in which a candidate is assessed against an established role and a ladder. A person who prefers to define the job should lean towards Israel, and a person who prefers the job to be defined should lean towards America, with the caveat that few jobs in either country fit the stereotype for long.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — A US employee at an Israeli-run cybersecurity startup wrote that American staff got disciplined or fired over small matters that coaching could have fixed, while Israeli colleagues seemed to face fewer consequences, and that one-on-one feedback was blunt. A reply from someone with roughly ten years working with Israelis said they are blunt, partly because of military life, will fire anyone, and show strong loyalty once trust is earned. Others said directness reads as rude to less direct cultures and advised adapting.*

> *Jweekly — Israelis working in Silicon Valley described interrupting and debating immediately, which they considered efficient, while Americans seemed quieter and more deliberate. The piece noted that elite military pedigree counts for little with American investors and that Valley business runs on in-person networking and not cold emails.*

> *Ynet News — A sociologist said American workplaces are procedure-heavy and plan-driven, with people expected to follow the manual, while Israelis improvise and look for the right person to solve a problem. The article acknowledges that this is based on anecdote and generalization.*

## Conclusion

If you prefer to work in a place where anyone can challenge anyone, and you do not mind that they will, choose Israel. If you prefer to work in a place where a plan is respected and a pause is permitted, choose the United States. Neither is easier, and the difference between them is mostly in the first six weeks.

Ask at the interview how decisions are made, how feedback is given, and whether there is a written plan for your first month. In Israel the honest answer may be that there is not. In the United States it may be that there is, and that it is eleven pages. Either way, you will have learned something about your new employer, which is more than most careers pages manage.
