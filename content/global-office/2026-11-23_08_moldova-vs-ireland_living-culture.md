# Moldova Counts Its Lei, Ireland Counts Its Crèche Invoices

🇲🇩 Moldova · 🇮🇪 Ireland
*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Moldova and Ireland offer a rare chance to compare a cheap country that is hard to earn in with an expensive one that is hard to afford. The National Bureau of Statistics puts average gross monthly earnings in Moldova at 16,355 lei in the last quarter of 2025. Meanwhile, the Irish Times, citing Eurostat, reports that Ireland is about 42 per cent more expensive than the EU average. One country pays modestly and charges modestly. The other pays well and charges for the air in the nursery. Neither has solved the problem of making a salary stretch to cover a family, but each has chosen a different place to leave the gap.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Moldova

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Secure a job before arriving, as local advice stresses that finding one is hard | Arrive on savings and expect the local labour market to oblige |
| Budget for the mandatory health premium if you are a non-employed resident or digital nomad | Assume voluntary insurance covers you once your residence permit is issued |
| Buy comprehensive private cover with medical evacuation | Rely on the public system alone for anything complicated |
| Apply to international schools well in advance if you have children | Expect an English-language school place to appear in August |
| Bring a supply of essential medication | Assume every drug will be on the pharmacy shelf |

### Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a childcare waiting list as early as you can, preferably before conceiving | Treat the childcare subsidy as a cap on your bill |
| Check which scheme your creche belongs to before signing | Assume fees stay fixed once the contract starts |
| Price private health insurance against GP fees, which are paid per visit | Assume the public system is free at the point of use for everyone |
| Search for rent outside Dublin and the commuter belt | Plan a budget on the national average rent |
| Compare supermarket prices rather than relying on one chain | Assume food prices resemble those of continental Europe |

## Moldova: Cheap Rent, Cheaper Wages

Moldova is inexpensive by European standards, but the arithmetic requires care. Expat Arrivals lists a one-bedroom flat in central Chisinau at about 11,500 lei a month in February 2026, falling to about 8,000 lei outside the centre, with basic utilities around 3,500 lei and a three-course meal for two at roughly 700 lei. Set against the national average gross wage of 16,355 lei, up 8.9 per cent on a year earlier, a central one-bedroom rental absorbs about 70 per cent of a typical gross salary. Expats working for embassies, NGOs or multinationals are reported to earn relatively high salaries, which is the polite way of saying that the lucky ones are paid in a different economy.

The welfare picture is thin and partly dated. The US Social Security Administration's 2018 summary says medical insurance is funded by contributions of 4.5 per cent of gross earnings from both employee and employer, with a birth grant of 5,600 lei and a child care allowance with a minimum of 540 lei a month. Those figures are from 2018 and should be checked before anyone plans a pregnancy around them. For foreign residents who are not employed, the Moldovan migration authority says a fixed mandatory health insurance premium applies, with a discounted 2,527 lei during the first three months of 2026 and 12,636 lei for digital nomads on temporary residence. Moldpres reports the same 12,636 lei as the full annual premium. That is about 77 per cent of one month's average gross wage, which counts as generous only if the alternative is none.

Childcare, schooling and private medicine are where the expat pays Western prices for Eastern European supply. Expat Arrivals notes that public schools teach in Romanian and that international schools are expensive and short of places. A member quoted on ExpatExchange describes a strained public health system and better private clinics, which many Moldovans cannot afford. A cheap country, then, until the family needs something. The ExpatExchange saver and the healthcare member are not contradicting each other. Both are right, and the difference is whether the person doing the saving has yet been to a clinic.

There is a further wrinkle in how the system treats those who pay and those who merely reside. A resident employee contributes through payroll, while a non-employed foreigner pays a fixed sum, so two people in the same queue can have arrived at it by very different routes. The Moldovan migration authority adds that voluntary insurance is available only while the residence permit is pending, after which the fixed premium becomes compulsory. In short, the choice is offered once, briefly, and then withdrawn.

## Ireland: Expensive, Documented and Quarterly

Ireland has the opposite problem. It pays well and charges more. The Irish Times reports that restaurants and hotels were 28 per cent above the EU average, energy 18 per cent and transport 15 per cent, while the Central Statistics Office found food 12 per cent above the EU27 average in 2024. Daft.ie data cited by the same paper put average Dublin rent between 2,294 and 2,704 euro a month in the third quarter of 2024, against a national average of 1,955. The country is therefore economical only in the sense that it is generous with its invoices.

Childcare is the signature item. TheJournal.ie, citing OECD figures from 2023, reports Ireland as joint fourth in Europe for net childcare costs for a couple on average earnings with two children, with parents spending almost twice the OECD average share of income. Pobal data puts average early years fees at about 190 euro a week nationally, and the paper says Dublin runs well above 200 a week. The state offers a National Childcare Scheme subsidy of 2.14 euro an hour up to 45 hours a week, and 15 free hours a week for children aged 2 to 5 under the ECCE scheme. The same article cites 77,000 children on waiting lists, with the caveat that some sit on several.

Health coverage is also a matter of arithmetic. The Irish Times says premiums have risen repeatedly, leaving more than two million people paying hundreds of euro a year more. A Mumsnet poster described private GP visits costing roughly 60 to 90 euro, with an employer-provided insurance plan covering much but not all of one injury. The welfare state exists, but it prefers to be consulted rather than relied upon.

Another parent who wrote to TheJournal.ie said fees at their Galway provider rose by 90 euro a week per child after it left the Core Funding scheme, taking a family with two children to 1,855 euro a month. The lesson for newcomers is that the subsidy is real, the fee is also real, and the contract determines which of the two wins. Check who runs the creche before checking who runs the country.

## The Reckoning

Head to head, Moldova is the cheaper place to be broke and Ireland is the more expensive place to be comfortable. A Moldovan average wage of 16,355 lei leaves little room once a central flat, utilities and a private clinic enter the budget. An Irish salary leaves more room, but a family with a creche place, a Dublin rent and a health plan can find the room reassigned within a month. Rent alone makes the point: the Moldovan central one-bedroom takes about 70 per cent of an average gross wage, while Dublin rent of more than 2,000 euro takes a share that depends entirely on whether the salary is Irish or imaginary. The honest comparison is not price level against price level. It is the share of income left after the unavoidable items.

On institutions, Ireland offers more state help, subsidies and legal certainty, and Moldova offers lower baselines and the need to arrange almost everything privately. Both leave the newcomer to pay for what the brochure called an affordable lifestyle. The difference is that in Ireland the bill is large and predictable, while in Moldova it is smaller and sometimes arrives as a surprise medical or schooling problem the system was never designed to solve. Hofstede Insights scores Ireland low on power distance (28) and high on individualism (70), which fits a country where each household is expected to settle its own invoices, but it offers no Moldova figure that could be verified, so that side of the comparison stays qualitative.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *ExpatExchange, Chisinau — One expat said Chisinau is a good place to save compared with other European cities, thanks to cheaper rent, utilities and food, though salaries are lower, which is the catch.*

> *ExpatExchange, Chisinau — A member described public healthcare as stretched by funding and staffing problems and by doctors emigrating, with private clinics offering better care that many locals cannot afford.*

> *Expat.com forum — A poster from around 2013 said Chisinau flats could be had from roughly 150 euro and that transport and food were very cheap, a reminder that forum prices age worse than milk.*

> *Mumsnet — A Dublin resident said GPs are private and cost around 60 to 90 euro a visit, and that insurance covered an MRI quickly but left physio and prescriptions partly unpaid.*

> *TheJournal.ie readers — A parent who returned from London said a UK nursery had cost about 1,700 euro a month, while in Ireland they now pay nearly 9,800 euro a year for one child, which has put a planned third child in doubt.*

## Conclusion

If your priority is a lower cost base and you have a remote salary or an employer who pays in euro, Moldova can work, provided you budget for private health cover, schooling and the mandatory premium if it applies to you. If you want a functioning labour market, a developed state and proper salaries, Ireland delivers all three and then sends the bill for housing, childcare and insurance.

Before accepting either offer, calculate disposable income after rent, health, childcare and schooling, not before. Ask what the employer covers, check whether your visa category triggers a fixed premium, and read the creche contract to the last clause. Both countries will welcome you warmly, and both will find a way to make your budget feel like a rumour.
