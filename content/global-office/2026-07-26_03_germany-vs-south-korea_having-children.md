# One Country Pays You to Take a Year Off. The Other Can't Get You to Take Two Weeks.

**🇩🇪 Germany · 🇰🇷 South Korea**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Germany will pay a new parent up to 65 percent of their salary, capped at €1,800 a month, for up to fourteen months, and roughly 92 percent of German mothers take between ten and twelve months of it. South Korea's total fertility rate had fallen to 0.72 by 2023, the lowest recorded anywhere, despite a government that has spent two decades steadily expanding parental leave on paper. The gap between these two facts is not really about money. It is about what happens to your career the moment you use the benefit you are legally entitled to.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for Elterngeld early — the paperwork window is real and unforgiving | Assume your employer will handle the application for you |
| Plan which months each parent will take — the system rewards splitting leave | Take all fourteen months as one parent alone if you want the full bonus period |
| Expect to keep your job — Elternzeit comes with strong legal protection | Worry that taking leave will visibly derail your career trajectory |
| Register for a Kita (daycare) spot as early as pregnancy — spots are scarce | Wait until the baby is born to start looking for childcare |
| Budget around the income cap — €1,800/month may be a real pay cut for high earners | Assume Elterngeld replaces your full salary — it doesn't, above the cap |

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your actual employer's culture, not just the legal entitlement | Assume a legal right to leave means it's socially safe to take it |
| Talk to colleagues who've had children about how leave was actually handled | Assume your job or team will be unchanged after a long absence |
| Look into the expanded paternity leave (now 20 days) if you're a father | Assume paternity leave uptake reflects real workplace acceptance — it's still low |
| Line up childcare well before you need it — private options fill up fast | Expect an easy return to your pre-leave role and responsibilities |
| Have an honest conversation with your manager about expectations early | Assume the "M-shaped" employment dip everyone talks about won't apply to you |

### Germany

German parental policy was deliberately redesigned in 2007, modeled partly on Scandinavian systems, with the explicit goal of making career and childcare compatible rather than treating them as competing claims on the same person. Elterngeld pays up to two-thirds of prior net income, and Elternzeit — the legal leave itself, distinct from the payment — protects a parent's job for up to three years per child, most of it unpaid beyond the initial paid window. The system also nudges fathers directly: couples that split the leave unlock two bonus "partner months," and current uptake shows roughly 80 percent of men now take at least some leave, even if their average stretch remains far shorter than women's.

The friction in Germany isn't legal, it's logistical: waiting lists for a Kita spot can stretch long enough that parents are advised, without much irony, to register during pregnancy. The system has removed most of the career risk and replaced it with a bureaucratic scavenger hunt — the leave is safe to take, but building the childcare infrastructure around it back into daily life is its own project.

### South Korea

On paper, South Korea's parental leave has expanded aggressively — paternity leave doubled to 20 days as of February 2025, and monthly benefits for the first three months after birth rose to 2.5 million won. The problem, as the OECD has documented at length, is that policy generosity and actual uptake are different variables entirely. South Korea remains the only OECD country where women's labor force participation follows an M-shaped curve across age — a sharp dip during the exact years most people are having and raising children, followed by a partial, incomplete recovery.

The mechanism is cultural as much as structural: rigid workplace hours and a persistent expectation that mothers carry the bulk of domestic and childcare labor mean that even generous leave goes underused, and even when it is used, returning mothers report having to outwork their prior selves just to prove the absence didn't cost the company anything. More than 40 percent of women who pause their careers for childcare do not return to the workforce within a decade. The result is a country simultaneously expanding the benefit and watching birth rates fall anyway, because the benefit was never really the bottleneck.

### The Reckoning

Germany solved the legal problem and is now fighting a logistics problem; South Korea has been expanding the legal solution for years while the actual obstacle — what taking leave signals about your commitment to your job — remains largely untouched. It's a useful reminder that policy generosity and cultural permission are not the same axis, and a country can lead on one while lagging badly on the other.

The irony cuts both ways for someone relocating. A German parent moving to Seoul may find the legal entitlements looking similar on paper and be blindsided by how differently taking them is read internally. A Korean parent moving to Berlin may find the reverse: fewer social judgments about taking leave, but a genuinely maddening hunt for a daycare spot that the legal system assumed would simply exist.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/germany — An American parent who relocated for a tech role described the Elterngeld application process as more bureaucratically dense than anything in the US system, but said the real shock was colleagues treating his fourteen months of leave as completely unremarkable — nobody asked when he was "really" coming back.*

> *r/korea — A foreign employee at a Seoul firm recounted a male colleague who was legally entitled to paternity leave but took only a fraction of it, explaining privately that using the full allotment would have marked him as uncommitted ahead of an upcoming promotion cycle.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a question on whether South Korean society supports working mothers wrote that the support exists mostly in law and marketing material, and that the real determinant of whether a mother's career survived having children was the specific manager she happened to report to.*

> *Internations Seoul — A European expat mother described being congratulated warmly on her pregnancy by colleagues, and then, months later, quietly reassigned off a high-visibility project without explanation shortly after she returned from leave — something she said no official policy would ever have predicted.*

> *r/expats — A German father who'd previously worked in Korea said the single biggest adjustment moving home was realizing he no longer had to justify taking parental leave to anyone — in Seoul it had required a conversation; in Germany it required a form.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Germany with a family in mind, treat the daycare hunt as a project that starts the day you find out you're expecting, not after — the legal safety net is real, but it doesn't come with a guaranteed place to put your child while you're at work. If you're moving to South Korea, read the legal entitlement as a floor, not a guarantee, and have a direct conversation with your manager about what taking leave will actually mean for your trajectory before you assume the answer is "nothing." The honest version I'd give a friend: Germany will make it easy to leave and hard to find childcare; Korea will make the paperwork simple and the unwritten cost of using it much higher than anyone will tell you upfront.
