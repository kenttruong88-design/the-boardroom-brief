# Home by Five in Amsterdam, Online Until Midnight in Jakarta: A Field Guide to Two Ideas of Enough

🇮🇩 Indonesia · 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The average Dutch worker puts in about 32 hours a week, the lowest figure in the European Union, according to Eurostat numbers cited by IamExpat. Nearly half of Dutch employees work part-time. In Indonesia, the statutory week is 40 hours, but a UGM economist reports that 25.47 percent of workers exceed 49 hours, based on the national labour force survey. One country treats the office as a place one leaves. The other treats it as a place one is seen to be, preferably with a phone nearby. The question for the prospective emigrant is not which approach is more humane. It is which one your nervous system has been trained for.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Indonesia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm overtime in writing; the rules require a written or digital instruction and employee agreement | Assume unrecorded extra hours will ever be paid |
| Learn what your contract counts as basic salary, since the overtime formula depends on it | Expect the rules to apply if you hold a senior role with unrestricted working time |
| Treat the workplace WhatsApp group as part of the job description | Mute it for a weekend without telling anyone |
| Address colleagues with bapak or ibu until invited to stop | Challenge a senior publicly in a meeting |
| Check whether you are hired directly or as a contractor, because protections differ | Accept an outsourced arrangement without asking what overtime terms apply |
| Show up on time for formal meetings, even if others run late | Display visible irritation when a meeting starts late |

### Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep your own log of overtime, since employees are expected to track it | Assume your employer will pay for extra hours automatically |
| Read the collective agreement for overtime, breaks and leave | Rely on the law for overtime rates; it sets none |
| Ask for different hours after six months under the Flexible Working Act | Treat part-time as a career penalty by default |
| Leave on time and say so plainly | Announce late nights as a badge of commitment |
| Take your 20-plus vacation days | Answer messages from the beach |
| Speak up in meetings, since silence may read as disinterest | Wait to be invited before offering a view |

## Indonesia: Overtime, Meticulously Legislated and Variably Observed

Indonesia does not lack rules. According to Gadjian's summary of Law 6/2023 and Government Regulation 35/2021, the regular week is 40 hours, spread over five eight-hour days or six seven-hour days. Overtime is capped at four hours a day and 18 hours a week on working days. Pay is calculated as 1.5 times the hourly rate for the first overtime hour and twice for each hour after, with the hourly rate derived by dividing monthly salary by 173. Rest days and holidays pay two, three and four times the hourly rate on a sliding scale. Employers must also provide at least 1,400 kilocalories of food and drink when overtime passes four hours, and the summary notes this cannot be swapped for cash. It is, in its way, the most thorough overtime snack regulation in the world.

The catch is in the preconditions. Overtime requires written or digital instruction, a list of employees and hours, and the employee's signature. Senior positions with unrestricted working time fall outside the rules. And much of the labour market sits outside the formal system altogether. The UGM economist cited above reports that more than 80 percent of the roughly 18 million jobs created between 2018 and 2024 were informal, and that the average starting wage in his team's study was about IDR 1.6 million a month. Long hours, in this account, are less a managerial preference than an economic necessity, with more than a quarter of workers holding more than one job. The same source reports that about 33.8 percent of the labour force works fewer than 35 hours a week, so the economy contains both too little work and too much, sometimes within a single household. He links the fatigue to relatively low hourly productivity compared with other ASEAN countries, which is a polite way of saying that the extra hours are not buying what they appear to buy.

Culture does the rest. The Jakarta Post's 2025 opinion column describes a hustle culture that glorifies overtime, noting a Jakarta minimum wage of Rp 5.3 million a month and living costs far above it. A first-person Jakarta Post column by an Irish-Australian communications consultant describes receiving hundreds of WhatsApp messages on busy days, arriving at all hours, with read receipts creating the expectation of a quick reply. RVO, the Dutch enterprise agency, adds that Indonesian workplaces show considerable respect for seniority, which makes the boss's message hard to ignore at 9pm.

## Netherlands: The Part-Time Republic

The Dutch version of work-life balance is less a perk than a national settlement. The OECD says nearly 60 percent of Dutch women work part-time, roughly three times the OECD average for women. Statistics Netherlands figures reported by NL Times put about 4.5 million of 9.4 million paid workers in part-time jobs, with men averaging 36 hours a week and women 28. The AOL report on four-day weeks adds that Dutch workers average 32.1 hours against an EU average of 36, and that the FNV union is lobbying to make the four-day week an official recommendation.

The law supports the habit. IamExpat's summary sets maximums of 12 hours per shift and 60 per week, an average cap of 48 hours over sixteen weeks, 11 hours of rest between shifts and 36 hours free each week. After six months in a job, employees at firms with ten or more staff can request different hours, and employers may refuse only for a strong, objective business reason. What the law does not do is set overtime rates. Those come from your contract or collective agreement, and, as IamExpat notes, employees are responsible for tracking their own extra hours. The Dutch have regulated the exit and left the extra hours to negotiation. Sunday is not a regular working day unless the contract says so, and employees are entitled to at least 13 free Sundays a year. The Working Hours Act also does not cover senior and managerial staff earning at least three times the minimum wage, so the executive class is, as elsewhere, exempt from its own country's wisdom.

There is a cost. The same AOL report quotes an OECD economist asking how sustainable the arrangement is, with productivity flat over 15 years and an ageing workforce shrinking the supply of hours. The balance also falls unevenly. A government analysis cited there finds three in four women and one in four men work fewer than 35 hours, and a 2024 study cited by a CBS researcher found that one in three Dutch people think mothers of children aged three or under should work no more than one day a week. The four-day week, in other words, has a gender distribution. A Dutch employee who signed off Slack at 5pm told an American manager, in a Reddit post reported by AOL, that late availability signals inefficiency or understaffing, and says a Dutch HR representative laughed. The anecdote is more cheerful than the economics.

## The Reckoning

Set the two side by side and the difference is not hours alone but what hours mean. In the Netherlands, long days suggest poor planning, and the law, the union and the culture agree on the point. In Indonesia, long days are tied to wages, informality and respect for seniority, and the law, however carefully drafted, is one input among several. The Dutch worker negotiates how little to work. The Indonesian worker, more often, negotiates how to be paid for what is already expected.

For a newcomer, the practical danger runs in opposite directions. A transplant to Amsterdam who performs eagerness by staying late will be read as disorganised, and may find colleagues gently unimpressed. A transplant to Jakarta who logs off promptly and silences the group chat may be read as detached from the team. Neither reading is fair, and both are predictable. Check your contract, find out which rulebook your employer actually follows, and do not assume that either country's headline statute describes your Tuesday.

## Conclusion

If you are choosing on hours alone, the Netherlands wins, and it will tell you so. Its law protects the exit, its culture rewards using it, and the main risk is that nobody will pay you for what you do after it. Indonesia offers a more detailed overtime statute and a harder environment in which to rely on it, particularly if you are a contractor or work in a role the rules exempt.

Whichever you pick, read the contract before the culture guide, ask how overtime is recorded, and decide in advance what you will do with the group chat. In one country the office closes at five. In the other it closes when the last message is answered, which is a different time entirely.
