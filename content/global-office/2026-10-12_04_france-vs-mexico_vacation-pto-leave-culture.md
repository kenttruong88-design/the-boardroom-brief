**France Will Give You Five Weeks Off and a Guilt Trip. Mexico Will Give You Twelve Days and a WhatsApp Message From Your Boss on Day Three.**

**🇫🇷 France · 🇲🇽 Mexico**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

France guarantees its workforce five weeks of paid leave a year, has for decades, and still managed to produce a 2013 survey in which 90% of French respondents said they felt "vacation deprived" — the highest rate of complaint among 24 countries, ahead of nations with a fraction of the time off. Mexico, by contrast, only raised its legal minimum from six days to twelve in 2023, ranks second-worst in the OECD for hours worked, and yet reported one of the lowest vacation-deprivation rates in that same global survey, at 38%. One country has the time and feels robbed of it. The other barely has any and seems almost at peace. This is either a lesson in gratitude, or proof that expectations are doing all the real work.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect your office, or whole town, to functionally close in August | Schedule anything urgent for late July through August and expect a quick answer |
| Plan family holidays around school breaks — colleagues will assume you do | Treat August closures as optional just because you personally don't need the time |
| Track your RTT hours if you work more than 35 hours a week — they're owed to you | Assume RTT applies if your contract is already a standard 35-hour week |
| Expect job titles and hierarchy to matter more socially than in the US or UK | Expect loud individual recognition for a job well done — it isn't the norm here |
| Ask about a sabbatical if you've got multiple years of tenure — they're genuinely common | Assume "sabbatical" means something rare or extraordinary, the way it might elsewhere |

#### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Know your legal minimum: 12 days after year one, rising with tenure, plus a 25% vacation bonus | Assume "vacation days" and "vacation premium pay" are the same line item — they're separate |
| Expect pressure to stay reachable even while formally on leave | Assume approved vacation means your phone goes untouched — budget for messages anyway |
| Take the full block of 12 consecutive days the law entitles you to in one stretch | Let your employer talk you into splitting your block into scattered single days |
| Recognize that burnout is a widely discussed national issue, not a personal failing | Internalize the "long hours equal dedication" stigma as something you have to prove too |
| Expect larger multinationals to offer meaningfully more than the legal floor | Assume every employer, especially smaller or family-run firms, will exceed the minimum |

### France: The Most Vacation, The Least Satisfied

France's paid-leave entitlement is genuinely generous by global standards: five weeks, or 25 working days, regardless of contract type or tenure, plus 11 public holidays, plus RTT — compensatory time off for hours worked beyond the statutory 35-hour week — for anyone whose contract runs longer. Add an office or regional closure in August, a near-sacred tradition that one French tourism professional described simply as being "in our blood," and the formal entitlement routinely stretches further still. None of this is a myth; France really does shut down more completely, more often, than almost anywhere else in the developed world.

And yet the 2013 Expedia "Vacation Deprivation" survey found French respondents the most likely of any nationality surveyed to say they felt short on time off — 90%, against 59% of Americans, who receive barely a third as many days. Expat accounts split on why: one American who spent seven years working in southwest France described a genuinely humane rhythm — reliable coffee breaks, two-hour lunches, widely used sabbaticals, fairness valued over individual achievement — while a French commenter with nearly 15 years in the workforce pushed back hard, saying that at some of her jobs there was "so much work" that finding time for a bathroom break, let alone a proper lunch, was a real struggle. Both accounts can be true at once: a strong legal floor doesn't guarantee a humane day-to-day, and a culture that talks about rest constantly can still, in practice, ration it unevenly by employer, industry, and region.

### Mexico: The Least Vacation, The Most Burnout

Mexico's Federal Labour Law, unchanged since 1970, offered just six vacation days after a worker's first year — the lowest statutory minimum in the entire OECD — until a 2023 reform doubled the starting entitlement to twelve, with two additional days per year of tenure after that, plus a legally mandated 25% vacation premium paid on top of base salary during that time off. It was a genuinely significant legislative win, decades in the making, and it arrived alongside some of the starkest labor statistics in the OECD: Mexican employees log roughly 2,124 hours a year, second only to Colombia, while the World Health Organization has ranked Mexico first in the world for work-related stress since 2017, ahead of China and the United States.

The reform's real-world bite, though, has been blunted by a workplace culture that treats rest as suspect. An HR consultant quoted in Equal Times put it plainly: Mexican workers are accustomed to "exploitation-oriented" conditions, and taking time off still carries a "stigma" linking long hours directly to dedication, according to labor advocate Regina Espinosa Athié. A 2026 report in El Imparcial found that 71% of Mexican workers end up working during their formally scheduled vacation anyway, driven by managerial pressure and understaffing — a gap the reporting frames as effectively invalidating the 2023 reform in practice — while 34% of employees report chronic stress as a direct result. Hofstede Insights scores Mexico at roughly 81 on power distance against France's 68 — both firmly hierarchical, but Mexico's steeper gradient helps explain why an employee asked to check email on their day off by a direct superior finds it much harder to say no.

### The Reckoning

The real story here isn't the day count, it's what each country's time off is actually for. In France, vacation is a civic institution with its own infrastructure — closed shops, empty offices, school calendars built around it — which paradoxically makes falling short of the ideal feel like a personal and national failure worth complaining about in a survey. In Mexico, vacation is a newly expanded legal right that the surrounding culture hasn't caught up to yet, so getting any of it, even imperfectly, can read as a win rather than a baseline. Satisfaction, in other words, tracks the gap between expectation and reality far more than it tracks the actual number of days on the contract.

There's a sharper irony underneath, too. France's problem is distributional — plenty of total leave, unevenly protected depending on your employer and industry, as the dueling expat accounts show. Mexico's problem is systemic — a reform that looks good on paper but runs headlong into a culture where 71% of people work through it anyway. A new arrival moving from a 10-day-vacation country to France will likely feel liberated almost immediately. The same move to Mexico, despite doubling the legal floor in 2023, may deliver less actual relief than the statute implies, right up until you learn to defend your twelve days as fiercely as your employer defends their right to text you on day three.

### Conclusion

If the day count were the whole story, this would be an easy call: take the French job, bank the five weeks, and never look back. But the honest comparison is about enforcement, not entitlement. France hands you a generous legal floor and a culture that mostly, if unevenly, respects it, which makes the disappointed 90% genuinely instructive about how high French expectations have climbed. Mexico hands you a newly doubled legal floor and a culture that, by its own reporting, still can't fully get out of its own way enough to let people use it. Ask less "how many days do I get" and more "will anyone actually leave me alone during them." That question matters more than the number on the offer letter, in either country.
