**Your New Boss in Shanghai Is Your Parent. Your New Boss in Manila Is Your Favorite Uncle.**

**🇨🇳 China · 🇵🇭 Philippines**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Both cultures will tell you, in the first week, that hierarchy matters. Neither will tell you, in the first week, what that actually costs you: in one country it costs you the right to disagree out loud, and in the other it costs you the right to be criticized out loud. By month three you'll know which trade you prefer. By month one, you'll just be confused about why nobody is saying what they mean.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Soften disagreement with a manager — "I see your point, but may I suggest an alternative" lands; "I disagree" does not | Push back in the exact language you'd use at home; directness reads as a challenge to authority, not candor |
| Spend your first weeks building relationships and listening before proposing changes | Announce on day one that you're here to show the team how things are done elsewhere |
| Treat team dinners and informal socializing as part of the job, not an optional extra | Decline every invitation to a work banquet; guanxi is built there, not in meetings |
| Give criticism privately, and consider the "praise, improve, encourage" sandwich | Correct someone in front of colleagues, even gently — it costs them face, publicly |
| Let a thoughtful silence in a meeting run its course before filling it | Assume silence means disengagement or disagreement; it's often just consideration |
| If you manage locals, explain decisions clearly in writing so no one has to guess what's final | Leave a decision ambiguous, assuming the team will "figure it out together" the way a flatter culture might |

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Let your team call you "sir" or "ma'am" if that's the norm, but tell them your first name is fine too | Insist immediately on first names; it can read as rejecting a gesture of respect, not as humility |
| Raise concerns in private one-on-ones — many employees won't surface a problem in a group setting | Expect dissent or bad news to come up unprompted in a team meeting |
| Deliver correction privately and constructively; praise publicly | Call out a mistake in front of the team — "hiya" (shame) makes public correction land much harder than intended |
| Show personal concern for your team's wellbeing, not just their output — *malasakit* is expected of a good manager | Treat check-ins as purely task-and-deadline conversations with no space for how someone's actually doing |
| Explain the "why" behind an assignment, not just the task | Assume task lists alone will generate buy-in; context matters more than instructions |
| Recognize loyalty and long service explicitly; it's taken seriously as a form of obligation (*utang na loob*) | Treat tenure as interchangeable with a new hire's enthusiasm — it reads as dismissive of real investment |

Confucian-rooted hierarchy in Chinese workplaces isn't merely decorative; it's structural. Decisions tend to move top-down, and a mid-level manager publicly challenging a senior leader can unsettle a room in a way Western visitors often fail to register. Tang Jun, a former Microsoft China CEO, once distilled the power structure for Knowledge@Wharton in blunt terms: "One person has centralized power, no one else does." That doesn't mean Chinese leadership is uniformly cold or top-down in style — Mary Fontaine of the Hay Group has compared the prevailing leadership approach to parenting, "affiliative and coercive," prioritizing people over tasks in a way some Western coaching-style managers initially misread as softness.

What a transferee notices fastest, though, is what doesn't get said. Silence in a meeting is frequently read by newcomers as disengagement; multiple expat-focused guides describe it instead as a sign of careful consideration, with the advice to let it breathe before jumping in with an open-ended question. A Shanghai-based American design manager, posting under the handle DesignerInShanghai on the design forum Core77, described the adjustment candidly: a "yes" in a Chinese professional context can function as a polite refusal, so he learned to reframe questions to give colleagues "a safety net" to admit a method won't work without anyone losing face — a workaround, not a fix, for a communication gap that never fully closes.

The Philippines runs its hierarchy through something closer to kinship than command structure. Respect markers are explicit and persistent — a junior employee calling a manager "sir" or "ma'am" is less a formality than a baseline, one guides describe as "normal and encouraged in the Philippines, a hierarchical society." But the deference runs alongside a genuine expectation of warmth flowing the other direction: management literature on Filipino leadership repeatedly centers on *malasakit*, a word usually translated as genuine concern for others, describing managers who enforce deadlines while also checking on an employee's family or personal hardship. Pair that with *hiya* — a strong aversion to public shame — and you get a workplace where the correction happens, but almost never where anyone else can see it.

### The Reckoning

The surface read is that China is hierarchical and the Philippines is warm, but that's too flattering to both. China's hierarchy is about authority and the chain of command; its warmth lives in the banquets and the guanxi built slowly over shared meals, which is a separate track from who gets to make the call. The Philippines' hierarchy is almost entirely about dignity management — who gets to witness your mistake — while actual decision-making can be more collaborative than it looks, provided you ask in private.

The genuinely counterintuitive part: both cultures consider direct, in-the-room criticism a serious breach, for almost opposite reasons. In China, it threatens the authority structure itself. In the Philippines, it threatens the person's social standing far more than it threatens any structure at all. A manager who learns to give feedback privately in Beijing and a manager who learns to give feedback privately in Manila will look, from the outside, like they've adapted to the same lesson. They haven't. They've adapted to two different fears.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — On a thread asking how the dynamic feels working for a Chinese boss, one commenter summed up the deference bluntly: "Works best when they say jump, you ask how high." Another, more measured, added the real survival rule beneath the joke: "As long as you save face, you should be good."*

> *Core77 forums — DesignerInShanghai, an American design manager based in Shanghai, described switching management registers depending on the room: a "more American, coaching style" with some staff, but admitted "from time to time, I have to resort to the more dictative, Chinese style of management" because junior designers, trained in a more passive system, sometimes needed direct instruction rather than open-ended coaching.*

> *Victoria Hoffarth's personal blog (on a Filipino business owner, "Mike") — Mike attributed some of his country's economic struggles to a lack of workplace systems and discipline, telling the author plainly: "I think we are not disciplined in our work attitudes," framing his own management style — organized, systems-first — as a deliberate corrective to that norm rather than an imported one.*

> *ikkyinchina.com (Shanghai-based expat blog) — The site's recurring advice to new managers is to treat the first hundred days as listening time, not teaching time: announcing you're there to show locals "how things are done" elsewhere, it warns, "won't go over very well" — a line that shows up, in some form, in nearly every serious guide to managing in China.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A third commenter on the same thread, working in a mixed-nationality tech team, offered the mildest and most common verdict: a former colleague's Chinese boss, based in Hong Kong, was simply "a very friendly guy" — a reminder that national generalizations dissolve fast once you're dealing with one specific human being and not a stereotype.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two moves on management style alone, the real question isn't "how hierarchical is it" — both are, more than most Western workplaces you've known. It's "what currency does the hierarchy run on." In China, it's authority and relationship capital, built slowly and spent carefully. In the Philippines, it's dignity, protected fiercely and in private. Get the currency wrong and you'll overpay for the wrong thing in either country.

What I'd tell a friend: in Shanghai, earn the right to disagree before you use it. In Manila, earn the right to be gentle before you assume it's already yours — the warmth is real, but it's a relationship you build, not a given you walk in with.
