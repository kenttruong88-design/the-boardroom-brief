# The Salary Talk: Where the Irish Ask and the Swedes Already Know

🇮🇪 Ireland · 🇸🇪 Sweden

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Dublin, your salary is a secret you guard and a number you're expected to fight for behind closed doors. In Stockholm, your salary is set months in advance by people who are not you, in a negotiation you were never really invited to. Both countries insist their system is more civilized than the other's. Both are, in their own way, correct — and also lying.

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate before you sign — Irish employers expect it and 79% of hiring managers say they respect candidates who do (LinkedIn, 2024) | Assume your quoted salary includes bonus, pension match, or health cover — get it itemized in writing |
| Benchmark against Dublin specifically, not "Ireland" — Cork and Galway pay noticeably less | Expect a 13th-month payment — annual salary in Ireland is the whole figure, no extra month tacked on |
| Budget for take-home pay, not gross — Ireland's tax wedge sits near 32.6%, below the OECD average (OECD Taxing Wages 2026) | Forget that USC and PRSI both bite before you see a cent |
| Ask about share options in tech and pharma roles — they're common and often undervalued by candidates | Threaten to walk during a negotiation — Irish recruiters consistently flag this as the fastest way to lose goodwill |
| Time your ask to the annual review cycle, usually Q1 | Discuss your salary loudly with colleagues — it's legal, but still socially awkward |

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Come to your *löneförhandling* (salary talk) with data — market comparisons, achievements, written case | Get emotional or make ultimatums — Swedish managers read this as unprofessional, not persuasive |
| Understand that your union, not you, sets the wage floor through collective bargaining covering roughly 3.5 million workers (Chambers and Partners, 2025) | Expect a signing bonus or RSUs — both are rare outside a handful of multinational tech offices |
| Ask your union rep or *skyddsombud* what your sector's *märket* (the wage-setting benchmark) is this year | Assume "transparent" means "negotiable" — pay bands are visible, but individual haggling room is narrow |
| Weigh total package — 30+ vacation days, parental leave, pension — against the lower headline number | Compare your Stockholm offer directly to a US or UK number without adjusting for the 30%+ employer social cost built in instead of on top |
| Join the relevant union early — most salary protections flow through membership | Bring up salary in casual conversation — it's one of the few genuinely private topics left in an otherwise transparent culture |

## Ireland: Ask Nicely, Ask Firmly, Ask Again

Ireland runs an Anglo-American negotiation culture wearing a soft Irish accent. There is no statutory pay transparency regime, no sector-wide bargaining setting your floor, and no taboo against asking for more — quite the opposite. A 2024 LinkedIn study found 79% of Irish hiring managers respect candidates who negotiate professionally, and career advisory firms treat the pre-offer window as the single highest-leverage moment in the relationship. The catch: everything is individually negotiated, so everything is individually variable — two people in the same Dublin office, same title, same tenure, can earn meaningfully different amounts, and neither is required to know it.

The number that makes Ireland look generous is the tax wedge — 32.6% for a single average earner in 2025, below the OECD average of roughly 34.9%, and 25.7% for working couples with children against an OECD average of 29.8% (OECD, Taxing Wages 2026). Combined with median software engineer total comp around €106,600 in Dublin (Levels.fyi), Ireland's headline numbers read well on a spreadsheet — until you hit Dublin rent, where a one-bedroom in the city centre now runs €2,500 or more a month.

## Sweden: The Union Already Had This Conversation Without You

Sweden's approach inverts the Irish model. There is no statutory minimum wage; pay is set through roughly 650 collective agreements negotiated annually between about 60 unions and 50 employer bodies, covering some 3.5 million employees (Chambers and Partners, 2025; WageIndicator, 2026). Your individual *löneförhandling* still happens, but inside a ceiling and floor your union already negotiated, via a benchmark figure called *märket*. The cultural style is distinctly Swedish: understated, non-confrontational, allergic to emotional appeals. Visible frustration or urgency in a salary talk tends to read as a red flag rather than conviction (The Local, 2026).

Average gross pay sits around SEK 42,900 a month, roughly €3,800 (Statistics Sweden, 2025), below Ireland's average of roughly €4,350. Employers also carry 30–35% in social costs on top of salary rather than folding them into gross pay — one reason Swedish nominal pay looks modest next to Denmark's despite comparable living standards. Sweden's low score of 5 on Hofstede's masculinity dimension is the structural echo: a culture that has explicitly deprioritized individual pay maximization in favor of collective outcomes (Hofstede Insights).

## The Reckoning

Put the two systems side by side and the irony sharpens: Ireland, the supposedly more "European" of the two on paper, runs the more American negotiation culture, while famously egalitarian Sweden runs a system where your individual voice in your own pay is, by design, one of the smaller inputs. An Irish worker who negotiates hard in Stockholm will find the room quietly closing rather than opening. A Swedish worker who waits for the union to handle it in Dublin will simply be underpaid — there is no union doing that unless they land in a specific unionized sector.

The purchasing-power math complicates any easy winner. Ireland keeps more of a given gross salary after tax — about 13.5 percentage points more than Sweden across comparable income bands (Global Finance Calculators, 2025) — but Sweden's cost of living runs roughly 31% lower, so the smaller number often stretches further. Neither brochure mentions that the "better deal" depends entirely on whether you value take-home flexibility or predictable collective security.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone comparing Nordic salaries wrote they were baffled at how low nominal Swedish pay looked next to Germany's despite Stockholm's cost of living rivaling much richer cities, and concluded the quality-of-life math only works once you stop converting everything back to euros.*

> *TeamBlind — An engineer who relocated from the Bay Area to a Stockholm tech office wrote that total compensation dropped sharply, signing bonuses and RSUs simply weren't part of the offer, and senior roles rarely cleared what mid-level paid back home — but that 30 vacation days and parental leave made the trade feel less lopsided after a year.*

> *The Local Sweden — A reader account of a first löneförhandling described walking in prepared to make an emotional case for a raise, then being thrown when the manager wanted spreadsheets instead of stories — learning belatedly that Swedish negotiations reward calm, data-backed arguments over passion.*

> *Forum for Expatriate Management — An HR practitioner's account of relocating employees noted that hires arriving in Ireland from countries with a standard 13th-month payment were repeatedly confused to learn their quoted salary was the whole figure, no extra month in December, and recommended getting total compensation itemized in writing before accepting.*

> *IrishJobs Career Advice forum — A recurring theme in reader questions was disbelief that recruiters genuinely wanted counter-offers, with several describing an initial fear of seeming greedy that cost them thousands — the advice repeated most often was simply: ask, because in Ireland almost nobody holds it against you.*

## Conclusion

If you're choosing between these postings on salary grounds alone, it reduces to one question: do you want control over your own number, or do you want someone else to have already fought for a reasonable one before you walked in? Ireland rewards people comfortable advocating for themselves and punishes those who assume the market will do it for them. Sweden removes most of that burden, and most of that upside, in the same motion. Move to Dublin expecting to negotiate like it's your job, because functionally, once a year, it is. Move to Stockholm expecting your union to have already had the argument you thought you were about to have — and bring a spreadsheet anyway, just in case anyone asks how you feel about it.
