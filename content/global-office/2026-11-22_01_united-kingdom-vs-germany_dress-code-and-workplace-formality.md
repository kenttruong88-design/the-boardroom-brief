# Sandals and Sie: How Germany Dressed Down, Spoke Up and Left the United Kingdom Guessing

🇬🇧 United Kingdom · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The nation that gave the world the bowler hat and the three-piece suit now has a workforce in which, according to a YouGov survey published in 2023, only 7 percent wear a suit to work. The nation that capitalises its nouns, its bureaucracy and its second-person pronoun of respect has, according to a 2026 YouGov survey for the retailer Galaxus, a male workforce in which 38 percent turn up in shorts. The two countries have, in short, exchanged reputations without informing their human resources departments, and the newcomer is expected to discover the new arrangement by observation, ideally before the first meeting.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the staff handbook before day one, since 74 percent of UK workers in one survey said their employer has a dress code | Assume that silence about clothes means there is no policy |
| Carry a jacket and tie to client meetings if you work in financial services, where formality survives | Assume London behaves like a creative agency in Newcastle |
| Use first names quickly, as colleagues will | Mistake first-name warmth for friendship or agreement |
| Read polite hedging carefully, because a phrase such as "perhaps we might consider" can carry a firm instruction | Take every compliment or "interesting idea" at face value |
| Raise religious or belief-based dress needs early, as the Equality Act 2010 covers them | Assume tattoos and piercings are protected, because they are not |
| Treat shorts as acceptable in a warm office, as a clear majority of Britons now do | Wear flip-flops, which only 28 percent of Britons find acceptable |

### In Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use Sie and surname until a senior colleague proposes du | Offer du first to someone more senior |
| Arrive punctually, because meetings start when scheduled and agendas are circulated | Treat the start time as a rough suggestion |
| Wear clean, practical clothes; jeans and sneakers are fine in most offices | Wear jeans to a bank or a law firm |
| Wear a suit and tie in banking and insurance | Assume the rest of the country dresses the way Frankfurt does |
| Match your colleagues' level of dress | Dress sharply every day, which invites questions about an important meeting |
| Say what you mean, plainly and early | Wait for praise, since silence usually means the work is acceptable |

## United Kingdom

British formality has moved from the wardrobe to the vocabulary. The YouGov survey from 2023 found that 34 percent of British workers describe their dress as smart casual, 26 percent as casual, 19 percent as uniform and 7 percent as business attire. A July 2026 YouGov poll reported by ITV News found that 68 percent of Britons consider shorts acceptable on men in the office, up from 37 percent a decade earlier, and that 93 percent accept a man without a tie. Data from Indeed, reported by Euronews in November 2025, showed that 3.3 percent of UK job postings now advertise casual dress, more than ten times the pre-pandemic level. London had the lowest share, 2.3 percent, which Indeed's economists attributed to the number of financial services jobs where formal dress is still expected.

The casualness is, however, administered. A 2023 survey by instantprint, reported by HRreview, found that 74 percent of respondents said their employer has a dress code, and 80 percent thought employers should be able to enforce one. A paper for Acas by King's College London observed that courts have been reluctant to interfere with employers' decisions on dress, provided the rules do not breach discrimination law. The Equality Act 2010 protects against dress rules that disadvantage people on grounds such as religion or sex, but the same paper notes that tattoos and decorative piercings are not protected characteristics. The British worker may wear what he likes, within a policy, in a manner the policy will not describe.

The informality is also largely linguistic. Rework's business culture guide notes that British communication relies on understatement, so that polite wording can carry a firm meaning. A Briton living in Germany, writing on the Forty Percent German blog, put the matter neatly: British people drop formality quickly with first names and small talk, but this does not mean they are close, and they tend to use humour in situations Germans consider serious. The country was long famous for formal dress and informal manners. It now offers informal dress and manners whose informality is itself a form of code.

## Germany

German dress is governed by a principle that Bernhard Roetzel, a fashion writer interviewed by The Local, describes as comfort and practicality. Jeans, a sweatshirt and sneakers cover most occasions, he says, with exceptions for banks and law firms. He adds a warning that deserves to be framed above every German desk: dress too smartly and colleagues will ask whether you have an important meeting or want a raise. The Galaxus survey bears this out in its own fashion. Of the men questioned in Germany, 69 percent wear T-shirts to work and 38 percent wear shorts, the highest shares among the five countries surveyed. A member of the Expat.com forum in Stuttgart reported that few people there wear suits and ties, even among business professionals, although shorts are still often read as children's or leisure wear for an adult man at work.

The country is casual, however, in a legally structured way. A GÖRG law firm update explains that German employers may set dress rules under their power to give instructions, but the works council has a right of co-determination under section 87 of the Works Constitution Act, and rules must respect employees' personality rights and the General Equal Treatment Act. In the Cologne cases it describes, rules on women's nail colour and men's hair colour were struck down, while a limit on nail length for security staff survived. Even informality, in other words, has a court record.

The address, meanwhile, remains stricter than the attire. A Wharton Knowledge article reports that tradition calls for the formal Sie with all colleagues regardless of rank, that start-ups and agencies have largely moved to du, and that Siemens and BMW were among the holdouts. An Expat.com member explained that Sie is used with people you do not deal with daily unless both sides agree to switch, that praise is rare, and that private life is kept firmly separate from work. A commenter on Hacker News, describing a German IT company, found it more hierarchical and old-fashioned than anywhere else he had worked, citing emails that all began with "Dear Mr." and a very elaborate style, while conceding that the firm delivered on time.

## The Reckoning

Taken together, the two countries have inverted the conventional formula. In the United Kingdom, the clothes are relaxed, the vocabulary is first-name and the real rules are implicit, enforceable through a handbook that new arrivals are expected to have found. In Germany, the clothes are relaxed, the vocabulary is last-name and the rules are explicit, enforceable through a works council that has read the handbook more carefully than the author. Hofstede Insights' country scores place the two at the same power distance, 35 each, but the United Kingdom scores 89 on individualism against Germany's 67, and 35 on uncertainty avoidance against Germany's 65. That is the arithmetic of a country that improvises and a country that files.

The practical difference lies in where the risk sits. In Britain a mistake is a misread signal: the suit that was unnecessary, the compliment that was a dismissal. In Germany a mistake is a broken rule: the premature du, the late arrival, the sweatshirt at a bank. The first error is forgiven by nobody and mentioned by nobody. The second is mentioned immediately and forgiven by most. An expatriate who prefers to be told is better suited to Germany, and one who enjoys decoding will thrive in the United Kingdom.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — a commenter who works with a German IT firm called it stuffier and more hierarchical than any employer he had known, with every email opening "Dear Mr." and written in elaborate formal language, though the work was excellent and on time*

> *Hacker News — a German replying in the same thread explained that formal salutations are standard in German business writing, that younger people use them less, and that non-native English speakers keep them to sound professional*

> *Expat.com — a member living in Stuttgart said few professionals there wear suits and ties, that office dress is more casual than in the United States, but that shorts are still widely seen as clothing for children or leisure*

> *Expat.com — another member advised that Sie is the default with people you do not work with daily, that praise is rare and silence means satisfaction, and that private problems should stay private*

> *The Local — fashion writer Bernhard Roetzel advised foreigners to dress as casually as the office allows, because dressing smartly makes colleagues suspect an important meeting*

## Conclusion

If you are moving from the United Kingdom to Germany, take your suit, wear it to the first interview, and then watch what the room is wearing and match it downward. Address everyone as Sie and by surname until told otherwise, arrive five minutes early, and say what you mean without the cushioning. If you are moving the other way, buy one decent jacket, learn to read a sentence that begins "with the greatest respect", and read the handbook before the first day, where the dress code will be found under a heading that does not mention dress.

Either way, the rules exist and someone is enforcing them. The only variable is whether they will tell you.
