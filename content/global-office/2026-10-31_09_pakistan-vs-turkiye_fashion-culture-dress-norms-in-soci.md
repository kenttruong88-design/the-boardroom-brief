**Two Countries, One Fabric Industry, Zero Agreement on Hemlines: A Dress-Code Survival Guide for Pakistan and Türkiye**

**🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇹🇷 Türkiye**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Pakistan exports roughly $19 billion a year in textiles and Türkiye exports tens of billions more in finished apparel, which means both countries are, in a literal sense, in the business of dressing the rest of the world while maintaining fairly strict opinions about how you dress in front of them. One nation debates whether banks can order women into abayas; the other spent decades deciding whether a headscarf disqualified you from a government job. Neither question has fully closed, and neither will close before your relocation flight lands. Pack accordingly, and pack for the office you'll actually be in, not the one the recruiter described.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to modest, loose, covered-to-the-wrist clothing for your first weeks in any office | Wear sleeveless tops or above-the-knee hemlines to a client meeting, even in liberal Karachi offices |
| Carry a dupatta or scarf even if you don't plan to use it daily — mosques, in-laws, and some ministries will ask | Assume Lahore's fashion scene (it has one) means the street or the workplace dresses the same way |
| Ask HR directly whether there is a written dress code before your first day — many companies have one and don't advertise it | Treat "business casual" as jeans; several banks and schools have formally banned jeans and T-shirts for staff in 2026 |
| Iron everything; creased clothing reads as unserious in a way Western offices don't fully register | Wear anything sheer or tight under office lighting — it will be noticed and commented on |
| Invest in good shoes; feet get judged almost as hard as hemlines in Pakistani corporate culture | Assume rules are gender-neutral — men get far more latitude in casual dress than women do |

#### 🇹🇷 Türkiye

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect genuine variation by sector: finance and law skew formal and secular-coded; many SMEs are far more relaxed | Assume a headscarf is either universally fine or universally a problem — both still happen depending on employer |
| Buy at least one good blazer; Turkish corporate culture still reads tailoring as a competence signal | Show up underdressed to a first meeting; Turks often dress up slightly more than the stated norm, not less |
| Note that Istanbul's business districts (Levent, Maslak) run noticeably more international and casual than Ankara's ministries | Wear shorts or flip-flops into any office, even on a casual Friday — it barely exists as a concept |
| Keep a cardigan or jacket in summer — some conservative-leaning workplaces still expect covered shoulders even without religious dress | Mistake Istanbul's cosmopolitan centre for the national baseline; small-city offices are considerably more conservative |
| Watch what your direct manager wears on day one and calibrate to that, not to Instagram | Assume secular dress codes from the 20th century still apply uniformly; post-2013 reforms changed public-sector rules substantially |

Pakistan's dress code problem is less about taste than about law, custom, and increasingly, HR policy memos. The World Bank estimates roughly 47 million working-age Pakistani women remain outside the labour force, and female labour-force participation sits among the lowest in the region even as the economy needs them; what women can and must wear to the handful of formal-sector jobs available is treated as a serious institutional question rather than a style footnote. In July 2026, the Senate Standing Committee on Finance and Revenue held hearings, reported by Dawn, after a witness testified that some Islamic banks were requiring female staff to wear abayas while male colleagues faced no equivalent rule; the committee barred the practice and asked for a uniform, gender-neutral "modest" dress policy instead, with the State Bank of Pakistan tasked with circulating it. That same year, Karachi's education authorities issued directives restricting teachers from wearing jeans, T-shirts, and tight-fitting clothing. None of this is theoretical for a new arrival: it means the office dress code is not a vibe, it is frequently a document, and asking to see it is a completely normal first-week question.

The ILO has separately flagged a gender pay gap of roughly 25 percent in Pakistan, part of a broader pattern in which the sartorial debate and the economic-participation debate are not actually separate conversations — employers who litigate hemlines tend to be the same ones under scrutiny for who they hire and promote at all. For men, by contrast, the range is wide and low-stakes: a collared shirt and trousers covers nearly every formal setting, a fact echoed in TalesMag's long-running Islamabad post reports, where one respondent simply described wearing "a button-down or polo shirt and khakis most days" without incident.

Türkiye's version of this debate is older and more constitutional. The country's 1980s-era ban on headscarves in public institutions — universities, courts, the civil service — wasn't formally lifted until 2013, when the government ended the prohibition in state offices, a reform covered at the time by Al Jazeera and VOA as a landmark break from decades of hard secularism under Atatürk's republic. More than a decade on, the practical effect is a genuinely patchwork workplace: a woman in a headscarf may walk into one Istanbul tech office without a second glance and be quietly steered out of another. An expat English teacher recounted on an Expat.com forum thread being offered only two options by a prospective employer — remove the headscarf in the classroom, or wear a wig — and finding it startling that this was still live in a majority-Muslim country. OECD figures put Turkish female labour-force participation at roughly one in three, the lowest in the OECD bloc, though rising; Hofstede Insights scores Türkiye as considerably less individualist than Western Europe but notably less so than Pakistan, which tracks with how dress reads less as personal expression and more as a signal about which Türkiye — secular-republican or pious-provincial — you're presumed to represent.

Fashion in both countries is also, unglamorously, the thing that keeps the lights on nationally, which makes the domestic dress-code disputes faintly absurd by comparison. Textile and apparel exports reached $17.8 billion in Pakistan's 2024-25 fiscal year, up 7.4 percent, according to Fibre2Fashion's tracking of State Bank data — a sector that dwarfs almost everything else the country sells abroad. Türkiye runs the same arithmetic at a different scale entirely: it ranks seventh globally in textile and ready-to-wear exports, with apparel shipments alone approaching $26 billion, per Daily Sabah's reporting on industry figures. Both countries clothe a meaningful share of the world for a living, yet domestic dress codes in each run on entirely separate rulebooks that have nothing to do with what's rolling off their own production lines for export.

**The Reckoning**

Here is the part that trips people up: both countries are Muslim-majority, both have textile economies the size of small nations, and both get described lazily by outsiders as having one uniform dress code. Neither does. Pakistan's variation runs mostly by city and class — Karachi's private sector, Lahore's design scene, and rural Punjab are three different planets. Türkiye's variation runs by ideology and institution — the same outfit can be unremarkable at a Levent startup and a career-limiting choice at a ministry in Ankara, regardless of the wearer's religion. A Pakistani headscarf is rarely a legal question; a Turkish one, even in 2026, occasionally still is.

The more useful contrast is who's deciding. In Pakistan, Senate committees and school boards are increasingly writing the rules, after the fact, in response to complaints — reactive governance. In Türkiye, the rules were rewritten constitutionally, in 2013, then left to individual employers to interpret, which is its own kind of chaos, just a calmer one. If you want predictability, Pakistan's written policies, however restrictive, are at least legible. If you want flexibility, Türkiye offers more of it — but you'll spend your first month reading the room, not the handbook.

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two postings on dress alone, the real question isn't which country is "stricter" — both can be, depending on the office — it's which kind of uncertainty you'd rather manage. Pakistan increasingly hands you something close to a rulebook, even if that rulebook occasionally gets rewritten by a Senate committee mid-career; Türkiye hands you a constitution that settled the big question in 2013 and then left ten thousand small ones to individual bosses, which means your actual experience depends enormously on which Türkiye your employer belongs to. Pack modest basics for Pakistan and a blazer for Türkiye, keep both receipts in case HR changes its mind, and in both cases, watch your manager's collar before you watch the Instagram feed. As I'd tell a friend over a drink: in Pakistan you're dressing for the institution, in Türkiye you're dressing for the room, and either way, nobody involved is dressing for themselves.
