**Coffee at Kaffibrennslan vs. a 12% Raise Every Two Years: Loyalty and Mobility in Iceland and Singapore**

**🇮🇸 Iceland · 🇸🇬 Singapore**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

One foreign worker in Reykjavík put it plainly: "I've seen more jobs filled over coffee at Kaffibrennslan than through LinkedIn." A Singaporean millennial, meanwhile, averages two years and nine months in a role before moving on — and Gen Z has trimmed that further to two years and three months — because a well-timed switch has recently been shown to deliver something close to a 12% pay bump that staying put simply doesn't. Iceland runs on reputation in a country small enough that everyone eventually hears about everyone. Singapore runs on a labor market efficient enough that loyalty has to be paid for, explicitly, or it evaporates within three years.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇸 Iceland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build genuine personal relationships in your industry — informal networking regularly outperforms job boards in a country this size | Rely primarily on cold LinkedIn applications; direct outreach and introductions carry far more weight |
| Signal real, visible commitment to living locally — hobbies, language classes, community ties — especially if your visa depends on employer sponsorship | Present yourself as passing through; in a market this small, a "just here for six months" reputation travels fast and follows you |
| Choose your first employer carefully if you're on a non-EEA permit — your residence status may be tied to them for the first few years | Assume you can freely job-hop early on if your visa status depends on a specific sponsoring employer; check the actual legal tie first |
| Expect hiring to move through recruitment agencies and personal contacts who know "the ins and outs" of a genuinely small marketplace | Underestimate how quickly professional reputation circulates; a small labor market means your last job's reputation precedes you everywhere |
| Play a longer game professionally — trust and standing build slowly and pay off over years, not job-hop cycles | Burn a bridge assuming it won't resurface; in a country this size, it very often does, sometimes at your next interview |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat a job switch every two to three years as a normal, even expected, part of a healthy career, not a red flag | Assume long tenure alone signals loyalty positively to employers; many now read short average tenures as the market norm, not a personal failing |
| Negotiate aggressively when you do move — data shows job switchers often out-earn those who stay through internal raises alone | Expect your current employer to match an external offer's pay growth without you actually testing the market |
| Time bigger, more frequent moves earlier in your career; expect scrutiny on tenure to increase as you approach 8+ years of experience | Keep hopping every 12-18 months indefinitely into a senior career stage; stability starts to matter more as roles get more senior |
| Research a prospective employer's actual pay competitiveness — job-hopping stigma, where it exists, tends to cluster at lower-paying firms | Take a low offer assuming raises will compound meaningfully over time; internal wage growth in Singapore often lags market-rate switches significantly |
| Read a low 2.2% unemployment rate alongside high turnover as a genuinely candidate-favorable market, and act accordingly | Feel guilty for prioritizing compensation growth over sentimental loyalty to one employer; the broader culture has already normalized this trade-off |

### Iceland: A Labor Market the Size of a Small Town

Iceland's job market operates on a scale where reputation isn't a metaphor — it's closer to a functional credit score. The country's own official job-hunting guide encourages direct outreach to specific employers over blanket applications, precisely because the market is small enough that identifying and approaching the right companies personally beats competing in a wide applicant pool. Recruitment agencies play an outsized role for the same reason: they hold "extensive contacts" in what amounts to a genuinely close-knit professional community.

One foreign worker's account of building a career there captured the texture of this vividly: "I've seen more jobs filled over coffee at Kaffibrennslan than through LinkedIn." That same worker described the deliberate work of signaling long-term intent — mentioning local hobbies, taking language classes — specifically "to signal that I wasn't just here for a six-month adventure," a strategy made more urgent by the fact that, for many non-EEA visa holders, a residence permit is tied directly to a specific sponsoring employer for the first several years. In a market this size, job loyalty isn't purely a virtue; it's partly structural, and partly the rational response to knowing that everyone in your field will, eventually, hear how your last job ended.

### Singapore: Mobility as Rational Career Strategy

Singapore's labor market runs on close to the opposite logic. Recent tenure data tells the story cleanly: millennials average two years and nine months in a role, Gen Z workers just two years and three months — compared to eight years and three months for baby boomers a generation earlier. This isn't purely generational restlessness; it tracks a real financial incentive. Widely cited analysis shows employees who switch jobs can net close to a 12% pay increase, compared to the far slower creep of internal annual raises — a gap large enough that staying loyal to one employer for the raise schedule alone is, in pure financial terms, often the worse bet.

Crucially, this isn't read as a red flag by the market the way it might be elsewhere. A Milieu study cited in local career reporting found that "job-hopping doesn't fall in a negative light" among Singaporean professionals, and unemployment has stayed low (around 2.2%) even as a record share of workers say they plan to switch roles — meaning people are moving between jobs, not out of the workforce. Community discussion among tech workers globally backs the pattern with specifics: one professional described a single job move that "increased TC by 100k," bluntly noting "no company will ever give me a 100k raise" through internal progression alone — though the same discussion flagged that the tolerance for frequent moves narrows once someone reaches roughly eight years of experience, when employers start looking for signs of stability in more senior hires.

### The Reckoning

The two systems are, in a real sense, mirror images built for opposite market conditions. Iceland's tiny, tightly networked labor market makes loyalty a rational strategy: reputation compounds, and burning a bridge in a country this size has a way of resurfacing at your next interview. Singapore's larger, more liquid labor market makes mobility the rational strategy: with enough employers competing for the same skilled workers, switching jobs is simply the most efficient mechanism for capturing your market value, and staying put mostly means leaving money on the table.

The trap for a transplant runs in predictable directions. Someone moving from Singapore's job-hopping norm to Iceland who treats a two-year stint as a routine career move will find their next opportunity harder to secure than expected, because the same small market that made networking efficient also made short tenures memorable in the wrong way. Someone moving from Iceland's relationship-first market to Singapore who stays loyal to one employer out of habit, expecting reputation and tenure to be rewarded the way they would be in Reykjavík, will watch colleagues who switched jobs twice in the same period out-earn them by a wide and growing margin.

### Conclusion

If you're deciding which system suits you, ask yourself honestly whether you're someone who builds value through relationships that compound over years, or through market timing that gets rewarded immediately. Iceland will reward the first instinct and quietly punish the second; Singapore does close to the reverse. Neither culture is being irrational — they've each built a career strategy that makes perfect sense for the size and liquidity of their own labor market. Just don't bring Singapore's job-hopping playbook to a country where the hiring manager at your next interview already knows your last one personally.
