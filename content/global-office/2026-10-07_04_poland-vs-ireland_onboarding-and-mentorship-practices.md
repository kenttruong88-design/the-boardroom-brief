**The Buddy System vs. The Pub System: Getting Started on the Job in Poland and Ireland**

**🇵🇱 Poland · 🇮🇪 Ireland**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Poland issued over 320,000 work permits to foreign nationals last year and responded, sensibly, by formalising the "buddy or mentoring system" into HR policy. Ireland has never needed to formalise anything, because its onboarding system has run on the same engine for decades: a firm handshake, a joke at your expense, and an invitation to the pub by week two.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly whether a formal buddy or mentor will be assigned — many larger employers now offer this explicitly | Assume your contract and onboarding paperwork are optional extras; registration with the Social Insurance Institution (ZUS) is mandatory |
| Expect directness, paired with real politeness, rather than indirect hinting | Mistake politeness for softness — punctuality and deadlines are taken seriously |
| Use your first weeks to build trust through reliability, not charm | Expect instant informality; trust with Polish colleagues is generally earned over time, not assumed on day one |
| Take advantage of employer-sponsored language courses if offered — many larger firms now provide them | Assume English alone will get you through every bureaucratic or everyday interaction; 41% of employers cite language as the top integration barrier |
| Watch and adapt early — observing how teammates interact is explicitly recommended by local hiring guides | Expect training and certification paths to be handed to you — strong performers are expected to actively pursue them |

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to read indirect feedback — ask explicitly, "what's the one thing I should do differently next time?" | Assume a joke at your expense during a meeting is personal; taking workplace humour lightly signals you fit in |
| Move to first names quickly; it's standard, not a sign of reduced seniority or accountability | Mistake informal hierarchy for an absence of accountability — reporting lines and deadlines still hold |
| Say yes to the post-work pub invitation at least once; real networking happens there | Discuss your salary in casual conversation — it remains a firm taboo |
| Use the "right to disconnect" — it's a real, recently legislated protection, not just etiquette | Assume every employer interprets work-life balance the same way; it varies significantly by sector |
| Request a structured check-in around the one-month mark if nobody offers one | Wait indefinitely for explicit criticism; it may be softened enough that you miss it entirely |

### Poland

Poland's onboarding culture has been forced to modernise fast, simply by volume: over 320,000 work permits were issued to foreign nationals in a single recent year, and employers who used to onboard almost exclusively Polish-speaking, Polish-educated hires are now managing real linguistic and bureaucratic complexity at scale. Surveyed employers name language barriers (41%) and bureaucratic complexity (35%) as the two biggest obstacles to integrating foreign staff, which is exactly why the recommended fix reads like policy rather than culture: "buddy or mentoring systems, where experienced team members guide new hires through company culture and everyday routines," paired with role-specific language training and manager workshops on cross-cultural communication.

The payoff for employers who actually do this, according to the same reporting, is measurable — faster ramp-up, fewer safety and communication errors, and better retention — and over half of Polish employers now cite addressing labour shortages as their main reason for hiring abroad, with roughly a quarter explicitly valuing the creative upside of a more diverse team. Hofstede's research scores Poland at 93 on uncertainty avoidance, one of the higher readings anywhere, which tracks precisely with what newcomers report: directness paired with real politeness, firm deadlines, and trust that has to be built through demonstrated reliability rather than assumed on arrival.

### Ireland

Ireland runs almost the opposite model, and largely gets away with it. The country's own workplace-culture guides note, with evident pride, that Irish business etiquette was voted the "easiest to understand" in Europe by foreign workers — a reasonable claim, given that the national tech sector alone employs over 100,000 people and is the world's second-largest exporter of computer and IT services, almost entirely staffed by a highly international workforce that has absorbed the local style without much formal onboarding infrastructure at all.

What passes for mentorship in Ireland is mostly social: first names from day one, humour folded into meetings as a genuine test of fit, and real relationship-building that happens in bars, restaurants, and — per more than one local guide — on golf courses, with non-drinkers expected to simply order something else and stay in the conversation. The catch, and it is a real one, is the feedback layer underneath the charm. As one newcomer-focused guide puts it plainly, "feedback may be delivered indirectly to preserve relationships, potentially leaving newcomers uncertain about required changes" — the Irish equivalent of Poland's uncertainty avoidance expressing itself not as bureaucracy, but as a joke that was actually a warning. Hofstede's Ireland score sits at just 28 on power distance against Poland's 68, which matches the first-names-and-pub-invites informality almost exactly — right up until the moment a newcomer realises informal does not mean unaccountable.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't in how friendly either country is — both, by most accounts, are genuinely welcoming — it's in where each one puts the effort. Poland has built its mentorship explicitly to solve a legal, linguistic, and bureaucratic onboarding problem, which means the support is real but arrives as paperwork and scheduled check-ins rather than warmth. Ireland has built essentially no formal onboarding infrastructure at all and instead relies on a culture so socially permissive that newcomers often don't notice they're being onboarded — until the moment they realise the joke in the Tuesday meeting was, in fact, the only feedback they were going to get on a mistake.

Both systems quietly ask something of the newcomer that neither one says out loud: in Poland, patience, while trust is deliberately built rather than extended on faith; in Ireland, fluency in a specific kind of humour-coded indirectness that the "easiest to understand in Europe" reputation rather undersells.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *CareersInPoland.com — Natnael, who moved from an internship into a full-time accounting role at Equinix Poland, described a genuinely pleasant surprise: "Everyone is incredibly supportive and eager to collaborate on projects," crediting a workforce drawn from over 40 nationalities for making the cultural adjustment easier than he expected.*

> *Blind — A commenter discussing relocating to a tech hub abroad offered a far blunter practical frustration, condensing an entire country's reputation among tech workers into one line: "European here, AMA about low salaries and lack of innovation" — a reminder that the social warmth doesn't always extend to the paycheck.*

> *IrishTalents.com workplace guide — The guide's single most useful piece of advice doubles as a cultural misread warning: because feedback is so often softened to preserve the relationship, newcomers are told to ask directly, "is this a priority issue, or an improvement for later?" — because the pleasant tone alone won't tell you.*

> *CareersInPoland.com employer research — Surveyed Polish employers were candid about their own limitations, naming language (41%) and bureaucratic complexity (35%) as the two biggest barriers they themselves still haven't solved — a rare admission, in employer-facing material, that the formal mentoring programs exist precisely because the informal version wasn't working.*

### Conclusion

If you need your first ninety days mapped out for you — a buddy, a training schedule, a reason your confusion is somebody else's documented job to fix — Poland's increasingly formalised systems will do that, provided you're patient while trust is built the slow way. If you'd rather feel at home within a week and figure out the real rules by osmosis, Ireland will welcome you faster than almost anywhere in Europe — just don't mistake the joke in Tuesday's meeting for just a joke.

Either way, ask the awkward direct question early: in Poland, "will someone be assigned to help me," and in Ireland, "was that notes, or was that a warning."
