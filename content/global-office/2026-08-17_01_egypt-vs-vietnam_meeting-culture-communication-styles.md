**Two Ways to Say No Without Saying It: Meetings in Egypt and Vietnam**

**🇪🇬 Egypt · 🇻🇳 Vietnam**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Cairo, a meeting scheduled for 10am might begin at 10:40, cover four unrelated topics at once, and end with someone insisting you stay for tea you did not ask for. In Hanoi, that same meeting will start on time, address one topic with visible order, and conclude with a "yes" that, on closer inspection, meant nothing of the kind. Both cultures consider themselves diplomatic. Neither one is lying to you, exactly. They are simply not telling you the whole truth in the room — that comes later, elsewhere, from someone else.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇪🇬 Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget real time for small talk before any agenda item — skipping it reads as rude, not efficient | Don't interpret "we'll see" as anything other than a soft no |
| Address the most senior person in the room first and let them set the pace of the discussion | Don't hand documents or food with your left hand |
| Expect an open-door meeting culture — colleagues wandering in mid-discussion is normal, not a breach | Don't schedule back-to-back meetings assuming each will end on time |
| Bring a business card and use titles (Doctor, Engineer, Professor) until invited to drop them | Don't push for a firm decision in the room — major calls get made afterward, privately |
| Dress conservatively and formally, even in creative industries | Don't take elaborate compliments or hospitality as literal commitments |

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up on time — Vietnamese meetings, unlike the regional stereotype, tend to start punctually | Don't take a "yes" at face value; listen for what follows the pause |
| Route disagreement through a private, one-on-one conversation with the senior person afterward | Don't ask junior staff to contradict a superior's view in open session |
| Send a written follow-up after the meeting — decisions are often finalized outside the room | Don't mistake silence for agreement; it is frequently the opposite |
| Learn the org chart before you walk in — who outranks whom shapes who speaks | Don't deliver criticism in front of a person's colleagues, even gently |
| Use a warm, unhurried tone even when the topic is tense | Don't assume a single meeting will produce a binding outcome |

---

Egypt's workplace runs on a polychronic clock and a hierarchical map. According to Commisceo Global and the Cultural Atlas, Egyptian business meetings routinely open with extended social pleasantries, cover multiple topics in parallel rather than sequence, and are conducted with an open-door norm in which interruptions are unremarkable. Hierarchy is not incidental — it is structural. Managers retain final say on major decisions even after consultation, and employees, per Commisceo's country guide, are frequently reluctant to disagree openly with a superior in a group setting. ILOSTAT puts Egypt's average working week at roughly 44 hours, modestly above the global average of 43.9 tracked by the ILO, which fits a culture where the meeting itself is only one, unhurried, part of getting anything done.

Vietnam runs on a tighter clock but a thicker layer of indirection. Meetings tend to start on time and stay focused on a single topic, a contrast with the Egyptian style — but the real decision-making, per multiple business-culture guides including Rivermate and Commisceo, happens afterward and privately, built through consensus rather than debate in the room. The much-cited feature of Vietnamese communication is the answer that begins with "yes" and ends somewhere else entirely: a "yes" frequently signals only that the listener understood the question, not that they agree with it, according to guides used to train foreign managers working with Vietnamese teams. Instructions and feedback move through layers of hierarchy, softened at each stage, and junior employees are described across multiple sources as reluctant to voice dissent in front of superiors — a dynamic Hofstede's power-distance dimension captures numerically, with both Egypt and Vietnam scoring well above the low-hierarchy Nordics on that measure, though Vietnam's collectivism pushes harmony-preservation even further than Egypt's.

**The Reckoning**

The counterintuitive part is that Egypt, the culture that looks looser on the surface — late starts, wandering topics, open doors — is actually more willing to have the real disagreement happen live, if delivered with enough deference and warmth. Vietnam, which looks tidier on the surface — punctual, single-threaded, orderly — pushes almost all substantive disagreement out of the room entirely, deferring it to private, after-the-fact conversations. A foreign manager who reads Egypt's informality as an invitation to push hard for a same-meeting decision will stall. One who reads Vietnam's punctuality and order as a signal that the meeting's outcome is final will be quietly corrected days later by an outcome nobody objected to in the room.

The other trap is temporal. Egypt's meetings expand to fill the relationship being built; ending one early, even when the agenda is finished, can read as transactional and cold. Vietnam's meetings are efficient with time but inefficient with truth — you can walk out with a full page of notes and zero real signal about what will actually happen next. Both systems require the same skill from an outsider, just applied differently: read the second, unspoken meeting that runs underneath the one you were invited to.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A contributor describing Vietnamese communication style noted that a colleague's "yes" is frequently just an acknowledgment that they heard the question, and that the actual answer — often a disagreement — tends to arrive afterward, folded into a longer sentence that starts with agreement and quietly reverses it.*

> *Team Blind — A tech worker weighing a move from a Bay Area company to a fast-growing Vietnamese startup like Tiki or Grab described the pull as financial curiosity colliding with real uncertainty about whether the slower, more hierarchical decision cadence at a Southeast Asian company would feel like a demotion in influence, not just compensation.*

> *Substack (expat blog, Hanoi) — An American who relocated to Hanoi for a job in 2019 and stayed for years past his original plan wrote that the adjustment that stuck with him longest wasn't the language, it was recalibrating how much unsaid disagreement could be sitting underneath an apparently settled meeting.*

> *Substack (expat blog, Cairo) — A long-term Cairo expat observed that newcomers who treated Egyptian meetings as inefficient — bristling at the small talk, the interruptions, the wandering agenda — tended to have a worse time overall than those who accepted the meeting as a relationship-building ritual first and a decision-making tool second.*

> *Cultural Atlas / practitioner guidance — Guides prepared for foreign managers repeatedly flag the same specific error: pushing an Egyptian counterpart for a firm yes-or-no answer in the room, which reads as an ambush rather than a request, and reliably produces a vague, noncommittal reply instead of the clarity being sought.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two, the practical question is not which culture is more polite — both are, extravagantly, by Western standards — but which kind of ambiguity you are better equipped to read. Egypt asks you to sit with mess in real time and build enough trust that people will eventually tell you what they think. Vietnam asks you to accept a tidy room and go find out what actually happened somewhere else, later, from someone who wasn't in it. Neither is more honest than the other. If a friend asked me over drinks, I'd say: in Cairo, learn to enjoy the meeting; in Hanoi, learn to distrust it.
