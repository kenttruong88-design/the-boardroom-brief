# In Singapore, Loyalty Is a Red Flag. In Tanzania, It's the Only One Available.

**🇸🇬 Singapore · 🇹🇿 Tanzania**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two workers, two offers, one fundamental disagreement about what a career is for. In Singapore, a résumé showing four jobs in five years used to be a liability; these days it is closer to the median, even as the Ministry of Manpower's own data shows job-switching at its lowest rate in six years — a labour market publicly arguing with itself, cautious in the aggregate while every individual in it claims to be restless. In Tanzania, the anxiety runs the other way: the question is rarely whether you've switched jobs too often, but whether you will ever hold a formal one to switch out of, since roughly three-quarters of employed youth work in agriculture and barely one in fifteen holds a public-sector wage job, per World Bank research. Loyalty means something different on each side of the Indian Ocean, and both versions will surprise anyone who moves there expecting the brochure's version of either.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate hard before signing — the first offer is a floor, not a ceiling. | Don't assume a counteroffer from your current employer means a real promotion is coming next cycle. |
| Keep your CV framed around outcomes; nobody hiring is impressed by "same job, nine years." | Don't badmouth a former employer in interviews — HR circles here are small and memories are long. |
| Benchmark your market rate against SkillsFuture and Progressive Wage Model tables before asking for a raise. | Don't job-hop faster than roughly every 18 months if you're angling for management — promotion panels read it as a flight risk. |
| Expect recruiters to message you first; an idle LinkedIn profile is read as disengagement. | Don't expect CPF or tenure to function as a loyalty bonus — it's retirement savings, not a reward for staying. |
| Treat a counteroffer as a data point, not a declaration of love. | Don't be surprised if "stability" and "satisfaction" turn out to be unrelated — they often are here. |

#### 🇹🇿 Tanzania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build relationships with your manager's superiors early — advancement travels through trust as much as output. | Don't assume a formal review cycle is the only route to a raise; many promotions happen through direct appeal. |
| Treat a government or parastatal post as a long-term anchor if one is offered — the security is the compensation. | Don't assume private-sector tenure comes with a pension-equivalent; most workers remain informally employed with no such cushion. |
| Network through family, religious communities, and university alumni ties — this is still how most formal jobs are filled. | Don't lead with a résumé of frequent switches; employers may read it as instability rather than ambition. |
| Expect loyalty to be repaid slowly, over years rather than a fiscal cycle. | Don't underestimate how few formal jobs exist; most entrants never reach the wage-earning sector at all. |
| Learn workplace etiquette around hierarchy and greetings — it shapes how seriously a request for advancement is taken. | Don't push hard for rapid promotion in year one; tenure itself functions as a credential. |

Singapore's relationship with job-hopping is the stuff of HR-conference keynote slides: equal parts moral panic and strategic necessity. LinkedIn's workforce data found that one in three Singaporean professionals have no intention of staying with their current employer beyond a year, and 42 percent describe themselves as actively, strongly open to something else — numbers that would read as a crisis in a smaller labour pool. And yet the Ministry of Manpower's most recent labour force figures show job-switching at a six-year low: just 14.7 percent of employees changed roles in the past two years, while the share of resident workers who have stuck with one employer for a decade or more has climbed to roughly one in three. Local outlets have taken to calling this "job hugging" — fewer resignations, longer tenures, a workforce clinging to stability even while telling pollsters it would rather be elsewhere.

The mechanics still reward strategic movement more than patience. A job switch commonly delivers the 10-to-20-percent pay jump that annual increments rarely match, and a meaningful share of professionals surveyed by local recruiters say they changed jobs chasing exactly that kind of raise — a pattern recruiters privately call the "mover's premium," as though loyalty itself were the asset being arbitraged away. But there are guardrails: hiring managers and promotion panels, by most accounts, treat anything faster than a move every 18 months as a flight-risk signal once you're angling for management, and schemes like SkillsFuture and the Progressive Wage Model exist precisely so employers can measure worth by skill rather than tenure — meaning the résumé gymnastics has to be justified, not merely performed. Median income from work has still climbed substantially over the past decade, even after inflation, which suggests the system is not broken so much as recalibrated around shorter, better-negotiated stays. The quiet irony, per Gallup's long-running engagement polling, is that Singaporean workers have ranked among the least engaged in the world for over a decade regardless of how often they move. Mobility, it turns out, is not the same thing as satisfaction.

Tanzania's career anxiety runs in the opposite direction. The formal job worth being loyal to — or leaving — is itself the scarce resource. World Bank research on the country's youth labour force found that about three-quarters of employed young people work in agriculture, and only 6.7 percent hold a public-sector wage job: the kind with a payslip, a contract, and something to actually be loyal about. Youth unemployment in Dar es Salaam runs roughly six times the rural rate, and more than one in five secondary-educated young people in the capital are unemployed, a figure that rises above half in Zanzibar. Against that backdrop, the government has leaned on external financing — including a $550 million World Bank package for jobs and social protection — to do what the formal private sector has not: generate enough wage employment for a youth population projected to reach 15 million by 2030.

Where formal jobs do exist, loyalty functions less as a virtue than a structural default, reinforced by how hiring actually works — through family networks, religious and university ties, and relationships with a manager's superiors that matter more than a review cycle most workplaces don't run. Of roughly 900,000 young Tanzanians entering the labour market each year, a large share leave school without finishing secondary education, which narrows the pool competing for the wage jobs that do exist and makes the ones who land one disproportionately cautious about leaving. The Citizen, a Tanzanian daily, profiled Benon, a 29-year-old IT specialist who has worked for six companies in roughly six years — proof that job-hopping exists here too, and that where it does, it looks remarkably like Singapore's version: a bet that the market, not the org chart, sets your price. But Benon is notable precisely because he's an exception; for most entrants to Tanzania's labour market, the operative question isn't how long to stay, but whether a formal job — any formal job — will materialize at all.

**The Reckoning.** Put the two economies side by side and the usual developed-versus-developing script inverts. Singapore, the wealthy, hyper-connected city-state, is where workers are growing more cautious, not less — tenures lengthening, resignations at record lows, a generation quietly job-hugging through a softer economy even as it tells pollsters it would rather be elsewhere. Tanzania, the country most outsiders assume runs on precarity, is where the handful of workers who land a genuinely formal job are often the ones with real leverage to move, because for roles requiring scarce skills, the employer — not the employee — is the scarcer party. Scarcity cuts both ways: scarce jobs make Singaporean workers cling to what they have, and scarce skilled labour makes Tanzanian job-hoppers like Benon viable in the first place. The two countries have, in effect, swapped the emotional register usually assigned to "developed" and "developing" labour markets, and neither swap is doing either workforce any particular favours.

Hofstede's cultural-dimensions framework gestures at why, though only partially. Hofstede Insights profiles Singapore individually — a notably collectivist, high-power-distance society — but does not profile Tanzania on its own, grouping it instead within a broader East African cluster. That gap is itself a small warning for anyone planning a move by statistics alone: the data infrastructure describing these two labour markets is built unevenly, much like the markets themselves. The more honest read is that "loyalty" in Singapore is a negotiating position, revisable every appraisal cycle, while in Tanzania it is closer to a structural condition — assigned by how few formal alternatives exist, rather than chosen because the culture reveres patience for its own sake.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/singaporefi — A job-hopper who'd changed roles four times in five years, each move worth a 20 to 30 percent raise, asked the subreddit at what point the pattern becomes a red flag; hiring managers in the replies admitted they'd hesitate, while others argued the raises spoke for themselves.*

> *r/singapore — A public-sector employee six months into a new role, lured by a hybrid-work promise that evaporated into near-daily office attendance, asked whether it was reasonable to leave before finishing probation; the consensus was to go, with one commenter noting they'd quit after four and a half months themselves and taken a pay cut without regret.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A backend engineer with eight years' experience and an existing Singapore offer asked how job-switching there compares with the Bay Area; the one reply, from a Singapore-based engineer at a major bank, warned the market had gone cold enough that even locals weren't getting responses.*

> *Expat.com forum — A prospective expat planning two months in Arusha to search for private-sector and NGO work found recruiters in Dar es Salaam simply wouldn't answer their phone, and left the thread's practical questions about work-permit timelines unanswered — a small, telling illustration of how informal the formal hiring process can feel from the outside.*

> *Comparably — At a Tanzania-based telecoms-infrastructure employer, staff reported an unusually high willingness to recommend their employer to friends, with the company citing that over half its leadership had been promoted from within — a rare glimpse of internal mobility working exactly as the brochure promises, notable precisely because it's uncommon.*

### Conclusion

The practical advice, stripped of the graphs and the HR euphemisms: in Singapore, plan your career the way you'd plan a stock portfolio — diversify across employers, time your exits, and don't mistake a counteroffer for genuine affection. In Tanzania, plan it the way you'd plan a long-term relationship in a town with very few eligible partners — invest in the people who control access to the job, because there may not be five more interviews lined up behind this one, and the formal job itself, not the employer's particular virtues, is usually the real prize.

Neither strategy is wrong, exactly; they're answers to different questions. Singapore is optimizing for the best career inside an abundance of employers; Tanzania is optimizing for a career at all inside a scarcity of them. Pick accordingly — and notice that somewhere between a résumé with six jobs and a résumé with one, there's a version of job security neither place is actually selling.
