# Welcome Aboard: Spain Hands You a Mentor and a Three-Hour Lunch. America Hands You a Login and a Slack Channel.

🇪🇸 Spain · 🇺🇸 USA

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Every company insists it has an onboarding "program." In Spain, this tends to mean a designated person who will personally walk you through the coffee machine's secret settings and the office's unwritten hierarchy over an unhurried lunch. In America, it tends to mean a Notion page with forty-one tabs, an I-9 form, and a calendar invite titled "Welcome!! 🎉" that nobody actually attends. Neither country has solved onboarding. They have simply failed at it in culturally distinct, entirely predictable ways.

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the long lunch invitation from your assigned mentor — it is unofficial onboarding | Assume no formal orientation means no one is paying attention to how you integrate |
| Learn names, titles, and who reports to whom before asking substantive questions | Skip the small talk and go straight to business on day one |
| Expect your contract type (indefinido vs. temporal) to shape how much investment you get | Be surprised if a temporary hire gets a lighter onboarding than a permanent one |
| Lean on your mentor for the unwritten rules — who actually makes decisions, who to avoid | Expect a written playbook; much of the culture is transmitted orally |
| Be patient during the first weeks; trust is built slowly through repeated contact | Push for fast feedback loops in week one; it can read as impatient or presumptuous |

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Front-load the paperwork — benefits, tax forms, compliance modules — as fast as possible | Expect anyone to walk you through the health insurance plans in person |
| Ask your manager directly for an assigned mentor if one isn't offered | Assume a "buddy" Slack ping counts as a mentorship relationship |
| Treat the first 90 days as an audition — expectations get set fast | Wait passively for feedback; ask for it, repeatedly |
| Show up to the optional "welcome" events; visibility compounds early | Skip them and assume good work alone will be noticed |
| Read the org chart and figure out who actually holds influence | Rely solely on your official manager as your only guide to the company |

## Spain: The Mentor Is a Person, Not a Portal

Formal onboarding infrastructure in Spain is often thin, but the informal kind is not. New hires are typically introduced to a manager or designated colleague in a one-on-one setting, and it is this person — not an HR system — who ends up transmitting the actual operating manual: who makes decisions, which meetings matter, and which colleague to route around. Mentorship research generally credits pairing new hires with a guide for accelerating both technical ramp-up and cultural integration, with mentored employees showing meaningfully higher retention than those left to figure it out alone.

The catch: how much mentorship you get can depend heavily on your contract type. Temporary contracts cover well over a quarter of Spanish workers aged 25 to 29, and Spain continues to register the highest rate of job precariousness in the EU, per Spain's statistical and social-observatory data. A company investing in a three-month mentor relationship for someone on a three-month contract has, correctly, done the math. Hofstede's comparison helps explain the texture: Spain scores notably higher than the U.S. on uncertainty avoidance and power distance, meaning new hires are expected to observe hierarchy carefully and tolerate ambiguity longer before anyone hands them a rulebook.

## USA: The Onboarding Program Exists. Whether Anyone Runs It Is Another Matter.

American companies, especially larger ones, have onboarding down as a documented process: checklists, cohort orientations, compliance modules, sometimes a formally assigned "onboarding buddy" distinct from your manager. Employees at large tech firms describe structured multi-week ramp periods, paired work, and even a second mentor outside their immediate team. On paper, this looks like the more rigorous system, and SHRM's data backs the intent: organizations with standardized onboarding see 50% greater new-hire productivity and are far more likely to retain people past the first year.

The gap is between design and delivery. SHRM finds up to 20% of all turnover happens within the first 45 days, a third of new hires leave within 90 days, and only 29% feel genuinely prepared after onboarding concludes. Gallup pinpoints why: new hires whose managers are actively, visibly present are 3.4 times more likely to call the experience exceptional — yet only 12% of employees strongly agree their organization onboards people well. The infrastructure exists; the manager showing up for it is optional, in a labor market where median job tenure has fallen to 3.9 years, per the Bureau of Labor Statistics, with workers under 35 staying barely half that long.

## The Reckoning

Put the two systems side by side and you get an uncomfortable symmetry: Spain has warm, personal mentorship gated by contract precarity; America has thorough, documented onboarding gated by managerial attention span. A new hire in Madrid on a permanent contract may get more genuine cultural absorption in month one than a new hire in Austin who is handed a login, a wiki, and a standing 1:1 that gets rescheduled twice. Meanwhile, a new hire in Madrid on a temporary contract may get almost nothing at all, while the Austin hire at least has an HR system tracking whether their compliance modules are complete.

Neither approach reliably produces what onboarding is theoretically for: a new employee who understands the job, the culture, and where they stand. Spain's version depends on the luck of getting a good, present mentor and a contract worth investing in. America's version depends on the luck of getting a manager who treats the onboarding calendar invite as more than decoration. Both systems, in other words, have quietly outsourced the actual work of onboarding to whichever individual happens to care.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who relocated to Spain for work wrote that the job search itself ran almost entirely on personal connections rather than formal applications, and that this informality initially felt exclusionary until they realized the same networks would later explain everything the HR handbook never covered.*

> *TeamBlind — An engineer describing onboarding at a large U.S. tech company noted being assigned both a team-level onboarding buddy and a separate mentor outside their group, with a two-month checklist of paired work and small starter projects — more structure, they said, than they'd expected walking in.*

> *Hacker News (Ask HN) — A commenter reflecting on "sink or swim" onboarding at a fast-moving company said the lack of structure was framed internally as a feature that rewarded self-starters, but in practice just meant redundant knowledge-sharing and a slower ramp for anyone without an existing internal contact.*

> *Expat blog (movingtransparent.com) — An American who moved to Spain for work wrote that the biggest surprise wasn't the paperwork but how much actual mentorship happened over long lunches with a colleague, calling it more personal guidance than any orientation session they'd sat through in the U.S.*

> *Quora — A foreign-qualified professional trying to break into the Spanish job market wrote that they wished someone had told them upfront how much a NIE, residency status, and contract type would shape not just whether they got hired, but how much onboarding support they'd receive once they did.*

## Conclusion

Onboarding, in both countries, turns out to be less a program than a bet: Spain bets on personal relationships absorbing the slack that formal structure leaves behind, and wins or loses depending on your contract status; America bets on documented process absorbing the slack that manager attention leaves behind, and wins or loses depending on whether anyone actually opens the onboarding checklist. If you're relocating for either, budget less energy for judging the paperwork and more for identifying, fast, whichever human is actually going to explain how the place works — because in both Madrid and Manhattan, that person was never going to be the org chart.
