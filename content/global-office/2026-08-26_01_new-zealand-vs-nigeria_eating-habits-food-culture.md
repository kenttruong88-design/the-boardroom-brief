# A Kiwi Eats Alone at His Desk. A Nigerian Eats With Forty Relatives. Someone Here Is Doing It Wrong.

🇳🇿 New Zealand · 🇳🇬 Nigeria

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Consider two lunch breaks. In Wellington, a mid-level analyst eats a sandwich at his desk, alone, because asking a colleague to join him would require a level of premeditation neither party has budgeted for. In Lagos, a mid-level analyst is currently three helpings into a shared plate of jollof rice at his aunt's cousin's engagement lunch, an event he did not RSVP to because RSVPing is not really how it works. Both men will tell you, sincerely, that they eat well. Both are correct, in ways that have almost nothing to do with each other — which is the entire premise of moving somewhere for work and discovering that "food culture" was never really about food.

#### 🇳🇿 New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether a restaurant is BYO (bring your own wine) — common, and considerably cheaper than the wine list | Assume a tip is expected; a service charge is rarely added and rounding up is plenty |
| Brace for supermarket sticker shock, especially on dairy, eggs, and anything out of season | Expect deep spice aisles or tropical produce at every store — the Foodstuffs/Woolworths duopoly keeps small-town range narrow |
| Bring a bottle of wine or a plate of food if invited to someone's home | Overstay; many casual Kiwi dinners wind down early, and lingering can read as presumptuous |
| Ask colleagues directly if you want lunch company — it's normal to eat solo | Expect a shared team lunch to be a daily ritual; desk lunches are the default, not an insult |
| Confirm dietary needs before hosting — vegetarian, vegan, and gluten-free are thoroughly mainstream | Assume a verbal "let's grab a coffee sometime" is a binding commitment |
| Try hunting, fishing, or foraged food if invited — it's a genuine point of local pride | Treat a "she'll be right" answer about timing literally; confirm the actual hour |

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept food or drink offered by a host, even a token portion, as a matter of basic respect | Refuse outright; a flat "no" can read as rejecting the host's hospitality itself |
| Eat and pass food with your right hand at traditional meals | Use your left hand for eating or serving — it carries hygienic, not culinary, associations |
| Wash your hands before a communal meal; a bowl of water is often brought to the table | Dig into a shared bowl before the host or elder signals it's time |
| Haggle gently at food markets — prices are a starting position, not a fact | Expect fixed menus or app delivery in every neighbourhood; cash and negotiation still dominate outside upscale areas |
| Treat long lunches as where business relationships actually get built | Schedule back-to-back meetings assuming a shared meal will be quick |
| Tip discreetly at upscale Lagos restaurants for good service | Assume tipping is expected at informal food stalls — it generally isn't |

### New Zealand

New Zealanders spend roughly NZD 240 a week feeding an average household, a figure that Rabobank and food-rescue charity KiwiHarvest reported in 2025 as having barely moved from 2023 despite years of food inflation — a stagnation that says less about restraint than about how little room the budget had left to give. Part of the pressure traces to structure: New Zealand's grocery sector is effectively a duopoly, with Foodstuffs and Woolworths controlling around 90 percent of the market, which keeps prices firm and choice, particularly outside the main centres, narrower than the country's culinary reputation suggests. According to FAO food balance data, New Zealanders now consume roughly 3,122 kilocalories per person per day, a diet that has quietly swapped its mid-century staples of milk and butter for poultry and vegetable oils — less a revolution than fifty years of the same national sandwich getting slowly re-filled.

What the statistics don't capture is the social architecture around the meal itself. Lunch, for a great many salaried New Zealanders, is a private, faintly transactional affair — a flat white here, a sandwich at the desk there — reflecting a culture Hofstede Insights scores as low on power distance and comparatively high on indulgence, prioritising personal leisure over collective obligation. That translates, in practice, to more freedom and considerably less scheduled communal eating than a newcomer might expect from a country so fond of the outdoor barbecue.

### Nigeria

Nigeria's food economy tells almost the inverse story. The National Bureau of Statistics' Nigeria Living Standard Survey found that food accounts for 56.65 percent of average household expenditure — more than half of what a family earns, spent simply on eating, a proportion that dwarfs anything comparable in Wellington or Auckland. The IMF has separately estimated that roughly 40 percent of the population is food insecure, a reminder that the exuberant hospitality culture described below coexists with real economic strain, not in spite of it. FAO data lists maize, rice, and cassava as the country's dietary backbone, dressed up into regional specialities — jollof rice, suya, pounded yam and stews — that vary enormously across the country's roughly 500 ethnic groups.

Socially, food in Nigeria functions less as sustenance and more as a running commentary on relationship and status, consistent with Hofstede's scoring of the country as high on power distance and firmly "masculine" in its emphasis on visible achievement. Refusing an offered meal is not read as a dietary choice but as a small social rupture; a shared plate carries obligations a Kiwi lunch simply does not.

### The Reckoning

Put plainly: New Zealand treats a meal as an individual's business, occasionally interrupted by friends. Nigeria treats a meal as a collective's business, occasionally interrupted by the individual who was supposed to be having it alone. Neither instinct is irrational — one is the product of a small, wealthy, sparsely populated economy that can afford privacy; the other of a densely networked society where food-sharing has long functioned as an informal social safety net that state systems have not fully replaced.

The financial contrast sharpens the point. A New Zealander spending NZD 240 a week on food is spending a moderate, fairly stable share of a high income. A Nigerian household spending 56.65 percent of its budget on food is spending nearly everything, on something that is also, simultaneously, an act of generosity toward relatives, colleagues, and unannounced guests. The Kiwi desk lunch and the Lagos extended-family plate are, in that sense, mirror-image solutions to entirely different problems — one optimised for autonomy, the other for resilience.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A respondent noted that New Zealand's food scene has grown noticeably spicier and more diverse over the past two decades, crediting immigration from South and Southeast Asia and the Pacific for pushing what was once a fairly bland, British-inflected national palate toward something considerably more adventurous.*

> *Six Miles Away (expat blog) — A German expat who settled in New Zealand described genuine grocery-store sticker shock, noting that imported New Zealand kiwifruit is sometimes cheaper to buy back home in Europe than in New Zealand itself, and that locals lean on hunting, fishing, and home gardens to soften the cost of an isolated island's food supply.*

> *Mumsnet — A contributor relocating to Nigeria was advised that refusing offered food outright is a serious social misstep, and that the polite script actually runs the other way: a host expects a guest to demur once before accepting, treating the initial "no" as courtesy rather than an actual answer.*

> *My Burnt Orange (expat blog) — A food blogger who relocated her family from London to Lagos wrote that the expat community there formed quickly and closely, partly out of shared need, and that settling into the city's food and social rhythms took active effort rather than happening automatically.*

> *Quora — A respondent explained that eating with the right hand and washing hands at the table before a communal meal remain widely observed traditions in Nigeria, description that first read to outsiders as a formality but functions socially as the literal precondition for being allowed to eat.*

## Conclusion

The practical advice, distilled: in New Zealand, budget more than you'd expect for groceries, don't take a solo desk lunch personally, and don't assume a dinner invitation comes with a fixed end time. In Nigeria, accept what's offered, mind which hand you use, and treat the lunch meeting as the actual meeting, not a break from it. Neither country's approach to food is more "correct" than the other's — they're simply optimised for different scarcities, one of money, one of solitude.

The honest summary for anyone weighing the move: New Zealand will feed you well and leave you alone to do it; Nigeria will feed you well and make sure you're never doing it alone — and only one of those arrangements will feel like a relief on your worst day, though which one depends entirely on what kind of day you've been having.
