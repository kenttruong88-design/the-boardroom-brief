**The Job for Life vs. the Job for Now: Jordan and Canada Disagree About What Loyalty Is For**

**🇯🇴 Jordan · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Amman, the dream job for a huge share of young people is still a government post or a military commission — not because it's exciting, but because it's safe, and safety is in short supply. In Toronto, 57% of professionals now say changing jobs every few years is actually good for your career, up sharply from just a few years ago. Jordan is chasing stability its economy can't fully supply. Canada is chasing mobility its own CFOs are quietly trying to punish. Neither country's workforce is wrong to want what it wants.

**Jordan: Loyalty Isn't Old-Fashioned, It's a Scarce Resource**

The honest starting point in Jordan is that job security is not a preference, it's a genuine structural anxiety, and the data backs that up directly. A UNICEF-backed study on Jordanian youth transitions, reported by The Jordan Times, found young Jordanians strongly favour the public sector specifically "due to its job security and better working conditions" — women gravitating toward teaching roles, men toward the military — but "only a small percentage of young people were able to obtain such jobs." Just 14% of employed young Jordanian women work in the public sector at all, fewer than three in four young men hold work lasting beyond six months, and of the 60% of Jordanian women who pursue higher education (compared with 40% of men), only about 35% of female degree-holders go on to land a job. The study frames much of this bluntly: "the jobs that young people aspire to have are unattainable," with knock-on effects on delayed marriage and financial independence.

Personal networks fill some of the gap that formal hiring structures leave open. Academic research on wasta — the Arabic term for personal connections that shape hiring, especially in banking and public-sector allocation — documents it as a durable, if gradually contested, feature of Jordanian employment, where strict anti-nepotism policy is absent. A 2022 regional survey of 2,473 respondents across 16 Arab countries, covered by The Jordan Times, found workplace environment is the single strongest hiring draw (68%, ahead of salary at 64%), that training and long-term development are the top retention drivers after hire, and that 68% of respondents have stayed with their current employer more than two years — Ola Haddad of Bayt.com noted plainly that "a large number of Jordanian professionals hold their current workplace in high regard." Riya, an Indian software engineer working in Amman, captured the social texture of that loyalty in a first-person account published by GlobalIndian.com, reassuring a newly arrived colleague at a company lunch: "Amman can feel overwhelming, but you're doing great. Soon this will feel like home" — the kind of workplace warmth that, multiplied across a tenure-heavy labour market, helps explain why people stay even when formal mobility is scarce.

**Canada: Mobility Is the Strategy, Not the Symptom**

Canada's workforce has moved in almost the opposite direction, and fairly quickly. A Robert Half survey found 57% of Canadian professionals believe changing roles every few years can be beneficial to a career — up 14 percentage points from a similar survey roughly four years earlier — with higher pay cited as the clearest upside. The generational and education splits are sharp: 63% of workers aged 18-34 see the benefit, versus 52% of those 55 and older, and workers with an undergraduate degree or higher are the most convinced of all, at 68%. Greg Scileppi of Robert Half's international staffing operations summarised the shift plainly: "many employers are cautious when considering candidates who make frequent moves," even as workers themselves increasingly see mobility as the rational play.

That caution is doing real work on the hiring side, and it's worth taking seriously before assuming job-hopping carries no cost. The same research found 59% of CFOs said they would be reluctant to hire someone with a visible history of frequent job changes, rising to 80% specifically at companies with more than 1,000 employees — a meaningful gap between how workers and senior finance leaders read the exact same résumé pattern. Professionals generally judge five job changes in 10 years as reasonable; CFOs draw their own line further out, at eight. The honest nuance is that "flight risk" (named by 42% of workers as job-hopping's single biggest drawback) is a two-sided cultural negotiation still being actively renegotiated, not a settled consensus either way.

There's a structural-values layer worth naming directly, and it cuts in a genuinely interesting direction here. Hofstede Insights scores Canada as strongly individualist (80), a number that tracks cleanly with a labour market where career decisions are framed as personal strategy rather than collective obligation to one employer. Jordan has no separately published individual Hofstede country profile — a real research gap, not a cultural judgment — and while neighbouring Arab-world cluster scores commonly run collectivist, the specific reference source checked for this piece doesn't explicitly list Jordan among the countries it covers, so that inference is flagged here rather than asserted as fact. What the Jordanian survey data does show directly, though, is a workforce whose loyalty looks less like deference to hierarchy and more like genuine appreciation for a stable, well-regarded workplace in an economy where that stability is scarce enough to be worth protecting.

**The Reckoning**

The real contrast isn't which workforce values loyalty more — both clearly do, just for different reasons. Jordanian loyalty is substantially a function of scarcity: formal, secure jobs (especially public-sector ones) are hard enough to get that staying put, once you have one, is a rational response to real structural risk. Canadian mobility is substantially a function of surplus: a labour market where moving roles every few years genuinely can produce higher pay is one where the risk calculus runs the opposite way, even as a sizeable chunk of senior hiring decision-makers quietly disagree with their own employees about whether that's smart.

Put differently: a Jordanian professional staying at one employer for years isn't necessarily choosing loyalty over ambition — they may simply be holding onto something genuinely difficult to replace. A Canadian professional changing jobs every two years isn't necessarily choosing ambition over loyalty — they may just be operating in a market where that's the only version of loyalty (to their own career, rather than to one employer) that currently pays.

### Conclusion

If you're deciding which system to build a career inside, the honest framing isn't "which country values commitment more," it's "which kind of risk you'd rather carry." In Jordan, the risk is scarcity — once you land a genuinely good job, especially in the public sector, walking away from it voluntarily is a much bigger gamble than it would be elsewhere, because the next equally good opportunity may simply not exist on a reasonable timeline. In Canada, the risk is perception — you can move as often as the market rewards you for moving, but you're betting that the hiring manager reading your résumé next time is one of the 41% who doesn't quietly dock you for it.

My honest line for a friend: in Jordan, don't mistake a long tenure for a lack of ambition — it's frequently the opposite, a hard-won foothold worth protecting. In Canada, don't assume every employer has caught up to how normal job-hopping has become among workers themselves — ask a prospective manager directly how they feel about your résumé's gaps and jumps, because plenty of them, especially at bigger companies, still haven't moved on from the old rule.
