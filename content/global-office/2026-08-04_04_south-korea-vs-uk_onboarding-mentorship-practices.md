# Assigned a Senior vs. Assigned a Buddy: Onboarding in South Korea and the UK

**🇰🇷 South Korea · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In South Korea, your first month at a new job comes with a designated senior colleague — a sunbae — who is informally on the hook for your entire integration, from unwritten office etiquette to who not to sit next to at the year-end dinner. In the UK, your first month comes with a "buddy," a laminated induction pack, and a calendar invite for a coffee catch-up that may or may not happen. Both countries call this mentorship. Only one of them treats it as a binding social contract.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn who your designated sunbae is in the first week and treat that relationship as a real professional obligation, not a formality | Skip a hoesik (team dinner) without a genuine, communicated reason — no-shows are noticed and remembered |
| Use both hands when receiving a drink or document from a senior colleague — small gestures of deference matter more than they first appear | Pour your own drink at a hoesik before offering to pour for seniors first — the ritual has a specific order |
| Expect long hours in your first months as part of proving reliability, even as the legal workweek has tightened | Assume South Korea's declining average working hours (1,833 in 2025, per OECD data) means a light early tenure — the country still logs roughly 300 more hours a year than the UK |
| Ask your sunbae directly for guidance on unwritten norms — this is expected and welcomed, not a sign of weakness | Bypass your sunbae to ask senior leadership questions directly; it can be read as skipping the chain of trust |
| Reciprocate as you gain seniority — sunbae-hoobae is a two-way obligation, not a one-time favor | Treat the relationship as purely functional; genuine rapport (jeong) is part of what makes the system work |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat your assigned "buddy" or mentor relationship as opt-in — the best outcomes come from you scheduling time proactively | Wait passively for mentorship to happen organically; UK onboarding is often structured on paper but informally executed in practice |
| Use graduate scheme rotations (common at larger UK employers) to build a network across departments, not just your immediate team | Assume a single mentor will manage your entire integration; UK mentorship tends to be lighter-touch and less centralized than Korea's sunbae model |
| Ask direct questions about unwritten office norms — dress code drift, meeting etiquette — since inductions rarely cover them explicitly | Expect the induction pack to substitute for real relationship-building; 87% of firms with active mentorship programmes report faster new-hire ramp-up, but plenty of firms have the scheme on paper only |
| Be self-directed about requesting feedback in your early weeks — UK managers often wait to be asked rather than volunteering it | Interpret a quiet first few weeks as disinterest; UK workplaces frequently give new hires space to self-orient before checking in |
| Attend informal social events (post-work drinks, team lunches) even briefly — this is where a lot of real onboarding happens in the UK | Assume socializing is optional for integration purposes; skipping it entirely slows relationship-building even if no one says so directly |

### 🇰🇷 South Korea

South Korean onboarding runs through a formal-informal hybrid structure built on the sunbae-hoobae (senior-junior) system, which organizes relationships at every level of a Korean workplace. When a new employee arrives, they are typically paired with a specific senior colleague responsible for their integration in the fullest sense — not just job function, but company culture, the personalities of key figures, and the tacit knowledge that separates competence from fluency. Crucially, this is understood as reciprocal: the sunbae owes the hoobae real guidance and protection, not just correction.

Hoesik — after-work team dinners — function as the primary onboarding venue outside the desk itself, and the etiquette is genuinely ritualized: pouring drinks for seniors with both hands, receiving them the same way, and treating attendance as close to mandatory in a new hire's first months. This sits alongside a work-hours culture that, while easing, remains demanding: South Korea logged 1,833 average annual working hours in 2025, per OECD-tracked data reported by the Korea Herald and Korea Times, still roughly 97 hours above the OECD average and the sixth-longest in the bloc. For a new arrival, the mentorship structure and the hours culture are tightly linked — the sunbae relationship exists partly to help a hoobae survive the demands of that schedule.

### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

UK onboarding tends toward a lighter, more self-directed model. Larger employers frequently run structured graduate schemes — often 12-month rotational programmes, as seen at organizations like UK Government Investments — that pair new hires with formal mentors and build career-planning support directly into the first year. Industry data suggests 87% of businesses running active onboarding mentorship programmes believe new-hire ramp-up is measurably accelerated, and inductions are generally treated as separate from the deeper cultural onboarding — inductions cover logistics and policy, while onboarding proper is meant to integrate someone into culture and relationships over a longer runway.

In practice, though, the UK's version of mentorship is considerably less binding than Korea's. There's no equivalent social penalty for skipping an optional coffee catch-up, and the relationship is generally framed as a resource to be used rather than an obligation to be honored. British workplace reserve compounds this: colleagues are often polite and welcoming without being especially proactive about checking in, which means a new hire who waits to be onboarded, rather than asking direct questions, can drift for weeks with only a vague sense of where they stand.

### The Reckoning

The core difference is obligation versus opportunity. Korea's sunbae-hoobae system creates a mentorship relationship with real social weight attached — showing up, reciprocating, and observing hierarchy are not optional extras, they're how trust gets built at all. The UK's mentor-buddy system creates an opportunity that a self-directed new hire can use well or barely use at all, with comparatively little social cost either way.

The counterintuitive twist is which country produces faster real integration. Korea's system, demanding as it is, tends to embed a new hire into the social fabric of a team quickly and thoroughly, because the structure leaves little room to opt out. The UK's lighter-touch system can leave capable new hires professionally competent but socially peripheral for months, simply because nothing forces the relationship-building to happen — it has to be chosen, repeatedly, by someone still learning the unwritten rules well enough to know what to choose.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/korea — A foreign hire at a Korean firm described their first hoesik as the moment the job actually started to make sense — three rounds of soju and a karaoke session revealed more about the internal team dynamics than two weeks of official onboarding documents had.*

> *Quora — Someone who relocated to Seoul for a corporate role wrote that the hardest early lesson wasn't the language but learning that their sunbae's casual-sounding suggestions during lunch were, functionally, instructions — treating them as optional advice caused real friction.*

> *Internations — A British professional who moved to a UK graduate scheme after working in Asia noted that the biggest surprise was how little anyone checked in after week one; the onboarding "buddy" system existed on paper, but real integration depended entirely on how many coffees she personally organized.*

> *teamblind.com — A contributor comparing early-career experiences across markets observed that Korean hierarchy, while demanding, at least made expectations explicit, whereas UK workplace politeness could leave a new hire genuinely unsure for months whether they were meeting the bar.*

> *Quora — A respondent addressing life as a foreigner working in Korea said the biggest adjustment was realizing that declining a hoesik invitation without a very good, clearly communicated reason had social consequences that lingered well past the event itself.*

### Conclusion

Anyone moving into a Korean workplace should treat the sunbae relationship as one of the most important professional assets they'll build in the first year — invest in it deliberately, attend the dinners, and reciprocate once you're senior enough to. Anyone moving into a UK workplace should assume nobody is going to chase them down with support, and should schedule their own coffees, ask their own blunt questions, and not mistake British politeness for disinterest. The honest version: Korea will hand you a mentor whether you want one or not, and the UK will hand you a mentor only if you remember to actually pick them up.
