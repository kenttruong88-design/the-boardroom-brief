# Poland Lets You Quit Every Two Years; Czechia Merely Wonders Why You Haven't Left Already

🇵🇱 Poland · 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Polish employee under 25 is more likely than not to have changed employers in the past two years. A Czech employee, according to Randstad, is less likely than the global average to quit an unsatisfactory job, and tends to remain at the same desk, privately dissatisfied. Both countries sit in the same region and have arrived at rather different temperaments about the question of whether to stay. For anyone planning a move, the difference is practical rather than philosophical: it determines how your CV is read, how your notice period feels, and whether your colleagues regard your arrival as a hire or a loan.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat two to three years in a role as the polite minimum, and one year as the absolute floor. | List three four-month stints without a one-line explanation ready. |
| Prepare a growth-based answer for any short tenure: what you learned, why the move was deliberate. | Say you left because the manager was impossible; Poland is a small professional world. |
| Check the notice period in your contract: it can run from two weeks to three months depending on service. | Resign before your new employer's paperwork is complete if you hold a work permit. |
| Negotiate salary at each move; changing employers is the market's accepted raise mechanism. | Wait for an annual raise to arrive on its own. |
| Keep former employers warm; careers in a given sector cross repeatedly. | Burn a bridge on your last day for the satisfaction of it. |

### In Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a two-month notice period as the common default and plan your start date around it. | Promise a new employer you can start in two weeks. |
| Ask where the role leads in one to three years; career growth is what Czech staff say they lack. | Assume a good salary will substitute for a visible path. |
| If you hold a non-EU employee card, learn the change-of-employer rules before you resign. | Quit first and sort out the Ministry of the Interior afterwards. |
| Show steadiness in interviews; emphasise reliability and depth over variety. | Present a rapid sequence of moves as evidence of ambition. |
| Treat quiet dissatisfaction as a signal worth acting on early, including your own. | Mistake a colleague's silence for contentment. |

## Poland

Polish loyalty is conditional, and the conditions are published annually. According to Gi Group Holding's Labour Market Barometer 2026, based on an employee survey run in February and March, 67 percent of Polish workers stayed with the same employer over the previous two years. That leaves a third who did not, and the distribution is steep. Some 55 percent of workers aged 18 to 24 changed employers, about a fifth of them more than once, against 33.5 percent of those aged 25 to 44 and 20 percent of those aged 45 to 54. The public sector held on to its people best, with 13.5 percent changing employer; transport and logistics lost half of theirs. Among those earning more than PLN 10,000 net a month, 51 percent moved. Mobility, in other words, rises with youth and with bargaining power, which is the sort of finding that economists describe as a labour market and everyone else describes as a Tuesday.

Yet the same culture retains a faint suspicion of the serial mover. A LiveCareer Poland survey of 1,358 people, reported by CareersinPoland.com in 2024, found that 84 percent had changed jobs at least once. About 40 percent of the employed wanted a new job, but 36 percent of those cited a lack of better offers, 26 percent feared financial instability, and only 14 percent cited loyalty to their employer. Loyalty, then, ranks behind a weak market and a healthy savings anxiety as a reason to remain. Recruiters nonetheless keep a ledger. Devire, a Polish recruitment firm, advises candidates to stay two to three years to look credible. A Substack essayist who has worked in Europe for about nine years reports that in Poland a stay under two years is regarded as questionable, with some people clocking two or three months just to reach a year. A Polish commenter on Hacker News took the opposite line in 2022, writing that moving every few years is probably the best strategy and carries no stigma on a CV. Both can be right, depending on whether the recruiter is under thirty-five.

Exit has a statutory price list. According to the Eurofound legislation database, the notice period for an ordinary Polish employment contract is two weeks for service under six months, one month from six months, and three months from three years. The longer the stay, the more expensive the departure, which supplies loyalty with a financial argument it might otherwise lack. Foreigners should add the paperwork: an Expat.com thread shows members trading their own understanding of voivode notification deadlines and residence card reapplication windows after leaving a job, with the sensible caveat that these are forum recollections and not guidance.

## Czechia

Czechia presents a quieter variant. The Wellbeing Index 2025, compiled by the portals JenPráce.cz and JenFirmy.cz from more than 10,000 employee evaluations and reported by Expats.cz, found that career development scored 2.97 out of 5, well behind job variety at 3.68. Czech employees leave, the report concludes, when growth stalls, not when the work itself disappoints, and salary alone does not buy loyalty. The authors call the resulting condition silent dissatisfaction: staff who stay, do not complain, and depart abruptly when an outside offer appears. A separate Up Benefity survey of more than 1,000 employees, reported by Expats.cz in January 2026, found that only 17 percent would recommend their workplace to friends and 52 percent would not. A workforce that will not recommend its employer and also does not leave is a population waiting for a reason.

The Randstad Workmonitor 2023, reported by Expats.cz, supports the temperament. Czechs were less likely than the global average to quit an unsatisfactory job, and 36 percent admitted to doing the minimum, against 31 percent worldwide. They were also less moved by wage anxiety: only 28 percent wanted a monthly cost-of-living bonus, the lowest of the groups surveyed, while Poland topped the table at 54 percent. A Reddit thread on Prague pay and living costs shows the same restraint. Commenters describe a country where IT and engineering salaries are competitive and ordinary jobs are hard to live on, and where people stay for tax advantages and talk about leaving only if those change. That thread concerns wages, not tenure, but its tone is recognisable: a calculation made quietly, with no announcement.

Formalities reinforce the pace. The Expat.com guide to working in Prague gives two months as the most common notice period, applying to both sides. For non-EU nationals the arrangement is stricter. Foreigners.cz notes that an employee-card holder generally needs to notify the Ministry of the Interior at least 30 days before a change takes effect, with an additional approval step if the card lacks free labour market access, a holding period of six months, and a ban on moving to an agency. Older Czech data gives an idea of the culture: Profesia.cz found in 2013 that CVs listed an average of 2.3 previous jobs, and HR News argued in 2016 that long tenure was no longer prized. Both are dated, and I could not find a current national tenure series, so treat them as colour rather than measurement.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, the countries differ less in how often people want to leave than in how openly they do it. Poland has a visible, age-graded job market in which changing employers is the accepted route to a raise, supported by recruiters who tolerate it up to a point. Czechia has a slower, more private market in which people are dissatisfied with development prospects but behave as if exit were an eccentricity. Hofstede Insights scores Poland at 93 for uncertainty avoidance against 74 for Czechia, and 68 for power distance against 57. Poland's score, which I read from a secondary page reproducing Hofstede data, suggests a population that dislikes ambiguity and changes employers regardless, which hints that the Barometer figures reflect incentives more than nerves.

The practical implication is that a Polish employer will judge your CV on the arithmetic of your tenure, while a Czech employer will judge your intentions on the quality of your explanation. In Poland you may leave quickly and be asked about it; in Czechia you may leave slowly and be asked nothing, which can be worse. Neither country pays for loyalty directly. Poland rewards the move, and Czechia rewards the patience to wait for the right one.

## Conclusion

If you move to Poland, plan to change employers, ideally with a rationale prepared and a notice period in mind. The data suggests that most young professionals do, that recruiters accept two to three years as a respectable stay, and that the market rewards negotiation at the point of exit. Keep your references warm, read the contract for notice lengths, and if you hold a permit, line up the paperwork before the resignation.

If you move to Czechia, plan for a longer arc: a two-month notice period, a quieter career conversation, and a workforce that will tell you what is wrong only after it has accepted another offer. Ask early where the role leads, and ask your manager what the next three years look like. Either way, the choice between loyalty and mobility turns out to be mostly a choice between being asked to explain yourself at the interview and being asked at the exit, and the exit has the better catering.
