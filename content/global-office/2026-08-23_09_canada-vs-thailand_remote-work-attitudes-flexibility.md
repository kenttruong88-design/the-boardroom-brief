# Two Ways to Answer an Email at 9 P.M.: Canada Legislates It, Thailand Just Lets You Refuse

🇨🇦 Canada · 🇹🇭 Thailand

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Ontario, an employer with 25 or more staff is legally required to have a written policy explaining whether you must answer emails after hours — the law does not say you can ignore them, only that someone must write down the rules. In Thailand, an amendment to the Labour Protection Act simply gives remote employees the right to refuse after-hours contact outright, no policy memo required. Two countries, two philosophies: one drafts a committee-approved document about your evening; the other just tells your boss to wait until morning.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read your employer's disconnecting-from-work policy before you assume you're off the hook at 6 p.m. | Assume "remote-friendly" job ads still apply once the return-to-office memo lands |
| Expect hybrid to mean a specific number of anchor days, tracked by badge swipes | Show up to a "flexible" hybrid role and skip the office days no one enforced last quarter |
| Negotiate remote terms in writing before you sign, especially in banking, insurance, or government | Assume your predecessor's fully-remote arrangement is grandfathered in |
| Budget for a commute creeping back into your week, especially in Ottawa-Gatineau, Toronto, or Vancouver | Rent an apartment based on a zero-commute assumption |
| Ask which meetings are camera-on by default | Multitask visibly on video calls; Canadian managers notice more than they let on |

#### 🇹🇭 Thailand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm whether your visa track actually permits the work you're doing (foreign-income remote work versus a Thai employer) | Assume a tourist or DTV visa lets you freelance for Thai clients without a work permit |
| Learn the after-hours refusal right exists — and use it sparingly at first | Invoke it in week one before your manager knows your work ethic |
| Treat "we'll see" and vague timelines as normal scheduling, not evasion | Chase a firm answer in a group chat where someone would have to lose face to give it |
| Use coworking spaces in Bangkok or Chiang Mai to build a routine outside your apartment | Expect the same fixed-desk office culture you left behind in Canada |
| Let a senior colleague speak first in meetings, even video ones | Correct a superior's mistake in front of the group, even gently |

Canada's remote-work story since the pandemic is one of steady, statistically documented retreat. According to Statistics Canada's Labour Force Survey, 18.7 percent of employed Canadians worked mostly from home as of May 2024, down from a 2021 peak of 24.3 percent, with the share working exclusively outside the home climbing back to 77.6 percent. Among the hybrid holdouts, 55.8 percent now spend at least half their working hours on-site, a four-point jump in a single year, and public administration — the sector that arguably proved remote work could function at scale — has swung hardest, with 61.5 percent of hybrid public servants now mostly in the office. The OECD's 2024/2025 Survey of Public Servants across member states found the same pattern playing out in government workforces generally: remote arrangements survive on paper more often than they survive contact with a new head of department.

What makes Canada distinctive is not that it is retreating from flexibility, but that it is legislating the retreat's edges. Ontario's Working for Workers Act, in force since June 2022, does not grant a right to disconnect so much as force employers to write down whatever policy they already had, a peculiarly Canadian solution: regulate the paperwork, leave the substance to the employer. The effect is a workplace culture where flexibility is negotiated, documented, and — increasingly, per the banking and public sectors — reversed by memo.

Thailand's approach reads almost like a rebuttal. The 2023 amendment to the Labour Protection Act gives remote workers a statutory right to refuse employer contact once normal hours end or an assigned task is complete, full stop, without the Canadian intermediate step of a written employer policy explaining the rule. This sits inside a labour framework that is otherwise stricter on paper than Canada's: standard hours are capped at 8 per day and 48 per week under Sections 23-25 of the Act, work in hazardous conditions is capped further, and Thailand's ILO-aligned overtime provisions require pay at 1.5 to 3 times the hourly wage. On paper, Thailand looks more rule-bound. In practice, the country's appeal to remote workers has little to do with domestic labour law and everything to do with the Destination Thailand Visa, which crossed 35,000 applicants within a year of its 2024 launch and permits up to 180 days per entry across a five-year term — provided the income is foreign-sourced, a distinction plenty of newcomers miss.

The Reckoning is where the two countries invert expectations. Canada, the wealthier, more "flexible-sounding" economy by reputation, is the one where remote work is shrinking under measurable, dated pressure from employers who once championed it. Thailand, associated in the popular imagination with beach laptops and DTV Instagram posts, is the one with an explicit statutory right to log off — a protection Canadian workers in six provinces still don't have in any form, let alone one with teeth.

The catch is that Thailand's flexibility is largely a foreigner's flexibility. The right to disconnect and the digital nomad visa apply cleanly to someone earning income from outside Thailand; working for a Thai employer inside the domestic system means Thai colleagues, Thai hierarchy, and the standard 48-hour week with all its face-saving formalities intact. Canada's flexibility, meanwhile, is shrinking but still legally anchored — a documented policy beats no policy, even a grudging one. Hofstede Insights data captures part of why: Canada scores 80 on individualism against Thailand's marked collectivism, which is a tidy way of explaining why a Canadian workplace resolves disputes over remote access through a written policy and a Thai one resolves them through consensus, hierarchy, and rarely saying no directly.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — a Big Five bank employee described feeling blindsided when a fully-remote arrangement, informally tolerated for over a year, was reversed by a return-to-office memo with weeks of notice and no extra desk space to accommodate the recalled staff.*

> *Hacker News — a commenter working remotely from Thailand for non-Thai clients noted the legal ambiguity was more about who your employer and clients are than about the country itself, and that most digital nomads never actually get asked to prove their income source.*

> *Quora — a respondent who relocated from Canada described the pleasant surprise of Chiang Mai's coworking infrastructure, noting there was nothing about Thailand that made remote work logistically harder than staying home, with WiFi as reliable as anywhere.*

> *Quora — a thread on Thai office culture flagged the misread most newcomers make: mistaking group harmony and a reluctance to disagree openly for consensus, when it often just means nobody wanted to be the one to say no in front of a senior colleague.*

> *Quora — a Canadian planning the move was advised to expect dual tax-filing complexity, since Thailand taxes residents on worldwide income while Canadian residency ties can persist depending on how cleanly you cut them, a detail that rarely makes it into visa blog posts.*

### Conclusion

The practical read for anyone comparing these two on remote-work terms alone: Canada offers structure, enforceable minimums, and a shrinking window in which to use them, while Thailand offers a genuinely strong disconnect right paired with a visa system that rewards precise paperwork over improvisation. Neither is the fantasy sold in recruiting decks — Canada's flexibility is being renegotiated downward in real time, and Thailand's is contingent on staying legally outside the domestic employment system you're living inside. Choose based on which bureaucracy you'd rather argue with: the one that makes your employer write a policy, or the one that makes you prove your income never touched the country you're living in.
