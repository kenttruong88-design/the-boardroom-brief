**Ankara, Kente, and the Diplomatic Incident Waiting to Happen in Accra**

🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇳🇬 Nigeria

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Tell a Ghanaian that Nigerians dress better and you will start a small war; tell a Nigerian the reverse and you will start the same war facing the other direction. In 2024 a Ghanaian blogger's wife made exactly this claim in public, a Ghanaian musician pushed back publicly, and the entertainment press covered it as news — which it was, because in both countries, what you wear to the office on a Friday is not a style choice. It is a position statement.

Neither rivalry is new, and neither is purely vanity. Fashion in both countries functions as a visible, continuously updated signal of how someone is actually doing — financially, socially, professionally — in a way that a quieter economy might leave to a bank statement instead. A new arrival who treats dress as a private matter of comfort, rather than a public one of standing, will find themselves quietly recalibrated by colleagues within the first month, usually without anyone saying so directly.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear cotton or linen — heat makes synthetic fabric genuinely unbearable by midday | Assume high heels are a safe daily choice — broken pavement and potholes make them impractical outside upscale venues |
| Mix traditional pieces (boubou, head-wraps) freely with Western dress | Assume Accra's eclectic style is the norm nationwide — rural and northern dress conventions are more conservative |
| Check dress codes at upscale restaurants and bars before assuming flip-flops are fine | Wear flip-flops to a venue that clearly expects more — "chale wote" sandals are everywhere, but not everywhere |
| Pay a premium for quality on key items you'll wear often | Assume cheap market "designer" fabric or sunglasses will last — you get what you pay for |
| Dress modestly at sacred sites and during religious observances | Treat Kente cloth as interchangeable with its mass-printed imitations — genuine woven Kente is heavier, slower-made, and priced accordingly |
| Ask a local colleague before a big meeting what the dress expectation actually is | Guess based on what you saw in a single Accra neighborhood |

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Observe your office's "Friday traditional dress day" if it has one — Ankara prints and Senator suits are the norm | Treat the custom as costume — it's an ongoing institutional practice, not a theme day |
| Get clothing custom-tailored locally — it's affordable and well regarded | Expect a single fitting to nail a pattern you've only shown as a photo — budget for revisions |
| Adapt regionally — hijab and northern fabrics in the Muslim north, modest church dress in the Christian south | Assume one national dress code applies everywhere you travel for work |
| Shop around across Lagos neighborhoods — prices vary enormously | Assume Lagos Island prices (sometimes under $10) apply citywide — "everything is expensive" is the more common complaint elsewhere |
| Buy larger sizes before arrival if you need them | Assume local stock will reliably carry larger sizes — multiple expats report it doesn't |
| Dress conservatively day-to-day — avoid short skirts, shorts, and revealing tops in public and office settings | Pack a wardrobe built only around weekend wear — the formal-casual line is stricter than it looks from outside |

Nigeria has, remarkably, institutionalized the fashion statement into the calendar. Friday is a recognized "traditional dress day" across many offices, schools, and public institutions, per reporting from Guardian Nigeria — a day when Ankara prints, aso-oke-trimmed blouses, and Senator suits replace the Monday-to-Thursday Western formal default. The country has also tried, and failed, to legislate dress nationally: a 2008 "Indecent Dressing" Bill aimed at regulating women's clothing was criticized as a human-rights overreach and did not pass, though institutional dress codes persist at the organizational level — the Nigerian Ports Authority, for instance, maintains rules against spaghetti straps, tight jeans, and scuffed shoes, per AllAfrica. Hofstede Insights data puts Nigeria's Power Distance at 80 and Indulgence at 84 — a combination that reads, in practice, as a culture that takes hierarchy seriously and self-expression just as seriously, often in the same outfit.

Ghana's fashion culture is, by regional consensus, more eclectic and less judgmental — Accra in particular has a reputation, per Culture Trip's reporting, for mixing traditional and Western pieces without anyone treating it as a statement either way. The one verified Hofstede figure available for Ghana is a strikingly low Long-Term Orientation score of 4, reported via StarrFM — a number so extreme that even the secondary literature treats it with some caution, since a separate academic source describes Ghana qualitatively as long-term oriented, a direct contradiction worth flagging rather than resolving by fiat. What both sources agree on is that Ghana and Nigeria each register high on Power Distance, which tracks with a shared cultural instinct: dress according to where you sit in the room, not just what the weather demands.

That instinct also shows up in the economics of the garment trade itself. Academic literature on Ghana's garment sector treats it as a researched economic category in its own right, not a cottage-industry footnote, and the country's long-running distinction between genuine woven Kente — heavier, slower to produce, priced like the craft it is — and the mass-printed imitations sold to tourists and casual buyers alike is treated by locals as a meaningful quality marker, not an aesthetic preference. Getting that distinction visibly wrong, by wearing an obvious imitation to an occasion that calls for the real thing, is a small but real misstep that a first-time visitor to either country can make without realizing it.

### The Reckoning

The genuine irony, surfaced by a Nigerian commenter on a Reddit thread recovered via a reachable mirror, is that the two countries' fashion cultures are closer than either side's rivalry suggests — both lean heavily on Ankara wax-print fabric, differing mainly in what they call the head-wrap (Nigerian "gele" versus Ghanaian "duku"). The fabric itself, worth noting for anyone keeping score in the superiority debate, is of Dutch design inspired by nineteenth-century Ethiopian textiles — not indigenously Ghanaian or Nigerian at all, a fact both countries have quietly agreed to overlook in the interest of national pride.

That shared fabric heritage also complicates any attempt to draw a clean north-south style boundary between the two countries. Nigerian "gele" head-wraps and Ghanaian "duku" serve functionally identical social purposes — marking an occasion as formal, signaling the wearer's status, and giving the tailor a genuine artistic showcase — even though each country insists its version is distinct, more skillful, and somehow more authentically "theirs." A newcomer who praises a Nigerian colleague's gele using the Ghanaian term, or vice versa, will be corrected, warmly but firmly, and should treat the correction as the opening move in what will be a long and good-natured education.

Where the two genuinely diverge is economic: Nigeria's World Happiness Report ranking (106th globally, down a notch) sits above Ghana's (125th), and fashion spending in both cases is less a discretionary indulgence than a visible, closely watched marker of how one is doing — which is precisely why the stakes of "who dresses better" never actually de-escalate.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum, Lagos — Several posters agree the climate decides the wardrobe before any cultural rule does — cotton and linen, no winter wear — but flag that prices vary enormously by neighborhood, from under $10 on Lagos Island to, as one put it, "everything is expensive and over priced" elsewhere in the city.*

> *TalesMag Real Post Report, Lagos — A diplomatic spouse reported that "Nigerians love to dress up" for gatherings, and specifically praised affordable custom and handmade clothing as one of the better surprises of the posting.*

> *TalesMag Real Post Report, Accra — A reporter noted office dress runs to "a lot of ties" but that business casual is broadly acceptable, and that Kente cloth and batik became popular purchases even among non-Ghanaian staff — a pleasant surprise for anyone expecting a stricter line.*

> *Reddit r/AskAnAfrican (via mirror) — A Nigerian commenter, discussing perceptions of Ghana, pointed out the two countries essentially share the same fashion vocabulary — right down to the fabric — and differ mainly in terminology, undercutting the rivalry's premise rather than settling it.*

> *ExpatExchange Q&A (Ghana and Nigeria guides) — Aggregated advice across both country pages converges on a single, slightly deflating piece of wisdom: watch what your actual local colleagues wear before assuming any general rule, Western or otherwise, applies to your specific office.*

### Conclusion

If you take one thing from this: the fashion rivalry is real, the fabric history undercuts it, and the only decision that actually matters for your first month is tactical — get something tailored locally before your first "big" meeting in either city, because showing up underdressed reads less like a style misstep and more like you didn't take the room seriously. Pack for heat, budget for tailoring, and whichever capital you land in, resist the urge to adjudicate the Accra-versus-Lagos debate out loud until you've been there at least a year — you will lose, and everyone will have opinions about your outfit while you do it.

One last practical note worth the space: both cities will happily sell you a version of "local dress" calibrated for tourists rather than colleagues, and the price difference between that version and the real thing is usually obvious the moment a Ghanaian or Nigerian coworker sees it on you. Ask before you buy. It will save you both money and a surprisingly specific kind of embarrassment. Your local colleagues, in both cities, will be flattered you asked rather than annoyed you didn't already know — a small act of humility that does more for your standing in month one than any outfit possibly could.
