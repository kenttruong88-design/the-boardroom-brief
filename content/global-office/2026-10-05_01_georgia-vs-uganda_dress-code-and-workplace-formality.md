**The Suit, the Supra, and the Smart-Casual Trap: Dressing for Work in Georgia and Uganda**

**🇬🇪 Georgia · 🇺🇬 Uganda**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Tbilisi, a khaki-and-button-down combination will get you through most office doors without comment. In Kampala, the same outfit will get you quietly filed under "visitor who does not yet understand the assignment." Both countries will tell you, sincerely, that they are relaxed about clothes. Neither of them means it the way you think.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇪 Georgia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to business casual — collared shirt, tailored trousers — and upgrade to a suit for first meetings with anyone senior | Assume "Georgians dress casually" means jeans are safe for a client meeting; it means jeans are safe for Saturday |
| Use professional titles with surnames until invited to switch to first names | Skip the handshake-and-eye-contact opener; it reads as evasive, not relaxed |
| Save your best outfit for a supra (feast) invitation — it is a business event in disguise | Bring a bottle of wine to a Georgian host and assume you've out-hosted them; you have not, and you will not |
| Translate one side of your business card into Georgian if you can | Rush the small talk before a negotiation — Georgians read haste as a lack of seriousness |
| Dress up for government offices and banks specifically; they run more formal than private-sector startups | Expect a fast "yes" — decisions move up the hierarchy before they move anywhere else |

#### 🇺🇬 Uganda

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pack real office clothes — pressed slacks, button-downs, a pencil skirt and blazer — even for a "casual" internship | Show up in the backpacker uniform you wore on safari; colleagues will register it, politely, as underdressed |
| Expect smart dress in public generally, not just at the office — Ugandans dress up to run errands | Treat shorts and tank tops as neutral daywear; modesty expectations run noticeably higher than in much of Western Europe or North America |
| Budget for dry cleaning and dressier wardrobe basics before you land, not after | Assume a Friday "no-formal-day" policy — Uganda doesn't really run casual Fridays the way the US does |
| Let a Ugandan colleague's compliment ("you dress like one of us now") land as the approval it is | Confuse formal dress with cold formality — a sharp outfit and a warm, relationship-first conversation are not in tension here |
| Reserve your most conservative options for upcountry travel, where norms run more traditional than in Kampala | Pack exclusively for the capital and assume it generalizes to every region |

### Georgia: Dressed for the Supra, Not the Spreadsheet

Georgia's workplace dress code sits closer to continental Europe than its Soviet-era reputation suggests, but the real tell isn't the clothes — it's who's wearing them. Only 55.6% of Georgian employment is informal, according to ILO estimates, meaning nearly half the working population already operates inside the kind of structured office environment that has opinions about collars. The REAB business culture guide for Georgia describes a workplace defined by hierarchy and formality: professional titles paired with surnames, handshakes initiated by the senior party, and eye contact treated as a proxy for trustworthiness rather than aggression.

What throws visitors is the gap between public-casual and business-formal. One American contributor to Tales Mag's Real Post Report on Tbilisi described the local dress code as "B-casual to B-formal depending on your section" — khakis survive in some offices, suits are expected at others, and nobody will warn you which is which before your first meeting. GDP per capita has climbed to roughly $11,574 as of 2026, per Worldometer figures, part of a broader economic rise that has brought Western-style business formality into sectors — banking, government, multinational offices — that didn't exist in their current form a generation ago.

The deeper logic, though, is relational rather than sartorial. Georgians negotiate through subtlety — body language and gesture carry real weight, per REAB's business-culture notes — and the supra, the ritualized feast with its toastmaster and rounds of wine, functions as an actual extension of the negotiation table. Dressing well for one and sloppily for the other is the kind of mistake that gets remembered.

### Uganda: Smart Is the Default Setting

Uganda inverts the Western assumption that formality scales with wealth. With 95.15% of employment still informal by ILO's 2021 measure — against a formal workforce estimated at 2.2 million people, split roughly 1.7 million private-sector and 500,000-plus public-sector jobs — Uganda's office culture is, in a real sense, a minority experience even among people who work. And that minority dresses accordingly sharply. Multiple Tales Mag Real Post Report contributors describe Kampala dress code in near-identical language: "business casual/formal," "everyone is always very put together," and the blunt verdict that "American shabby is never really acceptable."

This isn't a quirk of expat-heavy offices; it reflects a broader social norm. Ugandans, by multiple accounts, dress formally to run errands, not just to clock in. The Muse's field notes from an intern in Kampala describe arriving with backpacker basics and discovering that local colleagues wore "slacks and a button-down shirt for men, a pencil skirt, heels, and blouse for the ladies" as the baseline, not the ceiling — and that a local advisor's highest compliment, on seeing her upgrade her wardrobe, was that she now dressed "like an African." Academic research into Ugandan managerial values (Rarick, Winter & Nickerson) similarly describes a business culture where outward presentation and respect for hierarchy are tightly linked — looking the part is treated as a visible signal that you understand the social contract of the office, not as decoration on top of it.

### The Reckoning

Here's the inversion that trips people up: Georgia's reputation is loosened by its Soviet-casual past and its new digital-nomad economy, while Uganda's reputation is loosened — incorrectly — by assumptions about development and climate. In practice, Uganda's formality floor is higher and more consistently enforced across contexts (public and private) than Georgia's, where formality is sector-dependent and genuinely negotiable once you're inside. Hofstede Insights data places Uganda noticeably higher on power distance than most Western benchmarks, which tracks with a culture where how you present yourself to someone senior is not optional self-expression but a legible marker of respect.

The other divergence is economic structure. Georgia's growing formal sector is absorbing office-dress norms wholesale from the European and multinational companies setting up in Tbilisi; Uganda's formal sector is smaller, more self-contained, and has been setting its own dress expectations — independent of outside validation — for considerably longer. A new arrival who treats "developing economy" as shorthand for "casual" will be wrong in both places, but wrong in different directions.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Tales Mag Real Post Report, Tbilisi — One Foreign Service contributor described the local dress code as genuinely bidirectional: Georgians "dress up and down" with ease, so the real risk for newcomers isn't offending anyone with casual wear, it's misreading which days call for which register — khakis one day, a full suit the next, same office.*

> *Tales Mag Real Post Report, Kampala — A long-term contributor flagged the most common mistake outright: "American shabby is never really acceptable." Several other respondents independently converged on the same two words — "smart" and "put-together" — to describe the local baseline, suggesting it's less a personal opinion than a shared cultural rule.*

> *The Muse — An intern named Meredith described packing for a village posting and leaving her pencil skirt at home, only to find her Ugandan colleagues dressed sharply for the office regardless of context. Her advice to future arrivals was blunt: don't leave the pencil skirt behind, and expect a compliment, not a complaint, when you dress up rather than down.*

> *Euronews, Tbilisi — A digital nomad who has worked remotely across several countries wrote that Georgia's social warmth outpaced the clothes question entirely: after one running-club meetup, she found herself "feted with wine and food by people I've only just met," and absorbed into near-daily group messages — a reminder that in Georgia, the dress code is downstream of the relationship, not the other way around.*

---

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two postings on the basis of "which one will let me dress down," you're asking the wrong question in both cases. Georgia will let you dress down more often, but punishes misreading the few moments when it matters — a government meeting, a supra, a first introduction to someone senior. Uganda won't let you dress down much at all, but the rule is at least consistent: smart, always, no exceptions for casual Fridays or backpacker logic. The honest advice, for either, is the unglamorous kind: pack more structure than you think you'll need, and watch what the most senior person in the room is wearing before you decide the dress code is a suggestion.
