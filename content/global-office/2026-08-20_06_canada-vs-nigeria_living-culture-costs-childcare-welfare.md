# Two Kinds of Expensive: Living Costs in Canada and Nigeria

**🇨🇦 Canada · 🇳🇬 Nigeria**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A single person in Canada spends roughly C$3,300–3,800 a month on average, and the state quietly absorbs a huge share of the risk behind that number — subsidized childcare, universal healthcare, regulated tenancies. A single person in Lagos might spend far less on paper, around £913 a month by recent estimates, but almost every safety net a Canadian budget assumes — healthcare, security, power, water — has to be purchased individually, in cash, from the private market. Nigeria's poverty rate has climbed from 56% in 2023 to 63% in 2025, roughly 140 million people, while food alone now consumes 60 to 70% of many households' monthly income. Two very different definitions of what "affordable" means once you actually move.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for provincial health coverage immediately — it doesn't activate the moment you land in every province | Assume "free healthcare" means fast healthcare; expect months-long waits for a family doctor or specialist |
| Budget for supplemental insurance (dental, vision, prescriptions) at $75–200 CAD/month | Assume provincial insurance covers dental or vision — it typically doesn't |
| Get on childcare waitlists the moment you know you're moving, sometimes before you land | Expect $10-a-day childcare to be available immediately everywhere — Quebec and BC lead, other provinces lag |
| Compare cities carefully — Montreal is meaningfully cheaper than Toronto or Vancouver | Assume "Canada" has one cost of living; provincial and city variation is enormous |
| Budget housing as your largest line item, especially in Toronto or Vancouver | Expect to buy in a major city on an average salary without serious savings first |

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget separately for power (generator/inverter), water, and private security — they're not optional add-ons | Assume advertised rent is the full housing cost; utilities and security can rival rent itself |
| Negotiate rent and understand many landlords require one to two years paid upfront | Expect month-to-month rent norms common elsewhere; upfront lump-sum payment is standard |
| Get private health insurance before arrival — public healthcare infrastructure is inconsistent | Assume informal or public healthcare options will meet expat-level expectations |
| Choose your neighborhood based on real cost tiers: Ikoyi and VI expensive, Mainland considerably cheaper | Assume "Lagos" has one price point — Island and Mainland living costs diverge sharply |
| Build relationships with a reliable local network for logistics, repairs, and information | Rely purely on formal institutions for problems that get solved faster through personal networks |

### Canada: The State as Shock Absorber

Canada's system is built to socialize the biggest risks. Statistics Canada data underpinning most 2026 cost-of-living guides shows housing as the dominant line item — Toronto one-bedrooms averaging around $2,100 a month, Vancouver higher still — but that expense sits inside a broader system where the two other traditional budget-wreckers, healthcare and childcare, are heavily subsidized or capped. Canada's national childcare plan is pushing regulated fees toward CA$10 a day in most provinces by 2025–2026, with British Columbia already there and Ontario capped near $22 a day.

The tradeoff, and it's a real one, is speed and completeness. Provincial insurance doesn't cover dental, vision, or most prescriptions, pushing many households toward supplemental private plans, and finding a family doctor in several provinces now routinely takes months, with a wait of roughly four months minimum to see a specialist being commonly reported. Canada's model doesn't ask you to pay for the whole system yourself, but it does ask you to be patient with it.

### Nigeria: The Individual as Shock Absorber

Nigeria inverts this almost completely. Numbeo and Wise data both show headline living costs well below Canada's in absolute terms, but that headline number is misleading, because so much of what a Canadian budget gets from the state, a Lagos household has to purchase directly. Nairametrics' 2026 analysis of the Lagos housing market describes rent that increasingly "eats the economy" — a minimum-wage worker in Lagos cannot cover rent, food, and transport from that wage alone, which is a structural reason side businesses and informal income have become necessity rather than hustle-culture flavor text.

On top of rent, expat and local budgets alike have to independently fund power (generators or inverters, since grid electricity is unreliable), water, and private security — costs that don't show up in a simple rent comparison but functionally double the real cost of a comfortable residence. ERI's cost-of-living data and multiple Quora accounts describe expat budgets in Lagos running from a modest few hundred dollars a month in the Mainland to well over $3,000-4,000 a year just for housing in Ikoyi or Victoria Island, before any of the infrastructure workarounds are added on top.

### The Reckoning

The paradox is that "cheaper" and "cheaper to live well" are not the same claim, and Nigeria and Canada sit on opposite sides of that gap. Canada's official cost-of-living numbers look higher, but they include services baked into the price — you're not separately shopping for a functioning electrical grid. Nigeria's official numbers look lower, but achieving anything close to Canadian-equivalent reliability — power, water, healthcare, security — requires stacking multiple private purchases most Canadian budgets never have to think about, which means the real comparable cost of living in Lagos, for a household trying to replicate Canadian baseline reliability, closes much of that headline gap.

The other inversion: Canada asks you to trade money for time (you'll wait for care, but it's covered); Nigeria asks you to trade money for certainty (you'll pay more for reliability, but you get it faster). Neither is objectively better — it depends entirely on which kind of scarcity you're more able to tolerate.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone budgeting a move to Lagos on roughly $5,000 a month described the biggest shock not being rent itself, but realizing that comfortable Island living required paying separately, every month, for private security, a backup generator's fuel, and bottled or filtered water — expenses that together nearly matched the rent line.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A tech worker who relocated from the US to Canada posted a widely discussed "warning" thread a year in, describing free healthcare as real for emergencies but nearly unusable for anything routine, citing a multi-month wait just to be assigned a family doctor.*

> *r/PersonalFinanceCanada — A newcomer described childcare in Ontario as functionally a second rent payment before subsidy caps kicked in, and recommended getting on daycare waitlists before finalizing a move date, since some centers had two-year queues.*

> *Internations Lagos — An expat who relocated for an oil and gas role said the advice that stuck with her most wasn't about money at all — it was to build a trusted local network fast, since problems with rent, security, or repairs got solved through personal relationships far faster than through any formal system.*

> *r/expats — One Canadian transplant to Lagos, inverting the usual direction of these comparisons, said the thing that surprised them most was how much faster private healthcare moved in Lagos than public healthcare had moved back home — for a price, but without the months-long wait.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two moves, budget less on the headline number and more on what that number does and doesn't include. Canada's cost of living buys you a state that absorbs risk slowly but completely; Nigeria's cost of living requires you to absorb the risk yourself, quickly, in cash, item by item.

Here's the honest version I'd give a friend: don't compare the sticker price, compare the full stack — housing plus healthcare plus childcare plus the infrastructure you'll have to personally guarantee. Canada is expensive and mostly handles it for you. Nigeria looks cheap right up until you've priced in everything the state isn't doing, at which point it often isn't cheap at all — just differently expensive.
