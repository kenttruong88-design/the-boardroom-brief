**Hong Kong Will Rehash the Same Point for Two Days. Canada Will Just Email You.**

🇭🇰 Hong Kong · 🇨🇦 Canada

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

An advice column in Hong Kong's CPJobs once ran a letter, signed only "Drained," from an employee whose boss's meetings — by the writer's own estimate — consumed roughly two full working days a week once follow-up reporting was included. In Canada, the equivalent complaint doesn't really exist, because the culture's preferred communication tool is the two-business-day email reply, politely late if it has to be, honestly announced if it will be. Both countries will tell you they communicate efficiently. They mean entirely different things by it.

The discrepancy becomes measurable once you line up the official statistics against each other. One OECD-derived figure puts Hong Kong's average working week at a modest 33.2 hours; a separate Census-based analysis from the Hong Kong Confederation of Trade Unions puts a fifth of the entire workforce at 55-hour weeks. Both numbers are real, drawn from different methodologies measuring different populations — but the gap between them is itself a useful metaphor for Hong Kong meeting culture generally: the official account and the lived account rarely describe the same day.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet the most senior person in the room first; stand when someone senior enters | Switch to first names without being explicitly invited to |
| Exchange business cards with both hands immediately after greeting, holding the corners | Pass or receive cards, money, or contracts with one hand |
| Schedule meetings one to two weeks ahead and confirm the day before | Expect a single meeting to produce a final decision — rehashing and multiple rounds are standard, not disorganization |
| Speak slowly, avoid idioms, and expect repeated questions on the same point | Show visible frustration or strong emotion — it risks both parties "losing face" |
| Watch for subtle discomfort cues, like a sharp intake of breath, and rephrase rather than push | Keep your jacket or tie off even at informal-seeming dinners |
| Wait to be shown your seat if you're unsure — seating follows hierarchy | Assume a flat, egalitarian meeting structure just because the agenda looks informal |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Reply to emails within two business days, or say explicitly if you need longer | Apologize repeatedly for imperfect English or seem overly deferential — it reads as a lack of confidence, not politeness |
| Arrive on time, or call ahead with an honest estimate if you're running late | Name-drop connections, titles, or credentials to build credibility — it reads as boastful |
| Let your follow-through speak for you rather than self-promoting in the room | Pressure or guilt-trip someone who declines a request |
| Thank people explicitly for their time at the end of a meeting | Assume national uniformity — workplace norms and labour rules genuinely vary by province |
| Match the conversational register of whoever called the meeting | Get overly personal too quickly in a new working relationship |
| Listen for the actual ask inside a softly-worded piece of feedback | Assume Canadian indirectness means there's no clear answer at all |

Hong Kong's meeting culture is, by most available measures, a direct product of its labour conditions: a Hong Kong Confederation of Trade Unions analysis of Census data, reported by the South China Morning Post, found 20% of the territory's roughly three million employees worked an average 55 hours a week, with security and catering staff most likely to exceed 60. Gallup's most recent figures put employee engagement at just 5% — among the lowest recorded anywhere globally — alongside 45% reporting high stress the previous day and only 39% saying it's currently a good time to find a local job, down ten points year-on-year. Hofstede's figures assign Hong Kong a Power Distance of 68 and Individualism of just 25, a combination that explains both the card-exchange formality and the endurance required to sit through a meeting that exists, in no small part, to be seen happening rather than to conclude anything. A Campaign Asia / PRHK survey of the territory's PR industry specifically names "too many meetings" as a driver of overtime, with most employees reportedly declining to raise the frustration with management at all.

There's also a geographic dimension worth naming: a South China Morning Post opinion piece argues that Hong Kong's status as a financial centre depends specifically on face-to-face meetings being reachable within a fifteen-to-twenty-minute walk or taxi ride — the territory's dense, vertically stacked geography isn't incidental to its meeting culture, it's load-bearing. Remove that geographic compression and much of the rationale for in-person, repetitive, consensus-building meetings loses its practical justification, even if the cultural habit would likely persist regardless.

Canada's version of efficient communication looks almost suspiciously calm by comparison. Statistics Canada's 2024 "Quality of Employment" release found only 8.2% of employed Canadians worked 49 or more hours a week in 2023 — down sharply from a 1994–95 peak of 14.8% — and Gallup's engagement figure sits at 21%, four times Hong Kong's. Hofstede scores Canada at 80 on Individualism and just 39 on Power Distance, a profile that tracks with a workplace culture built around individual accountability and relatively flat communication: you're expected to say what you mean, reasonably quickly, without needing a title to say it. An HR Reporter piece relaying intercultural coach John Edward McGraw's work with recent immigrants captured the friction this creates for newcomers from more hierarchical backgrounds directly: several told him they'd avoided networking with a senior colleague because he "seemed very important and very busy," afraid that approaching him directly would cause offense — a completely reasonable instinct in Hong Kong, and a genuinely counterproductive one in Canada. Statistics Canada's own trend data makes the stakes of that misread clear: the long-hours share has fallen from a 1994–95 peak of 14.8% to 8.2% today, meaning the Canadian workplace a newcomer encounters is, structurally, not even trying to compete with Hong Kong's volume of hours — a hierarchical newcomer who assumes visible overwork earns respect will instead mostly just look like they can't manage their calendar.

### The Reckoning

The striking reversal here is that Hong Kong's meeting culture, for all its formality and length, is often explicitly about avoiding a clean, fast decision — repetition and deference buy time to reach consensus without anyone losing face along the way. Canada's meeting culture, for all its casualness, is built to produce a decision as quickly as courtesy allows, with the "softness" of the delivery doing the social work that Hong Kong's formal ritual does instead. Read one culture's directness as rudeness, or the other's indirectness as evasiveness, and you will misjudge the room every single time.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Glassdoor, Peplink (Hong Kong office) — An engineer described constant visible-screen-focus expectations from management, with bathroom breaks reportedly flagged by other teams' managers, and work frequently produced only to satisfy a manager's own boss rather than for practical use — called the environment "toxic" and said it "brainwashes you."*

> *Glassdoor, HSBC (Hong Kong office) — A sales employee described a rigid, obedience-rewarding culture where "management wants staff to be like a robot," with two managers overseeing more than thirty staff and promotion tracking physical and political proximity to one's boss rather than output.*

> *CPJobs advice column — A letter signed "Drained" described a boss whose meetings re-litigate already-settled points at such length that, once follow-up reporting is included, they consume roughly two working days out of every five — leaving almost no time for the actual deliverables those meetings were ostensibly about.*

> *Reddit, via a Narcity compilation of genuine upvoted r/AskACanadian threads — Multiple commenters described a transplanted American manager visibly frustrated that staff didn't reply to after-hours messages instantly; Canadian colleagues treated the slower pace as simply normal, not a performance problem, in a direct clash between imported and local expectations.*

> *HR Reporter, relaying intercultural coach John Edward McGraw's firsthand account of coaching new immigrants — Newcomers from more hierarchical cultures told him they hadn't reached out to a senior colleague because he "seemed very important and very busy" and they feared upsetting him — a specific, attributed illustration of a hierarchical instinct misfiring in a flatter culture.*

### Conclusion

If you're walking into either market cold: in Hong Kong, patience with repetition is the actual skill, not a tolerance you grudgingly build — the meeting that seems to go nowhere is doing real relationship work. In Canada, brevity and a prompt reply are the actual skill — the politeness is real, but it is not a substitute for clarity, and it is not an invitation to go find the senior person's assistant instead of just emailing them. Learn which culture rewards patience and which rewards speed before your first meeting, not after your fifth.

And if you're arriving from a third culture entirely, give yourself real grace in the first month. The specific mismatch the HR Reporter piece describes above — treating a busy-looking senior colleague as untouchable in Canada, or treating a quick, casual reply as disrespect in Hong Kong — is less a personal failing than the single most common and most easily corrected mistake either culture's own newcomers report making. Watch one meeting fully before you speak in the next one, and let the room teach you its own rhythm before you try to set it yourself. Neither culture is actually asking you to abandon directness or patience altogether — they're asking you to apply the right one, in the right room, at the right volume — which turns out to be the entire job description of a good meeting attendee, in any country, once you strip away the cultural packaging.
