# Portugal and Germany Agree That Rest Is Sacred, and Disagree on Who Has to File the Paperwork

🇵🇹 Portugal · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

On paper, Germany wins the vacation contest comfortably. According to the Portugal Resident, which reported on a study by the Inequality Observatory at the University Institute of Lisbon, German employees enjoy roughly 30 days of annual leave against Portugal's 22. In practice, the Portuguese work about 1,797 hours a year, while Germans work fewer than 1,700. The question for the prospective emigrant is therefore not who rests more, but who rests with fewer complications. The answer depends on whether one prefers a calendar or a custom.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Portugal
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your contract or collective agreement for the extra three days some employers grant for good attendance | Assume that 25 days is the legal default; the statutory floor is 22 working days |
| Book August leave early if your team has families, because half the country wants the same weeks | Schedule a major launch for the second half of August and expect a quorum |
| Remember that leave in your first year accrues at two days per month and can only be taken after six months | Plan a three-week honeymoon in month four |
| Tell colleagues plainly when you are away and set an out-of-office message | Check email from the beach and set a precedent you will regret |
| Expect traditional shops and restaurants near you to close for the month and plan your errands accordingly | Treat a shuttered neighbourhood café in August as a personal slight |

### Germany
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Submit leave requests early and in writing, because a colleague's prior claim to the dates usually wins | Assume your manager can refuse on a whim; refusal needs important operational reasons |
| Get a doctor's note from the first day if you fall ill on holiday, so those days are not deducted | Simply stay in bed and hope the payroll office takes your word |
| Take your leave within the year, since carry-over is the exception | Hoard days like a squirrel preparing for a very bureaucratic winter |
| Plan around school holidays, which vary by state and thin out offices | Expect projects to move briskly in a regional school-holiday week |
| Disconnect fully; a manager who contacts you on leave is behaving impolitely | Reply to messages from the airport to show dedication |

## Portugal: Twenty-Two Days and a Very Long August

The Portuguese government portal ePortugal states that workers are entitled to a minimum of 22 working days of holiday per year, with leave in the first year accruing at two days a month, up to 20, and available after six full months. Public holidays do not count against the allowance. Some employers go further. A Portuguese labour-law adviser, Fed Group, notes that 25 days usually come from a collective agreement or individual contract, often as a bonus of three days for workers with few or no absences in the previous year, and that unjustified absences return the worker to 22.

The ordinary week is longer than the German one. ePortugal gives the legal maximum as eight hours a day and 40 a week, and Eurostat data reported by the Catalan newspaper Ara put the average actual week at 37.5 hours in Portugal in 2024, against fewer than 34 in Germany. The Inequality Observatory's older figure, 41.1 hours a week in 2016, is measured differently and should not be read as a precise trend, but the direction of travel is plausible. Either way, the Portuguese remain on the long side of the European picture, which makes the 22 days feel less like generosity and more like a negotiated settlement. A worker who puts in a longer week for fewer days off has some grounds to regard the August closure as a debt being repaid.

What the statute does not capture is the August phenomenon. An expat newsletter, Love from Portugal, describes many Portuguese taking the whole of August off, with traditional shops and restaurants shuttering for the month while tourists arrive in larger numbers. It is a collective arrangement without any collective agreement. Hofstede Insights, as reproduced in a 2022-dated Lisbon thesis, scores Portugal at 63 for power distance and 27 for individualism, which squares with a country where the group decides when the group is on holiday.

## Germany: Thirty Days, Properly Documented

Germany's Federal Holidays Act sets a minimum of 20 vacation days for a five-day week, according to Destatis. The statistical office's 2018 Structure of Earnings Survey found that full-time employees in most sectors were entitled to at least 28 days, with an average of 30 in energy, finance, public administration and education. The legal minimum is, in other words, a floor that few employers stand on. Public holidays add up to 12 depending on the state, according to Lingoda, the language school.

The system is orderly to a degree that foreigners find either reassuring or faintly sinister. IamExpat explains that employers may refuse leave only for extraordinarily important operational reasons or because colleagues have already booked the same dates, that illness on holiday does not eat into the allowance if documented from day one, and that unused days generally cannot be hoarded. Companies do not close for a set summer period, according to posters on Expat.com, so leave is staggered and parents steer toward school holidays, which differ by state.

The culture reinforces the law. An American who moved from Arizona told CNBC Make It that requesting time off was simple and that her supervisor considered it impolite to discuss work during her leave. Germany's Hofstede score for power distance is 35 against Portugal's 63, in the same thesis, which is a respectable explanation for why a manager there feels less entitled to your holiday. A word of caution from Expat.com's magazine: nearly half of German employees reportedly still do some work while away. The right to disconnect is firm, but so is the habit of not exercising it.

The broader hours picture favours Germany too. Eurostat figures reported by Euronews in June 2026 put the average German working week at 33.9 hours, and Lingoda, citing OECD figures, says Germans work about 1,340 hours a year, among the lowest in the organisation. Lingoda adds that many jobs compensate overtime with time off in lieu rather than pay, and some contracts exclude overtime pay altogether, so the generous leave is partly funded by the flexible ledger. A prospective hire would do well to ask which of the two arrangements applies before assuming that the 30 days arrive without conditions. The same caution applies to the public holidays, which Lingoda counts at up to 12 depending on the state, so a move from Bavaria to Berlin is not leave-neutral and the relocation spreadsheet should say so.

## The Reckoning

Germany offers more days, firmer legal protection and a cultural norm that treats a manager's holiday phone call as a breach of manners. Portugal offers fewer days and a looser formal apparatus, but compensates with a social calendar in which August is simply understood to be elsewhere. A German employee has a right to leave and a procedure for claiming it. A Portuguese employee has a right to leave and, in summer, a nation that has already left.

The practical difference is predictability against spontaneity. In Germany, the number is higher, the paperwork is heavier and the outcome is nearly guaranteed once the form is in. In Portugal, the number is lower, the contract may quietly add three days, and the real constraint is not whether you may leave but whether anyone will be around to answer in September. For a newcomer who values certainty, Germany is the stronger offer. For one who values doing what the neighbourhood does, Portugal has charm that the Federal Holidays Act cannot legislate.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — Posters in the Germany holidays thread say companies do not shut for a set summer break, apart from the days between Christmas and New Year, so staff take leave when they like, and offices go quiet and slow during school holidays that differ by state.*

> *CNBC Make It — An American who relocated from Arizona to Germany said booking leave from 30 paid days was simple, that she tells colleagues openly where she is going, and that her supervisor refuses to discuss work while she is away.*

> *Daily Dot — Covering a Reddit thread, the report says a German in the United States called the lack of paid time off a culture shock, since he was used to five to six weeks of leave plus paid sick days and struggled to find jobs offering more than two weeks.*

> *Love from Portugal (Substack) — Two expats in Lisbon found that in August a broker was away for about two weeks, neighbourhood restaurants shut, and refuse collection became erratic, and they advised visiting in another season.*

## Conclusion

Neither country will leave you short of rest, but they will ask for different kinds of effort. In Germany, the effort is administrative: request early, document illness, take your days within the year and treat your colleagues' prior bookings with the reverence of land registry entries. In Portugal, the effort is anticipatory: find out what your contract adds beyond 22 days, book the summer weeks before everyone else does, and accept that August is not a month but a condition.

Before signing, ask for the leave policy in writing, ask how many colleagues actually use all of it, and ask who covers whom in the second half of August. Germany guarantees the holiday; Portugal guarantees that the holiday will be crowded; and in both cases the only person in the office still at their desk is the one who read the contract carelessly.
