# In Bangladesh the Day Never Ends, in Malaysia It Merely Overruns

🇧🇩 Bangladesh · 🇲🇾 Malaysia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Bangladesh and Malaysia have each written a limit on the working week into law, and each has developed a custom that treats the limit as an opening bid. Bangladesh sets a standard week of 48 hours and allows up to 60 with overtime. Malaysia cut its own ceiling to 45. The prospective migrant, reading the statutes, may conclude that the two countries are broadly civilised. The second paragraph of any honest guide begins with the word "however".

## Do's & Don'ts

### Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask in the interview what hours people actually keep | Take the 48-hour week in the contract as the real one |
| Confirm in writing how overtime is paid | Assume overtime will be offered, or refused |
| Expect hierarchy, and let seniors lead decisions | Bypass the chain of command to save time |
| Build a personal rapport with colleagues before the agenda | Expect meetings to start or finish on schedule |
| Tell colleagues your availability and stick to it | Answer work messages at midnight and then expect them to stop |
| Plan around a Sunday to Thursday week | Book anything important for a Friday or Saturday |

### Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the overtime rates before you accept the role | Work unpaid hours without checking your entitlement |
| Watch who leaves first for a month, then decide your own time | Announce your departure time on day one |
| Raise problems privately and gently | Criticise a colleague or manager in front of others |
| Request flexible arrangements through the proper channel | Assume the policy exists because the website says so |
| Respect the chain of command | Skip a level to settle something quickly |
| Finish the work before the stay-back starts | Mistake a visible desk for visible output |

## Bangladesh: Forty-Eight Hours, Allegedly

The Bangladesh Labour Act sets a standard week of 48 hours, eight a day over six days, with a weekly rest day, typically Friday. According to an October 2025 opinion piece in The Business Standard, it permits up to 60 hours a week once overtime conditions are met, and the author says the rules are often ignored in practice. A payroll-provider summary of the law puts overtime pay at twice the regular hourly wage and caps it at two hours a day. The Daily Star, citing the Labour Act, gives the same 48 and 60 hour limits.

The legal limits are mild next to the measured reality. The Business Standard, citing an International Labour Organization study, reports mean weekly hours in Bangladesh of 48.8, against a global average of 42.9. It adds that a report by the Bangladesh Institute of Development Studies found that over half of private-sector workers in Dhaka work more than 48 hours a week, and nearly 30 percent work more than 60. The Daily Star, citing a joint World Health Organization and ILO report, notes that more than 745,000 people worldwide died in 2016 from heart disease and stroke linked to working 55 or more hours a week. This is the sort of statistic that a human resources department prefers to read alone.

The culture does the rest. The Financial Express reports that long hours signal dedication and that leaving on time can be read as a lack of seriousness, particularly in private and informal sectors, where the expectation to stay available is often unspoken. Expat Arrivals describes the workplace as typically hierarchical and relationship-driven, with decisions often made centrally by senior management, meetings that open with rapport-building, and late starts and overruns that are fairly common. Hofstede Insights scores Bangladesh at 80 for power distance and 5 for individualism, which is the sort of arithmetic that explains why nobody wants to be first through the door. There is movement at the edges: the Business Standard describes a firm that counts a full day as at least nine hours and offers time off in lieu of overtime, and Expat Arrivals notes that NGOs and IT firms may offer more flexible schedules.

## Malaysia: Forty-Five Hours, Plus the Part That Counts

Malaysia's formal position is more generous. According to The Star, the working week under the Employment Act was to fall from 48 to 45 hours, a change the deputy human resources minister linked to ILO standards and the welfare of workers. Jobstreet Malaysia summarises the rules: normal hours of eight a day, a maximum of 12 hours a day including overtime, overtime capped at 104 hours a month, and pay at 1.5 times the hourly rate on an ordinary day, twice on a rest day and three times on a public holiday. It says overtime pay applies to employees earning under RM 4,000 a month, a figure worth confirming with the Department of Labour before you sign anything.

The practice is another matter. The Vibes reports that a study by security firm Kisi ranked Kuala Lumpur as the third most overworked city in the world, after Cape Town and Dubai. A Malay Mail column, citing a Jobstreet report from about a decade earlier, says roughly 60 percent of Malaysians reported work overload and little time for family, and more than three-quarters said they worked two to five hours past normal office hours. It also cites an AIA Malaysia study in which 51 percent of employees experienced sleep deprivation. Ministers have pointed to the right to request flexible arrangements under Sections 60P and 60Q of the Act, which is a right to ask, not a right to receive.

The tone is gentler than in Dhaka but no less firm. Expat.com's guide to Kuala Lumpur describes strong hierarchy, an emphasis on avoiding confrontation and saving face, and negative feedback delivered privately. It also notes that staying after hours to finish tasks is not uncommon, and that careers advance on trust and loyalty. The Vibes quotes a policy analyst at ISIS Malaysia attributing long hours to weak labour rights and to a social habit of valuing hours worked and presenteeism over productivity.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, the countries differ less in the length of the day than in what the law does about it. Malaysia has the lower ceiling, a published overtime table with multiples that rise on rest days and holidays, and a formal mechanism for asking for flexibility. Bangladesh has the higher ceiling, a single overtime multiple in the sources reviewed and a strong norm of being reachable. Both countries then neutralise the legal advantage the same way, by making the early departure a social act rather than a contractual one.

The practical gap is therefore in the negotiating position. In Malaysia you can at least point to a number: 45, 104, 1.5. In Bangladesh the most useful tool appears to be the one that workers themselves describe, which is announcing availability once and holding to it. Neither country rewards the person who leaves first on principle. Both tolerate the person who leaves on time with the work done, provided that person is patient about the eyebrows.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/malaysia (via SAYS Malaysia) — a new hire on staying-back culture*

A 26-year-old started at a new office where staying late signalled a hard worker. He arrived before nine and left on time, and colleagues whispered and teased him. A slightly older colleague admitted she had once waited for senior staff to leave first. He said he would stay for urgent work but regarded staying back without a reason as a waste of time, and commenters were split between asking colleagues directly and simply ignoring the habit.

> *Twitter/X via World of Buzz — a Malaysian back from overseas*

A woman who had worked in Melbourne, where staff were advised not to stay past official hours, said that on returning to Malaysia she was told it was Malaysian culture to give up personal time for inadequate pay. Another user described managers assigning three-day tasks due the next morning.

> *The Financial Express — a development-sector professional in Bangladesh*

A professional who had worked at four organisations said all of them, including national NGOs, shared the expectation of constant availability, summed up as a belief that if you are paid, you work around the clock. A corporate employee said he was asked why he needed a casual day off and saw the question as intrusive.

> *The Business Standard — a marketing executive in Dhaka*

A marketing executive decided to stop answering work messages after 9 pm. Colleagues were annoyed at first but learned not to expect a late reply. A psychologist in the same piece argued that constant availability is not dedication and is unsustainable.

> *The Vibes — a start-up engineer in Malaysia*

A start-up engineer said his day runs from 7 am to 10 pm and he rarely gets weekends off. A news-portal worker said she felt constantly switched on, even as hybrid work gave her no more time for personal pursuits.

## Conclusion

Read the statute, then ignore it, then read the room. In Bangladesh, ask what hours colleagues actually keep, get overtime terms in writing, and set an availability rule early, since the evidence suggests that a boundary announced once is easier to defend than one invented at midnight. In Malaysia, learn the overtime multiples and the flexibility provisions, confirm that you are covered by them, and spend the first month working out how much stay-back is custom and how much is merely habit.

In both countries the bargain is similar: the contract says one thing, the office says another, and the person who reconciles them gracefully is considered mature. It is a lovely compliment, and it pays at the standard rate.
