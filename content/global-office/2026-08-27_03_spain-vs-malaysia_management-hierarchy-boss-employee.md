## The Boss Is Always Right, Just at Different Speeds: Spain vs Malaysia

🇪🇸 Spain · 🇲🇾 Malaysia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Hofstede Insights puts Spain's power-distance score at 57 and Malaysia's at 100 — the highest recorded anywhere on the index — which is a polite statistical way of saying that in Madrid the boss is right after a lengthy hallway debate, and in Kuala Lumpur the boss was right before the meeting started ([Hofstede Insights](https://www.hofstede-insights.com/country-comparison/spain,malaysia/)). Both cultures answer to a superior. They disagree, sometimes loudly, on whether you're allowed to know that.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address your manager's manager through your manager, not around them | Assume a friendly "tú" means the org chart has dissolved |
| Argue your case in the meeting — Spanish teams genuinely debate before deciding | Expect the debate to change who signs off at the end |
| Build a personal rapport with your boss; trust travels faster than memos | Skip the coffee and small talk to "get straight to business" |
| Treat the final decision from a senior figure as final | Escalate in writing when a quiet word would do |
| Show up, even when there's nothing pressing — presence still reads as commitment | Assume remote-only performance speaks for itself everywhere |
| Let your boss's personality set the tone of the relationship | Expect uniform hierarchy — a startup and a ministry are different planets |

#### 🇲🇾 Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use titles (Encik, Puan, Mr, Datuk) until explicitly told otherwise | Use first names with a senior manager on day one |
| Raise concerns privately, after the meeting, not during it | Contradict your boss in front of the team, even gently |
| Let your manager know you respect their guidance before offering an alternative | Treat silence in a meeting as agreement or as absence of opinion |
| Expect your boss to take a personal interest in your wellbeing | Mistake that warmth for informality about decision rights |
| Route disagreement through a trusted intermediary if direct feedback feels risky | Assume "yes" always means yes — it sometimes means "I heard you" |
| Read seniority as a proxy for authority, not just job title | Assume younger, foreign hires outrank older local staff by default |

---

Spain's hierarchy is a performance with an audience-participation section. Managers are, per one widely used cross-cultural guide, "benevolent autocrats" — happy to hear you out, unlikely to be talked out of anything ([Commisceo Global](https://www.commisceo-global.com/resources/management-guides/spain-management-guide)). The debate is not decorative; Spanish teams do argue substance in the room. But the decision afterward belongs to whoever was senior enough to end the meeting. Layered onto this is "presentismo" — the office-hours theatre where being seen matters almost as much as being useful, a habit that persists even as Spain's actual annual hours worked, 1,632 in 2023, sit below the OECD average of 1,736 ([CaixaBank Research](https://www.caixabankresearch.com/en/economics-markets/activity-growth/hours-worked-and-productivity-spain-outlier-eu); [OECD-derived data](https://www.theglobaleconomy.com/Spain/hours_worked/)). Spaniards, in other words, are already working less than the postcard suggests — they just haven't stopped pretending otherwise in front of the boss.

What softens the formality is personality. Respect for a Spanish manager is, by most accounts, "directly proportionate to the personality of the boss" rather than to the title on the door — a charismatic jefe earns loyalty that an aloof one never will, regardless of rank ([Commisceo Global](https://www.commisceo-global.com/resources/management-guides/spain-management-guide)). That makes Spanish hierarchy oddly negotiable at the emotional level and almost entirely non-negotiable at the structural one: you can charm your boss, rarely can you outvote them.

Malaysia runs on a different operating system, and the Hofstede number is not an exaggeration — a 100 on power distance is the ceiling of the scale, not a data point near it. Managers are described as paternalistic, combining formal authority with genuine personal responsibility for their staff, and leadership itself is treated as inseparable from age and experience rather than job function ([Hogan Assessments](https://www.hoganassessments.com/news-events/updates/leadership-in-malaysia-cultural-psychological-perspective/)). Public criticism is close to unthinkable; feedback happens in private, and disagreement gets filtered through harmony-preserving indirection before it ever reaches a superior's ears ([Commisceo Global](https://www.commisceo-global.com/resources/management-guides/malaysia-management-guide)). Malaysians also work more hours for it: DOSM's Labour Force Survey put average weekly hours at 44.8 in December 2025, against a legal ceiling of 45 — a workforce running close to the speed limit as a matter of routine, not crisis ([Department of Statistics Malaysia](https://www.dosm.gov.my/portal-main/release-content/labour-productivity-q12026)).

The catch for incoming managers is that Malaysian deference is easy to misread as consensus. A quiet meeting is not necessarily an agreeing one; it may simply be a room full of people who have decided that voicing an objection publicly costs more than it's worth. Confirm buy-in one-on-one, and the real answer often looks nothing like the one you got in the room.

---

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the paradox is this: Spain, the supposedly informal, Mediterranean, everyone-on-a-first-name-basis culture, has a hierarchy you're expected to argue with before it decides against you. Malaysia, the culture with the highest deference score on the entire Hofstede index, is often the one where a foreign manager gets an unnervingly frictionless "yes" — right up until the work quietly doesn't happen the way it was agreed. Loud disagreement in Spain is closer to loyalty than rebellion. Silence in Malaysia is closer to a data point than an answer.

The practical inversion matters for anyone managing across the two: in Spain, skipping the argument reads as disengagement, so an employee who says nothing in the room is probably not on board. In Malaysia, an employee who says nothing in the room may be entirely on board, or entirely not — you won't know until you ask outside it. Import a Malaysian team's meeting etiquette into a Madrid office and you'll be mistaken for indifferent. Import Spain's combative brainstorm into a Kuala Lumpur one and you'll be mistaken for disrespectful, possibly both in the same afternoon.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora contributor — described joining a traditionally run Spanish firm expecting flexibility, and instead finding a strict "I am the boss, you are the employee" mentality with rigid office hours, alongside newer companies down the street offering the opposite.*

> *Quora contributor — recounted working under a European manager for the first time and being unsettled by how coldly instructions arrived: changes requested with no explanation given, apparent disinterest in the employee's own reasoning, attention paid only to the final numbers.*

> *Quora contributor — took a head-of-department role in Kuala Lumpur managing fourteen reports as a foreign hire, and questioned afterward whether the friction they hit was standard practice across Malaysian workplaces or specific to that company.*

> *Expat Focus — advised newcomers to Malaysian offices that criticism is essentially never delivered in front of a group; it happens quietly, one-on-one, and employees generally keep views about their superiors to themselves in open settings.*

> *The Local Spain — collected the stock phrases that mark a Spanish office as a Spanish office, evidence that beneath the formal hierarchy sits a specific, shared, slightly ironic office vocabulary that outsiders take months to pick up.*

### Conclusion

Neither country will hand you a flat org chart, so stop looking for one. Spain rewards employees who treat the boss's authority as real but their own voice as worth using before the decision is made; Malaysia rewards employees who treat the boss's authority as real and reserve their voice for a private word afterward. Mistake one system for the other and you will either look meek in Madrid or reckless in Kuala Lumpur. The honest version of "flat hierarchy" that global recruiters advertise for both markets does not exist — what exists is two different, well-documented protocols for finding out, politely, who actually gets to decide.
