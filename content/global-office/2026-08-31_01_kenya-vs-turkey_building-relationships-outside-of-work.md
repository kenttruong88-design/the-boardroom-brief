# In Kenya You Get Adopted; In Turkey You Get Fed Until You Confess Everything

🇰🇪 Kenya · 🇹🇷 Turkey

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Turkish employees log the longest average workweek in the OECD, at 47.7 hours, with 39 percent of them working 50 hours or more — yet somehow this is also a country where declining a colleague's third invitation to tea is treated as a small act of social violence. Kenyans, meanwhile, have made an entire international meme out of arriving whenever they feel like it, while simultaneously running some of the tightest informal trust networks on the continent. The through-line, once you look past the stereotypes, is that both countries have quietly concluded that the office is a terrible place to actually get to know someone — and built elaborate, unwritten systems around fixing that.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇪 Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept invitations to meals, church, or family events — refusing repeatedly reads as coldness | Rush straight into business before the small talk has run its course |
| Learn a handful of Swahili greetings; effort is noticed and rewarded | Take "Kenyan time" personally when a 10am meeting starts at 10:40 |
| Join a chama, sports club, or hobby group — this is where real trust gets built | Assume a warm, familiar conversation means you're owed money or favors immediately |
| Use professional or academic titles until told otherwise | Try to skip the hierarchy by being overly casual with senior colleagues early on |
| Show up to the harambee (fundraiser) if invited, even with a small contribution | Treat a declined third invitation as final — try again, warmly |
| Let relationships develop over weeks of shared meals, not one coffee | Assume no reply to a message means disinterest — Kenyan schedules run informally |

#### 🇹🇷 Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the tea. Every time it's offered, for as long as you're there | Cut a çay (tea) visit short to "get back to work" — it will be noticed |
| Ask after someone's family; it's the expected opener, not small talk filler | Steer conversation toward business before your host does |
| Show up to work dinners and after-hours gatherings — these are where trust actually forms | Bring up politics, religion, or Atatürk casually, even in a friendly setting |
| Learn a few Turkish courtesy phrases — genuine effort lands well | Assume friendliness at work means hierarchy has disappeared; titles still matter |
| Expect friendship to move fast once it starts — late invitations, long visits | Leave a home visit early; it can read as rejecting the host |
| Reciprocate hospitality, even modestly — a returned invitation matters more than a gift | Decline an invitation twice in a row without real explanation |

## 🇰🇪 Kenya: relationships as infrastructure

Kenyan business culture treats personal rapport as a prerequisite for professional trust, not a bonus feature. According to Hofstede Insights, East Africa scores 64 on power distance (Turkey scores a similar 66), which helps explain why hierarchy and titles matter even inside otherwise warm, informal relationships. What's harder to capture in a single number is Harambee — literally "all pull together," Kenya's independence-era rallying cry that migrated from barn-raising to boardrooms, per DHL's small business coverage of Kenyan commerce. Its more concrete modern descendant is the chama, an informal savings cooperative that, by various estimates, includes more than 40 percent of Kenyan adults. These aren't hobby clubs; they're functioning credit institutions built entirely on the premise that you actually know and trust the people in the room.

That trust, notably, does not extend evenly. Gallup World Poll data shows 62 percent of Kenyans believe a stranger-neighbor would return a lost wallet, versus just 23 percent who'd trust the police to do the same — a gap that says less about honesty and more about where Kenyans have learned to invest their confidence: in each other, not institutions. For a newcomer, the practical upshot is that professional credibility gets built the same way personal trust does — slowly, socially, and mostly outside the conference room, whether that's a shared meal, a church service, or a Saturday harambee contribution.

## 🇹🇷 Turkey: intensity as the default setting

If Kenya builds trust incrementally, Turkey seems to skip several steps entirely. Hofstede's framework rates Turkey high on collectivism, power distance, and uncertainty avoidance — a combination that, per the Cultural Atlas and Commisceo Global's business guides, translates into workplaces where loyalty to people matters more than loyalty to process, and where "getting acquainted" is treated as a formal prerequisite to getting anything done. This happens despite — or arguably because of — a workweek that leaves little obvious room for it: Hürriyet Daily News reports Turkey has the OECD's longest average hours, with 39 percent of employees logging 50-plus hours weekly. Somehow the tea ritual survives regardless.

The catch is that Turkish social trust, broadly, is not especially high. Pew Research's late-2025 global trust survey lists Turkey among the countries — alongside Kenya, Mexico, and Brazil — with the lowest overall social trust scores. What Turkey substitutes for generalized trust is intense, fast-moving interpersonal trust: expat accounts collected by sites like Yabangee describe Turkish friendships that "move faster than you expect," compressing months of gradual bonding into a single long dinner. The wariness is real, but so is the willingness to suspend it quickly for someone who shows up, sits down, and stays for the second pot of tea.

## The Reckoning

Put side by side, Kenya and Turkey arrive at similar outcomes — deep, loyalty-based professional relationships built mostly off the clock — via opposite mechanics. Kenya's system is patient and cumulative: trust compounds through repeated, low-stakes social contact (meals, clubs, chama meetings) over weeks or months, with institutional trust kept firmly at arm's length. Turkey's system front-loads the intensity: trust can form in a single extended meal, but it demands an almost theatrical level of hospitality and presence to get there, all while the formal workday already runs longer than almost anywhere else in the OECD.

The counterintuitive part is that the country with the more relaxed clock (Kenya, where meetings start "whenever") ends up asking more patience of newcomers than the country working the longest hours in the developed world. Turks will let you in fast if you show up properly; Kenyans will let you in for good, but only once you've shown up enough times that it stops being notable.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — an Istanbul newcomer noted that most social circles among Turkish colleagues still operate primarily in Turkish, so genuinely non-transactional friendships took real effort to find outside a handful of English-speaking hobby and language-exchange groups.*

> *Yabangee (expat blog) — a foreign resident described being thrown off by how quickly Turkish acquaintances turned into close friends, with near-strangers extending late-night invitations and expecting long visits — a pace that initially read as overwhelming before it became the thing they missed most after leaving.*

> *NPR — an American visitor's viral complaint about "Kenyan time" became a minor local meme, but Kenyans interviewed in response noted that punctuality anxiety mostly disappears once a newcomer stops treating a delayed start as a personal slight and starts treating it as the actual schedule.*

> *Hacker News (2021 thread, "On leaving Turkey") — a former resident reflected that the professional relationships that ended up mattering weren't built at networking events at all, but through small repeated gestures — checking in, remembering details, accepting the tea — accumulated over ordinary weeks.*

> *Expat.com Nairobi forum — a British newcomer to Nairobi found that formal networking mixers produced little, while joining a running club (the Hash House Harriers) and a local hobby group did more for her social and professional circle in a few months than any LinkedIn event.*

### Conclusion

The practical advice converges more than the cultures might suggest: in both Kenya and Turkey, the relationships that actually carry professional weight are built off-hours, over food, and on a timeline that rewards patience over efficiency — just a very different kind of patience in each case. In Kenya, that means showing up consistently over months without expecting a shortcut through the hierarchy. In Turkey, it means showing up fully in the moment, tea in hand, without an exit strategy. Neither approach optimizes for a newcomer's calendar, which is, of course, rather the point — a good working relationship, in either country, is not supposed to be efficient.
