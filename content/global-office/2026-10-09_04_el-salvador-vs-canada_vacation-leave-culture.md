**The Vacation You're Legally Owed vs. The Vacation You'll Actually Take**

**🇸🇻 El Salvador · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

El Salvador gives you fifteen paid vacation days by law, plus a mandatory December bonus equal to roughly a month's pay, specifically so you have both the time and the cash to enjoy it. Canada gives you, in most provinces, ten vacation days for your first several years — and then a culture so anxious about using even that much that 57% of employees describe themselves as vacation-deprived anyway. One of these countries has a smaller legal entitlement and a healthier relationship with it. It is not the one you'd guess from the GDP numbers.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇻 El Salvador

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect your first full vacation entitlement — 15 days — only after one full year of continuous service | Expect that leave to be unpaid; by law it comes with your normal salary plus an extra 30% specifically for the trip |
| Budget your December aguinaldo (mandatory 13th-month-style bonus, paid December 12–20) as real, predictable income | Assume it's a Christmas tip; it's a legal entitlement, prorated even if you leave the job before year-end |
| Plan around it: aguinaldo season is when flights, family gatherings, and big purchases genuinely concentrate | Schedule major personal travel assuming you can take it whenever; many smaller employers build the calendar around December and skip most other long absences |
| Take the three days of paternity leave within 15 days of a birth if that applies to you — it's short, fixed, and non-negotiable | Expect sick leave to pay in full indefinitely; employers cover only the first three days before social security (ISSS) takes over at 75% of salary |
| Ask early about marriage and bereavement leave; both are legally protected even though the exact day count isn't standardized | Assume your leave balance resets or carries over the same way your Canadian friends describe; the systems aren't comparable |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your specific province's minimum before you negotiate; Saskatchewan guarantees three weeks where most others guarantee two | Assume "two weeks" is generous by global standards; Canada ties for second-last among OECD peers on combined vacation and holiday days |
| Actually book the time once it's approved — 45% of Canadian workers left vacation days unused in a recent year | Feel like an outlier for feeling guilty about taking it; 47% worry about colleagues "picking up the slack" while they're away |
| Ask directly what "unlimited PTO" means in practice if it's offered; ambiguity, not generosity, is usually the point | Assume unlimited PTO means more time off; workers on capped plans often take more actual days than those on undefined "unlimited" ones |
| Treat chronic short-staffing as a real signal, not a personal failing, if it's the reason you can't get away | Wait for permission that will never explicitly arrive; 41% of workers say they feel they need to make excuses just to ask |
| Consider that 64% of Canadian workers say they'd change jobs for more vacation days — you're allowed to weigh it the same way | Assume your employer's stated support for time off ("72% say employers are supportive") translates into a team culture that makes it easy |

### El Salvador

El Salvador's leave system is unusually generous in structure for a country at its income level, and the structure is the point: fifteen paid vacation days after a year of service, paid at your normal salary plus a 30% vacation premium, exist specifically to make sure people can afford to actually travel on their days off rather than stay home to save money (rivermate.com). The mandatory aguinaldo — a year-end bonus paid between December 12 and 20 — functions as a second, parallel system: it's not vacation pay, but it concentrates real spending power at exactly the moment families travel, gather, and spend on the holidays, turning December into the de facto national vacation season regardless of what the calendar technically allows the rest of the year.

The tradeoff is thinner coverage everywhere else. Sick pay runs full salary for only three days before the social security system (ISSS) drops it to 75%; paternity leave is a fixed three days, taken within two weeks of the birth, with no flexibility to spread it out (rivermate.com). It's a system built around a few protected, predictable, well-funded windows rather than broad flexible discretion — which, as it turns out, may be the more honest design.

### Canada

Canada's statutory entitlement is thinner than most people assume: a CEPR comparison across 21 OECD countries found Canadian workers average 10 paid vacation days and 9 paid holidays — 19 days combined — tying for second-last in the group, ahead of only the United States, which guarantees none at all by law (pressprogress.ca). France guarantees 30 vacation days alone; Spain's combined total runs to 39. Most Canadian provinces only extend beyond two weeks after several years of tenure; Saskatchewan is the exception, guaranteeing three weeks from the outset.

What makes this more than a statistics footnote is what Canadians do with the days they're actually given: 57% describe themselves as vacation-deprived, up from 55% the year before, and 45% left vacation days unused entirely in 2022 (hcamag.com). Nearly half feel guilty that colleagues will have to cover for them, and a behaviour-change expert quoted in local coverage put her finger on the real driver — many employees "assume that taking time off will negatively impact career trajectory," a belief she says the evidence doesn't actually support (dailyhive.com). "Unlimited PTO," increasingly common at larger employers, doesn't fix this; on forums like Blind, workers describe it as functionally reducing time off, since the absence of a clear number shifts the real decision onto managers and unwritten team norms instead.

### The Reckoning

Hofstede Insights' indulgence dimension — how comfortable a culture is with gratifying its own desires rather than suppressing them — puts El Salvador at 89, one of the most indulgent societies measured, against Canada's more moderate 68. That gap tracks almost exactly onto vacation behavior: Salvadoran culture builds structural permission for enjoyment directly into labor law and the calendar, while Canadian culture, despite higher average individualism (80 versus El Salvador's 19), leaves each employee to privately negotiate permission for themselves — and a remarkable number quietly decide the negotiation isn't worth having.

The counterintuitive finding here is that El Salvador's lower, more rigidly structured entitlement produces less guilt than Canada's nominally flexible, discretion-heavy one. A fixed, legally mandated day with money attached is a day nobody has to ask permission for. A flexible day that depends on reading the room is a day a surprising number of people simply decide not to take.

### Conclusion

If the question is which system actually gets you on a plane, El Salvador's answer is structural: a protected day, a protected bonus, a protected season, and comparatively little discretion to negotiate any of it away. Canada's answer is theoretically more generous and practically more fragile — more nominal flexibility, more actual guilt, and a real chance you'll bank the days and never use them. The thing I'd tell a friend moving between these two systems: don't judge a country's work-life balance by its legal minimum. Judge it by whether taking the day off requires a negotiation with your conscience, or just a calendar.
