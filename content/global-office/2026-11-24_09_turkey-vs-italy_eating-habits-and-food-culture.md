# Turkey and Italy Settle the Lunch Question, Each Declaring Victory

🇹🇷 Turkey · 🇮🇹 Italy

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries on the Mediterranean have each concluded that a working day without a proper meal is a clerical error. They disagree only on the details, which is where relocating professionals tend to be caught out. In Italy, according to OECD time-use data reported by WineNews, residents spend roughly two hours and five minutes a day eating and drinking, second only to France. In Turkey, according to Euronews, the population is the world's biggest per-person consumer of tea, and workplace breaks are simply called tea breaks. Neither country regards food as a private matter to be handled discreetly at a desk, and anyone who has done so in either place has likely been politely asked whether they are unwell.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the tea when it is offered, and expect it to be offered again | Decline repeatedly without explanation, as hospitality is taken seriously |
| Treat a shared meal as part of the meeting, since food is a way to build relationships | Rush to the agenda before the plates have arrived |
| Invite colleagues to join your table, as people rarely eat alone | Eat alone at your desk and call it efficiency |
| Use the bread to finish the olive-oil dishes, and expect no butter | Ask for butter and then comment on its absence |
| Join the occasional after-work outing with colleagues | Treat the working day's end as the end of the relationship |
| Be on time, because foreign colleagues report that punctuality is not always reciprocated | Assume that a late lunch partner has been disrespectful |

### Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan for a lunch break that may run toward two hours | Schedule a one-pm call and expect attendance |
| Follow your host's lead at a business lunch, which may last up to three hours | Check your watch during the second course |
| Accept a lunch or dinner invitation as a sign the relationship is progressing | Treat it as a social obligation to be minimised |
| Expect the host to pay the bill, and thank them for it | Reach for the bill before the host does |
| Expect to leave the office around 7:30 or 8:00 pm after a long lunch | Assume the day ends at 5 pm because that is when it ends elsewhere |
| Leave shopping for before one o'clock or after four | Expect shops and services to oblige at midday |

## Turkey: Çay Is a Calendar Entry

The Turkish approach to food at work is communal, continuous and heavily caffeinated. According to Expat.com's guide to the work culture in Istanbul, the standard day runs from about nine to six and includes a lunch break, but meetings frequently continue over a meal because sharing food is considered a way to build relationships. Colleagues also gather for tea breaks and after-work outings, which the guide credits with closer professional bonds. Euronews adds that tea is drunk through the day and that people schedule meetings by suggesting they drink tea together, which is a more efficient use of a calendar invitation than most software manages.

The table itself follows rules. A Daily Sabah feature on eating like a Turk describes meals as social and unhurried, usually with at least three dishes, often a soup, a vegetable dish with rice or bulgur and a large salad, with dessert frequently reduced to fruit. People rarely eat alone, and the prevailing attitude toward guests is, in the writer's phrase, that the more there are, the merrier. Bread is plentiful and meant for soaking up olive-oil dishes rather than receiving butter. Tea follows the meal at any hour and is treated as part of digestion.

The food culture is not without its administrative side effects. An Expat.com thread on managing meals in Turkiye shows a prospective returnee expecting restaurant food to be tastier and cheaper than what they are used to in the United States, planning to eat out often and shop at weekly neighbourhood markets. Another thread on work culture records a member who found colleagues helpful and friendly, with the caveat that punctuality and keeping agreements can be an issue. For the OECD, the cost of this generosity appears elsewhere: in its How's Life data, Turkey recorded the lowest satisfaction with time use in the OECD, at 5.6 on a ten-point scale, though that figure dates from 2013 and is not specific to meals.

## Italy: The Two-Hour Lunch and Its Consequences

Italy treats the midday meal as a structural element of the working day. In a column for The Local, a writer who divides his time between Rimini and London describes shops and services often closing between one and four in the afternoon, and argues that the pausa pranzo gives the day a wave-like rhythm, allowing recovery before the afternoon. He cites research suggesting that most people manage about four hours of real focus, which he offers as evidence that the Italians are on to something. Since the column is an opinion piece, the claim should be read as an argument rather than an audit.

The practical consequences are documented more soberly by HousingAnywhere, which notes that lunch breaks can last as long as two hours and that many Italians go home to eat if they have no business lunch. The standard office day runs from roughly nine-thirty to six-thirty, and with a long lunch a newcomer should expect to leave around seven-thirty or eight. Business lunches and dinners may stretch to three hours, and an invitation is read as a sign that the working relationship is developing well. The host usually pays. Separately, the OECD's How's Life report finds that full-time employed Italians have the most time off of any OECD country, at about 16.5 hours a day, a category that includes sleep and personal care.

Italy's cultural distance from Turkey is often summarised through Hofstede Insights' framework, though the individual scores for these two countries could not be retrieved for this piece, so the comparison below rests on the reported behaviour rather than on the numbers. Both societies appear to value relationship over transaction. The difference is one of rhythm: Italian relationships are cultivated at length over a single long meal, while Turkish relationships are cultivated in smaller, more frequent doses of tea.

## The Reckoning

On the evidence available, Italy offers the more structured midday. The meal is long, the hours around it are predictable, and a newcomer who accepts that the afternoon starts late and ends late will be rarely disappointed. The cost is that the working day is stretched at both ends, and anyone who assumed a long lunch meant an early finish will learn otherwise at around eight in the evening. Turkey offers the more continuous version, with tea, shared plates and informal breaks distributed across the day, and a hospitality norm that makes refusing a glass a minor diplomatic act. Neither arrangement is forgiving of the hurried. The Italian timetable punishes the person who books a meeting at one o'clock, and the Turkish one punishes the person who declines the glass. In both cases the penalty is mild, social and delivered with perfect courtesy, which is usually the most effective kind.

For a relocating professional, the choice comes down to whether the preferred unit of social obligation is the long meal or the frequent glass. Italy rewards patience at the table and a willingness to keep the host happy for up to three hours. Turkey rewards availability, a high tolerance for tea, and a readiness to eat with whoever happens to be nearby. Neither country will let a newcomer eat sandwiches alone at a keyboard without remark, and both will, with some justification, regard the practice as a sign of a person who has misplaced their priorities.

## Conclusion

Anyone moving to either country should treat the meal as part of the job description. In Italy, block out the middle of the day, expect a late finish, and let the host lead and pay. In Turkey, accept the tea, share the table, and arrive on time yourself, since the local standard for punctuality may be more flexible than your own. In both, accept the lunch invitation, because it is probably the most reliable indicator of how the relationship is going.

The practical advice is to eat with your colleagues and to do so without checking your phone. Those who insist on productivity at the expense of lunch will find that both countries have already run the numbers and decided that the sandwich is not the answer.
