# Lithuania Lets Parents Demand the Spare Room; Norway Lets Everyone Ask, Then Mostly Goes to the Office

🇱🇹 Lithuania · 🇳🇴 Norway

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Statistics Norway reports that 45 percent of Norwegian employees aged 20 to 66 could work from home in 2024, and that only about 7 percent actually did so half their time or more. The gap between permission and practice is the single most useful fact about Norwegian remote work. Lithuania, meanwhile, wrote home-working rights into its Labour Code for specific categories of parent, and has spent the years since arguing about what counts as excessive cost. Someone moving to either country for a laptop job should know that the policy and the culture are not the same document.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Lithuania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether you fall into a legally protected category, such as a parent of a child under 8 | Assume remote work is a right available to everyone |
| Expect IT and business-services employers to be the most flexible | Assume a public body will match a tech firm |
| Put remote requests in writing, with the details of how you will stay reachable | Rely on a verbal "of course" from a manager |
| Accept that two to four office days a week is increasingly normal | Ask for a seaside Zoom call and expect applause |
| Use your commute-free days for visibly useful work | Give anyone a reason to cite long lunches as grounds for tightening the rules |
| Plan for isolation and arrange contact with a team | Assume home is automatically better for your career |

### In Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get any regular home-office arrangement in a written agreement covering days, hours and equipment | Treat a hallway assurance as a contract |
| Work within core hours, roughly 9 to 15, and set a status when unavailable | Expect to be reachable at ten at night |
| Leave on time to collect the children and say so | Treat overtime as proof of seriousness |
| Remember that home office is voluntary for both sides | Insist on it if your employer prefers the office |
| Ask your manager openly about expectations for in-person days | Assume that 45 percent eligibility means most people stay home |
| Build your network in person if you are new | Expect remote onboarding to produce friends |

## Lithuania

Lithuania's rules are more specific than its practices. According to CEE Legal Matters, a 2022 amendment to the Labour Code allows pregnant and breastfeeding employees, parents of children under 8, single parents of children under 14 (or disabled children under 18), disabled employees and carers to request full-time remote work. The employer may refuse only by demonstrating excessive costs. The same report notes that the change has produced more disputes and inconsistent rulings, and that some employers insist on at least three office days a week.

The direction since then has been a tightening. LRT English reported in June 2025 that the Kaunas municipality now requires off-site staff to be able to reach the office within an hour and to file a written request, with about half of its roughly 700 employees working remotely, typically two days a week. Software firm Telesoftas, by contrast, has about two-thirds of its staff working from home, and recruiters quoted by LRT say the move away from hybrid has been visible since 2024. The sector sample from Invest Lithuania's 2024 employee report fits the picture: most staff in the business-services and ICT industry work from home two to four days a week, with two the most popular, and 49 percent say a hybrid policy will definitely affect their next career move. Seventy-six percent think their employer offers flexibility, down from 79 percent the year before, a sector sample and not a national one, but a telling decline.

The result is a market in which the answer to "can I work remotely" depends on who you are, who employs you and which month it is. A recruiter quoted by LRT in 2023 observed that remote work demands self-discipline, and that some people change jobs to get back to a team. The newcomer should treat flexibility as a negotiated term and not an assumed one.

## Norway

Norway has the opposite arrangement: loose rules, strong norms, and a mild suspicion of anyone who wants to stay home permanently. According to Altinn, the official portal, home office is voluntary for both parties and neither can force it; a regular arrangement needs a written agreement covering extent, hours and equipment, and the Working Environment Act continues to apply. Employers and safety representatives cannot demand access to your home. Tekna, the union, describes a 37.5-hour week with flexible hours around core times of roughly 9 to 15, operating largely on trust.

The Statistics Norway series is stable to the point of boredom: the share able to work from home was 44 percent in 2022 and 2023 and 45 percent in 2024, while the share doing so at least half the time was 9, 7 and 7 percent. About one in five managers never works from home, and only 10 percent of under-25s have the option. The country that invented the cabin weekend has apparently concluded that the office is where one goes on weekdays.

The attitude underneath is that work should fit around life, and not the reverse. A French lawyer who has worked in Oslo for 15 years told the municipality's Voices from Oslo series that Norwegian workplaces run on trust, that people leave on time and that overtime is not a status symbol. An opinion piece by Telenor and SINTEF authors argues that employees now see remote work as an entitlement and react quickly to return-to-office pushes, while noting that new hires struggle to build networks. Norway offers flexibility without the drama, and the office remains where the networks are.

A wider comparison helps put the numbers in proportion. Euronews, citing Eurostat figures for 2025, put the EU average share of employees usually working from home at 8.8 percent, with Finland at 20.5 percent, and neither Lithuania nor Norway appeared in the extract available, so no national comparison is offered here. What can be said is that Norway's 7 percent working from home at least half the time is in the same range as the European norm, and that what distinguishes the country is the surrounding culture. The Expat.com guide to Oslo says it is not uncommon to work from home when children are sick, that people leave around 3 pm to collect children from kindergarten and that family often takes precedence over deadlines. That is flexibility by custom, and it does not appear in any statute.

## The Reckoning

Hofstede Insights, as recalled from the published scores, puts Norway at about 8 for masculinity and Lithuania at about 19, both strongly on the quality-of-life end, with Norway at roughly 31 for power distance and Lithuania at about 42. That similarity explains why neither country treats long hours as a virtue. It does not explain the divergence in remote policy, which is more about law and labour markets than values.

Lithuania regulates the category and lets employers contest the case. Norway regulates the process and lets employees and managers settle the substance. For a mover, the Lithuanian route means checking your eligibility and your sector before accepting an offer, while the Norwegian route means asking your prospective manager what the in-person expectations really are. In both cases the written agreement is worth more than the verbal promise.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — In a Norway thread from the pandemic period, a software worker said their firm was already set up for remote work and they had been in the office only twice since March, so little changed. A nurse in the same thread described far bigger disruption.*

> *LRT English, recruiter comment — A recruiter in Lithuania said teleworking requires strong self-discipline and that some people change jobs to get back to a team and an office.*

> *LRT English, management expert — A Kaunas management expert said Lithuanian IT salaries are around 20 to 30 percent below those in the United Kingdom, Germany and the Netherlands, and that the local offer is not yet equivalent.*

> *Oslo Municipality — A French lawyer who has worked in Oslo for 15 years said Norwegian workplaces run on trust, people leave on time and overtime is not used to signal status.*

> *Norwegian SciTech News — Authors from Telenor and SINTEF argued that employees now regard remote work as an entitlement and respond quickly to return-to-office pushes, and noted that Telenor's own research found no productivity change but difficulty building networks for new hires.*

## Conclusion

Move to Lithuania if you work in IT or business services, or if you fall within a legally protected category, and be prepared for the office to reclaim some of your week. Move to Norway if you want sensible hours and a flexible manager, and do not expect to work from your cabin on a Wednesday as a matter of course.

Whichever you choose, get the arrangement in writing, ask how many people in your team actually work from home on a given day, and find out whether the answer matches the policy. The two countries share a sensible attitude to the balance between work and life. One of them has put it in law, and the other has put it in the culture, and in neither does it appear in the job advertisement.
