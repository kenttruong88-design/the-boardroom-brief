# Moldova vs. Canada: Where Overtime Is Either Illegal or Imaginary

**🇲🇩 Moldova · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Moldova, the law caps your overtime at 120 hours a year, which sounds admirably strict until you learn that roughly one in five jobs in the country operates outside the law entirely, according to the International Labour Organization. In Canada, overtime is scrupulously regulated by not one but thirteen separate jurisdictions, each with its own threshold, which sounds admirably thorough until you learn that survey after survey finds Canadians quietly working six or more unpaid hours a week anyway. Neither country, in other words, has solved the problem of making people stop working. They have simply chosen different paperwork to ignore, and the paperwork, as always, is the most honest tell about what each economy actually values: Moldova writes rules for workers it cannot fully reach, and Canada writes rules precise enough to make the exceptions feel like someone else's problem.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇩 Moldova

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get the 40-hour week and the 120/240-hour annual overtime cap written into your contract | Don't accept a verbal assurance that "nobody really does overtime here" |
| Open a local bank account early; payroll delays are common in smaller firms | Don't assume direct deposit will land on the date promised |
| Expect many professionals to hold a second income stream, often remote freelance work | Don't assume one local salary, by itself, is the norm among your colleagues |
| Clarify what "flexible hours" means at your specific employer before you accept | Don't assume a 9 a.m. start is either strictly enforced or strictly optional |
| Plan around August, when much of the country effectively pauses for holiday | Don't schedule anything time-sensitive for late summer |
| Ask whether your sector is covered by a collective agreement before counting on the higher overtime cap | Don't assume the 240-hour ceiling applies to you by default |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your specific province's overtime threshold — it ranges from 40 to 48 hours | Don't assume federal rules apply; most jobs fall under provincial law instead |
| Sort your SIN, provincial health coverage, and banking in your first week | Don't delay the paperwork on the assumption it can wait until you're settled |
| Ask explicitly whether your role is "salaried-exempt" from overtime pay | Don't assume a salary means no unpaid extra hours are expected |
| Price housing against your actual offer before accepting a city | Don't assume a strong work-life-balance ranking offsets a brutal rent |
| Ask how unionized the workplace is — enforcement of overtime law varies accordingly | Don't assume every employer applies the law evenly |
| Expect two weeks of vacation as the entry-level norm, not five | Don't assume you'll start with the time off a ten-year colleague has earned |

### Moldova

Moldova's Labour Code is, on paper, a model of restraint: a standard 40-hour week, overtime capped at 120 hours a year (240 under a collective agreement), and premium pay of 150 percent for the first two overtime hours and 200 percent beyond that. It is the kind of statute a labour economist could admire, and the kind that reads, line by line, as if someone genuinely sat down and tried to protect a worker's evening. The trouble, as the ILO's statistics on informal employment make clear, is that a meaningful share of Moldovan jobs — commonly estimated around a fifth of total employment — sit outside the formal system the Labour Code governs, which means the cap is a ceiling that applies mainly to the workers least likely to need protecting from it. If your employment relationship was never written down to begin with, a 120-hour annual cap is a number on a government website, not a term of your actual job.

The more telling statistic isn't about hours at all. The World Bank has repeatedly flagged Moldova as having one of the highest rates of labour emigration relative to population size anywhere in the world, with remittances from Moldovans working abroad making up a share of GDP that regularly ranks among the highest globally. Moldovans have not, broadly speaking, negotiated for better overtime terms at home. They have left, in large numbers, for Poland, Italy, and points further west, and sent money back instead. The National Bureau of Statistics tracks wage growth that is real and recently respectable — average real wages rose meaningfully year on year — but it is growth from a low base, and the work-life-balance conversation in Moldova is less "how many hours" and more "is it worth staying for the hours on offer at all." A new arrival weighing a Moldovan posting is, in effect, competing for a formal-sector job against an entire domestic workforce that has already voted with its passport.

### Canada

Canada's problem is almost the inverse: the hours look reasonable, and that's precisely what makes the unpaid portion so easy to miss. The OECD's working-time data puts Canadian average annual hours worked at roughly 1,687 — fewer than the United States, fewer than most of Asia, comfortably inside the pack of "developed economy, functioning adult" territory. Statistics Canada's own hours-of-work series backs this up: nothing about the topline numbers screams crisis, and a newcomer scanning them before a move would be forgiven for assuming the matter was settled.

What the topline numbers don't capture is the thirteen-jurisdiction patchwork of overtime law — a 40-hour threshold in Quebec and Manitoba, 44 in Ontario and Alberta, 48 in Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island, with British Columbia alone requiring double time after twelve hours in a single day — nor the surveys showing a majority of Canadian employees logging overtime that is functionally voluntary, which is to say not quite voluntary at all. Industry groups that track this describe the phenomenon with admirable understatement as employees who treat overtime as a "choice" they nonetheless keep making. Canada ranks well on the OECD's broader well-being work-life measures, and that ranking is not wrong; it is simply measuring something narrower than whether your Tuesday evening belongs to you, and a salaried contract with no overtime clause at all can sit entirely within the law while quietly absorbing however many hours the job actually requires.

### The Reckoning

Here is the part that should unsettle anyone doing a side-by-side spreadsheet: Moldova's overtime law is, by the letter, more protective than most of Canada's provincial regimes — a hard annual cap is a blunter, simpler instrument than a sliding scale of daily and weekly thresholds. And yet almost nobody moving abroad for work treats Moldova as the work-life-balance destination, while Canada routinely appears on exactly those lists. The explanation isn't the law. It's enforcement capacity, formal-sector coverage, and the fact that a strict rule nobody can make a firm follow is weaker, in practice, than a loose rule a functioning labour inspectorate occasionally checks.

The second irony cuts the other way. Canada's reputation for balance is a national average smoothed across wildly different realities — a unionized provincial government job in Halifax and an unpaid-overtime grind at a Toronto consultancy are both "Canada" on paper, averaged together into a ranking that satisfies neither worker. Moldova's reputation for hardship is, similarly, an average across a formal sector with real protections and an informal sector with none, plus a sizeable diaspora that opted out of the comparison altogether by leaving. Neither country is one thing. Both brochures are summarizing a fight that's still being had, province by province or sector by sector, inside each, and the number that lands on a glossy relocation deck is whichever average happened to be convenient that quarter.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — a Canadian tech-sector commenter notes that working remotely for a US company while based in Canada can mean roughly Bay Area pace of work for about half the pay, and that the "balance" Canadian firms themselves offer tends to come with a proportionate hit to compensation, not a free lunch.*

> *Expat.com — a poster in a Toronto work-life thread, who had previously worked in Vancouver, found that a short walk to the office there left evenings genuinely free, but warned that Toronto's longer commutes can quietly consume exactly the hours a shorter workweek was supposed to protect.*

> *Expat.com — a Toronto-based respondent on the same thread cautioned newcomers that schedule flexibility in Canada is usually earned over time rather than granted on arrival, and that leaning on sick days early in a new job can read as a risk rather than a right.*

> *Expat.com — a commenter on a Moldova work forum thread argued that well-paid office roles in accounting or management are, in practice, largely closed to non-citizens, who tend to be funneled toward physical or entry-level work regardless of qualifications — a cultural misread that no overtime statistic captures.*

> *Expat Exchange — an expat in Moldova praised the low cost of living and the warmth of local friendships, but named slow internet and patchy infrastructure as the recurring frustration, a detail with particular bite for anyone planning to work remotely from there.*

### Conclusion

The honest comparison isn't "which country respects your evenings more." It's "which gap between the law and your actual experience are you better equipped to manage." In Canada, the law is detailed and province-specific, and your risk is a slow accumulation of unpaid hours inside a system that looks, from outside, entirely reasonable. In Moldova, the law is simpler and arguably stricter, and your risk is that the formal system with those protections may not be the one you're actually hired into. Hofstede Insights has not published an individual country profile for Moldova, so the usual cultural-dimension shorthand isn't available here — one more reminder that the data infrastructure around this move is itself part of what you're signing up for.

Either way, the due diligence is the same: read the contract, ask who else at the company has actually taken their full vacation this year, and treat any national ranking as a weather forecast rather than a guarantee — informative about the climate, useless for tomorrow's commute.

If a friend asked me over a drink: go to Canada for the paperwork and the plausible deniability of a reasonable life, and go to Moldova only if you've already secured the formal-sector job with the contract that actually says what the law says — because in both places, the brochure describes the average, and you will be living the specific case.
