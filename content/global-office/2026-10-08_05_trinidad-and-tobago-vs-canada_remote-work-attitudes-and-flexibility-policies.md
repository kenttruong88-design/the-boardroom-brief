**Remote-First Islands, Back-to-the-Tower Banks: Trinidad and Tobago vs. Canada on Working From Home**

**🇹🇹 Trinidad and Tobago · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Trinidad and Tobago's own Public Administration Minister told the Senate the government would roll out a formal civil-service remote work policy in 2025 — a small Caribbean nation, writing WFH rules from scratch, mid-decade. Canada's three biggest banks spent the same window doing the opposite: RBC, Scotiabank, and BMO all moved to four office days a week starting last September, citing "in-person, human connection" as core to their culture. One country is cautiously building remote work infrastructure for the first time. The other built it, used it, and is now actively dismantling it for its highest earners.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇹 Trinidad and Tobago

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm your specific employer's remote policy individually — there's no single national standard yet | Assume a formal, government-wide remote work framework already exists |
| Expect genuinely strong internet infrastructure if you're in or near Port of Spain — one telecom exec rated it "the very higher end of good" | Assume Caribbean infrastructure automatically means unreliable internet |
| Treat a hybrid two-day-a-week arrangement as the most common model you'll be offered | Expect unlimited remote flexibility across every industry — manufacturing, retail, and distribution largely can't offer it |
| Factor in real commuting costs if you're not remote — estimates run from roughly $558 to $1,037 a month per worker | Underestimate how much a daily commute actually costs here before negotiating office days |
| Ask specifically how KPIs and deadlines will be tracked before accepting a remote role — employers are still building these systems | Assume "remote" means unmonitored or unstructured |
| Note that disability-inclusive hiring is one of the stated goals of the incoming public-service policy | Assume remote-work debates here are purely about convenience rather than labour-market access |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clarify your specific employer's in-office requirement before accepting — it varies hugely by sector and even by bank | Assume Canada's famously high remote-work rate (1.9 days a week, the highest of 40 countries surveyed) still applies evenly everywhere |
| Expect finance and big-bank roles to trend toward four or five office days | Take a 2023-era "three days a week" offer as a durable, unchanging policy |
| Budget socially for office life if you're coming from a fully remote background — isolation and friend-making are named concerns among remote workers here too | Assume remote work solves loneliness by default; many Canadians report the opposite |
| Raise transportation costs directly in a return-to-office negotiation — it's the single most cited internal objection to recent mandates | Treat a sudden in-office mandate as something to just quietly absorb without pushing back |
| Expect substantially more resistance to RTO in tech, finance, and other knowledge-work roles (68% prefer mostly remote) | Expect the same resistance level in sectors where remote was never really the norm |
| Ask about mentorship and career-development plans specifically — it's the stated justification banks are using for their mandates | Assume the stated "culture" reasoning for RTO is purely about real estate costs |

Trinidad and Tobago's remote work story is one of fast, forced improvisation that worked better than expected. Nearshore Americas documented two of the country's larger business process employers moving home almost overnight in 2020: Digicel shifted roughly 400 contact-center agents within two days, while DirecOne, a medical-transcription-rooted firm, had already spent five years quietly building a work-from-home model before the pandemic made it urgent. DirecOne's own leadership admitted real doubt going in — "a lot of thought was put into first of all who we would allow to WFH" — and came out surprised by the results: "We were pleasantly surprised. That is the honest answer." On infrastructure, Digicel Business's Liam Donnelly rated the country bluntly: "I would rate Trinidad and Tobago at the very higher end of the good infrastructure," with DirecOne agents commonly getting over 50 Mbps at home. What the country still lacks, five years later, is the policy layer — the T&T Chamber of Industry and Commerce only reported "measurable productivity improvements" from its own two-day hybrid model in January 2025, and the government's own civil-service remote work policy was still in draft form as of that same year, pending Cabinet review.

Canada's story runs in the opposite direction: a remote-work culture that was, by international standards, unusually deep and is now being actively walked back at the top of the market. Stanford Institute researchers found Canadians average 1.9 work-from-home days a week — the highest of 40 countries surveyed, ahead of the UK, the US, and Australia. But KPMG's 2024 CEO survey found 83 percent of Canadian business leaders expecting a fully in-office workforce within three years, up sharply from 55 percent in 2023 — and the country's three largest banks backed that prediction with policy: RBC, Scotiabank, and BMO all moved to four in-office days starting September 2025, each citing culture and mentorship rather than real estate costs as the justification. An Angus Reid Institute survey the same year found the gap this creates starkly: 59 percent of Canadian workers would choose to work mostly from home if they could, rising to 76 percent among those with real remote experience — and 24 percent of currently remote workers facing a forced return said they'd simply quit rather than comply.

### The Reckoning

Here's the real divergence: Trinidad and Tobago's remote-work debate is fundamentally about access — expanding who can participate in the labour market at all, including explicitly, per the government's own stated goals, people with disabilities who face real commuting barriers. Canada's remote-work debate is fundamentally about retention and leverage — a workforce that already has flexibility trying to keep it, against employers who already have market power trying to claw it back. Nobody at the T&T Chamber is debating whether remote work erodes "culture." Everybody at RBC apparently is. Hofstede Insights has not published an individual score for Trinidad and Tobago, but Canada's combination of low power distance (39) and high individualism (80) lines up with its comparatively decentralized, autonomy-first approach to where and how work gets done.

The irony worth sitting with: the small Caribbean economy still building its remote-work rules from scratch may end up, within a few years, more genuinely flexible than the large wealthy one currently rolling its flexibility back for its highest-paid workers. Policy maturity and actual flexibility are not the same axis, and right now they're moving in opposite directions in these two countries.

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two for a remote-friendly career, the honest read is almost a role reversal of reputation: Trinidad and Tobago offers a smaller, less formalized, but genuinely improvised and currently functional flexibility, built by companies who tried it under pressure and liked the results. Canada offers a famous, statistically real remote-work culture that is actively narrowing at the exact moment — the top of the corporate ladder, in finance specifically — where you'd most want it to hold.

Before you take either country's reputation at face value, ask the one question neither brochure answers: not "does this country support remote work," but "does my specific employer, this specific year, still believe it does." In Trinidad and Tobago that answer is still being written. In Canada, for a growing number of the best-paying jobs, it's already been rewritten once.
