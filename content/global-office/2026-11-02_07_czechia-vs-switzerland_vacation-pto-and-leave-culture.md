**Twenty Days, Five Weeks, and the Fine Art of Not Quite Resting: Czechia and Switzerland on Leave**

🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Switzerland, a country that can get a train to arrive within ninety seconds of its scheduled time, legislates only four weeks of paid vacation a year, putting it among the stingiest leave regimes in Europe, a notch above Turkey. Czechia legislates the identical four weeks and then throws in thirteen public holidays on top, which sounds like an act of generosity until you learn that barely more than a quarter of Czechs report actually feeling rested afterward. Both countries, in short, have found their own way to write a respectable number into law and then let culture quietly chip away at it: the Swiss by letting contracts nudge the real average up while employees keep answering email from the chairlift, the Czechs by stuffing the calendar with holidays nobody quite manages to enjoy on their own terms. This is a guide to the gap between what the statute promises and what the body actually receives.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book your summer week early — July and August fill fast in a country with only twenty statutory days to go around | Assume unused days simply vanish on December 31 — most employers allow carryover, but only with a written request submitted in advance |
| Ask your manager directly before blocking out calendar time — approval culture here is personal, not automatic | Expect two full weeks to be guaranteed without discussion — employers can still recall you from leave, with compensation, if the work demands it |
| Treat the thirteen public holidays as real days off | Expect compensation when one lands on a Saturday — it is simply absorbed with no makeup day |
| Negotiate extra vacation days into your contract, especially in banking, insurance, or IT, where roughly a third of employers already top up the legal minimum | Expect that top-up outside those sectors — most employers still stop exactly at twenty days |
| Plan a trip that actually lets you switch off | Assume the itinerary needs to be packed — Czech workers increasingly report coming back more exhausted than they left |

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read your contract closely — the legal floor is only four weeks, and most of the real generosity lives in the fine print, not the law | Expect a sixth week to appear voluntarily — Swiss voters rejected mandating one, twice, at the ballot box |
| Learn to "bridge": take the single day before or after a public holiday to turn it into a five-day break | Assume a uniform holiday calendar — cantons set their own, so what closes Zurich may not close Geneva |
| Check whether your industry banks overtime hours as extra leave — several do | Assume that applies everywhere — some employers cap recoverable hours strictly |
| Ask older colleagues how they handle Christmas week — many firms shut between the holidays by custom, not law | Expect the office to be fully staffed in late December — but also don't assume you'll be told this in writing |
| Build in genuine downtime before a trip | Assume being "out of office" means being out of work — most Swiss employees report working during vacation at least occasionally |

### Czechia: Generous on Paper, Negotiable in Practice

Czech labour law (the Zákoník práce) sets a floor of twenty days of paid annual leave for full-time employees, with some professions — teachers prominent among them — able to reach eight weeks through collective agreements, according to leave-policy data compiled by Vacationtracker. On top of that statutory floor sit thirteen public holidays, more than most of Western Europe grants, and unused leave can generally be carried into the following year provided the request is made in writing before the deadline. On paper, this reads like one of the better leave packages on the continent, especially once you add in the roughly third of Czech employers — closer to half in banking, insurance, and energy, per a Platy.cz benefits survey reported by Expats.cz — who voluntarily add days beyond the legal minimum.

There is also a procedural quirk worth knowing before you sign anything: Czech employees generally need to accumulate at least sixty days of work with an employer before any leave becomes payable, and full-time staff are typically entitled to take two consecutive weeks at a stretch, a provision clearly written with the classic European August exodus in mind rather than the long-weekend culture now common elsewhere.

The trouble is what happens once the leave is actually booked. A zamestnanci.com poll cited by Radio Prague International found that only 28 percent of Czechs said they felt reinvigorated after their most recent holiday, down from 39 percent in 2008 — a decline of more than a decade's worth of ambition about what a vacation is supposed to accomplish. The same reporting describes a drift toward shorter trips, usually about a week, stuffed with more destinations than a week can comfortably hold, with many workers staying reachable by phone for "pressing" issues throughout. Czechia, in other words, has quietly invented a new subgenre of time off: generously allotted, densely packed, and only partially disconnected from the office.

### Switzerland: A Stingy Floor, a Climbing Average

Switzerland's statutory minimum of four weeks places it, alongside Ireland, the Netherlands, and Turkey, among the handful of European countries offering fewer than thirty combined days of leave and public holidays, according to a comparative analysis by Landgeist — a notable position for a country that otherwise tops nearly every quality-of-life ranking going. Swiss voters had the chance to change this in 2012 and declined, rejecting a referendum to mandate six weeks by a margin of roughly two to one, a decision the press at the time attributed to concern over the cost to the economy rather than any shortage of ambition for rest. What has changed since is the market rather than the law: the Swiss Federal Statistical Office puts the average leave entitlement actually written into contracts at 5.1 weeks, about half a week higher than when records began two decades ago, with education and training workers averaging 6.3 weeks and agricultural workers closer to 4.6.

Entitlement, however, is not the same as use. A 2025 survey of Swiss workers conducted by Indeed and Appinio found that 65 percent work during their vacation — nearly a quarter of them regularly — and that 75 percent put in extra hours beforehand just to clear the decks, with nearly a fifth of that group logging more than five hours of pre-trip overtime alone. More than half said they had no adequate replacement while away, and 54 percent did not feel refreshed afterward. Over 40 percent said they feel economic pressure to stay available even while technically off, citing a slowing job market and the general unease of the AI moment, which suggests the anxiety isn't really about the four weeks at all — it's about what happens to whoever doesn't use them most visibly. The Swiss have, in effect, engineered a leave system precise enough to have its own workflow, and about as relaxing.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the obvious story — generous Czechia, stingy Switzerland — turns out to be the less interesting one. Czechia's twenty days plus thirteen fixed public holidays technically outguns Switzerland's twenty days plus a scattering of cantonal observances that vary by postal code. Yet the country with the nominally better package produces the worse outcome: a 28 percent "felt rested" rate against a Swiss figure implying something closer to 45 percent, despite the fact that more Swiss workers, not fewer, report working through their time off. The lesson isn't that one country rests more than the other — neither does, particularly — it's that neither has a legal right to disconnect, and both rely entirely on individual employers to decide how seriously "paid leave" is meant to be taken.

Hofstede Insights' cultural-dimension data makes the divergence easier to name, if not to fix. Switzerland scores 66 on indulgence, comfortably in the "indulgent" half of the scale, where leisure and impulse carry some social legitimacy; Czechia scores 29, squarely "restrained," where caution and self-control are the more rewarded instincts. That gap helps explain why a Swiss worker's guilt about taking a second day off reads, from Prague, like an entirely foreign problem — and why a Czech worker's eight-day, five-city summer holiday would strike a Swiss colleague as faintly reckless. It also explains, perhaps, why the two countries' scores sit almost backward from what you'd predict from their laws: the indulgent Swiss keep voting down more statutory leave while their employers quietly add it anyway, and the restrained Czechs legislate a generous package of holidays that their own caution then prevents them from fully enjoying.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Mustachianpost Forum — several Swiss-based professionals compared notes on annual leave and found enormous variance by employer: one banking employee reported 25 days plus roughly three bank-closing days, while an IT worker at an international organization described six weeks plus two paid weeks at Christmas, with no single figure representing a "typical" Swiss job.*

> *Reddit — commenters discussing Czech leave culture noted that the legal twenty days is treated as a floor, not a target, with "most ok employers" offering 25, though one pointed out that approval still runs through whichever manager happens to be in charge that year.*

> *Metafilter — an American who had spent years with a single week of annual leave described genuine disorientation on discovering a Swiss-linked employer offered ten days as a starting allotment, taking it as a sign of how low her previous baseline had actually been.*

> *The Local Switzerland — practical reader guidance on "bridging" shows how little actual leave the Swiss calendar requires to manufacture a long weekend: a single day taken before or after Ascension, timed correctly, turns into four consecutive days off.*

> *Blind — a tech employee's post described feeling guilty about asking for a second day off so soon after a public holiday, prompting colleagues to point out that earned leave isn't a favor being requested; the exchange reads almost quaint set against a Czech or Swiss context, where the days are already banked and the problem is using them, not asking for them.*

### Conclusion

The number that actually matters for anyone weighing these two countries isn't the one printed in the employment contract — it's whether the organization you join treats paid leave as a line item to be minimized or a resource to be used. Switzerland's statutory floor is lower than Czechia's effective package, but Swiss market pressure has pushed real entitlements higher than the law requires; Czechia's package looks better on paper but is undermined by a culture that keeps people half-reachable throughout and leaves most of them feeling no better for the trouble. Ask about actual usage in the interview, not just the headline number, and ask what happens to the person who covers for you while you're gone — that answer tells you more than either country's labour code ever will.

If a friend asked me over a drink, I'd say: take the Swiss contract for the number, but take the Czech advice and actually leave your phone at the hotel — neither country's legal minimum was designed with your well-being in mind, only with not annoying its voters.
