**Two Countries That Both Guard Their Time Off Fiercely, Except Now Their Twenty-Six-Year-Olds Are Guarding It From Everyone Else Too**

**🇫🇷 France · 🇪🇸 Spain**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

France invented the 35-hour work week to protect its employees from overwork, and Spain's national identity runs on the sacred, employer-defying lunch break — and yet the fiercest workplace negotiation in both countries right now isn't between labor and management, it's between a 58-year-old département head and the 24-year-old who just asked, in a meeting, why the meeting needed to happen at all.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address senior colleagues formally (vous, titles) until invited to do otherwise, regardless of your own generation | Suggest process changes in a large meeting before you've built standing — it reads as overstepping |
| Expect real pushback on France's 35-hour framework — most professionals actually log 39+ hours a week | Assume the legal work week reflects the actual one; it rarely does |
| Take your full vacation allocation — not taking it is viewed with suspicion, not admiration | Treat "right to disconnect" emails outside hours as optional to ignore — younger staff enforce this norm, older staff often quietly don't |
| Expect younger colleagues to negotiate flexible hours and mission alignment explicitly | Assume a Gen Z hire who asks about purpose is being difficult — EDHEC research finds this is now the norm, not the exception |
| Build relationships slowly — trust in French offices is earned over months, not one good presentation | Mistake formality for coldness; it is a professional register, not a personality |

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Respect the family-run, multigenerational feel of many Spanish businesses even at large firms | Rush relationship-building — Spanish business culture rewards patience and personal rapport over speed |
| Expect a long midday break to still shape the working day in many sectors, even as younger firms move toward continuous hours | Schedule a hard 1 p.m. deadline meeting without checking local norms first |
| Notice that younger Spanish professionals are pushing harder than prior generations for flexible and remote arrangements | Assume Spain's relaxed reputation means low ambition — youth unemployment and precarious contracts make competition fierce |
| Use personal warmth and informality to build trust across generations — it travels further here than a polished slide deck | Confuse informality with a lack of hierarchy — decisions still often move up before they move down |
| Expect a multigenerational team to genuinely value loyalty and tenure | Job-hop too visibly early in a Spanish career — it can still read as instability to older evaluators |

---

**France** has spent a quarter-century building formal protections around employee time — the 35-hour week, generous statutory leave, a legal "right to disconnect" — yet OECD data shows French employees often work more than counterparts in Germany, the UK, or the Netherlands, because the legal ceiling and the cultural expectation have never fully agreed with each other. Into that gap has walked a generation that takes the legal promise literally. An EDHEC Business School NewGen Talent Centre survey of over 9,000 young Europeans found French Gen Z workers explicitly want flexible hours, more say in a company's mission, and room to move between roles — a direct contrast to the tenure-and-title model still practiced by managers who came up in the grandes écoles pipeline. Youth unemployment sitting near 21.7% by some measures has sharpened the tension rather than softened it: younger workers are simultaneously more precarious and more vocal about what they'll tolerate, a combination that shows up in meetings as polite but persistent questioning of decisions that used to go unquestioned.

**Spain** carries a reputation for a gentler pace of life that undersells how competitive its labor market actually is underneath. Research on Spanish professionals published in Frontiers in Sociology found that age-based differences in work engagement are real but resist the tidy "lazy Gen Z, loyal Boomer" narrative — engagement gaps in Spain track more closely with job security and contract type than with birth year alone. Family-centered decision-making remains a defining feature of the Spanish business landscape even inside large firms, meaning generational change often has to negotiate not just with a manager's expectations but with an actual family hierarchy sitting above them. What's shifting fastest is flexibility: younger Spanish professionals are pushing for remote and hybrid arrangements at a pace that outstrips what many mid-sized Spanish firms are structurally prepared to offer.

**The Reckoning:** Both countries built their national identity partly around protecting workers from overwork, which makes it almost funny that both are now watching a younger cohort accuse the system of not protecting them enough. In France, the fight is explicit and structural — Gen Z workers cite the 2023 pension-age protests as a formative moment, and they carry that skepticism of institutional promises directly into the office. In Spain, the fight is quieter and more familial, playing out inside businesses where the "boss" might literally be your colleague's father, and where pushing back on a norm means pushing back on a relationship, not a policy. The counterintuitive twist: France, the country with the stronger legal scaffolding, has the louder generational conflict, while Spain, with weaker formal protections, resolves more of it through informal, relationship-based accommodation.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who worked in both French and Spanish offices over a decade-long career wrote that the biggest surprise wasn't hierarchy in either country, but how differently "informal" is defined — a first-name basis in Madrid still came with an unspoken deference to seniority that took years to notice.*

> *Internations — A Dutch consultant based in Paris described being pulled aside by an older colleague after openly disagreeing with a director in a meeting, and told privately that the disagreement was correct but the setting was wrong — a distinction he said no relocation guide had prepared him for.*

> *r/expats (via secondary reporting) — One American transplant in Barcelona described the long lunch break as the single hardest habit to adjust to, not because of the food but because it reset his entire sense of when the workday actually started and ended.*

> *Quora — A French professional in her late twenties wrote that she had to explicitly negotiate her right to leave her phone off after hours, describing older colleagues as "supportive in principle, surprised in practice" when she actually did it.*

> *Internations Madrid — A British marketing manager noted that being visibly ambitious too early in a Spanish firm drew more scrutiny than praise, and that the safer path was demonstrating loyalty first and asking for advancement only once tenure made the request unremarkable.*

### Conclusion

If you're arriving in France, expect the generational tension to be loud, legalistic, and openly debated — align yourself with whichever camp's norms you actually intend to follow, because straddling both reads as indecisive. If you're arriving in Spain, expect the same tension to move underground, mediated through family ties and long-standing personal relationships rather than open debate — patience will get you further than a well-argued case. In both places, the safest early move is the same: watch what the thirty-somethings do quietly before you copy what the twenty-somethings say loudly. One drink of honest advice — the loudest generational complaint in the room is rarely the one actually deciding your next promotion.
