# The Philippines Salutes the Boss, Britain Merely Tolerates Him

🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom
*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries, one organisational chart, and wildly different opinions about what the boxes on it mean. In Manila, addressing your manager by his first name is a reliable way to learn what a quiet room feels like. In London, addressing him as Sir is a reliable way to be asked whether you are feeling all right. According to Hofstede Insights, the Philippines scores 94 on power distance against 35 for Great Britain, which is roughly the gap between a cathedral and a pub quiz. The numbers hint at the rest. One workforce expects authority to be visible and deferred to, the other expects it to be modest and, ideally, absent from the room.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address managers as sir or ma'am, or by professional title, until told otherwise | Use a senior colleague's first name on day one, however casual the office seems |
| Read tone, silence and body language alongside the words | Take a cheerful yes at face value when the question carried an obvious difficulty |
| Raise problems privately and gently, ideally before the meeting | Contradict your boss in front of the team |
| Invest in small talk and rapport before getting to the point | Open every exchange with a blunt request |
| Dress formally Monday to Thursday and dress down on Friday | Assume overtime is optional because the contract says forty hours |

### United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use first names with managers from the start | Insist on titles, which reads as mockery or alarm |
| Soften criticism with understatement and a joke | Say a plan is bad when you could say it is interesting |
| Expect to be consulted, and offer a view when asked | Wait to be told what to do; initiative is assumed |
| Disagree openly in meetings, with courtesy | Treat a polite no as a hidden yes |
| Put decisions and agreements in a follow-up email | Rely on a corridor chat as a binding commitment |

## The Philippines: Rank Is a Relationship

The Philippine workplace treats hierarchy as something closer to family structure than a reporting line. Expat.com's guide to working in Manila notes that subordinates are expected to use a title or sir and ma'am, that the familiar kuya and ate for older brother and sister carry over into the office, and that using a boss's first name can cause real trouble. Respect for rank shapes who speaks, who waits, and who is permitted to look unconvinced.

The corollary is the national dislike of confrontation. The same guide describes Manila as a high-context environment where anger and raised voices are shameful, where colleagues may say yes to avoid embarrassment, and where tone and status matter as much as vocabulary. For the newcomer, this means that the meeting is rarely where the real information lives. It lives in the hallway, in the pause before an answer, and in the phrase that sounds like agreement and is actually a request to change the subject.

None of this is simply benign. A Manila Bulletin column on why Filipinos tolerate toxic workplaces says many stay put to support their families and out of fear of long spells without work, and lists micromanagement, unrealistic workloads and poor communication among the warning signs. The piece offers no statistics, so treat it as informed commentary rather than measurement. Still, a system that rewards deference and punishes open dissent leaves a bad boss with few natural predators. There is also a practical consequence for newcomers. Feedback that would be delivered in a sentence elsewhere may arrive as a hint, a delay or a change in who is invited to lunch, and a foreign manager who waits for someone to object will wait a long time. The upside is loyalty, warmth and a manager who is expected to look after his people. The downside is that being looked after and being managed are not always distinguishable.

Daily routine reinforces the point. The Expat.com guide says formal dress is typical from Monday to Thursday, with Friday allowed to dress down, and that working overtime a few days a week is expected. The dress code is itself a hierarchy, visible from the lobby, and the overtime is rarely negotiated because negotiating it would require saying no to someone senior. A newcomer who leaves at the contracted hour while the boss stays will not be reprimanded. He will simply be noticed, which in this system is the more durable penalty.

## The United Kingdom: Rank Is a Rumour

British management culture prefers to pretend that the hierarchy is a misunderstanding. Titles are shed on the first day, the chief executive may be on first-name terms with the post room, and open disagreement is permitted so long as it arrives wrapped in several layers of courtesy. This does not mean authority has gone away. It means authority is expressed in tone, and everyone is expected to hear it.

The surveys suggest a workforce that is comfortable with the informality and sceptical of the people it applies to. In the Boss Barometer Report from Kimble Applications, covering 1,000 full-time UK employees and reported by HR Magazine, 65 per cent said they could do their job as well or better without their manager's input, 46 per cent had little faith in their managers, 36 per cent said they were micromanaged and 31 per cent said a manager had taken credit for their work. Yet 74 per cent felt respected by their manager and 69 per cent found it easy to be honest with him. A 2018 YouGov and MHR survey of 1,174 employees, published by the Chartered Management Institute, found 33 per cent felt their managers were not supportive enough. A 2005 MORI poll for Investors in People found only 34 per cent saw their manager as a role model. The data is dated and the sample sizes modest, but the pattern is consistent. The British employee will happily call you Dave while privately reserving judgment.

There is a structural reason this works at all. When everyone may speak, the boss is spared the burden of being obeyed and takes on the harder burden of being persuaded. Managers who confuse informality with weakness tend to discover it at the first redundancy round, and those who mistake it for equality tend to discover it at the first performance review. The relationship is warm in manner and conditional in substance, which is precisely the kind of arrangement that suits a country with a national talent for saying one thing and meaning an adjacent one.

## The Reckoning

Taken together, the countries trade one kind of discomfort for another. The Philippines offers clarity of rank and ambiguity of speech: you always know who the boss is, and rarely know what he thinks. Britain offers clarity of speech and ambiguity of rank: everyone talks like an equal, yet someone will still decide your bonus, and no one will say when. A Filipino manager moving to London may find that his instructions are treated as a first draft. A British manager moving to Manila may find that his open invitation to criticise him has produced a silence he mistakes for approval.

The sensible expatriate adapts in the direction of the local default and keeps the home instincts for private use. In Manila, that means formality, patience and doing the disagreeing in advance and in private. In Britain, it means speaking up, using first names, and learning that when a colleague says something is brave, the plan is probably doomed. Salary negotiations show the same split. In Manila, asking for more is best done through a trusted intermediary or at a moment the boss can grant it graciously. In Britain, it is done directly and apologetically, using the phrase "I wonder if" to ask for a raise as if requesting directions.

Neither system is better at producing good bosses. Each is simply better at hiding what it produces from the people involved.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum, Philippines — A long-term resident argued that local workers are often trained to wait for instruction rather than think independently, so initiative is thin, and that foreigners are better off with Western employers than with Filipino companies, which pay far less.*

> *Expat.com forum, Philippines — A foreign manager of younger agency teams said that productive individuals slow down when collaborating, and that managers must step in firmly and fairly when deadlines slip, because extra meetings and revision requests pile up.*

> *Reddit r/phcareers — One commenter said a manager who behaves like a ruler is simply not fit for the role, that the company probably never trained its managers, and that leaving is a perfectly reasonable response.*

> *Expat.com forum, England — An American expat described an unhappy run of UK jobs, including a supervisor who resented initiative and unclear instructions, while another member in the same thread replied flatly that none of it matched their own experience.*

> *Blind — Several UK tech workers said that in British-led teams senior people sometimes take credit for technical work and that polish matters more than substance, while others replied that this was overstated and that experiences vary by region.*

## Conclusion

If you thrive on clear structure, personal loyalty and a boss who behaves like a slightly strict relative, the Philippines will suit you, provided you can learn to hear what is not being said. If you prefer first names, argument and being treated as a competent adult until proven otherwise, Britain will suit you, provided you can learn to hear what is being said in code.

Either way, check how your prospective manager actually behaves rather than how the national stereotype says he should. Ask a current employee how disagreements are handled, and watch how the team addresses him. Then adjust your vocabulary, since the one thing both cultures agree on is that the new person should not be the loudest. The boss is always right in one country and always wrong in the other, and in both he is the last to hear about it.
