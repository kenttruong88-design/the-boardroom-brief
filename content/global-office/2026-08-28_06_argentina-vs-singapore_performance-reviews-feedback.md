# The Annual Review, Translated: Buenos Aires Says It to Your Face, Singapore Says It in a Follow-Up Email

🇦🇷 Argentina · 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Somewhere between the performance cycle and the exit interview lies the most honest document any company produces about itself: how it tells people they are falling short. Argentina tends to say it over coffee, loudly, with hand gestures. Singapore tends to say it in a document with three tracked-change rounds and a meeting to discuss the document. Neither is dishonest. They are simply optimizing for different disasters.

#### 🇦🇷 Argentina

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect debate — a good review is a conversation, not a monologue | Mistake bluntness for hostility; it's usually just candor |
| Build the relationship before the ratings conversation happens | Deliver criticism cold, without context or personal rapport first |
| Ask what inflation and cost-of-living pressure means for the raise attached to the review | Assume the review's rating and the pay outcome move together automatically |
| Let people push back on their score in the room | Treat pushback as insubordination |
| Pair criticism with acknowledgment of effort | Skip the informal check-in and go straight to the paperwork |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read between the lines — "room for improvement" can mean "serious problem" | Take indirect phrasing at face value and assume all is well |
| Ask for specifics privately if a review feels vague | Publicly challenge a manager's assessment in a group setting |
| Expect a structured, numbers-heavy process, often with forced distribution | Assume a single soft conversation counts as the whole review |
| Treat the written appraisal as the actual record of consequence | Ignore the paperwork because the meeting felt friendly |
| Watch for repeated soft feedback — it may be a serious signal, not small talk | Wait for someone to say "you're underperforming" outright |

## Argentina: The Review as Relationship, Renegotiated Annually

Argentine workplaces are hierarchical on the org chart and startlingly informal in the room. Hofstede Insights scores Argentina at a middling 49 on power distance — low enough that employees expect the boss to remain the boss, but not so low that they'll salute (hofstede-insights.com/country-comparison/argentina,singapore/). What that produces in practice is a management guide's favorite paradox: managers keep formal authority while inviting direct, sometimes theatrical, pushback in the actual meeting (commisceo-global.com). Feedback conversations in Buenos Aires tend to be verbal, immediate, and personal, built on the assumption that a relationship exists to absorb the friction of honest criticism (thinkglobal-hr.com). Delivered without that relational cushion, the same words land as an attack rather than a note.

The bigger complication isn't tone, it's arithmetic. Argentina's inflation ran at roughly 33 percent year-on-year as of mid-2026, and while wages finally outpaced prices in the first half of 2025 — up 20.7 percent against 15.1 percent inflation — that was a reversal after years of erosion, and collective bargaining agreements now renegotiate pay three or four times annually just to keep up (batimes.com.ar). An annual review cycle imported wholesale from a low-inflation country is, functionally, a joke here: the rating you earned in March is attached to a salary figure that may be stale by June. Employees know this, which is part of why the review conversation itself, not the number on the form, carries the real weight.

## Singapore: The Review as Documentation, Softened on Delivery

Singapore inverts the equation. Power distance runs high — 74 on the Hofstede scale, reflecting a Confucian-influenced regard for hierarchy and face — while the appraisal process itself is often rigorously structured: numeric ratings, competency frameworks, sometimes a forced distribution across a team (hofstede-insights.com/country-comparison/argentina,singapore/; hrmlabs.com). The tension is that the harmony-preserving instinct which governs delivery frequently defeats the precision the paperwork is trying to achieve. Feedback gets softened, hedged, or deferred to a private follow-up, because direct criticism risks causing someone to lose face in front of peers (aihr.com). HR practitioners writing for Singapore audiences increasingly flag this as a design flaw, not a feature: managers recycle the same gently-worded note cycle after cycle, employees nod, and nothing changes, because the message was never sharp enough to act on (reeracoen.sg; omnihr.co).

Gallup's global engagement data offers a blunt coda: worldwide engagement fell to 21 percent in 2024, and only 26 percent of employees globally report receiving adequate recognition for their work — a shortfall that a review process built on ambiguity is poorly positioned to fix (gallup.com). Singapore's system is not indifferent to performance; it is arguably more quantified than Argentina's. It is simply quantified in a document that the conversation itself declines to fully narrate.

## The Reckoning

Put the two systems side by side and you get a case study in where honesty lives. Argentina puts it in the room — loud, immediate, occasionally uncomfortable, but rarely ambiguous about where you stand once the debate settles. Singapore puts it on the page — precise, ranked, defensible in an audit, but frequently obscured by a delivery style engineered to avoid friction. An employee moving from Buenos Aires to Singapore will likely leave their first review convinced it went fine, only to discover months later that "room for improvement" was a warning shot. An employee moving the other way may find the directness of a Argentine review meeting alarming, mistaking passionate disagreement for a prelude to termination, when it's often just Tuesday.

Neither culture has solved the actual problem performance reviews exist to fix, which is that most people are bad at hearing they're doing something wrong. Argentina compensates with relationship capital; Singapore compensates with process rigor. Both, per the research, still land well short of what employees say they need (aihr.com; gallup.com).

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who had worked in Argentina described the constant back-and-forth in meetings as exhausting at first, then oddly reassuring, once they realized colleagues argued hardest with people they respected most.*

> *Quora — A commenter reflecting on Singaporean company culture noted that critical Glassdoor reviews often cite office politics and unclear feedback loops rather than compensation, suggesting the ambiguity problem is widely felt, not just an expat's misread.*

> *Expat.com forum, Singapore — A poster new to the local office described being startled that leaving before 8pm, even while meeting every target, quietly marked them as a "slacker" in some teams, a signal no one stated outright.*

> *Mothership.sg — An expat reviewing five years in Singapore wrote that leisure itself gets reframed as "recharge," productivity as constant and visible, so a lukewarm-sounding review can coexist with real dissatisfaction that nobody says aloud.*

> *Expat.com guide, Buenos Aires — A contributor advised newcomers to build a personal rapport with managers before the first real feedback conversation, warning that criticism delivered without that groundwork gets received as a personal insult rather than professional input.*

## Conclusion

Neither Buenos Aires nor Singapore has built the mythical review system where feedback is both kind and unambiguous — that system does not appear to exist anywhere, per Gallup's global numbers on recognition and engagement (gallup.com). What each has built is a coping mechanism suited to its own risk: Argentina insures against emotional distance, Singapore insures against public embarrassment. The practical advice for anyone relocating is almost insultingly simple — in Argentina, listen to the argument, not just the verdict; in Singapore, listen to the paperwork, not just the tone. Bring a translator for feedback, not for language.
