**Italy Will Tell You You're Doing Fine Right Up Until You're Not Doing Fine Anymore. South Africa Will Tell You, Gently, the Whole Time**

🇮🇹 Italy · 🇿🇦 South Africa

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

An Italian manager who is quietly unhappy with your work will often say almost nothing until the annual review, at which point the feedback arrives all at once, wrapped in enough warmth that you might miss how serious it actually is. A South African manager, shaped by a workplace culture that formally theorizes feedback as a collective, relational act rather than a private transaction between boss and employee, is more likely to raise a concern early, framed around the team's shared standing rather than your individual shortfall. Both styles are trying to avoid the same thing — public humiliation — they just space the discomfort out completely differently.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇹 Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Listen for what's implied in a warmly delivered comment — the warmth doesn't cancel the critique | Take a pleasant, friendly review conversation at face value as "no real issues" |
| Expect feedback to arrive privately, often weeks after the actual incident prompted it | Expect real-time, in-the-moment correction the way some Anglophone offices deliver it |
| Build a personal relationship with your manager — it genuinely changes how directly they'll level with you | Treat the manager relationship as purely transactional; it limits how honest feedback gets |
| Read relative silence on a topic as a signal worth probing, not a clean bill of health | Assume no news is good news between formal review cycles |
| Accept that loyalty and personal rapport can visibly soften how critical feedback is delivered | Expect identical review treatment to a colleague with a different relationship to the boss |

#### 🇿🇦 South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect feedback framed around team and collective impact, not just individual metrics | Assume a critique framed around "the team" is somehow less serious than one framed around you personally |
| Engage with feedback as a two-way, relational conversation, not a one-directional verdict | Treat a performance review as a monologue you simply receive and leave |
| Recognize that corporate South African firms often run formal, KPI-driven review systems in parallel with this relational style | Assume "Ubuntu-influenced" means informal or unstructured; many reviews are rigorously documented |
| Expect feedback to arrive closer to real-time than the Italian annual-cycle model | Wait for a scheduled review to raise or expect to hear about an ongoing concern |
| Account for South Africa's genuinely diverse workplace norms across industries and regions | Assume one company's review culture generalizes to the whole country |

### Italy

Italian feedback culture runs on a deferral that frustrates many foreign hires before they understand its logic: direct, in-the-moment criticism is comparatively rare, and multiple cross-cultural management guides — Talaera's performance-review playbook among them — specifically flag Italy as a culture where feedback is softened, contextualized, and often delayed until a formal review moment rather than delivered as it happens. The goal isn't evasiveness; it's face-preservation, for both parties, in a workplace culture where personal relationships and reputation carry real weight. A manager who likes you personally may genuinely struggle to deliver a harsh professional verdict without first establishing, warmly, that the relationship itself isn't at risk.

The practical consequence, described across several Quora threads on Italian workplace challenges, is that foreign employees sometimes don't register how serious an issue has become until the formal review lands it all at once — not because nobody noticed earlier, but because nobody said so earlier. Italy's workplace culture also varies meaningfully by region and sector, a point Quora respondents comparing northern and southern Italian work culture raise directly, with northern, internationally-oriented firms generally running closer to a documented, Anglophone-style review cadence than smaller family-run Southern businesses.

### South Africa

South African workplace feedback culture draws on a genuinely distinct philosophical foundation that shows up in the academic and HR literature far more explicitly than in most countries covered in this column: Ubuntu, the communal ethic of interconnected personhood ("I am because we are"), has been directly studied as a leadership and performance-management framework in South African peer-reviewed HR research, including comparative work on Ubuntu leadership's effect on employee engagement. In practice, this tends to frame feedback as something owed to the collective — your performance affects the team's standing, and the conversation often acknowledges that explicitly — rather than as a purely individual verdict handed down from above.

This sits alongside, not in place of, a highly structured corporate HR apparatus: large, JSE-listed South African employers run formal, KPI-driven performance-management systems that would look thoroughly familiar to a London or Toronto HR department, per Commisceo Global's business-culture guide. The genuinely useful takeaway for a foreign hire is that South African feedback culture isn't softer than Italy's by default — a 2026 comparative academic study even looked specifically at how Ubuntu-style leadership plays out differently across South African and UK contexts — it's organized around a different center of gravity: the team's shared position, delivered on a more continuous timeline than Italy's deferred, annual-cycle model.

### The Reckoning

Both cultures prioritize relationship preservation over blunt efficiency — they just pick different units to protect. Italy protects the individual relationship between manager and employee, which produces warmth-wrapped, delayed, occasionally confusing feedback that only makes full sense in retrospect. South Africa, where Ubuntu-influenced practice is genuinely present, protects the collective standing of the team, which produces feedback that may feel more exposed in the moment (your performance is framed as the group's business) but tends to arrive earlier and more continuously rather than saved up for one annual reckoning.

The timing mismatch is the real trap for a foreign manager moving between the two. Import Italy's "save it for the formal review" instinct into a South African team, and you'll be read as withholding and unsupportive. Import an expectation of constant real-time check-ins into an Italian team without first building the underlying relationship, and you'll come across as abrupt, even if the content of the feedback is identical either way.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A contributor answering on the biggest challenges in Italian workplace culture described realizing, well into a role, that a manager's consistently pleasant check-ins had been quietly covering for mounting dissatisfaction that only surfaced, all at once, at the annual review — a pattern they described as more disorienting than any single piece of harsh feedback would have been.*

> *Quora — A respondent comparing northern and southern Italian work culture noted that feedback directness varied as much by company size and region as by any single "Italian" national style, with larger, internationally exposed northern firms running noticeably closer to documented, scheduled review norms.*

> *SAJHRM (South African Journal of Human Resource Management, Ubuntu leadership study) — The research itself notes that employees under Ubuntu-influenced leadership report higher engagement specifically because feedback is experienced as relational investment rather than individual judgment — a framing the authors argue changes how criticism is received, not just how it's delivered.*

> *Quora — A respondent describing what it's like working in Italy under a multinational ownership structure noted that the "national" feedback style shifted considerably depending on which country's management layer was actually delivering the review, a reminder that company ownership can override local default as much as it reflects it.*

### Conclusion

If you're heading into an Italian review culture, don't mistake a warm tone for a clean bill of health — ask directly, outside the formal cycle, how things are actually going, because nobody is going to volunteer bad news early out of politeness. If you're heading into a South African team shaped by Ubuntu-influenced practice, expect feedback to show up sooner and be framed collectively, and don't read "the team" language as a way of softening a personal critique — it's usually the opposite: a sign you're being taken seriously as part of something shared. Either way, the review itself was never really the information. The timing of when you hear it was.
