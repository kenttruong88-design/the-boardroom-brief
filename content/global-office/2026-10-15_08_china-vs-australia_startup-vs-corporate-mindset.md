**996 vs. the Tall Poppy: China and Australia Can't Agree on How Hard "Hard Work" Should Look**

**🇨🇳 China · 🇦🇺 Australia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Shenzhen, a 25-year-old programmer at Tencent recently told his bosses, in writing, that he'd rather resign than keep working past midnight to build a messaging app — and the company's own director thanked him for the feedback. In Sydney, a startup co-founder came home from two weeks in San Francisco, posted on LinkedIn that Australians needed to stop clocking off at a reasonable hour, and got torn apart in the comments for it. Both countries are having the exact same argument about hustle. They're just losing it from opposite directions.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect "996" (9am-9pm, six days a week) to still be a real, if increasingly contested, norm at many tech firms and startups | Assume 996 is universally loved or universally legal; China's own Supreme People's Court has ruled standard 996 schedules illegal under the Labor Law, even though enforcement lags |
| Ask directly about appraisal systems before joining — some firms are experimenting with output-based reviews instead of hours-based ones | Assume a state-owned enterprise and a scrappy startup run on the same clock; SOEs and multinationals often keep far closer to the legal 40-44 hour standard |
| Recognise that demographic pressure (a shrinking, more selective young workforce) is a real reason some employers are softening overtime expectations | Mistake public pushback (like the GitHub "996.ICU" protest) for the norm actually disappearing; it's a visible crack, not a collapse |
| Treat a company's response to employee pushback as a real signal — some leadership teams genuinely do adjust appraisal systems when challenged | Assume raising concerns about hours will end your career there; one widely discussed case saw a company director publicly thank an employee for exactly that feedback |
| Factor China's high long-term orientation (87 on Hofstede's scale) into your expectations — enduring short-term hardship for a longer payoff is a culturally reinforced norm, not just a corporate one | Assume every young Chinese professional accepts this trade-off happily; a documented, vocal minority is actively pushing back, sometimes at real career risk |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect most corporate and startup roles to run on genuinely standard hours, with a legal "right to disconnect" now protecting you from after-hours contact | Assume every founder agrees with that norm; a visible, vocal subset of Australia's startup scene argues openly for US-style, San Francisco-paced hours |
| Negotiate hard on hourly-equivalent pay if a role asks for unusually long hours — commenters on one viral case calculated a six-day, 12-hour schedule can work out to less per hour than retail pay with penalty rates | Assume "founder mode" rhetoric describes how most Australian startups actually operate; it's a loud online minority opinion, not the median workplace |
| Recognise Australia's declining early-stage risk appetite as a real, documented trend (government programs like LaunchVic have been wound back in some states) rather than a personal failure if funding feels harder to find | Assume Australia's "tall poppy" caution about ambition is pure negativity; it's also a genuine, longstanding cultural check against overclaiming and overworking |
| Use Australia's relatively low power distance (36 on Hofstede's scale) to your advantage — pushing back on a founder's unreasonable expectations is culturally more acceptable here than in many other markets | Expect sympathy if you publicly argue employees should match founder-level hours without founder-level equity; recent cases show this lands badly, fast |
| Ask pointed questions about actual expected hours, not just stated "flexibility," before joining an early-stage startup specifically | Assume a startup's funding stage tells you nothing about its hours culture; earlier-stage, scrappier firms more often expect founder-adjacent intensity |

**China: The Clock Everyone Argues About but Few Can Fully Escape**

"996" — 9am to 9pm, six days a week, for a nominal 72-hour week — became shorthand for Chinese tech-sector overwork after Alibaba's Jack Ma and other prominent executives publicly defended it as a necessary sacrifice for ambitious young workers, sparking the 2019 "996.ICU" protest on GitHub (the name a bitter joke: work 996, end up in the ICU). China's own Supreme People's Court has since ruled that standard, unpaid 996 schedules violate the Labor Law's cap on overtime, and TechNode's coverage of the pattern notes that demographic pressure — a shrinking pool of young workers who increasingly have other options — is a real, structural reason some employers have started softening the expectation rather than purely a moral awakening.

The pushback is not purely theoretical, and it doesn't always end badly for the person doing the pushing back. Sixth Tone's reporting on Tencent's WeCom team captured a 25-year-old programmer who objected, in an internal feedback channel, to managers praising a team for working 20-hour days in its annual review, asking bluntly, "Do you have to make the developers work over 20 hours to make a product that you are satisfied with?" and adding that he'd rather resign than continue. A colleague, surnamed Zhang, publicly backed him, calling the problem one "shared by all of China's tech companies" that needs a "radical solution." WeCom's director, Huang Tieming, responded directly rather than punishing the complaint: "I appreciate the employee's candid feedback, which urges us to roll out the adjustment as soon as possible," and proposed moving toward an appraisal system that doesn't simply reward hours logged. Hofstede Insights scores China at 87 on long-term orientation — one of the highest in the world — a number that helps explain why enduring short-term hardship for a distant payoff remains culturally legible even as individual workers increasingly ask, out loud, whether the payoff is actually worth it.

**Australia: The Startup Scene Arguing With Itself in Public**

Australia's startup-versus-corporate divide looks completely different, and the argument is less about hours worked than about whether hustle itself is even the right goal. StartupDaily.net's coverage of Lyra co-founder and COO Anh Dao's viral LinkedIn post captured the fault line precisely: after two weeks in San Francisco, Dao wrote that "the culture here runs till late," described seeing "people still in the office at 11.30pm," and argued that Australia's own "tall poppy mindset" makes ambition hard to sustain — concluding that his own startup would be "fully adopting this culture." The backlash was immediate and specific: one commenter calculated that a $130,000 salary worked across a 12-hour, six-day schedule falls to roughly $35 an hour, "less than retail work pays once overtime and penalty rates are included," while another asked pointedly, "Are you offering 4x market rate?" One commenter called the post "hot garbage" and said it revealed the kind of manager Dao would be; Dao later clarified his message was aimed at "founders and builders," not a demand that staff match his hours, writing that "some want balance, some want to push limits. Both belong here."

Underneath the viral moment sits a quieter, structural story. A startupdaily.net opinion piece by Jim Cooper argued Australia's early-stage risk culture has genuinely contracted — citing the disbandment of state programs like Victoria's LaunchVic as part of a broader re-privatization of startup risk that, he argues, selects for founders who can already absorb failure financially rather than founders with the best ideas. Hofstede Insights scores Australia at a notably low 36 on power distance, a number that helps explain why a founder's "work till midnight" post gets publicly, bluntly contested by employees and commenters rather than quietly absorbed — in a flatter hierarchy, pushing back on the boss isn't a career risk, it's Tuesday. Australia's right-to-disconnect law, now in force federally, adds a legal backstop most Chinese tech workers currently don't have, even as individual Australian founders occasionally argue, loudly and publicly, that the backstop is the problem.

**The Reckoning**

The real contrast isn't who works longer hours on average — it's who gets to argue about it safely. In China, a programmer who publicly objected to 20-hour days got a public thank-you from leadership rather than a quiet blacklisting, a genuinely encouraging data point, but the underlying legal and cultural infrastructure (a Supreme Court ruling that's hard to enforce, a demographic squeeze doing more of the softening than policy is) means the outcome still depends heavily on which specific company you land at. In Australia, a founder who publicly argued for longer hours got torn apart by his own commentariat within days, but the legal infrastructure (the right to disconnect, strong at-will pushback norms) means the argument itself is now happening mostly in public, not resolved by it.

Put differently: China's hustle-culture fight is being won case by case, inside individual companies, often by workers willing to risk their own position to say something. Australia's hustle-culture fight is being won in comment sections, loudly and fast, by workers who face comparatively little risk in saying the same thing — which is either the healthier dynamic or simply the lower-stakes one, depending on how much you trust a viral LinkedIn thread to actually change a founder's mind.

### Conclusion

If you're weighing a startup move to either country, the honest question isn't "how many hours will I work," it's "what happens to me if I push back on the hours." In China, pushing back is still a real career gamble that occasionally — genuinely, documentedly — pays off, but you're betting on your specific employer's culture, not on the law protecting you reliably. In Australia, pushing back is comparatively low-risk and often rewarded with public support, but the flip side is that founders feeling genuine pressure to match Silicon Valley's pace have correspondingly less room to ask for it openly without getting publicly flamed for trying.

My honest line for a friend: in China, read the room at your specific company before assuming either 996 or its opposite is the norm, and know that speaking up has real documented precedent for working out fine. In Australia, don't let one loud LinkedIn post convince you the whole startup scene has gone full hustle-culture — it hasn't, and the comments under that exact post are the proof.
