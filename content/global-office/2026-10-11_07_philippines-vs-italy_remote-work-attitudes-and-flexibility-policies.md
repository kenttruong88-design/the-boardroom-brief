# The Tax Incentive and the Espresso Bar: Philippines vs. Italy

**🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇮🇹 Italy**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

The Philippine government ended pandemic-era work-from-home for BPO staff by threatening to revoke tax incentives from any company that let it continue. Italy didn't need a law to end remote work's momentum — it just let Italians discover that a laptop at a café table is, culturally, a contradiction in terms. Two very different mechanisms, one shared outcome: neither country's workers are getting the flexible future a global survey of executives once promised them.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect hybrid, not full remote, to be the realistic ask — 83% of workers already prefer it to full on-site | Assume a BPO employer will default to full work-from-home without a specific client or tax arrangement behind it |
| Raise productivity data (88% report being more efficient hybrid) if negotiating flexibility | Assume the government's tax-incentive policy on seat location is settled for good — it has shifted before |
| Ask directly whether your account/client allows work-from-home under current incentive rules | Plan your housing or commute around remote work being permanent |
| Join or at least know your sector's labor association (like ACW) — it has real negotiating leverage | Assume protest or mass resignation threats alone will reverse a return-to-office order |
| Factor in the real pull of family time and lower cost of living outside Metro Manila when weighing offers | Underestimate how much of the "prefer remote" sentiment is about commute and family, not the work itself |

#### 🇮🇹 Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect your actual leverage over return-to-office to be real — 70% of workers say they'd contest it | Assume working from a café is a viable daily option outside a handful of northern cities |
| Clock out fully at the end of the day; remote work blurring hours is specifically distrusted | Expect flexibility to be the cultural default just because the legal "smart working" framework exists |
| Negotiate a hybrid arrangement explicitly if your role allows it — large companies already have | Assume rural or southern postings will have reliable high-speed home internet |
| Invest time in in-person relationship-building — "le conoscenze contano" (who you know matters) still shapes careers | Treat a request for full remote work as a neutral ask; many employers read it with real suspicion |
| Price your own flexibility: workers who'd accept losing it want roughly 30% more pay to compensate | Assume a return-to-office mandate will go unchallenged — three-quarters say they'd leave rather than comply |

### Philippines: Flexible on Paper, Policy-Dependent in Practice

Hofstede Insights scores the Philippines at 94 on power distance, among the highest readings anywhere, which helps explain why a shift as consequential as ending work-from-home arrived not through workplace negotiation but through a single government directive: Finance Secretary Carlos Dominguez's office tied continued remote work directly to the loss of tax incentives for BPO employers, effectively forcing a return-to-office deadline of April 1, 2022, regardless of what individual companies or workers preferred. The stated rationale wasn't really about productivity at all — officials argued on-site work would benefit the property sector and nearby restaurants and cafés, a justification that put commercial real estate ahead of the workers actually doing the commuting.

The resistance that followed was real but structurally limited. The Alliance of Call Centre Workers, representing roughly 1,400 members, found about one in five would rather resign than return on-site, with many having relocated to home provinces outside Metro Manila during the pandemic specifically to be closer to family. ACW co-convenor Emman David was careful to frame this as attrition rather than organized protest — "it's not really mass resign as in protesta," he said — which captures something true about Philippine labor relations more broadly: discontent expresses itself through quiet, individual exit rather than collective confrontation, consistent with the same high-power-distance culture that makes direct pushback against a government directive rare. The IBPAP's own Employee Sentiment Survey found the appetite was never really for full remote anyway — 83% wanted hybrid, just 12% wanted fully remote — meaning the policy fight was really over degree, not principle, even if it got reported as an all-or-nothing standoff.

The policy itself has also proven less permanent than its original framing suggested, which matters for anyone weighing a BPO offer on the assumption that today's rule is tomorrow's rule. Various 30% work-from-home carve-outs have been proposed, extended, and renegotiated in the years since the original April 2022 deadline, and industry coverage has repeatedly framed the real decision less as a blanket national policy and more as a running negotiation between individual BPO employers, their specific clients, and whichever government office currently controls the incentive scheme — a level of policy volatility that a worker signing a one-year lease near their new hybrid office would do well to price in.

### Italy: Legally Flexible, Culturally Resistant

Italy's "smart working" framework is, on paper, one of Europe's more established remote-work legal structures, and the numbers back a real, if uneven, adoption: the Politecnico di Milano's Smart Working Observatory found 96% of large companies had consolidated remote arrangements by 2024, with international smart working permitted at 29% of them. But that corporate adoption curve coexists with a specific cultural resistance that has nothing to do with law. Hofstede's uncertainty avoidance score for Italy sits at 75, among the highest in Western Europe, and a widely read feature essay in The Local Italy by writer Silvia Marchetti captured what that means on the ground: Italians, she argues, distrust the blurred boundary remote work creates between "available" and "working," prize the strictly observed workweek specifically because clocking out means the work is genuinely over, and — more bluntly — "flexibility at work is as feared as the plague."

Marchetti's sharper point concerns career mechanics rather than temperament: Italian career advancement runs substantially on *le conoscenze* — who you know — which makes the after-lunch espresso with "important colleagues" a more valuable career investment, in her father's own framing, than a 12-hour shift alone at a laptop. The resistance isn't uniform, though — SME remote-work numbers actually fell between 2023 and 2024 while large-company numbers held near pandemic peaks, suggesting Italy's flexibility gap is increasingly a gap between big employers and everyone else, not simply a national allergy to the idea.

The generational and geographic texture matters too. Public-sector remote work actually dropped between 2023 and 2024 even as 43% of public administrations said they expected it to increase — a gap between stated intention and actual practice that mirrors the broader Italian pattern of smart working existing more robustly in policy documents than in daily behavior outside a handful of large northern employers. For an arriving professional, the practical lesson is to judge any given Italian employer's real flexibility by its size and sector rather than by the national law, which promises considerably more than most workplaces actually deliver.

### The Reckoning

Both countries ended up in roughly the same place — hybrid as the practical default, full remote as the exception — but arrived there through opposite mechanisms. The Philippines got there via blunt government policy overriding worker preference from above; Italy got there via cultural and relational incentives that undercut a genuinely permissive legal framework from within. An employer assuming "the law allows it, so it'll happen" will be wrong in Italy for cultural reasons, and an employer assuming "workers want it, so it'll happen" will be wrong in the Philippines for policy reasons. Neither country's remote-work story is really about whether people want flexibility — overwhelming majorities in both say they do — it's about who actually gets to decide.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *thelocal.it — Writer Silvia Marchetti described her own lack of home Wi-Fi as unremarkable rather than unusual, noting that even if "all of Italy was hooked up with supersonic internet," she believes most Italians would still prefer commuting to a physical workplace over working remotely — a preference she frames as cultural rather than infrastructural.*

> *thelocal.it — Marchetti also noted that Italian authorities view digital nomads with real suspicion, as hard-to-classify workers who might be dodging taxes — a bureaucratic wariness that quietly shapes how remote arrangements get treated even when they're technically legal.*

> *officepro.ph — Emman David of the Alliance of Call Centre Workers, describing the mood among BPO staff facing the return-to-office order, resisted the framing of mass resignation as organized protest, insisting the departures happening were individual choices rather than a coordinated stand.*

> *hrmasia.com — Reporting on the same standoff, one coverage piece noted that many of the workers most resistant to returning on-site had specifically relocated to home provinces during the pandemic to be nearer family — reframing the fight as being, at bottom, about geography and family time rather than remote work as an abstract principle.*

> *Advice worth having before you land: in the Philippines, don't assume your contract's remote-work clause survives the next policy shift — ask what happens to your specific account if incentives change again. In Italy, don't request full remote work as if it's a neutral ask; budget real time for in-person relationship-building regardless of what your contract technically permits.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing which country will actually let you work from home, the honest answer in both cases is: less than the headlines suggest, and for reasons that have very little to do with whether the work itself requires your physical presence. The thing I'd tell a friend over a drink: in the Philippines, read the fine print on your employer's tax status before you sign a lease near your parents; in Italy, show up for the coffee even if you could've sent the email — your career, by Marchetti's father's logic, is being decided at that counter either way.
