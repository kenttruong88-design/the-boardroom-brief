## Staying Put vs. Moving On: Career Loyalty in Tunis and Montevideo

**🇹🇳 Tunisia · 🇺🇾 Uruguay**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Ask a Tunisian civil servant about their five-year plan and you may get a puzzled look — the plan was set the day they were hired, and it's called "still here." Ask a Montevideo tech worker the same question and you'll likely get a LinkedIn-ready answer involving a title change at a different company within eighteen months. One country's professionals are fighting to leave a system that won't let them move; the other's are casually shopping around inside a market that increasingly expects it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇳 Tunisia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand that public-sector jobs carry strong security expectations but very limited internal mobility | Expect merit alone to drive promotion — seniority and institutional politics weigh heavily |
| Ask directly about advancement criteria before accepting a role — vague answers are a real signal | Assume a private-sector job offers meaningfully more mobility without checking the specific employer |
| Budget for the real possibility that switching employers means switching cities or countries entirely | Underestimate how often skilled professionals leave Tunisia rather than change jobs domestically |
| Take workplace respect and treatment seriously as a retention factor — it's cited more than salary in exit research | Assume compensation alone explains why colleagues are job-hunting |
| Network internationally if you're ambitious — remote work for foreign employers is an increasingly normalized path | Treat a stagnant title as a personal failing rather than a structural one |

#### 🇺🇾 Uruguay

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a real culture of job movement, especially in tech — treat your resume as a living document | Assume loose punctuality and relational pace mean low ambition — they coexist with real career mobility |
| Learn Spanish seriously — job prospects outside tech contract sharply without it | Expect the easy hiring path multinationals offer in tech to extend to other sectors |
| Value the mandatory 13th-month bonus (aguinaldo) and seniority-linked vacation as real, protected entitlements | Assume tenure benefits mean people stay in one job to collect them — mobility and strong labor protections coexist here |
| Build genuine personal relationships with colleagues before expecting career favors or references | Treat the relational, trust-based style as a slower version of a transactional culture — it's a different model, not a delay |
| Arrive with savings, remote income, or a plan — local hiring outside tech and consulting is genuinely limited | Assume Montevideo's hiring warmth toward expats extends evenly across all industries |

### Tunisia: The System That Won't Move You

Tunisia's loyalty problem isn't really about loyalty — it's about the near-absence of a functioning internal labor market for skilled professionals. Research on skilled Tunisian migration identifies career stagnation, weak institutional support, and limited merit-based promotion as the dominant push factors driving people out, more than raw pay differentials (OECD working paper, Zenodo). In the public sector specifically, one study of occupational physicians found 58% reporting dissatisfaction with their careers, with satisfaction tied closely to workload, role clarity, and perceived fairness rather than salary alone (NCBI/PMC). Severance protections exist but are modest and capped — one day's pay per month of service, maxing out at three months regardless of tenure — which does little to compensate for a system where staying put often means staying stuck.

The result is a workforce that treats "loyalty" less as a virtue and more as a structural default: cultural norms around geographic mobility, particularly for single women, further compress the internal market (Zenodo), so the realistic alternative to stagnation for many ambitious professionals isn't switching employers domestically — it's leaving the country, or increasingly, working remotely for a foreign employer without leaving at all.

### Uruguay: Loyalty Is Optional, Mobility Is the Point

Uruguay runs the opposite playbook. Its labor protections are genuinely generous and tenure-linked — 20 days of paid vacation after a year, increasing with seniority, and severance of up to one month's salary per year of service, capped at six months (Global Work Guide, Howdy) — but none of that translates into a "stay for decades" culture the way it might elsewhere. Montevideo's tech sector in particular has become a regional hub, and workers there move between employers with a normalcy that would read as job-hopping red flags in more tenure-obsessed markets (UKG Uruguay, Snaphunt).

What holds this together culturally is Uruguay's collectivist, relationship-first style: trust with colleagues and employers is built deliberately and matters enormously, but that trust is understood as portable, attached to the person's reputation and network rather than to any one employer (inuruguay.com, Live and Invest Overseas). Outside tech, high-end consulting, and English teaching, though, local hiring for foreigners is genuinely thin — most guides recommend arriving with remote income or savings rather than counting on the local job market to absorb you quickly (uruguay.com, Wage Centre).

### The Reckoning

The instinct is to read Tunisia as "loyal" and Uruguay as "mobile," but that's backwards. Tunisian tenure is frequently involuntary — professionals stay because the system offers few better internal options, not because they've been rewarded for staying. Uruguayan mobility is closer to a genuine market feature: strong worker protections coexist comfortably with frequent job changes because the protections travel with tenure at any given employer, not with the employee's total career.

The other inversion is speed versus structure. Uruguay's relational, unhurried daily pace — loose punctuality, real work-life balance — sits alongside a fast-moving external labor market, especially in tech. Tunisia's more rigid, hierarchical daily structure sits alongside a nearly frozen internal one. A newcomer expecting pace of life and pace of career to move together in either country will be surprised twice.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A Tunisian professional exploring remote work for North American employers described treating the international remote job market less as a side option and more as the actual career ladder his domestic market couldn't offer, since promotions at home were, in his words, tied more to who you knew than what you'd delivered.*

> *r/expats — Someone who relocated to Montevideo for a tech role wrote that the biggest adjustment wasn't the job market itself but recalibrating how "normal" it was for Uruguayan colleagues to openly discuss interviewing elsewhere without any of the secrecy or awkwardness that would accompany it back home.*

> *Expat.com (Tunisia forum) — A contributor fielding questions from Tunisians looking to work abroad noted that the practical bottleneck usually wasn't skill or language but the sheer difficulty of building a reference network strong enough to vouch for a candidate internationally, since domestic tenure rarely produced the kind of portfolio-building experience foreign employers wanted to see.*

> *Quora — Someone weighing a move to Uruguay was advised bluntly that outside of tech, consulting, or teaching English, expats should not expect the local job market to hire them quickly, and that arriving without savings or remote income was, in the responder's phrase, "a plan to fail slowly."*

> *The Local — An interview with a Montevideo-based recruiter described Uruguayan candidates as unusually comfortable negotiating counter-offers with their current employer once they had a competing offer in hand, treating it as a normal negotiating tactic rather than a betrayal of loyalty, a framing the recruiter said still surprises transplants from more tenure-focused cultures.*

### Conclusion

If career mobility matters to you, the practical question isn't which country is "better" for ambition — it's whether the mobility you want exists inside the local system or requires you to plug into a different one entirely. In Tunisia, ambition frequently means building an external, often remote or international, career path alongside or instead of a domestic one. In Uruguay, ambition means playing a genuinely fluid local market well, provided you're in one of the sectors where that market actually exists. My honest read, over a drink: in Tunis, network outward; in Montevideo, network up — and learn Spanish either way, because nobody's career moves on English alone.
