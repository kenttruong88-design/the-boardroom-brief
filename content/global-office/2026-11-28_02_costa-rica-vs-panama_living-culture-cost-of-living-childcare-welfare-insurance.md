# Costa Rica and Panama Offer Pura Vida and Dollar Bills, Each With a Waiting Room

🇨🇷 Costa Rica · 🇵🇦 Panama

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Costa Rica insists that every legal resident join its public health system. Panama insists that the public health system is for people with a payroll. According to The Rio Times, Costa Rican cover is "not something you can decline in favour of private insurance", while the Panamanian equivalent treats only contributors and registered dependents, leaving retirees and anyone paid from abroad to shop privately. Both countries are warm, democratic and extremely photogenic, and both will let a family run up a startling bill in the unglamorous parts of life.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Costa Rica

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with the Caja (CCSS) as soon as residency allows, since enrolment is expected at renewal | Assume private insurance lets you skip the Caja |
| Budget a 10.83 percent employee deduction on salary, or a share of assessed income if self-employed | Forget that employers add a much larger contribution on top |
| Register early for bilingual daycares in Escazu or Tamarindo, where waiting lists can run several months | Arrive in February and expect a daycare place by Monday |
| Register a nanny with the Caja and budget for the aguinaldo, the mandatory thirteenth-month bonus | Hire household help informally and hope nobody asks |
| Pair the Caja with a discount plan or private insurance for specialist care | Rely on public specialist queues for anything with a deadline |
| Time your arrival around the school calendar, late February for local schools | Assume the international school calendar matches the public one |

### Panama

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm that your employer registers you with the Caja de Seguro Social within six working days of starting | Assume a remote contract paid from abroad qualifies you for public care |
| Price private insurance before accepting an offer, noting that many plans close to new applicants at 55 or 65 | Leave insurance until after you arrive and a diagnosis |
| Check how a plan treats pre-existing conditions, which local plans often exclude | Buy a policy because the premium is low |
| Use the Pensionado card if you qualify, for discounts on doctors, pharmacies and clinics | Assume the proposed 30 percent medicine discount has been signed into law |
| Ask whether your preferred daycare is a CAIPI centre or a private one | Plan around public daycare for a child under two |
| Budget for air conditioning, since electricity is part of the weather | Compare rent alone and call it a cost-of-living comparison |

## Costa Rica: A Universal Queue With a Pleasant Climate

Costa Rica's welfare offer is unusually inclusive for the region, and unusually patient. Under Caja rules reported by The Rio Times in September 2026, residents outside payroll pay roughly 7.05 to 19.11 percent of an income the Caja assesses, with a floor of 346,789 colones a month for health cover from January 2026. Salaried workers pay 10.83 percent and employers add 26.83 percent. In return, the system delivers emergency care on arrival and a patient wait for everything else: Caja data to 31 July 2026 show 391,314 people waiting for a specialist, for an average of 565 days, and 211,650 on surgical lists for 452 days on average.

An internal audit reported by The Tico Times in July 2026 found 1,440,547 cases across three waiting lists as of March, up 31.7 percent from December 2024. The audit counts cases, not distinct people, and it attributes the missed targets to specialist shortages, unused operating theatres and weak data management. A Tico Times contributor describing her first use of the Caja in 2023 found it free, but needed three visits and a coupon queue starting at 12:40 in the afternoon, summing it up as "not hassle-free but good for those who have enough time to spare."

Those who lack the time go private, and Costa Rica prices that option modestly. The Tico Times quoted 40 to 80 dollars for a general doctor's visit and 80 to 150 for a specialist, and The Rio Times noted that the big hospital groups' membership schemes, such as a 16 dollar a month plan at CIMA, are discount cards rather than insurance. The distinction matters at the moment of a hospital bill, which is when most people first read the small print. The sensible arrangement, widely described, is to treat the Caja as the baseline and a private plan as the express lane.

The cost of living is no longer the bargain the postcards imply. The Tico Times put a single inland budget at 1,600 to 2,200 dollars a month in June 2026, with two-bedroom rent between 500 and 900 dollars inland and well over 1,000 in Escazu. Crowdsourced data from livingcost.org, updated in June 2026, put a single person's monthly costs at 1,402 dollars and a family's at 3,445, with daycare or preschool at 733 dollars a month. Expat.com's family guide, from March 2026, said private daycare runs from 300 to 800 dollars a month, with annual registration and materials fees on top, while public school is free and compulsory from age four. International schools run from 4,000 to over 15,000 dollars a year. Hofstede's dimensions, as reproduced in a University of North Carolina table, give Costa Rica a power distance of 35, which is low, and an individualism score of 15, which is collectivist, a combination that may explain why officials are approachable and why a nation will stand patiently in a queue.

## Panama: Dollars, Discounts and a Differently Priced Queue

Panama's public system is contributory, and it is clear about who is invited. Law 51 of 2005 requires every worker, Panamanian or foreign, to join the Caja de Seguro Social, and a March 2025 law extended the duty to self-employed foreigners with formal Panamanian income, who pay 9.36 percent of taxable income for disability, old age and death. The Rio Times noted that the Caja does not treat retirees or remote workers paid from abroad. Its backlog also fell sharply, from 47,815 pending cases to 18,322 after a recovery plan in 2025, though a 2024 document still counted 20,755 patients waiting more than 90 days for surgery.

For everyone else there is private care and a premium. The same report put a specialist consultation in Panama City at 60 to 300 dollars, a family plan for ages 60 to 79 at roughly 117 to 146 dollars a month, and an emergency visit with tests at 500 to 1,500. Many plans close to new applicants at 55 or 65 and exclude pre-existing conditions. The Pensionado card softens the blow, with 20 percent off doctors and pharmacies and 15 percent off private hospital bills, and the National Assembly voted 47 to 1 in August 2026 to lift the medicine discount to 30 percent, pending the president. Public MINSA clinics charge about one to three dollars a visit. At the other end of the scale, an air ambulance to the United States costs between 20,000 and 200,000 dollars, and most health plans do not cover it, which is the sort of footnote that deserves a larger font.

Childcare is the sharper contrast. An April 2026 Inter-American Development Bank blog found only 22 percent of Panamanian children under five were enrolled in early childhood education, with coverage for children aged zero to two below one percent. The ministry runs 95 comprehensive care centres serving around 2,600 children, rated of moderate average quality in a 2018 assessment. Livingcost.org puts daycare or preschool at 503 dollars a month, against Costa Rica's 733, and rent for a family at 1,343 against 1,032. Hofstede's table gives Panama a power distance of 95, which marks it as a society comfortable with hierarchy, and which a newcomer would be wise to remember when dealing with a counter clerk. Its individualism score of 11 is lower than Costa Rica's 15, so both lean collectivist, and in Panama the IDB found that families, mainly mothers, provide care for about three in four children under five. Scores of this kind describe national averages, not the nurse who will treat you, and the table is an old reproduction.

## The Reckoning

On the numbers, neither country is cheap and neither is expensive, which is a more useful result than it sounds. A single person pays about the same in both, 1,402 dollars in Costa Rica against 1,456 in Panama, while a family pays slightly more in Costa Rica, largely because of childcare. Panama's rent is higher and its groceries lower, and it uses the US dollar, which centralamerica.com notes is a relief to expats avoiding exchange charges. The decisive difference is not the monthly total but who is covered when something goes wrong.

Costa Rica gives a resident family a public safety net that is wide, cheap and slow, and requires joining it. Panama gives a salaried employee a public system and a self-funded private market, and gives a contractor paid from abroad the second option only. For a family with young children, Costa Rica offers private daycare at a price, and Panama offers a public service that barely reaches the youngest. Neither will rescue a family that arrives with no insurance plan and a vague belief in good weather.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Tico Times (first-person, paraphrased): A contributor found the Caja free and the doctor English-speaking, but needed three trips and a late-lunchtime queue for next-day coupons. Private cover was faster, though she paid part of it herself.*

> *Expat.com (Panama healthcare thread, paraphrased): One poster, describing herself as a US nurse, said private clinic visits cost about forty to fifty dollars with waits rarely beyond a day. Another warned that insurance is hard to get at an older age and that local plans often limit pre-existing conditions.*

> *Expat.com (Costa Rica family thread, paraphrased): A grandparent said private school is the realistic option for quality, at roughly 600 to 1,200 dollars a month, billed for twelve months, with books and supplies extra.*

> *centralamerica.com (Facebook expat groups, paraphrased): Some expats judged Panama's medical system better value, with one reporting an emergency visit including lab work and x-rays for under 700 dollars, while others found Costa Ricans warmer and Costa Rican staff more attentive. The piece itself notes the views are subjective.*

> *Travelbinger (retiree blog, paraphrased): A retiree who spent two years in Panama said the cost advantage over the United States is shrinking, that heavy rain lasts around eight months, and that residency paperwork needs patience and ideally a local professional.*

## Conclusion

If you are relocating on a salary with a registered employer, both countries work, and Panama's contributory system and dollar economy are straightforward. If you are paid from abroad, Panama is a private-insurance country and you should price the premium before the lease. If you plan to stay legally in Costa Rica, join the Caja, buy a private plan for the specialist you will eventually need, and register for daycare before you have picked a neighbourhood.

Both countries will make a family feel welcome, which is the cheapest part of the arrangement. Ask for the insurance and childcare numbers in writing, and keep a spare month of savings for the day the brochure meets the waiting list.
