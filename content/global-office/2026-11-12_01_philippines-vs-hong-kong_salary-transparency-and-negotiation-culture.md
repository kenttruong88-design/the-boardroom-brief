# The Philippines Guarantees a Thirteenth Month and Dreads the Number; Hong Kong Keeps the Number Secret and Welcomes the Asking

🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two economies in the same time zone have arrived at opposite habits about the one subject every offer letter eventually forces. In the Philippines, the law obliges nearly every rank-and-file employer to pay a thirteenth month, yet naming your own price in an interview can still feel like boasting. In Hong Kong, no law requires an employer to tell you anything, and a bonus is a custom rather than a right, yet the city treats a polite counteroffer as ordinary commerce. According to the 2026 Salary Pulse survey from Jobsdb by SEEK, 60 percent of Filipino respondents had asked for a rise, against 48 percent in Hong Kong, the least proactive market in the region. Anyone moving between the two should expect to unlearn something.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In the Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate at the offer stage, when the employer has already spent time and money choosing you | Accept the first figure out of politeness, which is the habit the culture quietly trains |
| Mention a competing offer, which commenters report reliably moves a recruiter's number | Invent an offer you do not hold, which is hard to sustain once a recruiter asks for details |
| Check that your offer includes the statutory thirteenth month, due by December 24 | Assume managerial grades are covered, because the rule generally excludes them |
| Ask for the role's budget before naming a figure | Lower your ask after naming it, which reads as weakness in a managerial hire |
| Weigh allowances, leave and remote days alongside base pay | Treat a gross figure as comparable across employers without checking the allowances |
| Prepare a short evidence sheet of results before the conversation | Present your case as personal need rather than market value |

### In Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Name a specific figure, and let the employer speak first if possible | Offer a round number with no research behind it |
| Ask whether the bonus is guaranteed, and in how many months | Count a year-end bonus as base pay before it is in writing |
| Expect to be asked for your last drawn salary, and prepare a reasoned answer | Be surprised when a modest rise is called normal and a large one needs a case |
| Negotiate housing, health cover and relocation if you are moving in | Assume the employer will account for a spouse who cannot easily earn |
| Present specialist skills and measurable results when asking above the usual range | Wait for a review cycle in a year of flat pay settlements |
| Get every agreed term in the contract | Rely on a verbal promise from a manager who may be gone by spring |

## Philippines

The Philippines has a statute where many countries have a hope. Presidential Decree 851 requires covered employers to pay one-twelfth of an employee's annual basic salary as a thirteenth month, no later than December 24. According to the summary at laborlaw.ph, regular, probationary, casual and fixed-term rank-and-file workers qualify, while managerial staff and purely commission-based workers generally do not. This is a floor, and it is a good one. The ceiling is the awkward part, because the culture around reaching for it is more reticent than the law.

JobStreet Philippines observes that many Filipinos are raised to be modest about money, and some are taught to accept an offer rather than counter it. Describing your worth aloud can feel, in the Tagalog phrase it quotes, like blowing your own horn. The Post, a Philippine outlet, recalled a viral 2021 exchange in which a fresh graduate reportedly declined a 37,000-peso offer expecting 60,000; the figures came from a social media post and were never verified, but the public argument that followed treated open talk about pay as unusually taboo. The same piece argued that the ability to refuse an offer is a privilege, because many workers accept whatever arrives to cover bills.

Yet the numbers suggest that asking works. In Salary Pulse, 60 percent of Filipino respondents had requested a rise and 85 percent of those succeeded, among the highest success rates in the region. Forum evidence agrees: Filipino developers describe recruiters opening low and moving once a competing offer appears. Pay secrecy is the practical hurdle. An Inquirer business columnist advised employers that banning wage talk is unlikely to work and tends to backfire, which tells you that the ban is being attempted. JobStreet suggests that where an employer will not meet your figure, you can accept a lower offer with a written salary review after about six months, and that perks such as remote days, extra leave or health cover are worth pricing in. Expect to be asked for an expected salary early, and expect to be underpaid if you are modest.

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong is more candid about the market and less candid about the individual. Lewis Silkin notes that no Hong Kong legislation prohibits pay confidentiality clauses or mandates pay transparency, so a secrecy clause in your contract is generally binding, though discrimination law may complicate one that hides unequal pay. Michael Page reports that some clients ask it to remove salary ranges from advertisements, particularly for replacement hires, and that larger conglomerates generally prefer to keep pay confidential. The same firm says employers often want your last drawn salary to justify an offer internally, and that job changers commonly aim for a rise of roughly 10 to 20 percent. The ritual is ordinary; the information is rationed. Michael Page also cites a 2022 Jobsdb finding that 30 percent of Hong Kong respondents look at the salary in an advertisement first, which explains why the advertisements that omit it irritate people.

The negotiation itself is courteous and numerate. Robert Half advises naming a precise figure rather than a round one, letting the employer go first where possible, and negotiating the package: bonus, flexibility and development, not just salary. The package matters because the headline is not generous. The Jobsdb 2027 survey, reported by Human Resources Online, found employers expecting an average pay change of 1.44 percent next year, with information technology highest at 2.97 percent. Eighty percent of employers had paid a bonus or double pay in the past twelve months, averaging 1.34 months of salary, which is why the question of whether it is guaranteed deserves asking aloud. The same survey found 47 percent of employers saying hiring is becoming harder, which is the leverage a candidate in an in-demand field can quietly use.

Employees are less bold than the process allows. Salary Pulse found that 48 percent of Hong Kong respondents had asked for a rise and 77 percent of those received one, roughly 37 percent of everyone surveyed. About 70 percent felt fairly or well paid, but only 34 percent were satisfied with their salary, and a quarter of those refused a raise said they would simply look for another job. Silence is a strategy here, and a costly one: the people who leave rarely negotiate on the way out.

## The Reckoning

On the three questions that matter to a mover, the contest is closer than the law suggests. On transparency, neither country offers much; the Philippines mandates one benefit but not disclosure of ranges, and Hong Kong mandates neither. On willingness to ask, Filipino workers report doing so more often and winning more often, despite a culture that frames it as immodest. On the quality of the conversation, Hong Kong's is cooler and more transactional, which suits anyone who finds warmth an obstacle to arithmetic. Hofstede Insights scores the Philippines at 94 for power distance against 68 for Hong Kong, figures I could only reach through secondary compilations of the Hofstede data, but they fit the pattern: where deference is highest, the opening number is most likely to be the employer's.

The practical conclusion is that the Philippines rewards the candidate who overcomes a social instinct, while Hong Kong rewards the candidate who has done the homework. A Filipino offer may be low because you were polite; a Hong Kong offer may be low because the year is. Neither city will volunteer the range, so bring your own. Treat the thirteenth month as a legal entitlement in one and a negotiating point in the other, and read the contract before the handshake rather than after.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/PinoyProgrammer — a fresh-graduate developer turned down a 20k offer, collected others around 30k and 40k, told a foreign employer about the competition and saw a 40k offer rise to 45k. Commenters agreed that mentioning other offers moves HR numbers, and one cited a friend whose pay jumped from 25k to 65k.*

> *Reddit r/HongKong — a parent who relocated a family of six described an offer in the region of HK$70,000 to 80,000 as a steep cut. One commenter judged the figure solid by local standards, reminded them that rent and dependants carry tax benefits, and confirmed that negotiating salary in Hong Kong is acceptable.*

> *Levels.fyi community — a candidate for a Hong Kong tech role who had named US$102K as an expectation worried about underselling. The only reply said that looked low and urged a higher ask, while admitting it lacked direct Hong Kong data. This voice speaks to tech hiring, an adjacent theme.*

> *Expat.com forum — a person weighing a Hong Kong offer for a spouse was advised to rank the package: health cover for both partners first, then a housing allowance even at the cost of base pay, then flights and a fortnight of hotel on arrival. This voice speaks to expat packages, an adjacent theme.*

## Conclusion

If you are weighing the two, decide which discomfort you tolerate better. The Philippines asks you to speak up in a culture that prefers modesty, and pays off with a statutory thirteenth month and a record of success for those who ask. Hong Kong asks you to bargain in a market that will not publish its ranges, with pay settlements averaging low single digits and a bonus that depends on the year.

In both, the strategy is the same and slightly undignified: research the range, name a number, and put every agreed term in writing. The one honest difference is that in Manila you will be thanked for the effort, and in Hong Kong you will merely be paid, or not, with equal courtesy.
