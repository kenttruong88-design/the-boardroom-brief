# Mint Tea vs. the Mentorship Matrix: Getting Onboarded in Casablanca and Singapore

**🇲🇦 Morocco · 🇸🇬 Singapore**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Singapore will assign you a mentor on your first day, hand you an orientation deck covering the company's mission and values, and schedule your first check-in before you've found the good coffee machine. Morocco will pour you mint tea and simply wait — sometimes for weeks — until enough shared conversation has accumulated that colleagues decide you can actually be trusted with anything that matters. Both are onboarding. Only one of them shows up in a calendar invite.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇦 Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept every offer of tea, coffee, or a shared meal in your first weeks — it's the real onboarding | Rush past hospitality rituals to "get to the point" |
| Expect a long lunch break (1–2 hours) as standard, not a special occasion | Schedule tight midday meetings that assume a Western lunch hour |
| Let trust build gradually before pushing for decisions or commitments | Expect colleagues to open up or commit quickly just because you're new and eager |
| Ask questions in informal settings — over tea, not just in meetings | Treat all business as something that only happens at a desk |
| Respect the hierarchy while still building warm personal ties across levels | Assume hierarchy and warmth are mutually exclusive |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a structured, scheduled onboarding with an assigned mentor from day one | Assume you'll be left to figure out unspoken norms alone |
| Use your mentor to learn the real, unwritten rules — not just the employee handbook | Rely solely on official documentation for how things actually work |
| Match the pace — Singapore's work culture rewards visible competence quickly | Assume a slow, relationship-first ramp-up period like other markets |
| Build genuine professional connections; they matter for advancement | Treat networking as optional once you're technically onboarded |
| Prepare for real performance pressure from the start | Expect an extended, low-stakes grace period as a newcomer |

---

Morocco's onboarding is, by design, undocumented. Commisceo Global's business guide is explicit that Moroccans "are mainly comfortable dealing with people they know" and take real time to extend trust, meaning the actual onboarding process is social before it's procedural — a new hire's most important task in month one isn't learning the org chart, it's being seen enough, over enough shared tea and long lunches, to be read as known. Expat Focus's guide to Casablanca office culture backs this with texture: the working day runs a fairly standard 9-to-5, but with a genuinely long midday break (one to two hours) that isn't downtime so much as another venue for the same relationship-building that happens in meetings — colleagues who spend that hour together are doing onboarding, whether or not anyone calls it that. The practical risk for a foreign hire is impatience: pushing to "get productive" faster than the social trust curve allows can read as presumptuous rather than diligent.

Singapore runs the opposite model — onboarding as an explicitly engineered HR function. Local guidance from the Access Group and Singapore's own MyCareersFuture platform describe a structured orientation covering company mission and values, department introductions, and — critically — an assigned mentor whose specific job is translating "the unspoken rules and expectations... traditions, employee jokes, and other casual insider information" that would otherwise take months to absorb informally. This isn't a soft perk: a frequently-cited Sun Microsystems study found mentee retention at 72% versus 49% for unmentored hires, and Singaporean employers have clearly internalized the number, building mentorship into onboarding as standard infrastructure rather than an optional nicety. The tradeoff is pace — the same structure that gets you productive fast also means you're expected to be visibly competent fast, in a workplace culture Quora respondents describe bluntly as workaholic, with intense pressure to perform well from a very early stage.

### The Reckoning

The ironic reversal is which country's system is actually faster for a genuinely competent newcomer. Singapore's structured onboarding gets you functionally productive within days, but real acceptance — the informal trust that makes people advocate for you — still has to be earned separately and can lag well behind your technical ramp-up, especially given the "pleasant but not warm" social reputation several Quora respondents flagged. Morocco's onboarding looks slower and less legible on paper, but once the social trust threshold is crossed, it tends to produce a more complete, personally-vouched-for form of belonging than a fast HR process alone typically achieves — the mint tea was never wasted time, it was the actual mechanism. A newcomer optimizing for the wrong metric — speed in Morocco, warmth in Singapore — will misjudge how well their onboarding is actually going in both places.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Responding to a question on whether it's worth working in Singapore as a foreigner, one respondent described Singaporeans as "pleasant but not particularly warm," and noted that professional respect arrived faster than any real social inclusion did — a distinction the poster wished someone had flagged before they mistook politeness for friendship.*

> *Quora — Another answer on Singapore's work culture referenced "kiasu" (a Hokkien term rooted in fear of losing out) as the underlying anxiety driving the country's intensity, explaining that new hires who don't visibly demonstrate competence quickly can find themselves quietly deprioritized regardless of formal onboarding support.*

> *Expat.com Casablanca guide (aggregated contributor account) — A contributor described initially feeling like onboarding "wasn't really happening" in their first two weeks in a Casablanca office, before realizing that the long lunches and tea breaks they'd been treating as downtime were, in fact, the entire process.*

> *Expat Focus Morocco office guide — One recurring piece of advice for newcomers was to actively accept every informal invitation in the first month rather than waiting to be formally briefed, since colleagues' willingness to share real information tracked closely with how much unstructured time had been spent together first.*

> *Quora — A respondent to "What is it like to work and live in Singapore" specifically for non-citizens noted that professional connections mattered enormously for career progression, and that newcomers who treated their assigned mentor purely as a technical resource — rather than a relationship worth investing in past the formal onboarding period — often missed opportunities that came through informal channels later.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving for a role in Morocco, resist the instinct to treat hospitality as a distraction from "real work" — the tea and the long lunch are the onboarding, and skipping them to look productive will actually slow down how quickly you're trusted with anything meaningful. If you're moving to Singapore, take the structured mentor program seriously as more than orientation paperwork, and understand that surviving the onboarding deck is table stakes — the real test, and the real pressure, starts immediately after. The honest version I'd give a friend: in Morocco, slow down on purpose; in Singapore, speed up on purpose — and in both places, the mentor or the tea companion in front of you is worth more than the handbook.
