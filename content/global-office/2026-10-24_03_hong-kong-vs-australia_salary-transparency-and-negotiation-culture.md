### Hong Kong Keeps Its Payslip in a Locked Drawer. Australia Just Mailed You a Copy.

🇭🇰 Hong Kong · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Hong Kong, asking a colleague what they earn is roughly as socially acceptable as asking what they weigh. In Australia, since December 2022, your employer can no longer legally stop you from asking at all. Only 5.5% of Hong Kong professionals say they are comfortable discussing their pay with coworkers, according to Robert Walters' latest Salary Survey, while across the Tasman-adjacent mainland, Canberra has made pay secrecy clauses unenforceable by statute. One jurisdiction treats salary as a state secret; the other has started treating it as a workplace right. Guess which one still has a 22.8% gender pay gap anyway.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate hard before signing — renegotiation windows close fast after | Don't ask a colleague's salary in month one; it reads as a breach, not small talk |
| Check recruiter portals (Michael Page, Robert Walters) since public data is thin | Don't expect a salary band on the job ad — only 48% of seekers even see one |
| Treat the 13th-month bonus and allowances as negotiable, not fixed | Don't assume a confidentiality clause won't be enforced — it commonly is |
| Use a competing offer as leverage — the most reliable lever here | Don't mention a teammate's pay in a review; it reads as a trust violation |
| Ask about total package (housing, medical, flights) over base pay | Don't expect HR to volunteer a range unprompted |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the Fair Work award rate before any interview — most bands are public | Don't lowball assuming no one checks; colleagues now compare notes freely |
| Expect to start near the bottom of the range without scarce skills | Don't let a post-2022 pay-secrecy clause intimidate you — it's unenforceable |
| Factor superannuation (12%) into any total-package comparison | Don't assume a verbal offer is locked — get it in writing before resigning |
| Use the ad's stated range as your opening anchor, not your ceiling | Don't skip asking about penalty rates if the role is award-covered |
| Push back if HR "clarifies" a figure downward after you've accepted | Don't assume transparency has closed the pay gap — it hasn't |

Hong Kong's reticence around pay is not folklore; it is measurable. Robert Walters' most recent Salary Survey found 24% of Gen Z professionals are willing to discuss earnings with colleagues, a figure that drops to 4% for Millennials and 2% for Gen X — a culture that gets quieter about money precisely as workers accumulate more of it. There is, notably, no legislation in Hong Kong prohibiting pay confidentiality clauses or mandating transparency, per employment law commentary circulated via Mondaq, so the taboo runs on custom and contract rather than statute. The upside, perversely, is that those who do ask tend to do well: Jobsdb by SEEK's Salary Pulse: Hong Kong, surveying 1,010 workers in February 2026, found 48% had asked for a raise, and 77% of those who asked received one. The market rewards the bold and punishes the polite.

Negotiation here happens in one-on-one brinkmanship before signing, not in open comparison afterward. Recruiters at Michael Page and Randstad advise treating housing allowances, medical coverage, and the traditional double-pay month as negotiable, since the base salary figure is often where employers have the least room to move. Hofstede Insights' cultural dimension scores place Hong Kong considerably higher on Power Distance than Australia, consistent with a workplace where hierarchy, not peer comparison, governs whether pay gets discussed at all.

Australia took the opposite legislative path. The Fair Work Legislation Amendment (Secure Jobs, Better Pay) Act 2022 made pay secrecy clauses in new contracts unenforceable from 7 December 2022, giving employees a workplace right to disclose, discuss, or ask about pay without fear of adverse action. From January 2023, Section 536AA of the Fair Work Act also barred job ads from advertising pay below the applicable minimum, and the award wage system administered by the Fair Work Commission publishes pay rates for a large share of entry-level and trade roles before a candidate ever interviews.

Yet transparency hasn't closed the gap it targeted. The Workplace Gender Equality Agency's latest scorecard puts Australia's gender pay gap at 22.8%, though research cited alongside the reforms suggests women's wages run 4 to 12 points higher in jurisdictions that ban pay secrecy — implying the gap would likely be worse without the law. The practical experience on the ground, per recruiters placing skilled migrants, is that most new arrivals simply don't negotiate at all, landing at the bottom of the band unless they bring scarce skills.

### The Reckoning

Here is the irony that should keep a management consultant up at night: Hong Kong, with zero legal requirement for pay transparency and a taboo thick enough to suppress discussion across three generations, produces a 77% success rate for employees who actually ask for a raise. Australia, with statutory pay-secrecy bans, published award rates, and penalties for underpaying job ads, still carries a gender pay gap nearly five times the width of Hong Kong's most tight-lipped quintile. Transparency, it turns out, is necessary but nowhere near sufficient — you can hand people the information and still watch them not use it.

The deeper divergence is about who does the asking. Hong Kong's system assumes the individual will fight their own corner, armed with a competing offer and a poker face, in a market built to reward exactly that. Australia's system assumes structure — awards, legislated bands, commission oversight — will do the fighting on the worker's behalf, which serves the award-covered majority well and the white-collar negotiator considerably less, since they still have to ask for anything above the advertised floor.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — a software engineer weighing an Apple role in Hong Kong posted their current pay (US$80K) and target ask (US$102K) openly, unable to find a public benchmark on Levels.fyi; when another user joked that passion for the work shouldn't require caring about money, they replied that passion alone doesn't cover rent.*

> *r/AusFinance — one poster described HR calling back after an accepted offer to say the stated salary had "mistakenly" excluded superannuation; they held firm they'd accepted based on the number given and were prepared to walk, and HR restored the original figure within fifteen minutes.*

> *r/AusFinance — another described a tactic of reacting to any offer with performed disappointment — a scoff, a thank-you, then a figure roughly $10,000 above the offer — on the theory employers hold back $5,000 to $6,000 in reserve and will meet in the middle.*

> *Expat.com Australia forum — a recruiter who placed a tradesperson noted most people arriving in Australia simply don't negotiate, starting at the bottom of the band unless they bring scarce skills, and that total package (super, overtime, bonuses) often matters more than the base figure.*

> *Expat.com Australia forum — a different poster said that without strong local networks, it was hard to land better-paying roles at all, and that some locals seemed to treat new arrivals as a source of money rather than colleagues to negotiate with fairly.*

### Conclusion

If the question is simply "where will I be paid more fairly," Australia's legal framework gives you more to point to and more recourse if a number quietly shrinks after you've accepted. If the question is "where will asking actually get me more money," Hong Kong's own data — a 77% success rate among those who ask — suggests the opaque market rewards nerve more generously than the transparent one rewards patience. The honest answer for anyone moving abroad for work is that neither country will negotiate on your behalf; one just gives you better paperwork to negotiate with.

What I'd tell a friend over a drink: in Hong Kong, find out what you're worth before you sign, because nobody will tell you afterward; in Australia, read the award rate before the interview, then ask for more than it anyway, because the law protecting your right to ask has been on the books since 2022 and most people still haven't used it.
