# Poland's Gen Z Just Got the EU's Sharpest Unemployment Spike. Italy's Has Been Living With It for Years.

**🇵🇱 Poland · 🇮🇹 Italy**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Poland's youth unemployment jumped from 10.6 to 13.5 percent in a single year — the steepest rise anywhere in the EU. Italy's youth unemployment sits at 21.6 percent and has for years, high enough that it barely makes headlines anymore. One country is discovering generational precariousness for the first time in a generation; the other built an entire workplace culture around managing it. Neither country's young workers are thrilled about either arrangement.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect Gen Z colleagues to hold Master's degrees as a baseline, not a differentiator | Assume academic credentials alone signal readiness for corporate pace — many report feeling underprepared |
| Push for flexible hours explicitly — 65% of Polish Gen Z demand it, per Cpl Poland | Assume flexibility is a fringe request rather than a near-majority expectation |
| Bridge communication styles deliberately — younger staff favor informal messaging, older staff favor email | Let generational messaging habits calcify into two separate, non-communicating camps |
| Take the 2023 Work-Life Balance Directive seriously as a real legal shift, not a suggestion | Treat pre-2023 overtime norms as still standard practice |
| Recognize Poland's aging demographic makes younger workers disproportionately relied on for digital skills | Assume digital literacy gaps run only one direction; older colleagues bring different value |
| Watch youth unemployment trends closely if job security matters to your decision | Assume Poland's traditionally low unemployment still fully applies to entry-level hiring |

#### 🇮🇹 Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect fixed-term contracts to be the normal entry point for workers under 30 | Assume a fixed-term offer signals lower commitment from the employer — it's structural, not personal |
| Learn Italian seriously — genuine workplace comfort depends on it, even in international firms | Assume English alone gets you fully included in office relationships |
| Build in the long lunch — up to two hours in Milan is standard, not indulgent | Treat lunch breaks as a productivity leak to minimize |
| Understand Gen Z's high mobility here — average tenure of 1.1 years in early career | Read frequent job changes among young Italian hires as a red flag; it's now the norm |
| Value the emphasis on relationships and hierarchy that spans generations | Expect flat, Anglo-style informality just because younger colleagues dress casually |
| Recognize regional disparities — job markets vary sharply between the north and south | Apply a Milan-based expectation of opportunity to the rest of the country uniformly |

### Poland: A New Precarity, Fast

Poland's generational story is one of sudden change rather than long-standing tension. The country recorded the EU's steepest youth unemployment increase over the past year — a 2.9 percentage-point jump to 13.5 percent, according to [Notes from Poland](https://notesfrompoland.com/2025/07/21/poland-sees-eus-biggest-rise-in-youth-unemployment/) — even as overall unemployment remains among the bloc's lowest. That's a genuinely new experience for a country whose labor market had been comparatively tight for years.

Meanwhile, Poland's Gen Z is arriving in the workforce more credentialed than any generation before it — commonly with Master's degrees — but [Verita HR's research](https://veritahr.com/gen-z-in-the-workplace-what-polands-hr-leaders-need-to-know/) notes that this academic intensity has left many feeling exhausted and underprepared for the actual pace of corporate work. A 2024 Cpl Poland survey found 75 percent of young Polish workers prioritize work-life balance and 65 percent demand flexible hours — expectations reinforced by the EU Work-Life Balance Directive taking legal effect in April 2023. Layer onto this Poland's status as one of Europe's fastest-aging societies, and younger workers increasingly find themselves cast as the office's default "digital natives," a role [EPALE's research](https://epale.ec.europa.eu/en/blog/european-snapshot-generational-diversity-workplace) notes brings its own quiet burden alongside the informal authority it grants.

### Italy: Precarity as the Long-Running Norm

Italy's generational divide isn't new — it's structural and well-worn. At 21.6 percent, Italian youth unemployment is among the highest in the EU, a condition that has persisted long enough to reshape how young Italians actually approach careers. Fixed-term contracts are the default entry point, capped at 36 months under the 2015 Jobs Act, with government incentives nudging companies to hire workers aged 15 to 29 — a policy response to a problem old enough to need one.

The resulting behavior looks like rational adaptation rather than generational impatience: Italian Gen Z workers change jobs at the fastest rate of any generation, averaging just 1.1 years of tenure in their first five career years, and 37 percent say they plan to resign within the year. This creates what researchers describe as a "sandwich" effect — a growing population of workers 55 and older on one side, precarious younger cohorts on the other, and Gen X caught managing both. Notably, though, [academic research specific to Italy](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-81938-4_15) cautions that the popular narrative of sharp generational conflict may itself be overstated, with limited empirical evidence backing the stereotypes HR departments love to cite.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't the unemployment numbers themselves — it's what each culture has had time to build around them. Italy's decades-long familiarity with youth precarity has produced institutional scaffolding: hiring incentives, contract caps, an accepted norm of job-hopping that no longer reads as instability. Poland is encountering a milder version of the same pressure for the first time, without that scaffolding yet in place, which is part of why the shift feels sharper even though Italy's absolute numbers remain worse.

There's a irony worth sitting with: Poland's older, more traditional labor market is producing a workforce that increasingly resembles Italy's — mobile, flexibility-demanding, distrustful of long hours — while Italy's young workers, hardened by over a decade of precarity, have made peace with a level of instability Polish Gen Z is only just starting to encounter.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — A commenter on a viral thread about a startup manager struggling with Gen Z engineers pointed out, as a Baby Boomer working at a large tech company, that the exact same complaint got made about millennials when they arrived, and about Gen X before that — arguing every incoming generation looks chaotic to whoever trained under the previous rules.*

> *Quora — Someone asking about IT hiring in Poland was told bluntly that fluency in Polish still mattered enormously even in the tech sector, and that foreign candidates underestimating this requirement were consistently surprised by how much slower their job search went compared to expectations set by English-language job boards.*

> *Expat.com, Italy forum — A contributor described the two-hour Milan lunch as the actual site of office relationship-building across generations, noting that younger colleagues who treated it as optional or rushed through it were quietly read as less committed, regardless of their actual output.*

> *Quora — A respondent describing daily life in Poland noted that big multinational corporations dominate Warsaw's entry-level hiring pipeline, but observed there simply aren't enough homegrown startups to absorb the country's highly credentialed Gen Z graduates, pushing many toward corporate roles that don't match their expectations.*

> *Expat.com, Milan guide — Another poster warned that fixed-term contracts, while completely normal for young Italian hires, catch foreign transplants off guard when they assume a time-limited offer signals the employer doesn't want to keep them — when in most cases it simply reflects standard entry-level structure.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two on generational dynamics: Poland offers a system still adjusting to new pressure, with institutions and expectations not yet fully reshaped around it — more volatility, less established playbook. Italy offers a system that has already absorbed the shock and built real, if imperfect, structures around it — more predictability, but a longer runway to permanence. Per [Eurostat's latest figures](https://ec.europa.eu/eurostat/statistics-explained/index.php?title=Unemployment_statistics), neither country's youth labor market is easy right now — the difference is just how long each has had to get used to it.

Priya's honest take, over a drink: if you're young and want to at least be surprised by the instability, go to Warsaw. If you'd rather the instability be a known, well-worn quantity with an actual playbook, Milan's your city — just budget two hours for lunch either way.
