# Portugal Passed a Law Against the Boss's Evening Email; France Would Prefer to See You at Your Desk

🇵🇹 Portugal · 🇫🇷 France

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Portugal's Law 83/2021, in force since January 2022, forbids employers from contacting remote staff in their rest periods, makes them pay a share of the electricity and internet bills and bans them from monitoring you at home. France, which has a right to disconnect of its own and a reputation for reading its own legislation more closely, has no automatic right to work remotely at all. If you are choosing where to take a remote or hybrid job, the more protective country on paper is the one that rarely gets the credit, while in France your manager may treat a request to work from home like a request for a day off.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the legal protections: employers must stay out of your rest periods and cover a share of utilities and equipment | Accept being monitored at home; the law forbids it |
| Ask about remote work if you have a child under eight | Assume the right applies to everyone |
| Check your residence permit and tax residency before you start; the 90-day Schengen limit applies | Assume a foreign employer plus a Lisbon flat equals legal |
| Choose the right visa route for your situation, such as the D8 or the D2 | Rely on the tourist stamp |
| Consider smaller towns such as Lagos, Ericeira or Braga | Expect English to work everywhere outside the cities |
| Allow time for bureaucracy | Treat a long lunch as laziness |

### In France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Put any remote-work agreement in writing, including contact hours | Rely on a verbal "yes" from a manager |
| Claim equipment and receipted costs, and keep your meal vouchers | Let your employer withdraw your vouchers because you work from home |
| Expect to be in the office most days, particularly in Paris | Count on working from home most of the week |
| Ask for a reason in writing if a long-term request is refused | Assume there is an automatic right |
| Agree contact hours explicitly | Use the 2017 disconnect law as a substitute for agreement |
| Look at tech and non-customer-facing roles first | Expect a bank branch to let you stay home |

## Portugal

The legal framework is the more interesting half of the Portuguese story. Under Law 83/2021, as summarised by Garrigues, HCA Magazine and Mayer Brown, employers must refrain from contacting staff in rest periods except in cases of force majeure, must not retaliate against employees, must provide equipment and cover a share of the costs of electricity and internet, and may not monitor staff at home. Parents of children up to eight years old can request remote work. The sources agree on the rules and none gives the fines.

Whether Portuguese employers apply those rules enthusiastically is harder to say. The Global Survey of Working Arrangements, run in spring 2023 and reported by Fortune, recorded 1.0 remote days a week in Portugal, against 0.9 in France, a difference that fits within the margin of a decent excuse. Idealista Portugal's report on the nomad scene describes a relaxed pace, long lunches and good work-life balance, with smaller towns more welcoming and the downsides being bureaucracy, a few worries among locals about rents and limited English outside the cities.

For a foreigner, the legal complications are less about the law of telework than about everything around it. Replies on an Expat.com thread stressed that remote work does not override immigration rules: the 90-day Schengen limit applies, a residence permit is needed for work in Portugal and the visa routes depend on the client's location. Another thread, on how to be paid by a foreign employer without a Portuguese entity, produced the unglamorous list of options: employer of record, contractor through an umbrella, or your own Portuguese company, with double-tax treaties covering more than 78 countries. Hofstede's figures for Portugal, from a textbook table, show a power distance of 63 and an individualism of 27, against France's 68 and 71, which describes a country where the team matters more and the manager still matters quite a lot.

## France

France's picture is more mixed than its stereotype suggests. According to Eurostat figures reported by Euronews France on 12 June 2026, 11 percent of French workers in 2025 usually worked from home, above the EU average of 8.8 percent. INSEE's working paper reports that, using a broader definition, 33.7 percent of employed adults in France did some work from home in 2023, against an EU average of 21.4 percent, and private-sector telework was around 23 percent. Yet The Local France, citing 2023 Ifo/Econpol data, reported that the French averaged only 0.6 remote days a week, that employers offered 0.72 and that workers wanted 1.4. The Centre for Cities found that Paris offices average 3.5 days a week, and that 5 percent of Paris workers are fully remote.

The law gives an employee a case without giving them a right. According to The Local France, there is no automatic right to remote work, though employers must give a reason if they refuse a long-term request. They must provide and maintain equipment, fixed costs can be reimbursed on receipts and meal vouchers cannot be withdrawn because someone works from home. A 2017 right to disconnect exists, though agreeing contact hours in the remote arrangement remains the safest practice.

The attitude is the harder part. A commenter with French family in an r/AskEurope thread said most French companies and managers resist remote work out of distrust, and that one manager treated a work-from-home request like a request for a day off. Another, a recruiter and worker in France, said remote work is now common for roles without in-person customer contact, and that resistance comes mainly from firms that own their offices and from managers who want close oversight. Both can be true: the number of people who telework occasionally is large, and the number who do so as a right is small.

The comparison is easiest to read as a question of who bears the cost of the arrangement. In Portugal, the law moves some of it to the employer: electricity, internet and equipment, plus a ban on monitoring and a duty to leave you alone in your rest periods. In France, equipment and receipted costs are also the employer's responsibility, and meal vouchers survive, but the larger cost, the manager's permission, is not regulated at all. A worker choosing between the two should therefore ask a different question in each: in Portugal, whether the employer will honour the law in practice, and in France, whether the manager will honour the agreement. Put both in writing, and keep a copy.

## The Reckoning

If you take the statute books at face value, Portugal is the better employer. It protects your evenings, shares your electricity bill and forbids the manager from watching you in your kitchen. France gives you a case to make and a manager to make it to. The survey numbers, though, put the two countries at around one remote day a week each, which means the Portuguese law is generous mostly to people who already have the job and the arrangement, and the French culture is stubborn mostly to people who do not.

The deciding factor for a foreign worker is paperwork. In Portugal, the remote-work question is largely a visa and tax question: where you are resident, who employs you and whether your employer has an entity there. In France, it is a management question: whether your boss trusts you to work out of sight. A Portuguese immigration lawyer and a French manager are not interchangeable, and each country has far more of one than the other.

## Conclusion

If you intend to work remotely from Portugal for a foreign employer, start with the immigration and tax questions, because the working-time law will not rescue you from a wrong visa. If you intend to work remotely for a Portuguese employer, read your rights under the 2021 law, and use them. If you are moving to France, put the arrangement in writing, expect to be in the office more than you hoped and keep your meal vouchers.

The best predictor in either country is the individual manager. Ask in the interview how many people on the team work from home, and watch how long it takes to answer.
