# Chile Quits Early and Often, Spain Is Learning to Stay Put on Its Third Contract

🇨🇱 Chile · 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Chile, 28% of dependent workers have held their current job for less than a year. The OECD average is 19.6%. Spain, for its part, spent several years employing nearly a third of its Social Security affiliates on temporary contracts, then legislated them into something better. One country is learning to move on. The other is learning to stay. For someone choosing where to build a career, the question is whether loyalty is a virtue or a clause in the contract.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for the contract in writing and read the termination clause before the salary clause. | Assume severance is automatic; it generally depends on the reason for dismissal. |
| Keep a clear record of your length of service; the severance rule counts completed years. | Resign at month eleven of year one without checking what you forgo. |
| Build relationships; offices run on them, and personal and professional lives tend to blur. | Treat a colleague's invitation home as a social obligation you can ignore. |
| Expect a competitive offer to shift your pay quickly; the data suggests many move for conditions. | Present a job move as disloyalty; the market treats it as routine. |
| Learn local Spanish and its idioms, which expats say matter at work. | Expect meetings to start on time. |

### Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask which type of contract you are being offered, indefinido, temporal or fijo discontinuo. | Assume an indefinite contract guarantees you the job indefinitely. |
| Read the finiquito before signing, and keep a copy. | Sign without checking what the employer owes you. |
| Track your years of service; unfair dismissal compensation is calculated by year. | Resign casually if you expect to claim unemployment benefit; expat threads suggest this is where it goes wrong. |
| Treat your first year as probation by another name. | Assume a permanent contract survives its first year. |
| Take a second opinion from a labour adviser when something looks off. | Rely on a forum thread, including this article. |

## Chile: The Country That Left the Building

OECD data, reported by the Chilean newspaper Emol in February 2026, put the share of dependent workers with under a year of tenure at 28%, against an OECD average of 19.6%. Chile ranked fourth in the group, behind Colombia, Turkey and South Korea, according to the analysis by Juan Bravo of the OCEC at Universidad Diego Portales. Bravo used the figure to argue for replacing the severance mechanism with a more robust unemployment insurance system, an idea that has been included in a document drafted by a group of economists. In other words, the people who study the churn would like the safety net to follow the worker rather than the job, which is a reasonable view and a mildly alarming one for employers who budget for loyalty. The Inter-American Development Bank adds a regional comparison: Latin American workers stay with one employer for about 7.7 years, 2.5 years less than in advanced OECD countries. That figure covers the region, not Chile alone.

The mobility is not purely rebellious. Randstad Chile, as reported by Diario Financiero in June 2019, put the job-change rate at 26.7% in the first quarter of that year, with workers aged 18 to 24 changing jobs most, at 38%. The top trigger was improving employment conditions, cited by 37% of respondents. The same Randstad manager conceded that a substantial raise tends to prompt a move, and that changing jobs can keep workers motivated and learning, while adding that frequent moves worry employers, who value loyalty and commitment. This is the sort of thing employers say while posting vacancies. The survey period is now several years old, so it is better read as a direction than a current rate. The 2026 Mercer Global Talent Trends survey, reported by 24horas, found that workers stay for positive culture (52%), control over when and where they work (40%), and fair pay (37%), with job security at 34%. Pay, it seems, is no longer enough by itself, which employers will find a novelty.

The legal backdrop matters. According to 24horas, severance for years of service is 30 days of the last monthly pay per year, capped at 330 days, or 11 years, and at a monthly base of 90 UF. It requires a year of service and applies when the employer ends the contract for business needs. Meanwhile Hofstede Insights scores Chile at 86 for uncertainty avoidance, 63 for power distance and 49 for individualism, a profile that suggests a preference for rules and hierarchy. Chile appears to have found a way to be rule-bound and restless at the same time. The Mercer data suggest it is not obviously a contradiction: workers say they stay for culture and flexibility, which are matters of management rather than of national character. People who move freely tend to be the ones whose contracts allow it.

## Spain: The Permanent Contract, Reconsidered

Spain's story begins with an unusually high temporary employment rate. According to CaixaBank Research, temporary employment among Social Security affiliates averaged 29.7% from 2014 to 2019 and fell to 12.7% at the end of 2024, after the labour reform adopted in December 2021. The same analysis finds that contract turnover fell too: contracts registered fell from about 20.3 million a year to 15.4 million in 2024. On the evidence, Spaniards are signing fewer contracts and keeping more of them. The overall monthly job turnover rate fell from 1.30% to 1.10%, though turnover among temporary workers rose from 3.84% to 4.32%, so the reform has thinned the temporary workforce without calming those who remain in it. Temporary contracts that survive a year improved from 11% to 16%, which is progress of the kind that is easier to celebrate in a press release than in a payslip.

The reform has not made every contract durable. CaixaBank Research reports that the share of permanent contracts, excluding fijo discontinuo, still in force one year after signing fell from 52.5% to 48%, which suggests that some of the instability moved rather than vanished. Turnover among permanent workers rose from 0.25% to 0.67% monthly. The share of employed people becoming unemployed in the next quarter fell from 3.9% to 2.9%, against an EU average the analysis puts at 1.3%. Spain has therefore achieved the feat of improving stability while still sitting well above the European norm for job loss.

For those who are dismissed, Spain's rule is fixed in law. According to a legal explainer on unfair dismissal, compensation is 33 days of salary per year of service, with a cap of 24 monthly payments. Spaniards tend to stay where the contract is secure, and the data suggest the contract itself, more than any national temperament, decides how long they stay. Hofstede Insights scores for Spain could not be retrieved, so no cultural comparison is offered.

## The Reckoning

Chile and Spain have arrived at opposite ends of the same dilemma. Chile offers a flexible market in which short tenure is common and, by its own survey evidence, tied to conditions as much as to pay. Spain offers a market that has recently narrowed the gap between temporary and permanent work, while still losing a large share of new permanent contracts within a year. In Chile, staying is a choice made repeatedly. In Spain, staying has been made somewhat more likely, and therefore somewhat more rewarding.

For the mobile professional, the practical difference is who carries the risk. In Chile, severance for years of service rewards long tenure only up to eleven years and only when the employer ends the contract, which leaves the worker with the incentive to move when conditions are better. In Spain, the 33-day rule rewards tenure for longer. Neither country has produced loyalty by decree, though Spain is attempting it with a clearer instrument. The newcomer should also note that the Spanish figures describe affiliates to the general Social Security regime, and the Chilean ones describe dependent employees, so the two sets of numbers illustrate direction rather than allow a strict ranking.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — a poster in Chile described employers as closed to other points of view on how work should be done, and advised becoming self-employed rather than depending on one.*

> *Expat.com forum — a former expat described Chilean offices where personal and professional life blur, colleagues expect to be invited home, and meetings start late.*

> *Expat Arrivals — a long-term expat married to a Chilean said the job market is difficult for outsiders to enter and that learning Spanish is essential.*

> *Expat.com forum — in a 2014 Spanish thread, one poster said an employee who resigns loses the finiquito and another said they keep it but lose unemployment benefit, which is the sort of disagreement a contract exists to settle.*

> *British Expats forum — posters agreed that without a signed finiquito there is no formal claim against the employer, and that unemployment benefit cannot be claimed until it is signed.*

## Conclusion

If you intend to change employers every few years, Chile will not object, and the data suggest you will have company. Read the severance rule first, because it rewards tenure to a point and not beyond. If you want security, Spain's post-2021 market offers more of it than it used to, but keep a copy of everything and do not assume that a permanent contract is a lifelong one.

Loyalty, in both countries, turns out to be a function of what leaving costs. Employers who wish to be loved should start by making departure less attractive.
