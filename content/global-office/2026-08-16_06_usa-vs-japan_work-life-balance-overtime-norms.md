# America Doesn't Take Its Vacation Days. Japan Doesn't Even Report Its Overtime.

**🇺🇸 USA · 🇯🇵 Japan**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The United States is the only advanced economy with no federal law guaranteeing any paid leave at all, and the workers it does grant vacation to — an average of 15 days after enough tenure — still leave roughly 3 unused every year, part of a national total north of 375 million forfeited paid vacation days annually. Japan guarantees leave by law and still can't get its own workforce to take it: employees are allotted about 19 paid vacation days a year and use only 63% of them, with 63% of respondents in one survey admitting they feel guilty taking the days they're owed. Two entirely different systems have converged on the same outcome — a workforce that won't rest — by entirely different mechanisms of guilt.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate PTO explicitly during hiring — it's rarely standardized, so the number on offer is often negotiable | Assume any number of vacation days is guaranteed by law — the US has zero federal minimum, unlike almost every peer economy |
| Expect after-hours email responsiveness to be quietly monitored, even without a stated policy | Assume "unlimited PTO" policies mean more time off in practice — research consistently shows employees under them take less, not more, out of ambiguity about what's appropriate |
| Take your allotted vacation days — using them is not, structurally, a career risk in most white-collar roles | Feel obligated to justify time off with an explanation — but budget for the cultural guilt anyway, since a third of Americans report feeling it regardless |
| Track hours worked past 40/week if hourly — FLSA overtime protections are real and enforceable | Assume salaried status means no leverage over hours — many salaried workers still negotiate boundaries around evening and weekend responsiveness |
| Ask directly what "urgent" means at a new employer | Assume a midnight Slack reply from a colleague sets the expected norm — reply-speed culture varies enormously company to company |

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Target foreign-affiliated (gaishikei) firms or startups if work-life balance is the priority | Assume all Japanese employers run on identical hours — Rakuten, LINE, and many tech startups operate closer to Western schedules |
| Leave when your work is done, where the office culture allows it | Stay late purely because colleagues are still at their desks — "presence overtime," staying visible past 6pm without needed work, remains a real, if declining, pressure |
| Use your paid leave deliberately, in advance, framed around a specific reason | Assume unused leave rolls over indefinitely or is viewed neutrally — under 19% of workers use their full annual allotment, and guilt is the documented reason why |
| Know the legal overtime cap — 45 hours a month for most workers under the 2019 Work Style Reform Law | Assume reported overtime equals actual overtime — workers have reported averaging 40.3 hours of unpaid overtime against just 22.7 hours actually compensated |
| Watch for signs of karoshi risk in yourself or colleagues — it's a recognized legal cause of death, not hyperbole | Dismiss the 80-hour-a-month overtime threshold as an exaggeration — Japan's own Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare treats it as a documented risk marker |

### The Republic of the Forfeited Day

America's work-life imbalance isn't primarily a law-enforcement problem — it's a design problem. With no federal paid leave mandate, the entire system runs on employer discretion and cultural pressure: BLS data shows 79% of private-sector employees do get some paid vacation, averaging out to roughly 15 days after meaningful tenure, but Americans use only about 12 of those, and 33% report feeling guilty taking time off at all, nearly double the 18% rate reported by Europeans in the same surveys. Forty percent of Americans work 41 to 50 hours a week, against 26% of Europeans — a gap OECD data confirms structurally, with Americans logging roughly 1,810 hours annually against an OECD peer average closer to 1,555.

The mechanism is less legal compulsion than always-on responsiveness: colleagues who reply to email within five minutes at midnight or 6am set an informal floor that spreads through a team regardless of what any handbook says. "Unlimited PTO" policies, now common at white-collar employers, compound rather than solve this — without a fixed allotment to signal what's normal, employees under them tend to take less time off, not more, unsure what "reasonable" looks like without a number to anchor against.

### The Empire of the Uncounted Hour

Japan's version runs through law rather than around it, and still lands in a similar place. The 2019 Work Style Reform Law caps overtime at 45 hours a month for most workers — a genuine legal ceiling America has no equivalent to — but workers have reported averaging 40.3 hours of overtime monthly while getting compensated for just 22.7, a gap that suggests the cap constrains what gets reported more reliably than what gets worked. Roughly one in ten Japanese employees still logs over 80 hours of monthly overtime, the threshold Japan's own Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare treats as strongly correlated with karoshi — death from overwork, a legally recognized cause of death with no linguistic equivalent in English because, as one comparative account bluntly put it, Japan built a word for the outcome before it built one for the balance that might have prevented it.

The paid leave paradox is where the two countries converge most clearly despite their opposite legal starting points: Japan guarantees roughly 19 vacation days by law, and workers still use only 63% of them, driven by the same guilt mechanism — fear of appearing less committed than a colleague still at their desk — that drives American underuse of a much smaller, entirely discretionary allotment. InterNations' expat survey ranked Tokyo dead last among 50 cities specifically on Work Culture & Satisfaction, with "outdated work culture" and "long working hours and demanding supervisors" among the most common complaints from the nearly 12,000 respondents surveyed.

### The Reckoning

Strip away the legal frameworks and the two countries are running the identical underlying algorithm: work hard enough, visibly enough, for long enough, that taking your entitled rest starts to feel like a debt against your reputation rather than a right you're owed. America gets there through the absence of a floor — no mandated leave, so any time off feels like a favor. Japan gets there despite a solid legal floor — mandated leave that culture has quietly re-priced as an act of disloyalty anyway.

The practical difference for a newcomer is where the leverage sits. In America, the fix is largely individual and negotiable — ask for more PTO, ask what "urgent" means, use what you're given. In Japan, the fix is more structural and requires picking your employer carefully — a gaishikei firm or Western-style startup can hand you the same legal 19 days with none of the guilt architecture built around them at a traditional keiretsu-style employer.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *InterNations Tokyo — A relocating professional described the surprise of realizing that leaving the office at the legally reasonable hour still drew visible attention from colleagues, and that the actual signal of commitment in his office was staying visibly present past 6pm regardless of whether there was remaining work to do.*

> *Quora — Someone answering why Americans tolerate a comparatively poor work-life balance despite lower job security and vacation time pointed to the absence of any federal guarantee as the root cause, arguing that without a legal floor, "reasonable" simply became whatever the last person to leave the office had normalized.*

> *Substack (software engineer, Japan/US/Europe comparison) — An engineer who'd worked in all three regions wrote that American work culture was, if anything, faster-moving day to day than Japan's, but that Japan's overtime was structurally harder to escape because presence, not output, was still the primary visible signal of effort in many traditional firms.*

> *Blind — A tech employee who transferred from a US office to a Japanese subsidiary of the same company described the disorientation of watching identical work get evaluated on completely different criteria — output and deadlines in California, visible hours and deference in Tokyo — despite both offices reporting to the same global leadership.*

> *Tokyo Weekender (reader comment thread) — An expat cited the InterNations city ranking data as validating something they'd struggled to explain to friends back home: that Japan's legal protections around overtime existed largely on paper for anyone working at a company that hadn't culturally adopted them.*

### Conclusion

Neither country will hand you real rest — you have to take it, and the tactics differ. In America, negotiate your PTO number explicitly and use every day of it; nobody is coming to protect that time for you, and the law certainly won't. In Japan, choose your employer as carefully as your role — the same legally guaranteed 19 days means something completely different at a gaishikei startup than at a traditional firm still running on presence-based loyalty. If a friend asked me over a drink which country would actually let them use their vacation days: neither, automatically — but at least in Japan, you'd have the law on your side when you finally decided to fight for it.
