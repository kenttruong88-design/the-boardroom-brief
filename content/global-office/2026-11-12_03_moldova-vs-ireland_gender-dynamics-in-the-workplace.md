# Moldova Still Asks Whether She Can Do the Job; Ireland Has Merely Made Her Pay for Childcare to Find Out

🇲🇩 Moldova · 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Ireland recently achieved something its statisticians had not recorded before: a negative median gender pay gap. The Central Statistics Office reports that women's median hourly pay in its 2025 report sits 1.7 percent above men's, while the mean gap has narrowed to 3.5 percent from 6.9 percent a year earlier. Moldova, meanwhile, records women earning about 16.6 percent less than men on average, with 40 percent of women in work against 45.9 percent of men. The reader weighing a move should note that statistics describe what has been counted, and that each country has found a distinctive way of making the counted part look better than the lived part.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Moldova

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask about salary bands in writing before accepting, since the gap is widest among highly educated women and hard to see from one offer | Assume a public-sector or formal contract is the same as an informal one, when the gap shrinks sharply under formal employment |
| Ask directly about parental leave and what the employer does when a pregnancy is announced | Announce a pregnancy before you understand how the employer has handled it before |
| Learn the Council for the Prevention and Elimination of Discrimination, which has ruled in women's favour before | Expect colleagues to back a complaint openly, as many women tacitly accept such treatment |
| Pay attention to who runs the room, because women hold roughly 45 percent of management posts nationally but far fewer at the top of private firms | Read a female manager as evidence of a level field |
| Budget for domestic help, which many households use to cover the care burden | Assume a partner will split housework evenly because you do |

### In Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Price childcare before accepting the offer, and treat it as a deduction from salary | Assume the national pay gap figure covers the cost of having a child |
| Ask whether the employer publishes gender pay gap data and read the grade breakdown | Take a headline median at face value when grade drives most of the difference |
| Negotiate flexible or hybrid arrangements in writing, with a trial period | Rely on a verbal agreement to a reduced week |
| Report microaggressions early and in writing, since surveys show many go unaddressed | Treat repeated unfair criticism as a personality matter |
| Join an employee network or sector women's group in the first month | Assume that a polite workplace is a neutral one |

## Moldova

According to the ILO's October 2024 analysis, women in Moldova earn about 15 percent less than men in monthly wages, a gap that has narrowed over the past decade. The National Bureau of Statistics, as reported by Logos Press, puts the unadjusted 2024 figure at 16.6 percent, or 12,788 lei a month for women against 15,336 for men. The ILO finds the gap widest among women aged 25 to 34, which it attributes plausibly to mothers returning to flexible, lower-paid work in a country short of childcare. It is smaller in the public sector, among formal employees and among union members, which suggests that the practical protection is a contract and a regulator, not goodwill.

The World Bank notes that women's labour force participation, close to men's in 2005, has since fallen below 40 percent, leaving a gap of about ten points. It adds that female unemployment is the highest in Eastern Europe, that women make up under 18 percent of top managers in private firms, and that coverage for children under three remains thin, particularly outside Chisinau. Nationally, women held 44.8 percent of managerial posts in 2024, a figure that sounds progressive until one reads the World Bank's number for the top. The pattern is familiar: women are well represented in the middle, absent from the room where the budget is decided.

Attitudes lag the law. A report carried by IPN found that many Moldovan women tacitly accept discrimination rather than report it, citing the case of an IT specialist rejected for a systems administrator job because the employer wanted a man; the equality council ruled that discriminatory in 2016. A Women's Law Center study cited in the same report found that 90.5 percent of men and 81.5 percent of women considered housework more important for a woman. That article is several years old and the picture has softened, but a reader should treat it as a description of the baseline against which progress is measured. The practical upside is that employers are legally on notice and the formal sector offers real protection. The practical downside is that you will need to use it yourself.

There are signs of movement. Emerging Europe reports that a 2021 EU-funded gender profile found stereotypes about women being weaker in business still shape confidence, and that the government launched a three-year, 1.5 million euro programme for female founders in October 2023. The World Bank estimates that closing the employment gap with men could raise GDP per capita by about 20 percent in the long run, an argument that has the unusual merit of appealing to people who are not persuaded by fairness.

## Ireland

Ireland's headline numbers are the envy of most of Europe. The CSO says about 1.3 million women were in employment at the end of 2025, up 82.1 percent from 729,900 in 2000, and that 63.6 percent of employed women hold a third-level degree against 52.8 percent of men, as The Irish Times reported in March 2026. Women make up 75.9 percent of employees in health and social work and 74.9 percent in education, which is to say that the sectors are where the women are and the pay is not. Women's share of the top 1 percent of earners rose from 22.6 percent in 2019 to 27.6 percent in 2024. The CSO's 2025 pay gap report identifies grade as the largest factor in average hourly pay, which is a polite way of saying that the question is who gets promoted, not who gets paid fairly for the same job. Median weekly earnings in 2024 were still €654.07 for women and €802.14 for men, which is where the tidy hourly median meets the weekly pay packet.

The softer data is less flattering. A February 2023 HR Buddy survey of 500 Irish women, reported by the Irish Examiner, found that one in three had experienced gender-based discrimination, 19 percent reported sexual harassment, and 75 percent said women are more likely to receive unfair criticism. Seven in ten believed women are more likely to be passed over for promotion, and nearly three-quarters said their employer had not invested in training to prevent such discrimination. A 2024 Web Summit survey of 1,000 women in technology, cited in The Irish Times, found that half had experienced sexism at work in the previous year; the article does not say how many respondents were based in Ireland, so apply the appropriate scepticism.

None of this is quite the picture on the recruitment page, but it is a picture that employers can be argued with about. Irish workplaces tend to resolve friction through understatement, which makes mildly uncomfortable behaviour harder to name and easier to repeat. The structural cost sits elsewhere. Childcare, as the next section shows, is where Irish gender equality meets the household budget and frequently loses. Women who move for work should assume the pay gap figure will treat them kindly and the creche invoice will not.

## The Reckoning

Hofstede Insights does not publish a Moldova profile that I could retrieve, so the nearest proxy is a Moldovan academic paper reproducing the Insights figures: it gives Moldova a masculinity score of 39 and a power distance of 90, against 42 and 90 for neighbouring Romania. Ireland scores 68 on masculinity and 28 on power distance. The numbers are blunter than the reality, but they point in a sensible direction: Moldovan workplaces are hierarchical and relationship-driven, while Irish ones are flatter, more competitive and more willing to tell you where you stand, in the most oblique way available.

Head to head, Ireland offers women a more reliable legal framework, data that employers must publish and a visible cohort of senior women. Moldova offers a smaller gap in some formal settings, long paid maternity leave according to one expat, and an economy where family networks soften the care burden that Irish families purchase at market rates. For a woman earning an Irish salary in a Dublin multinational, Ireland is easier. For a woman who needs the least friction on day one and has family nearby, neither country is simple. The difference is that Ireland loses you money quietly, while Moldova loses you opportunity openly.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Boards.ie — a new mother returning from maternity leave found her employer had reversed an agreed three-day week and now wanted her full time. Replies suggested compressed hours, parental leave days, mixed creche and family care, and returning to the employer with costings and a six-month trial.*

> *Irish Times readers — a Dublin doctor wrote that two children's creche fees run to about €2,200 a month, meaning her first €50,000 of earnings goes to childcare, and that she is considering leaving work. Another reader contrasted a €30 monthly state kindergarten in Hungary with Dublin fees.*

> *openDemocracy (relaying a Chisinau teacher) — a kindergarten teacher was dismissed after telling her boss she was pregnant, with no compensation or maternity allowance, and won a finding of discrimination from the equality council in December 2015. The case is from 2016, so treat it as a baseline, not a forecast.*

> *TalesMag — a diplomatic expat who lived in Chisinau from 2021 to 2023 said women have more rights there than in the United States in some respects, including a longer paid maternity leave, while noting that local salary scales are much lower and that nannies are commonly employed. The account says little else about gender roles at work.*

> *Emerging Europe (via an incubator organiser) — an observer at a Chisinau start-up incubation programme said openness has grown since the war, but that childcare and housework still fall mostly on women, so they often need to work at a slower pace.*

## Conclusion

If your priority is pay transparency, enforceable rights and a large cohort of women in senior roles, Ireland is the stronger bet, provided you cost childcare first and negotiate hours in writing. If you are moving to Moldova, do so with a formal contract, an understanding of the equality council, and realistic expectations about who will back you in a dispute. Read both countries' headline gaps as the beginning of the question, not the answer.

Choose the country whose problems you are best equipped to argue with, because both will give you the opportunity.
