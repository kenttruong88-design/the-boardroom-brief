# South Africa Works the Clock Round; Australia Works It Quietly, Then Sends the Invoice to Nobody

🇿🇦 South Africa · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The workers of South Africa put in around 2,181 hours a year, according to Our World in Data figures tabulated for 2023. The workers of Australia put in about 1,727. That is a gap of roughly 450 hours, or eleven working weeks, and it is the single most useful statistic for anyone choosing between the two. Both countries hold forth on braais, beaches and the sacredness of the weekend. Only one of them has legislated, in recent memory, the right to ignore your boss's text message.

## Do's & Don'ts

### South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your contract for overtime terms; the law caps it at ten hours a week | Accept open-ended "flexibility" without a written agreement |
| Claim overtime at the statutory rate of at least 150 percent, or agree time off instead | Assume managers will volunteer the premium |
| Ask whether your pay sits above the earnings threshold, since the hours protections fall away above it | Assume the Act covers you automatically |
| Take your lunch break deliberately, whatever the room is doing | Eat at your desk because everyone else does |
| Match the city: Johannesburg moves fast, Cape Town less so | Treat all South African offices as one culture |
| Accept the braai invitation | Treat it as optional networking |

### Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive a few minutes early and finish on time | Treat five minutes late as a rounding error |
| Use the right to disconnect when a message arrives at 9pm | Reply instantly in your first month to prove commitment |
| Say "no worries" to reasonable requests, and mean a limit | Say it to everything, then resent it silently |
| Take the morning tea break with colleagues | Skip it to look productive |
| Be direct and brief in meetings | Pad every point with qualifications |
| Accept after-work drinks occasionally | Decline every invitation for a year |

## South Africa: The Law Is Sensible; The Office Is Optional

On paper, South Africa is orderly. The Basic Conditions of Employment Act sets a maximum of 45 ordinary hours a week, 12 hours of rest between shifts, and overtime of no more than ten hours a week at a minimum of 150 percent pay, according to WageIndicator's summary. Sunday work pays 150 or 200 percent depending on custom. The protections apply to employees earning below an annual threshold, currently set at R261,748.45, so the people most likely to be asked for long hours are often the ones the Act has quietly stopped counting.

In practice the clock has teeth. Statistics South Africa's Quarterly Labour Force Survey for the second quarter of 2026, as reported by BusinessTech, counted 4.53 million employed people working more than 45 hours a week, up from 4.22 million in 2010. About 57 percent work the standard 40 to 45. Meanwhile unemployment sits at 33.6 percent, which concentrates the mind of anyone considering whether to leave at five.

The mood data is blunt. According to Gallup's 2026 State of the Global Workplace report, as cited by TimesLive, daily stress among South African employees runs around 35 to 40 percent. A Sadag survey of 963 employees, which is the advocacy group's own and not an independent study, found that 75 percent could not stop thinking about work when off duty and that four in ten feared losing their jobs. Expat Arrivals describes Johannesburg as "work hard, play hard," and Cape Town and Durban as more balanced, a view echoed by business-etiquette guides that call Johannesburg fast-paced and results-oriented and Cape Town slightly more relaxed. The Act also excludes platform workers treated as independent contractors, which is a growing slice of the economy. The same TimesLive report lists long hours, tight deadlines and inadequate pay among the top workplace complaints, and notes that 61 percent of the Sadag respondents wanted to leave their jobs but could not afford to. An earlier Citizen report, also citing Gallup, put the disengaged share of the workforce above 71 percent. Overtime in this setting is less a decision than a weather condition.

## Australia: The Law Is Generous; The Habit Is Not

Australia has the better reputation, and in some respects it has earned it. From 26 August 2024 most employees gained a right under the Fair Work Act to refuse to monitor or respond to contact outside working hours, unless the refusal is unreasonable, and small-business staff followed a year later, according to Australian Unions. Workplaces are relatively flat, meetings are expected to end when they say they will, and Australian guides describe the culture as direct, with a good deal of dry humour and some swearing.

The unpaid-overtime numbers complicate the postcard. According to the Australia Institute's Go Home On Time Day 2024 analysis, the average worker does 3.6 hours of unpaid overtime a week, and full-time employees 4.1, with workers aged 18 to 29 doing the most at 4.4. That adds up to about $91 billion a year, or roughly five weeks of unpaid work and about $7,713 per worker. Of those doing it, 70 percent say their bosses expect it, and the main causes cited are too much work and staff shortages.

So Australia's advantage is less that nobody works late than that the culture is gentler about it. Hofstede's data scores Australia at 36 for power distance and 90 for individualism, against 49 and 65 for South Africa, which describes an office where you may say no to the boss and are then expected to own the consequences. Australian management guides note that workdays often start around 8:30, that staff rarely refuse extra work, and that a casual "no worries" is the usual reply, which is not the same as "no." The same guides add that brief lateness back from lunch goes unreported because people put in extra time elsewhere, a system of mutual unrecorded generosity that works beautifully until someone audits it.

## The Reckoning

The headline gap, roughly 450 hours a year, does not necessarily mean South Africans are less productive or more diligent. It means that in a country with a one-in-three unemployment rate, the person who works late is insuring against a job market that offers few second chances. Australian workers, with lower risk and a stronger legal backstop, can afford to treat evenings as private property, and many of them still donate those evenings to their employer without invoice.

Consider the scale of the Australian habit. The Australia Institute's figures work out to nearly $300 a fortnight handed over to employers for nothing, and more than a third of bosses reportedly expect staff to work beyond rostered hours. The South African equivalent is harder to price, because it is bundled with job insecurity, commuting and the cost of electricity. The practical difference is leverage. In South Africa the law is adequate and the bargaining power is thin. In Australia the law is newer and the bargaining power is real, though it must be exercised, politely and in writing, by the person with the overtime. If you value ending the day when the day ends, Australia gives you a better chance of doing it. If you value a strong social life attached to your job, Johannesburg will gladly supply the colleagues.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Ask a Manager — paraphrased experience: A commenter in a thread on office culture around the world reported that in South Africa break laws exist but are rarely enforced in offices, so people often work through lunch or take shorter breaks. Another contributor described Australian workplaces as blunt and businesslike with little small talk, though tea breaks were valued, while a different commenter found them friendly and laid-back.*

> *British Expats — paraphrased experience: A South African working in the United States concluded, from colleagues and clients at multinational financial firms, that Australian employers were less fair about overtime pay than American ones. The poster conceded these were informal discussions rather than polls, and the thread is old, so read it as a prompt for questions, not evidence.*

> *Hacker News — paraphrased experience: In a thread about average annual hours by country, one commenter said Australia's standard four weeks of leave is too short for a proper trip and wished for flexitime that would let people trade extra hours for time off. Another reminded the thread that hours figures measure time present, not productive output.*

> *Wereldwyd — paraphrased experience: A South African who moved to the Sunshine Coast said the work culture in Australia felt better for her and her husband, who felt welcome among colleagues. She mentioned a shared morning tea break that everyone takes at the same time, and noted that her experience in New Zealand had depended heavily on the supervisor.*

## Conclusion

Move to South Africa for the money, the weather and the colleagues, and read your contract with the attention it deserves, because the clock is not on your side and neither, particularly, is the labour market. Move to Australia for the legal protection and the better odds of an evening, and keep a record of the hours you give away, because the national tradition of the free extra hour is alive and well.

Either way, the sensible approach is the same: agree the hours in writing, and treat "no worries" as a statement of mood, never a contract. Work-life balance is available in both countries, provided you are prepared to negotiate for it, which is the one form of overtime nobody pays for.
