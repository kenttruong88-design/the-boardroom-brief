# The Elder and the Line Manager: Ghana vs. UK

**🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Ghana, age confers authority that a job title can complicate but rarely overrides — a 25-year-old manager supervising a 50-year-old subordinate is still, quietly, expected to defer to them in certain rooms. In the UK, 45% of workers under 35 say a generational divide exists at their workplace at all, more than double the rate reported by anyone over 55, which suggests the UK's problem isn't too much deference to elders — it's too little shared vocabulary between the age brackets to begin with.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show visible deference to older colleagues regardless of your formal title over them | Assume a job title alone settles who defers to whom in the room |
| Build credibility with older staff through demonstrated competence before asserting authority | Delegate the unglamorous early tasks immediately — work alongside staff first |
| Address age-related friction privately and gently if it affects someone's work | Treat age discrimination as a non-issue just because it draws little public attention |
| Let confidence, not just position, do the work of earning respect from elders | Expect a young manager's authority to be accepted on paper alone |
| Recognize that elder deference is a cultural norm, not merely office politics | Mistake quiet deference for actual agreement on a decision |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect real differences in what's acceptable: slang, casualwear, discussing personal matters, all skew by age | Assume your own generation's norms (on dress, language, openness) are simply "normal" and others are breaches |
| Create deliberate space for younger colleagues to contribute ideas, not just tasks | Assume a younger colleague's confidence or directness is disrespect |
| Offer and accept help across the age gap on digital tools without embarrassment either way | Let a one-off generational misunderstanding harden into a lasting assumption about "that generation" |
| Ask directly how someone prefers to communicate rather than guessing by age | Assume younger colleagues want a same-age manager or team — not all do, but many report wanting it |
| Treat generational friction as a known, documented workplace issue worth managing | Dismiss a younger colleague's complaint about feeling unheard as generational sensitivity rather than a real signal |

### Ghana: Elder Authority, Formally Undocumented

Ghana has no individually published Hofstede country score of its own; it is measured only within Hofstede's broader "West Africa" cluster, which registers high power distance (77) and strongly collectivist individualism (20) — context worth stating plainly rather than inventing a Ghana-specific figure that doesn't exist. What that regional data captures in the abstract, a 2014 KNUST study by researcher Boakye Manu states outright: Ghanaian society holds that elders are "always right, wiser and more respectable than the young," and that belief travels directly into the workplace, shaping who gets deferred to in a meeting regardless of what the org chart says.

The same study's more striking finding is how quietly this plays out. Surveying staff across several Ghanaian financial and development organizations, the researcher concluded age discrimination isn't treated as a major public issue in these workplaces, even though it measurably affects productivity and commitment, and ageist remarks can make daily work genuinely unbearable for whoever's on the receiving end of them — a problem significant enough to harm the business but not significant enough, apparently, to get discussed openly. Business commentary aimed at young Ghanaian leaders backs this up from the other direction: advice pieces consistently counsel new managers to work alongside older staff on early, unglamorous tasks rather than delegating immediately, because — as one UK-based young entrepreneur's widely cited advice on managing older teams put it — "respect from your elders can often come down to how confident they perceive you to be," authority earned through demonstrated competence rather than claimed through title.

The recommended fix, where anyone has proposed one, points toward the same destination as the UK's own diagnosis: making the unspoken hierarchy a little more spoken. The KNUST research explicitly frames an "age-inclusive workplace climate" as a retention and productivity lever, not just an abstract fairness goal, which implies the current arrangement — real deference, zero formal acknowledgment that friction exists — is leaving real performance on the table precisely because nobody's measuring or managing it the way the UK, for all its documented awkwardness, at least attempts to.

### UK: No Elder Deference, But No Shared Vocabulary Either

The UK sits at the opposite end of Hofstede's individualism scale from Ghana's cluster score, at 89, which removes the formal deference structure entirely — nobody is expected to defer to a colleague simply because of their age. What replaces it, according to a national survey of 2,000 UK working adults commissioned by Applied, is a workplace where 32% overall report a generational divide, rising to 45% among 16-to-34-year-olds against just 22% among those 55 and over — meaning younger workers perceive far more friction than older ones even notice. The specific fault lines are almost comically granular: 16% of younger workers consider casualwear acceptable in the office against 10% of older ones, 14% think discussing personal matters with colleagues is fine against 7%, and 12% find slang acceptable in work emails against just 5% — none of it dramatic individually, all of it adding up to 31% of respondents across every age bracket saying these value and communication differences genuinely make it hard to talk to colleagues from other generations.

A 2025 Kahoot! survey of UK Gen Z office workers put sharper numbers on the younger side of that gap: 44% reported criticism from older colleagues tied directly to generational stereotypes — being seen as "too passionate" (47%), using informal language (46%), or simply setting boundaries (44%) — and 39% had seriously considered quitting within their first year specifically because of it. Perhaps the most quietly damning statistic: 57% said they'd had to teach their own manager basic digital tasks, a role reversal the old Ghanaian framework would find almost unthinkable, where competence, not tenure, briefly determines who's teaching whom.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast is about where the friction gets absorbed. In Ghana, generational hierarchy is explicit, culturally sanctioned, and largely unspoken about as a "problem" even when it quietly damages morale and output — the deference is the system working as designed, with discontent kept private. In the UK, there's no equivalent sanctioned hierarchy at all, which should, in theory, make things more equal — instead it produces open, documented friction precisely because nobody's sure which generation's norms are supposed to win in any given disagreement about dress, language, or digital competence. A Ghanaian manager arriving in a UK office may be startled by how openly younger staff contest decisions that would simply never be voiced aloud back home; a British manager arriving in Ghana may mistake polite deference from older staff for genuine agreement, only to find decisions quietly resisted in practice.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *teamblind.com — One younger manager, roughly two decades junior to some of their reports, wrote that age "never seems to factor in any of my decisions, what shines is the thought and excellent work that they do," adding that they're frequently coached by their older engineers and treat it as a genuine opportunity to improve rather than a status threat.*

> *teamblind.com — A 40-year-old employee on a team of mostly early-30s colleagues, in the same thread, admitted "sometimes I feel I am out of crowd in the team," even though other teams at the same company include people closer to their own age — and asked openly whether their much younger manager harbored any quiet bias about Gen X's capacity to innovate.*

> *melmagazine.com — In a personal essay, writer Meg Mankins described discovering, after some light social-media investigation, that a boss she'd assumed was older by years was in fact only a few months older than her — and that having her work proofread by a near-peer felt less like mentorship and more like being "flung back to the ranks of a college sophomore" after years of paying her dues.*

> *ghanaweb.com — Jordan Daykin, a young UK entrepreneur whose advice on managing older teams circulates widely in Ghanaian business commentary, advised new leaders not to "immediately delegate" the early grunt work, arguing that visible hands-on effort does more to earn an older team's respect than any title ever will on its own.*

> *Advice worth having before you land: in Ghana, earn deference through demonstrated competence and patience before you lean on your title — it will be extended eventually, but rarely immediately. In the UK, don't assume a younger colleague's bluntness is disrespect, or an older colleague's formality is coldness; both are just dialects of the same underlying uncertainty about whose norms apply.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two for how generational friction actually gets handled, the honest framing is: Ghana manages it through an old, largely unspoken hierarchy that works until it quietly doesn't, and the UK fails to manage it at all, which is at least more visible and, arguably, more fixable precisely because people are willing to name it in a survey. The thing I'd tell a friend over a drink: in Ghana, earn it slowly and let the title catch up later; in the UK, say the awkward thing out loud, because apparently 31% of your colleagues already agree it's awkward and nobody's said so yet.
