**Poland vs. Singapore: Where Gen Z Wants a Purpose Statement and Where It Still Just Wants Permission to Speak**

**🇵🇱 Poland · 🇸🇬 Singapore**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Poland is one of Europe's fastest-ageing societies, which means its youngest workers are often the only "digital native" in the room, training colleagues twice their age on tools the company adopted six months ago. Singapore's youngest workers face almost the opposite problem: 94% say having a sense of purpose is essential to job satisfaction, according to Deloitte's regional Gen Z and Millennial survey, but the workplace they've inherited still runs on a hierarchy where openly disagreeing with a superior is treated as a genuine breach of etiquette. Two very different generational gaps, same basic question — who gets to speak first in a meeting, and does anyone actually listen.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to informally train older colleagues on new software — it's a normal, expected role for younger hires | Assume seniority is purely about age; Poland's 68 Power Distance score means hierarchy is still taken seriously regardless of who holds the knowledge |
| Ask about remote or hybrid policy explicitly — about a third of companies offer it, concentrated heavily in finance and insurance (62%) | Assume flexible work is universal; two-thirds of Polish companies still don't offer it |
| Use the Work-Life Balance Directive (in force since April 2023) as real leverage — it's now a statutory right for parents and carers | Expect informal norms to have caught up with the law yet; older managers are still adjusting to codified flexibility |
| Bring data when pushing for change — Poland's individualist streak (60 on Hofstede's scale) means self-advocacy is respected more than group consensus-building | Expect open generational conflict to be aired directly in meetings; frustration tends to surface more in private grumbling than confrontation |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to raise disagreement privately and indirectly — public correction of a superior is read as a serious breach of harmony | Voice pointed criticism of a decision in open meetings, however constructive your intent |
| Ask pointed, specific questions about purpose and growth in interviews — younger colleagues will expect and respect it | Assume "hierarchical" means uninterested in your input; it means input has a preferred channel |
| Expect fast feedback loops if you're managing Gen Z staff — they explicitly want more frequent, direct dialogue than prior generations received | Rely on the old model of annual, indirect feedback for younger hires; it reads as disengagement, not restraint |
| Take cost-of-living concerns seriously in any retention conversation — it's the top concern for 49% of Gen Z workers surveyed | Assume loyalty follows automatically from a respected employer brand; 25% of Gen Z workers don't trust employers to invest in their future skills |

### Poland

Poland's generational story is shaped less by ideology and more by demographics: it's ageing faster than most of Europe, which puts unusual pressure on a comparatively small cohort of younger workers to modernise institutions built by, and largely still run by, an older generation. Hofstede Insights scores Poland at 68 on Power Distance — a hierarchical culture where rank is respected — combined with a relatively high Individualism score of 60, a combination that produces workplaces where younger employees are expected to defer on authority but are also expected to advocate for themselves rather than wait to be noticed.

The Work-Life Balance Directive, transposed into Polish law in April 2023, formalised something younger workers had already started demanding informally: parental and carer leave protections and clearer boundaries around flexible work. About a third of Polish companies now offer remote or hybrid arrangements, concentrated heavily in finance and insurance, where 62% of firms report offering it — meaning the generational flexibility gap in Poland is really an industry gap wearing a generational costume.

### Singapore

Singapore runs on a more explicitly Confucian-inflected hierarchy: Hofstede scores it at 74 on Power Distance and just 20 on Individualism, among the most collectivist profiles in the world, with a strong long-term orientation that prizes stability and face-saving over disruption. In practice, that means clear, respected chains of command, indirect communication around disagreement, and a cultural premium on not making a superior look bad in front of others — norms that sit uneasily against Deloitte's 2025 survey finding that Singaporean Gen Z and millennial workers are explicitly prioritising purpose, wellbeing, and stability, with 40% saying they've left a job that felt meaningless and 34% having rejected an employer outright over ethical concerns.

The tension shows up most clearly around feedback and trust: Randstad Singapore's research found meaningfully lower trust in leadership among Gen Z workers compared with millennials and Gen X, including 25% who don't believe their employer will invest in AI-relevant skills training. Cost of living is the dominant financial anxiety, cited by 49% of Gen Z respondents, with half saying they've delayed major life milestones as a result — a very practical concern colliding with a very traditional workplace structure.

### The Reckoning

Poland's generational gap is fundamentally a skills and demographics problem wearing cultural clothing — young workers know the tools, old structures hold the rank, and the country's individualist streak means people mostly just say so. Singapore's gap is more structurally entrenched: even where younger workers have clear, well-documented priorities around purpose and feedback, the collectivist, face-saving communication norms make it genuinely harder to voice disagreement without real social cost.

The practical difference for anyone managing across these two markets: in Poland, generational friction gets litigated fairly openly, if grumpily; in Singapore, it gets absorbed, redirected into indirect channels, and can look like harmony right up until someone quietly resigns.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone answering "Is there any 'generation gap' issue in Singapore?" wrote that many older Singaporeans actively appreciate the country's strict, hierarchical rules, viewing them as what has kept the society from swinging toward the volatility they associate with more individualistic countries — framing the hierarchy not as an accident but as a deliberate value older colleagues expect younger staff to eventually appreciate too.*

> *Reddit (compiled generational-workplace thread) — Contributors describing "proper" workplace behaviors older colleagues still insist on — rigid clock-watching, discomfort with casual dress, distrust of remote work — said younger staff experienced the enforcement of these norms as arbitrary rather than as genuinely about output, a friction point that maps closely onto Poland's own hierarchical-but-individualist mix of deference and quiet pushback.*

> *Blind — An engineer asked how to manage a working relationship with a manager roughly a decade younger than themselves, describing the discomfort as less about competence and more about unlearning the instinct to expect deference by tenure — a reversal that Poland's ageing, digitally-lagging workforce is starting to encounter more often as younger staff move into technical leadership faster than the culture around them has adjusted.*

> *Blind — In a related thread on ageism in management, one commenter noted that being visibly older in a fast-moving tech team could cut both ways: assumed authority in some rooms, assumed obsolescence in others, depending entirely on whether the company's culture still equated seniority with competence.*

### Conclusion

If you're managing across these two markets, the mistake to avoid isn't the same mistake twice. In Poland, the fix is mostly structural — formalise flexibility, document the new legal minimums, and let people advocate for themselves; the friction is loud but honest. In Singapore, the fix is more delicate — build real, private channels for disagreement and feedback, because the collectivist instinct to preserve harmony means problems don't surface until they're already serious. The version I'd give a friend heading into either job: in Poland, expect people to tell you what's wrong; in Singapore, you'll have to ask, privately, more than once.
