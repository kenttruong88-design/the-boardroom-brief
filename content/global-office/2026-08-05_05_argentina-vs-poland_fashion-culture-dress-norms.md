# Argentina Dresses to Be Envied; Poland Dresses to Be Left Alone

🇦🇷 Argentina · 🇵🇱 Poland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Argentina, a country that has spent much of the past decade renegotiating its relationship with triple-digit inflation, shoes are still expected to be leather, polished, and unimpeachable. In Poland, a country that quietly became a trillion-dollar economy and overtook Japan in GDP per capita, the office dress code rewards you for looking like you didn't try very hard. Between them sits a tidy little paradox: the country with less money to spend on appearances spends more effort on them, and the country with more money to spend has decided that spending it visibly is slightly embarrassing.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇷 Argentina
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in quality leather shoes and keep them polished — footwear is scrutinized closely | Wear sneakers to a client meeting, even at a self-styled "casual" startup |
| Wear a blazer on your first day, even in a business-casual office | Over-accessorize; jewelry should be discreet, not a statement |
| Favor a tailored, fitted silhouette — dark navy, grey, camel, black | Assume a Buenos Aires heatwave excuses shorts or flip-flops indoors |
| Iron everything; wrinkled clothing reads as not caring | Wear obviously cheap or synthetic fabric if you can help it — it's noticed |
| Dress up even for "informal" after-work drinks with colleagues | Mistake the relaxed social atmosphere for a relaxed dress code |

#### 🇵🇱 Poland
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to simple, conservative pieces in muted tones | Wear anything flashy or logo-heavy in your first weeks — it reads as try-hard |
| Bring a proper suit for client-facing meetings, even at a relaxed tech firm | Assume "startup casual" means jeans on day one — watch the room first |
| Dress more simply as you move up the hierarchy, not more elaborately | Out-dress your manager dramatically — it can read as jostling for status |
| Take casual Friday cues from your specific office, not a general assumption | Wear shorts, sandals, or beachwear indoors even during a heatwave |
| Treat punctuality as part of your professional appearance | Be late — it undercuts even a perfectly assembled outfit |

## Argentina

Argentine business dress correlates less with the health of the peso than one might expect, which is itself the story. The apparel market was valued at roughly USD 12.4 billion in 2024 and is forecast to keep growing at a compound rate above 4% even as clothing and footwear prices spiked 57.2% in a single year during the worst of the inflationary period and have continued to swing sharply month to month since ([IMARC Group](https://www.imarcgroup.com/argentina-apparel-market); MercoPress-linked inflation reporting). Argentines have apparently concluded that looking prosperous is not optional simply because being prosperous has become harder. Business etiquette guides note flatly that "appearances are very important in both business circles and society at large in Argentina," and that people are "partly judged on the way you look" — dark suits, conservative ties, discreet jewelry, and shoes that are expected to be both leather and immaculate ([World Business Culture](https://www.worldbusinessculture.com/country-profiles/argentina/business-dress-code/)).

What makes this more interesting than a generic "dress for success" mandate is the contrast with the country's famously elastic relationship with time. Meetings run long, punctuality is respected in principle but negotiated in practice, and small talk before business is not a formality to survive but the actual point of the first twenty minutes. Personal presentation, in other words, is doing more social work in Argentina than the clock is. A well-cut jacket signals reliability in a culture where reliability is otherwise established through relationship rather than schedule.

The recent liberalization of import rules has also reshaped what "dressing well" costs. Clothing imports rose roughly 77% in value and 186% in volume in early 2025 as fast-fashion platforms like Shein, AliExpress, and Temu became newly accessible, undercutting a domestic apparel sector that had long protected itself with tariffs (market data via IMARC and related industry reporting). The polished national look is increasingly assembled from the same globalized, low-cost supply chains as everywhere else — it simply hasn't stopped being a look people bother to assemble.

## Poland

Poland's approach inverts the Argentine logic almost precisely: the richer the country gets, the more its office dress code insists on understatement. Business guidance is consistent on this point — Poles are described as "a nation of simple tastes" who, "in professional contacts, value modesty and class," where "brighter, more ostentatious clothes may seem out of place" and can register as a lack of restraint rather than a display of confidence ([World Business Culture](https://www.worldbusinessculture.com/country-profiles/poland/business-dress-style/)). Tellingly, seniority runs in the opposite direction from what a Wall Street or Buenos Aires banker might expect: the higher up the corporate hierarchy a Pole sits, the plainer and more classic their clothing tends to become, not the reverse.

This restraint sits oddly alongside the numbers. Household spending on clothing and footwear in Poland reached roughly 85.6 billion zloty in 2022, and the luxury fashion and lifestyle segment is estimated at around USD 5 billion, propelled by rising disposable income and an expanding middle class in Warsaw, Kraków, and Wrocław (Eurostat/GUS-linked data; Ken Research). Poland crossed the trillion-dollar GDP threshold in 2025. None of this has translated into a louder office wardrobe — clothing and footwear's share of total household spending has actually held close to flat, around 4.5%, even as overall spending power climbed (Eurostat data). Big corporations formalize the conservative dress code explicitly; smaller firms leave it unwritten but no less enforced by social pressure, and tech startups are the rare exception where standing out is tolerated rather than penalized.

## The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the counterintuitive part comes into focus: Argentina treats visible spending on appearance as a rational response to economic instability, while Poland treats visible spending on appearance as faintly suspicious despite growing genuinely wealthier. Hofstede Insights' comparison tool is instructive here in a single data point worth citing once — Argentina scores markedly higher on Indulgence (62) than Poland (29), meaning Argentine culture is comparatively more accepting of overt self-expression and gratification, while Polish culture leans toward restraint and control, a pattern that maps almost too neatly onto hemlines and lapels ([Hofstede Insights](https://www.hofstede-insights.com/country-comparison-tool)).

The practical effect for a newcomer is that identical instincts produce opposite mistakes. Show up in Buenos Aires looking deliberately understated and colleagues will read it as low effort or low regard for the meeting. Show up in Warsaw looking deliberately polished beyond your rank and colleagues will read it as overreach. Both countries are, in their own idiom, extremely attentive to what clothing communicates. They have simply agreed to communicate opposite things with it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

*One Quora thread on Argentine style put the practical frustration plainly: even amid currency instability and grinding inflation, Argentines reportedly prioritize looking put-together over almost any other discretionary expense, with some contributors noting that clothing and shoe budgets survive cuts that groceries and entertainment do not.*

*A separate Quora discussion on Polish fashion sensibilities described a cultural misread that trips up newcomers — flashy logos, shiny synthetic fabrics, and anything that looks conspicuously expensive are quietly coded as low-status in Poland, the inverse of what a visitor from a logo-forward market might assume signals success.*

*On the Buenos Aires Expats Community forum, one long-running clothing thread carried a pleasant surprise for arrivals: despite the city's reputation for chaos and economic headlines, day-to-day style in Buenos Aires was described as reserved and smart-casual rather than flashy, with muted colors and well-tailored basics doing the work rather than logos or trends.*

*Expat.com's guidance for newcomers to Polish offices offered the advice many wished they'd had sooner — dress formally and conservatively until you have actually clocked your specific office's real norms, because the gap between a company's stated dress code and its lived one can be wide, and it is much easier to dress down gradually than to walk back an overdressed first impression.*

*A thread on corporate culture in Buenos Aires captured a common misread from the other direction — expats arriving from more punctuality-strict business cultures assumed the relaxed, sociable pace of Argentine meetings meant a relaxed dress code too, and were quietly recalibrated after showing up underdressed to what turned out to be a fairly formal room.*

### Conclusion

Neither country's rulebook is complicated once you know it: in Argentina, spend on the shoes and iron the shirt, because a currency in flux is no excuse for a wrinkled collar; in Poland, buy the good basics and then say less with them the further you rise. The two systems are not actually contradictory so much as differently calibrated answers to the same question — what does looking competent cost, and who is it meant to impress. Argentina bets that impressing everyone else is worth the visible effort. Poland bets that impressing no one in particular is the more sophisticated flex. Pack accordingly, and remember that the country judging you the least loudly is often judging you the most.
