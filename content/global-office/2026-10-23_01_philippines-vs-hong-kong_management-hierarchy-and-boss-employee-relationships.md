**Two Flights, One Org Chart: Why Your New Boss Outranks You Differently in Manila Than in Hong Kong**

🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Somewhere in a relocation packet, a line promises that your new regional role will come with "a flatter, more empowering management structure." It will not say where. In Manila, flat means your boss will ask about your mother before assigning the deliverable. In Hong Kong, flat means the org chart was drawn by someone in Shenzhen who has never met you and is not available for comment. Both cities will teach you new things about what "reporting to someone" actually means — just not the same things, and not on the same schedule.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address superiors as "Sir" or "Ma'am" plus first name, even in casual Slack messages, until told otherwise | Call a Filipino boss by first name unprompted — treat it as an invitation they extend, not a default |
| Deliver criticism of a colleague's work privately, one-on-one | Correct or embarrass a subordinate in front of peers; it can trigger "hiya" and shut down communication for weeks |
| Attend the team lunch, Friday after-work drinks, and the office Christmas party as a loyalty signal | Mistake politeness or silence for agreement — a quiet team member may be signaling disagreement they won't voice directly |
| Build rapport before diving into task details | Skip the small talk and launch straight into business; it reads as transactional and cold |
| Confirm understanding by asking the employee to repeat the task back to you | Assume long tenure guarantees loyalty; unrecognized effort quietly erodes reciprocity and the employee disengages |

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan to be visibly at your desk until your boss leaves, especially in finance, trading, or mainland-headquartered firms | Challenge a decision in the room where your boss is present, even diplomatically; raise it after, one-on-one |
| Clarify before accepting an offer whether "flexible hours" means output-based or just always-online | Assume a British-style "flat" multinational culture just because the office happens to sit in Hong Kong |
| Learn who actually holds decision rights — many roles report up to the mainland, not the local MD | Negotiate salary assuming parity with European or US peers at the same title; base-pay gaps are common |
| Keep a written record of approvals and sign-offs; hierarchy here runs through title, not informal influence | Treat a 6pm calendar event as the end of the day; it may be the start of the real one once Europe wakes up |
| Ask directly, early, about overtime and bonus structure | Assume your manager's silence after a proposal means disapproval; in hierarchical firms, decisions wait for someone not in the room |

The Philippines runs on one of the steepest power-distance scores in Asia: Hofstede Insights puts it at 94 out of 100, alongside a notably low individualism score of 32, meaning the group, not the person, is the default unit of workplace life. In practice this produces management that is authoritative but personally invested — what Manila-based recruiters describe as *malasakit*, a kind of parental concern that coexists with firm deadlines. A boss who publicly praises a long-serving assistant and privately absorbs their mistake is not being soft; he is maintaining the implicit loan of loyalty, or *utang na loob*, that keeps the relationship running in both directions.

The complications show up at the edges of that warmth. The ILO has noted that the Philippines is the rare country where women do not work fewer hours than men — many clock more than 64 hours a week, often paid by the day with no premium for overtime. And the same deference that makes Filipino teams pleasant to manage can quietly misfire: a dispute surfaced on the Philippine subreddit r/AntiworkPH over an employee who addressed a superior as "Hi Maria" instead of "Hi Madam Maria," and an expat guide on Expat.com warns newcomers that inviting a Filipino boss to use first names, meant as a kindness, can instead read as a breach of order. A "yes" in a status meeting, multiple guides agree, can mean agreement, confusion, or simply a wish not to cause anyone discomfort — reading between those lines is the actual job description of managing here.

None of this means Filipino staff are passive. Workforce surveys cited by Philippine business press repeatedly flag disengagement, not rebellion, as the real cost of mismanagement: an employee who feels their *utang na loob* has gone unanswered does not quit loudly, they simply stop volunteering extra effort and wait to be asked directly for everything. For an incoming foreign manager, that makes the job less about issuing instructions and more about noticing the slow withdrawal before it becomes a resignation letter.

Hong Kong scores lower on power distance (68) but even lower on individualism (25), and the gap between those two numbers is the whole story. This is not a culture of flat, chatty Scandinavian consensus; it is a culture where the group matters enormously and the senior person in the group still outranks everyone in it, formally and without debate. A 2019 Legislative Council research brief found Hong Kong employees worked a median 42 hours a week in 2018 — more than workers in Japan, South Korea, the US, the UK, or Germany, with only Singapore running longer — and that figure undercounts the professional-services norm of staying until the boss leaves, regardless of what the task list says.

Where the system strains is at the seam between local offices and distant head offices. The South China Morning Post, citing a Michael Page survey of nearly 3,000 mainland-headquartered firms, found that roughly 70% of Hongkongers who quit a China-based employer cited rigid office culture and pay below expectations, with one leaver recalling that "the atmosphere became so stiff once the boss stepped into the room." A Glassdoor review of a German-headquartered firm's Hong Kong office put it more bluntly, warning new hires to "expect retaliation if you don't respect the hierarchy" and describing a boss treated as unchallengeable by default. A Blind thread from a Big Four consultant in Hong Kong adds the practical texture: engagements running to 8pm on weekdays, routine night calls to sync with European and American teams, and long client lunches that quietly extend everyone's day regardless of rank.

The picture is not uniformly grim. An HSBC survey cited alongside InterNations's Expat Insider data found that expatriate women rated Hong Kong among the best places in the world to advance a career, even as the same women ranked the city's work-life balance a dismal 53rd out of 191 locations surveyed. That contradiction is the honest summary of the place: the hierarchy is steep and the hours are long, but the ladder inside that hierarchy is real, and climbing it tends to move faster than it would in a flatter, friendlier organization back home.

The reckoning, then, is less about which country has "more hierarchy" and more about where that hierarchy lives. In the Philippines, the chain of command is personal and close — your boss knows your family, absorbs your excuses, and expects loyalty in a currency more emotional than contractual. In Hong Kong, the chain of command is formal and often remote — your boss may answer to someone in another city who has never seen your desk, and the hierarchy shows up less in warmth than in who is allowed to speak first in a meeting and who has to wait until 9pm to find out the meeting is happening at all.

That difference should change what you actually prepare for. Move to Manila expecting to manage relationships, not just reporting lines, and budget emotional bandwidth, not just working hours. Move to Hong Kong expecting the opposite: the relationships may stay cordial and professional while the real power sits somewhere you cannot see, on a time zone that is not yours, with sign-off procedures that will outlast your patience long before they outlast the org chart.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (r/AntiworkPH) — An employee was reprimanded by their manager for emailing "Hi Maria" instead of "Hi Madam Maria," turning a routine message into a formal dispute over whether honorifics are mandatory even among colleagues who work together daily.*

> *Expat.com forum and guide — A recurring warning to new arrivals in Manila: telling your Filipino boss "just call me by my first name" as a gesture of friendliness is sometimes received not as warmth but as an unwelcome flattening of rank, and should be offered carefully, if at all.*

> *Blind — A Big Four consultant in Hong Kong described engagements regularly running to 8pm on weekdays, driven by night calls with European and American teams, slower legacy processes, and long client lunches, with another poster from a US office asking whether that pace was normal before taking the transfer.*

> *Glassdoor — A former employee at a German-headquartered firm's Hong Kong office wrote that the local office "operates like a local company" despite its multinational branding, warning that "you should expect retaliation if you don't respect the hierarchy" and that the boss's decisions were treated as beyond question.*

> *Lazy Leverage newsletter — A manager of Filipino remote staff noted that many value job security, leave, and medical benefits over a slightly higher wage elsewhere, and that colleagues are often described, without irony, as a "second family" — a loyalty that cuts both ways once it's established.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between these two postings on the strength of an org chart alone, you are reading the wrong document. The org chart in Manila will tell you almost nothing about how decisions actually move, because decisions move through relationships, favors remembered, and faces saved; the org chart in Hong Kong will tell you quite a lot, because rank there is closer to load-bearing, and the person three boxes above you may genuinely decide your fate from an office you will never visit.

Pick the Philippines if you do better work when you like the people you report to and are willing to invest in that liking. Pick Hong Kong if you do better work when the rules of engagement are explicit, even when those rules are not in your favor. Either way, read the fine print on who actually signs off on your bonus — because in both cities, that person is probably not who your business card says it is.
