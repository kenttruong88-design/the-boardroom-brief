# Blend In or Suit Up: Dressing for Work in the Netherlands vs. Spain

**🇳🇱 Netherlands · 🇪🇸 Spain**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The Dutch have a saying — "just be normal, that is crazy enough" — and it governs their wardrobes as much as their conversation: dress to blend in, not to impress. Spain runs on the opposite principle, at least in Madrid's law firms and Barcelona's design studios, where what you wear is read as a direct signal of how seriously you take the room you're in. Move between the two and you'll either be underdressed for your first Spanish client meeting or wildly overdressed for your first Dutch one — there is no safe default outfit that works in both.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to business casual — blazer, trousers, smart shoes | Overdress with a formal suit unless the industry specifically requires it |
| Dress for the bike commute — practical, weatherproof layers are normal | Wear anything that signals you're trying too hard to stand out |
| Expect open-neck shirts and jeans to be completely normal in tech and creative sectors | Assume banking-style formality applies across all Dutch industries |
| Choose muted, practical colors — black, denim, army green, tan | Show up in flashy, statement-heavy pieces expecting to impress colleagues |
| Wear the same outfit from the office to a casual after-work drink | Change outfits between work and social settings — the Dutch mostly don't |

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in well-fitted basics — Spaniards judge fit and coordination closely | Wear athletic wear, cargo shorts, or hiking sandals in a professional setting |
| Expect real regional variation — Madrid formal, Barcelona fashion-forward | Assume Barcelona's creative dress code applies in conservative Madrid sectors |
| Take business dress seriously — Spain treats it more formally than the US | Underdress for client meetings in law, finance, or government |
| Choose neutral trousers over jeans for a polished daily look | Default to jeans as your everyday professional uniform |
| Lean into color and texture in creative industries — it reads as confidence | Wear an all-black, minimal look expecting it to read as fashion-forward everywhere |

---

Dutch dress culture is, per multiple guides including FashionUnited's market overview, built around comfort, practicality, and a specific cultural discomfort with standing out. The commute matters structurally here: with much of the country cycling to work through unpredictable weather, wardrobes lean toward weatherproof layering — trench coats, denim, sturdy jackets — over anything that would wilt in the rain or snag in bike gears. The workplace extension of "just be normal" is business casual as the default setting: blazers and trousers in most white-collar settings, but formality genuinely varies by sector, with banking still expecting suits while tech and entertainment run comfortably in open-neck shirts and jeans. What's culturally distinct is the Dutch habit of wearing the same outfit from desk to pub — there's little of the "changing for the evening" instinct common elsewhere in Europe, because the entire philosophy discourages visibly dressing to impress in the first place.

Spain inverts this almost entirely. Bows-N-Ties' guide to Spanish business etiquette states plainly that dress is taken far more seriously there than in the United States, and that assessment holds even as more relaxed, cosmopolitan norms creep into tech, consulting, and real estate. Regional variation is real and specific: Madrid remains conservative and classic, where lawyers, bankers, and government professionals still favor full suits or precisely coordinated separates, while Barcelona's business culture runs more creative and international, rewarding color, texture, and fashion-forward choices that would read as unprofessional in the capital. Even casual Spanish dress carries more intention than its Dutch counterpart — everyday style favors neutral trousers over jeans specifically because trousers read as more polished while remaining comfortable in the Mediterranean climate, and Zara and Mango's ubiquity means trend-consciousness is baked into ordinary wardrobes at every income level, not reserved for special occasions.

### The Reckoning

The genuine irony is that the Netherlands' high score on Hofstede's Indulgence dimension — a culture explicitly raised to enjoy life and give in to impulse — produces one of Europe's most restrained, function-first dress cultures, while Spain's warmer reputation for spontaneity produces one of its more visibly considered ones. The Dutch impulse toward enjoyment gets expressed elsewhere (directness, leisure time, informality of manner) rather than through clothing, which stays deliberately understated as a social leveler. Spain's formality, by contrast, isn't about restraint at all — it's about legibility: what you wear communicates where you fit in a still meaningfully hierarchical professional culture, particularly in Madrid's more traditional sectors.

The practical trap for a transplant is overcorrecting in the wrong direction. Someone moving from image-conscious Madrid to Amsterdam and continuing to dress sharply risks being read, subtly, as trying too hard — a mismatch with the "just be normal" ethos that can read as vanity rather than professionalism. Someone moving from casual Amsterdam to conservative Madrid and keeping their Dutch business-casual default risks being read as underprepared or insufficiently serious about the meeting, especially in law, finance, or government settings where the suit is still doing real communicative work. Barcelona sits in between, forgiving more experimentation but still expecting more visible effort than Dutch norms would ever require.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone asking how Dutch men and women dress casually was told that men are unafraid of color — pink, light purple, yellow — worn with well-fitted, almost tailored shirts even in casual settings, a detail that surprised the asker, who'd expected Dutch style to be uniformly muted.*

> *DutchReview — A recurring piece of practical advice for newcomers is to invest early in genuinely weatherproof closet staples, since underestimating how central the bike commute is to daily Dutch life leads most expats to relearn their entire wardrobe within the first few wet months.*

> *Quora — Someone asking how to dress like a Spaniard while visiting Granada was advised to avoid anything read as tourist-coded — cargo shorts, sports sandals, baseball caps — because Spanish everyday style prizes deliberate, effortless-looking coordination even in casual settings, and visibly "functional" clothing reads as a giveaway.*

> *Quora — A respondent explaining why some Spaniards wear simple, inexpensive outfits clarified that affordability doesn't mean carelessness — Zara and Mango are so embedded in everyday Spanish wardrobes that trend-consciousness and simplicity coexist, and locals are typically judged more on fit and coordination than on how much anything cost.*

> *Quora — Someone asking what to wear in the Netherlands was told that layering is the real skill to learn, since a single outfit often has to handle a bike ride, an office day, and an unpredictable change in weather without a wardrobe change in between.*

### Conclusion

If you're the kind of professional who reads a well-cut suit as a form of respect, Spain will reward that instinct and the Netherlands will quietly read it as overcompensation. If you'd rather disappear into the room than command it, the Dutch will welcome you and Madrid, at least, may read you as underprepared. Barcelona splits the difference and forgives more. The honest advice I'd give a friend packing for either move: for Amsterdam, buy one good raincoat and forget the rest; for Madrid, buy one good blazer and wear it more than you think you need to.
