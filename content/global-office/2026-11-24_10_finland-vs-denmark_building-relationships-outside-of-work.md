# Finland and Denmark Are Happy, Reserved and Both Entirely Booked

🇫🇮 Finland · 🇩🇰 Denmark

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Finland and Denmark are regularly named among the happiest countries on earth, a distinction that rests on the satisfaction of people who already live there. Newcomers are surveyed separately and tend to be less enthusiastic. In the 2025 Expat Insider survey from InterNations, Finland placed 43rd of 46 destinations and ranked last for expats' satisfaction with social life. Denmark has long fared little better on the specific matter of making friends. Both countries have worked out how to be content without much outside assistance, which is an enviable arrangement for the residents and a logistical problem for everyone else. The OECD's How's Life report, using 2013 data, recorded satisfaction with time use of 7.8 out of ten in Denmark, the highest in the OECD, and 7.7 in Finland. Residents, in other words, find their own company and their own schedules entirely adequate, which may explain why they are not looking for yours.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Make the first move, because locals are unlikely to approach you | Wait to be invited and conclude that nobody likes you |
| Join a hobby group, a Finnish course or a sports class where people meet regularly | Rely on open-ended offers to grab a drink sometime |
| Try to make one genuine Finnish friend first, who can then bring you to local events | Expect a wide circle within the first season |
| Respect personal space and keep small talk short | Fill silences on principle |
| Accept coffee with colleagues, which is a social ritual in its own right | Assume that a quiet colleague is an unhappy one |
| Treat the sauna as an optional setting for conversation | Feel obliged to join, since neither sauna nor alcohol is required |

### Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Suggest a specific plan, such as a coffee, a meal or a film | Say that you should meet up sometime and leave it there |
| Plan weeks ahead and offer another date when told someone is busy | Read a full diary as a rejection |
| Join a club or association, of which there are more than 100,000 | Expect friendships to form at the office after four o'clock |
| Learn some Danish, which a Copenhagen Post guide calls the best route to integration | Insist that everyone switch to English indefinitely |
| Accept that close friendships, and invitations home, may take years | Push into private life before you have been invited |
| Ask a Danish colleague for coffee or lunch | Mistake a polite colleague for a friend |

## Finland: Content, Silent and Very Fond of Its Own Company

The numbers for Finland are unusually severe for a country that tops happiness rankings. According to InterNations' Expat Insider 2025, Finland ranked 46th of 46 both for expats' satisfaction with their social life and for how difficult they find making local friends. The survey reports that 36 percent of expats in Finland do not feel at home, against 23 percent globally, and 28 percent do not feel welcome, against 18 percent globally. The 2024 edition, as reported by Xataka On, placed Finland third from the bottom of 53 countries, with expats' satisfaction with life falling from 15th to 52nd in a single year. InterNations noted that the language is among the hardest to learn, and some respondents called Finns inward-looking.

The Finnish perspective is gentler, though not very different. A thread on Expat.com about finding friends in Finland includes a Finnish member who says Finns tend to be shy and advises newcomers to take the first step. Another member, in Kuopio, finds daily life and socialising hard without the language, while a third is impressed by how helpful people are even though close friendships are slow to form. The University of Jyvaskyla offers the same advice in more institutional language: be the first to approach, find one real Finnish friend who can bring you to events, and use structured settings such as classes and speed-friending. It adds that Finns generally speak good English and that the first Finnish friendship is the hardest.

Finnair's Bluewings feature on its foreign colleagues reports that foreign employees tend to appreciate the reserve, since one Japanese colleague finds Finns careful about personal space and not especially fond of small talk, which suits her. Another says that sharing a sauna with colleagues or strangers took years to get used to. None of this makes the loneliness imaginary, and the same programme offers a modestly practical corrective: shared interests, groups and meetups, and the observation that in a small city one keeps meeting the same people, which is how acquaintances eventually become something else. The loneliness is real nonetheless. Citing the Finnish Institute for Health and Welfare, an Yle radio programme reports that about one in ten Finns experience prolonged loneliness, rising to one in five among young foreign men.

## Denmark: A Hundred Thousand Clubs and a Four O'Clock Exit

Denmark has the more organised social reputation and, on the survey evidence, a comparable record for friendship. The Copenhagen Post, reporting on the 2019 InterNations survey, noted that Denmark ranked third worst of 68 countries for making friends abroad, with 36 percent of expats reporting difficulty, against 37 percent in Finland. In a 2018 blog, the Confederation of Danish Industry cited an earlier edition in which Denmark ranked 64th for ease of settling in, and pointed out that Danes typically leave work around four, leaving newcomers without family free for a long evening. After-work socialising with colleagues, the blog says, is less common than in many other countries.

Researchers van Bakel and Vance, writing in the Journal of Global Mobility in 2023, interviewed 16 expatriates and identified three cultural factors: a relatively homogeneous society, a strong emphasis on equality, and a clear divide between public and private life. They also link the difficulty of earning trust to Denmark's high institutional collectivism score in the GLOBE study. Hofstede Insights' framework gives Denmark a power distance of 18 and an uncertainty avoidance of 23 according to a secondary summary, which is consistent with a flat, low-anxiety culture that sees little need to hurry intimacy.

The alternative is organised. According to Denmark.dk, the country has more than 100,000 active associations for 5.7 million people, and about 41 percent of Danes volunteer through associations. A profile by the Danish engineers' union IDA reports that colleagues are often the only Danes an expat meets regularly, since work and personal life are kept apart, and that a coffee invitation costs a Danish colleague nothing and means a great deal. Danish diaries, according to The Local's contributors, fill weeks ahead, which means spontaneity is a skill reserved for the very patient and the very early planner. The Local's Expat Stories feature found five internationals who made Danish friends through classes, work and university, with the lessons that Danes plan weeks ahead and that a busy diary is not a rejection.

## The Reckoning

On the evidence, neither country offers an easy social landing, and the real difference lies in the scaffolding. Denmark provides infrastructure: a dense network of clubs, a culture of scheduled invitations, and a well-documented route in through shared activity. A newcomer who joins something, suggests a specific plan and waits patiently has a reasonable chance of a friendship within a year or so, although close ones may take longer. Finland offers fewer visible handholds and, according to the most recent survey, the last place in InterNations' social life ranking.

The Finnish upside is that reserve is not hostility, and several foreign residents describe it as restful. Where Denmark is polite and booked, Finland is quiet and available, which suits people who like being left alone and can tolerate being left alone a little more than planned. Neither country will arrange a social life on a newcomer's behalf. Both reward the person who turns up to the same class, club or sauna often enough to become a familiar presence, and both will eventually concede, in a measured and unsentimental fashion, that this counts as friendship.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — A Finnish member says that Finns are shy and unlikely to approach strangers, and advises newcomers to make the first move and join hobby groups, while another member in Kuopio finds socialising hard without Finnish.*

> *InterNations Expat Insider — A German expat in Espoo says it is difficult to get in contact with locals, while a Singaporean expat finds Finns friendlier in summer than in the long, dark winter.*

> *Yle — A guest on the programme says meeting people is not especially hard in a small city where you keep running into the same faces, and that owning a dog and visiting a dog park leads quickly to conversation.*

> *IDA — An expat engineer in Denmark learned that colleagues remain colleagues, so he avoids Friday afternoon meetings, found like-minded people through peer groups, and wishes Danes would simply invite newcomers for coffee.*

> *The Local — Internationals in Copenhagen who made Danish friends say it began with a pole-dancing class, a work lunch, or a university pairing, and that a specific invitation worked better than a vague one.*

## Conclusion

Anyone planning a move to either country should budget for a quiet first year. In Denmark, join an association early, suggest concrete plans, and accept that the diary fills up weeks in advance. In Finland, make the first move, find one local friend through a shared activity, and consider Finnish lessons as both a skill and a social venue. In both, remember that colleagues may be pleasant and entirely unavailable after four o'clock.

Employers sending staff to either country would do well to treat the social side of relocation as part of the package rather than a personal matter. A buddy, a club membership or a language course costs little. The alternative is a highly qualified employee in a very happy country, with no one to tell.
