# Flat but Distant, Steep but Formal: Germany and Poland Disagree About What a Boss Is For

**🇩🇪 Germany · 🇵🇱 Poland**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A German manager will tell you exactly what's wrong with your work in a team meeting, in plain language, and expect you to thank them for the clarity. A Polish manager is far more likely to make the final call alone and expect you to execute it without visible debate, but the correction, if it comes, will happen quietly, formally, and privately. Germany's hierarchy is comparatively flat and brutally direct. Poland's hierarchy is steep and carefully cushioned. Neither one is what most newcomers expect from "efficient, orderly" Central Europe.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take direct, critical feedback as useful information, not a personal attack | Cushion your own feedback so heavily that the actual point gets lost |
| Use titles and formal address (Sie) until explicitly invited to switch to informal (du) | Assume flat hierarchy means no hierarchy — expertise still commands real deference |
| Prepare thoroughly before raising an issue — vague complaints get little traction | Pad a meeting with small talk — most German work interactions are objective-focused |
| Expect your manager to give precise, unambiguous task instructions | Expect praise as often as you might elsewhere — silence often just means "fine" |
| Push back on a decision with facts and structure — it's respected, not resented | Work visibly late to signal commitment — it isn't admired here the way it is elsewhere |

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address your boss formally, even if peer-to-peer conversation is more relaxed | Directly contradict your boss in an open meeting — raise concerns privately instead |
| Expect decisions to concentrate at the top, especially in traditional companies | Assume independent initiative from junior staff is automatically welcomed |
| Follow established procedures precisely — Poland's high uncertainty avoidance rewards it | Mistake formality with a superior for coldness — it's a register, not a relationship signal |
| Notice whether your company skews traditional-hierarchical or modern-flat — both exist | Assume every Polish company runs the same way — younger, international firms often don't |
| Value direct speech among peers, even while staying formal with superiors | Push back on standard procedure without proposing a structured, well-justified alternative |

### Germany: Flat Structure, High Voltage Feedback

German management culture presents a genuine paradox to newcomers: the hierarchy is structurally flat by global standards — Hofstede scores Germany at just 35 on power distance — yet the boss-employee relationship remains notably formal and, per multiple business-culture guides including Commisceo Global, genuinely distant, especially at more traditional or larger companies. What replaces informal warmth is precision: managers are expected to give clear, unambiguous direction, and authority is earned through demonstrated expertise rather than rank alone.

The signature feature, though, is feedback delivery. iamExpat.de and multiple workplace guides describe direct, unvarnished criticism as standard practice — not softened, not buried in a "compliment sandwich," and explicitly framed as a sign of respect and professional seriousness rather than an attack. Hofstede's relatively high uncertainty-avoidance score for Germany (65) helps explain the appetite for clear rules and structured processes underneath the directness: ambiguity is the actual enemy, and blunt feedback is simply the fastest way to eliminate it. For a newcomer from a more indirect culture, the first team meeting where a manager critiques a deliverable point-by-point, in front of the group, without any cushioning, can feel personal. It isn't meant to be.

### Poland: Steep Hierarchy, Careful Delivery

Polish management culture runs in nearly the opposite configuration. Hofstede scores Poland at 68 on power distance — genuinely hierarchical — compounded by the highest uncertainty-avoidance score of any country in this comparison set, 93, reflecting a strong institutional preference for formal rules and predictable procedure. World Business Culture and Cultural Atlas both describe decision-making as concentrated at senior levels, particularly in more traditional companies, with independent initiative from junior staff not always welcomed the way it might be elsewhere.

Crucially, though, the directness Poles are known to value among peers doesn't extend the same way upward: guides consistently warn that directly contradicting a boss in an open meeting is unwise, even for cultures otherwise comfortable with blunt speech, and that the far safer path is raising disagreement privately and formally. Communication with superiors runs noticeably more formal than communication between colleagues of equal rank — a register shift that surprises newcomers who assume Polish directness is uniform across every relationship in the office. It's worth noting the generational and sectoral split here too: multiple sources note that this steep, formal hierarchy is more characteristic of older, more traditional Polish companies, while younger, internationally connected firms increasingly run flatter.

### The Reckoning

The irony sitting under both cultures: Germany, the flatter hierarchy on paper, delivers criticism with more force and less cushioning than Poland, the steeper hierarchy on paper, actually does. Poland's real hierarchy gets managed quietly and formally — disagreement moves into private, respectful channels rather than open confrontation — while Germany's comparatively egalitarian structure comes wrapped in a bluntness that can feel, in the moment, more hierarchical than it structurally is. A newcomer who reads German directness as evidence of a harsh, top-down culture will misjudge how much real influence a well-argued pushback actually carries there. A newcomer who reads Polish formality with superiors as proof of a rigid, closed-off culture will miss how much genuine directness is available to them — just not upward, and not in the room.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *iamExpat.de — A newcomer to a German company described their first team meeting as being "barraged" with pointed questions and critical remarks about their work, and only later understood that engaging with the critique rather than defending against it was what actually earned colleagues' respect.*

> *Quora — Responding to a question about whether Poland's leadership style matches descriptions in Erin Meyer's "Culture Map," a Polish respondent agreed it was broadly accurate but noted the hierarchy was gentler and more procedural than commonly assumed — less about fear, more about a strong cultural preference for established process.*

> *Reddit — A foreign hire at a German firm described the disorientation of realizing that a manager's blunt line-by-line critique of a project, delivered flatly in front of the team, was intended as useful and was in fact forgotten by everyone else in the room within the hour.*

> *Quora — Someone describing autocratic leadership in a Central European context noted that the real skill for a foreign employee wasn't avoiding hierarchy but learning exactly which procedural channel disagreement was supposed to travel through — direct in Germany, private and formal in Poland.*

> *Reddit — A newcomer working in a traditional Polish company described being quietly taken aside after proposing a process change in an open meeting, and learning that the idea itself had been well received — just not the setting in which it was raised.*

---

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Germany, prepare for blunt, specific feedback and treat it as data, not judgment — and don't mistake the flat org chart for an absence of real expertise-based authority. If you're moving to Poland, save your disagreement for a private, formal conversation, and don't assume the hierarchy you're seeing at a legacy company describes every Polish workplace. The honest version for a friend: Germany will hurt your feelings briefly and tell you exactly how to fix the problem; Poland will spare your feelings and expect you to figure out the right room to raise it in. Pick your discomfort.
