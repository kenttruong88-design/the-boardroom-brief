# In Prague You Earn a Friend One Beer at a Time. In Zagreb You're Handed One, Pending Review.

🇨🇿 Czech Republic · 🇭🇷 Croatia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Czechia has one of the lowest loneliness rates in the EU, under 10 percent per the 2022 EU Loneliness Survey, and 96 percent of Czechs say they know someone they could rely on in a crisis, above the OECD average of 91 percent. Yet InterNations' 2026 Expat Insider survey finds only 31 percent of foreign residents think Czechs are friendly toward newcomers, and 59 percent call making local friends difficult. The math isn't complicated: Czechs aren't short on friends, they're short on vacancies. Croatia runs a different waitlist, one where the entry fee is a single cup of coffee that doubles as a background check.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇿 Czech Republic

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept an invitation to "jedno pivo" (one beer) even if you don't drink | Expect it to actually be one beer |
| Let colleagues keep formal address (vykání) until they switch first | Rush intimacy with "ty" or first names in week one |
| Join a class or club with a fixed weekly roster | Expect one mixer to produce a lasting friendship |
| Bring flowers or wine if invited to someone's home | Show up empty-handed, or treat a coffee as a home invite |
| Treat dry complaints about work or bureaucracy as bonding | Respond earnestly, as if it were a request for solutions |
| Show up reliably, week after week, even when little happens | Read reserved small talk as personal rejection |

#### 🇭🇷 Croatia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat "idemo na kavu" (let's get coffee) as a real event | Rush it, or check your phone throughout |
| Show up for the extended family lunch if invited | Decline to "keep the weekend open" |
| Learn which football club your acquaintances support | Stay neutral about club rivalries |
| Expect formality — surnames, titles — even from warm people | Mistake early warmth for instant closeness |
| Join in if invited to sing klapa or share a toast | Treat it as a tourist performance |
| Let bonds build through small favors and neighborhood presence | Network your way in with LinkedIn-style small talk |

### The Republic of Already-Full Friend Groups

Czechia's social infrastructure is excellent; it's simply not built for expansion. The OECD's *Social Connections and Loneliness* report finds Czechs hold dense, stable support networks built over decades of shared schooling and neighborhood continuity, not the transactional networking common in more mobile societies. Hofstede Insights scores Czechia at 70 on individualism versus a regional low of 41 for Croatia, a gap that looks, on paper, like Czechs should be easier to walk into. In practice, individualism here means self-sufficiency, not an active search for new members. Nobody is recruiting for a friend group that already works fine.

That closedness meets a workplace that keeps its distance on principle. Czech offices retain formal address between colleagues for years, sometimes permanently, and the line between work and home is load-bearing, a habit expats often mistake for coldness. The workaround is the hospoda, the neighborhood pub where "jedno pivo" after work extends the office informally, low-stakes and recurring, and where friendships assemble in small increments rather than a single gesture. Prague's international workforce, now over a quarter of the city's labor pool, makes this easy to skip entirely, since English-speaking colleagues offer a comfortable substitute that never requires cracking the local code.

### The Republic of the Two-Hour Coffee

Croatia's version is warmer to look at and, per several accounts, just as hard to solve. "Idemo na kavu" is less an invitation than an audition: an unhurried sit-down that screens whether a relationship goes further. Rush it or treat it as a caffeine transaction, and there's usually no second round. Pass it, and the door opens fast, often straight to a family Sunday lunch, which functions less as a meal than an admissions committee.

Underneath sits a genuinely more collectivist structure — Croatia's lower Hofstede score reflects extended family networks, football-club identity, and neighborhood ties that remain a person's core social world well into adulthood. That's good news for newcomers who clear the first coffee: acceptance tends to be immediate and total, extending to relatives and neighbors at the table. The catch, per multiple expat accounts, is that Croatians don't use small talk to meet strangers the way Americans or Britons do; they use it to maintain bonds already inside the circle. The system runs on being vouched in, not networked in.

### The Reckoning

Put the two together and the irony sharpens: the more individualist country, Czechia, demands the longer campaign — friendship arrives through dozens of small, repeated encounters at the pub or the language class, with no single breakthrough moment. The more collectivist country, Croatia, front-loads the vetting into one high-stakes ritual, after which the reward is immediate, near-total inclusion into an existing family structure that Czech incrementalism never quite offers.

The failure modes mirror each other. In Czechia, expats fail by being too eager — pushing for first names or expecting a networking event to substitute for months of quiet consistency. In Croatia, they fail by being too transactional — nailing the coffee and the lunch invite, then not showing up again, which reads as disinterest, a worse offense than never trying.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A prospective IT relocator to Czechia, only conversational in Czech, asked how realistic it was to make local friends at all, worried the language gap would keep them permanently on the outside regardless of effort.*

> *teamblind.com — A tech worker relocated to Prague for a multinational's office described how easy it is to go months speaking only English, cycling through the same colleagues for after-work beers, without meeting a Czech person outside the building.*

> *Expat.com forum — An expat in Croatia assumed a friendly first coffee with a new acquaintance had sealed a friendship, then realized weeks later the single "kava" had been a screening round, and the missing second invitation was the actual verdict.*

> *Chasing the Donkey (expat blog) — A first-year expat in Croatia described coming up empty on local friendships for months, until small neighborly gestures — returning borrowed tools, waving from the balcony — eventually produced an invitation to a family Sunday lunch that changed everything.*

> *expats.cz reader thread — A longtime Prague resident argued the real issue isn't Czech coldness but venue: friend-making rarely happens through generic meetups the way it does in the US, but through direct personal introduction, a coworker, classmate, or neighbor, and expats chasing the wrong venue simply never get introduced to anyone.*

### Conclusion

Neither country is unfriendly; both are running different admissions processes, and expats who apply the wrong country's rules tend to conclude the whole continent hates them. In Czechia, the strategy is patience — commit to one recurring fixture, a language class or a Tuesday hospoda visit, and let months of low-stakes contact do what no grand gesture can. In Croatia, the strategy is follow-through — accept the coffee, accept the lunch that follows, and keep showing up, because the invitation tends to come around only once.

The honest summary: in Prague, friendship is a subscription you slowly earn; in Zagreb, it's a trial membership you can lose by not renewing on time.
