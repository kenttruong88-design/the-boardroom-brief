# Jamaica Has More Female Bosses Than Almost Anywhere on Earth. Britain Still Can't Close a 13-Point Spreadsheet.

**🇯🇲 Jamaica · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Jamaica has, by one measure, the most female-led workforce on the planet: a majority of its managers are women, a statistic most Scandinavian governments would hold a press conference about. The United Kingdom, meanwhile, requires every large employer to publish its gender pay gap by law, has done so for nearly a decade, and in 2026 that gap is still 12.8 percent. One country achieved representation without legislating for it. The other legislated for it and is still waiting on the arithmetic to catch up. Neither, it turns out, is the feminist finish line the relocation brochures imply, and both have a fairly specific, fairly different set of things you'll want to know before you sign a lease.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇯🇲 Jamaica

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect your direct manager to quite plausibly be a woman — it's the statistical norm, not the exception | Assume a female-majority management layer means a uniformly supportive office; colleagues describe plenty of friction between women at different levels |
| Dress with some formality in finance, government, and corporate offices, regardless of gender | Treat "island casual" as an office dress code; tailored and conservative is the default in professional settings |
| Communicate directly and expect the same back | Mistake Jamaican bluntness for personal hostility — it reads the same whether your manager is a man or a woman |
| Ask how maternity leave is actually handled on your specific team, not just what the law says | Assume legal protection equals practical tolerance; pregnancy has reportedly cost people favor with superiors |
| Notice who sits on the board, not just who runs the floor | Confuse a female-majority staff with female-majority ownership or governance — boardrooms often still skew male |
| Follow the local conversation about boys falling behind in school and work | Assume Jamaica's gender debate maps neatly onto the "women need more opportunity" framing you may know from home |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the gender pay gap report before you accept an offer — companies over 250 staff must publish one | Assume the published number reflects your own team; gaps vary wildly by seniority band and location |
| Show up to the pub after work occasionally if you want visibility | Skip it entirely thinking it's optional small talk; a surprising amount of informal networking happens over a pint |
| Check the actual composition of the specific team you're joining, especially in finance and tech | Assume a diverse-looking leadership page means a diverse engineering floor; the two often diverge sharply |
| Use formal HR and grievance channels — they exist and are relatively well-documented | Expect speed; formal process in Britain often means slow process |
| Bring your own cake for your office birthday | Expect a party; a card and mild embarrassment is closer to the standard |
| Note which sectors skew which way — public sector and healthcare lean female, finance and engineering lean male | Generalize "the UK workplace" as one culture; it is several, badly stitched together |

### Jamaica

The headline number is genuinely remarkable. In a global survey of 108 countries, the International Labour Organization found Jamaica had the highest share of women in management anywhere in the world — 59.3 percent, ahead of Colombia and the Dominican Republic. The World Bank's gender data portal puts women in senior and middle management at 63.9 percent, placing Jamaica in the top tier of countries that track the figure at all. For a relocating professional, this is the kind of statistic that sounds like a trick question, because it is one: Jamaica's overall female labor force participation rate was 61.4 percent in 2023 against 75.8 percent for men, according to the same World Bank data — a meaningfully lower share of women working at all, even as the women who do work are disproportionately the ones giving the instructions.

The Jamaica Gleaner's 2026 look at VM Group, one of the island's larger financial institutions, shows the shape of the paradox at company scale: women made up 72 percent of staff and 52.8 percent of senior leadership, yet held only two of eight board seats. Management is a glass floor women walk on comfortably. The boardroom above it is a different story. And the texture underneath the statistic is more complicated than a brochure would suggest — a 2024 Jamaica Observer feature collected accounts from five women describing controlling, class-conscious, or dismissive treatment under female bosses specifically, including one manager who reportedly drove to an employee's home to verify a sick day. None of this disproves the headline figure. It simply means "mostly run by women" and "good for women" are not the same claim, and Jamaica's own press treats them as separate conversations — alongside a parallel, very public domestic debate about boys and men falling behind in school and the workforce, a framing that will feel unfamiliar to anyone arriving with assumptions shaped elsewhere. The practical upshot for a newcomer is that the relevant unit of analysis is never "Jamaica" — it's the specific office, the specific owner, and whether that owner answers to a board that looks nothing like the staff below it.

### United Kingdom

Britain's approach is the opposite instinct: fewer dramatic headline numbers, far more paperwork. The ONS reports the UK's gender pay gap for all employees stood at 12.8 percent in April 2025, down from 13.1 percent a year earlier, with the full-time figure at 6.9 percent — both measures having fallen by roughly a quarter over the past decade, which is to say: slowly, and by law rather than by culture. Mandatory gender pay gap reporting for large employers has existed since 2017, which means the UK is one of the few countries where a job applicant can simply look up how badly a specific company is doing before accepting an offer.

What the spreadsheet doesn't show is sectoral concentration. Public administration, education, and the NHS lean female; finance, engineering, and much of tech do not. A 2026 post on the professional forum Blind, from an engineer in London, described a common pattern bluntly: most development teams she'd seen were effectively all-male, with women funnelled toward management, business analysis, or short-lived graduate cohorts — isolating enough that she'd considered leaving the industry altogether. Hofstede Insights scores the UK 66 on its Masculinity dimension, comfortably above the global average and indicative of a culture that prizes competition, assertiveness, and visible achievement — a cultural trait that helps explain why formal equality policy and informal exclusion coexist so comfortably in the same building. Jamaica has no individually published Hofstede score at all, having never been part of the original IBM-era survey set, so the comparison stops there rather than being stretched into something it isn't. What the UK number does predict reasonably well is tone: meetings where credit-taking is explicit, performance reviews that reward self-promotion, and a workplace vocabulary — "banter," "bants," the pub debrief — that quietly rewards whoever is most comfortable occupying the room.

### The Reckoning

If your metric is simply who is in charge day to day, Jamaica wins without much contest: a majority-female management class is not something most relocating professionals will have experienced, and it is not nothing. But the UK's lower-looking headline gap sits inside a far more formalized infrastructure — statutory reporting, employment tribunals, DEI functions with actual budgets — tools that barely exist in Jamaica's smaller, more personality-driven corporate culture, where redress for a bad boss is more often informal than legal.

So the honest comparison is between two different failure modes. Jamaica offers representation without much institutional scaffolding to make that representation consistently humane, boardrooms notwithstanding. The UK offers scaffolding — audits, published numbers, grievance procedures — in service of a gap that moves at roughly a percentage point a decade. Pick your frustration: unwritten rules enforced by people, or written rules enforced slowly. Neither failure mode is fatal to a career, but each demands a different kind of vigilance, and assuming the wrong one is the fastest way to be blindsided by the right one.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — a London-based engineer described being the only woman on her development team, with most of the company's women funnelled into management or short graduate cohorts instead of engineering roles, and admitted that on bad days she considered leaving the industry entirely.*

> *Expat Exchange forum, Kingston — a longer-term American expat said she hadn't personally encountered workplace gender prejudice in Jamaica and pointed to the national motto of unity as part of the local self-image, while acknowledging she had no insight into how local salary negotiations or hiring actually worked.*

> *Jamaica Observer reader interviews — several women described female managers who policed their clothing, home address, or family situation rather than offering solidarity, with one manager reportedly showing up uninvited to confirm a sick day was genuine.*

> *Expat.com forum, England — one poster new to a UK office found herself excluded from the real social and professional network, which ran through after-work drinking and smoking-break small talk rather than anything written down; a second, longer-resident poster disputed that this was typical and noted that bringing your own birthday cake is simply how it's done.*

> *TalesMag Real Post Report, Kingston — a foreign service spouse noted that professional dress for women in Jamaica trends more conservative than visitors expect, with slacks and covered shoulders standard in government and office settings despite the climate.*

### Conclusion

Neither country should be chosen on the strength of a single statistic, because both statistics are, in their own way, misleading once you're actually sitting at a desk. Ask a prospective employer in Jamaica who is actually on the board, not just who runs the floor. Ask one in the UK what their published pay gap report says about your specific grade and team, not the company average. The national number gets you in the door; the team's actual composition decides what your next five years look like.

Pack accordingly: tailored clothing and a thicker skin for Kingston, a willingness to show your face at the pub and read the fine print of a PDF for London. If there's a single honest takeaway, it's that representation and reward are two different line items, and no country has yet figured out how to fully reconcile them — Jamaica just files that discrepancy under "culture," while Britain files it, appropriately, as an annual compliance report.
