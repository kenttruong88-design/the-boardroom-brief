**Who Talks First: The Boss Question in Beijing and Boston**

🇨🇳 China · 🇺🇸 USA

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

On Hofstede's power-distance index, China scores close to 80 and the United States sits near 40 — a gap wide enough that researchers studying the 2019 Ohio auto-glass plant chronicled in the documentary "American Factory" found it could sink a joint venture on its own. In an American office, a junior analyst emailing a VP directly is initiative. In a Chinese office, the same move can read as a serious breach of protocol — you address the highest-ranking person in the room first, and you very rarely skip a level. Neither instinct is a personality flaw. They are two entirely different theories of what a boss is for.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address the most senior person in the room first, in meetings and in email chains | Skip a level of hierarchy to raise an issue, even with good intentions |
| Invest in guanxi (relationship-building) — meals, favors, and informal socializing with your boss | Treat after-hours socializing with leadership as optional networking rather than expected practice |
| Expect centralized decisions made by senior leadership based on precedent | Push for open dissent in a group meeting — raise concerns privately instead |
| Show deference through action (staying visible until your boss leaves) rather than verbal praise alone | Assume a quiet meeting means no one has an opinion — opinions surface in private, afterward |
| Learn that criticism of your work, when it comes, may arrive indirectly or through a third party | Take the absence of direct feedback as a sign you're doing fine |

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Speak up in meetings — managers expect input and read silence as disengagement | Wait to be explicitly invited to share your opinion |
| Address your manager by first name and treat access to them as normal | Assume a friendly, informal tone with your boss means the relationship lacks real authority |
| Document decisions and agreements in writing after meetings | Rely on verbal understanding alone — American workplaces run on the paper trail |
| Advocate directly for your own promotion or raise | Wait passively for tenure or loyalty to be automatically rewarded |
| Treat your manager as a resource and coach, not solely a gatekeeper | Assume hierarchy doesn't matter just because the tone is casual — final authority is still concentrated |

---

China's high power-distance score, per Hofstede's research and widely cited applications of his framework, traces to a Confucian tradition in which subordinates are expected to respect and generally not question senior authority, and where rank is explicitly observed in every interaction — including something as small as the order in which people are greeted in a meeting. Decision-making in Chinese firms tends to be centralized, often shaped by precedent and by the judgment of a senior figure rather than open debate among the team. This is frequently paired with a management style built on guanxi — the cultivation of personal relationship and obligation — meaning the boss you drink baijiu with on a Thursday night is not doing something separate from work; that relationship *is* part of the job. Cross-cultural management research on the "American Factory" case found that Chinese executives who imported long hours, minimal worker feedback loops, and rigid hierarchy into a US plant produced significant friction with American employees who expected to be consulted.

The United States, by contrast, scores in the low-to-middle range on power distance, and American management theory has spent decades formalizing the idea that hierarchy should be "for convenience" rather than status — a framing echoed across business-culture literature comparing the two countries. Managers are expected to be accessible, employees are expected to contribute opinions rather than simply execute instructions, and self-advocacy (asking directly for a raise or a promotion) is normalized rather than seen as presumptuous. The trade-off is that American hierarchy, while less ceremonially visible, is not actually flatter in terms of who holds final authority — it is just less openly performed. A new hire who mistakes the first-name basis and open-door policy for an absence of hierarchy will misjudge how decisions actually get made.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely disorienting part for anyone moving between these two systems is that silence means opposite things in each. In a Chinese meeting, a quiet room after a proposal is presented is often not agreement — dissent, per multiple cross-cultural accounts, tends to surface afterward, privately, one level at a time, rather than in the room. In an American meeting, silence is more often read literally: no objection raised generally means no objection exists, and a manager who later learns a team member privately disagreed the whole time will often feel misled rather than respected.

There's a pay dimension too, worth noting without overstating it: foreign employees working for Chinese firms are frequently reported (per cross-cultural workplace accounts) to earn a premium over local colleagues in similar roles, while working comparatively standard hours against colleagues logging considerably longer ones — a dynamic that can create its own quiet resentment, however generous it looks on the expat's own payslip.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *TeamBlind — An engineer who transferred from a US tech firm to its Shanghai office described being confused when his direct reports agreed with every proposal in meetings, only to learn much later — through his own manager — that several had serious reservations they'd voiced privately rather than in the room.*

> *Quora — Someone who worked for a Chinese manufacturing company for three years wrote that the clearest sign you'd fallen out of favor wasn't anything said to your face — it was being left off an informal dinner invitation that everyone else on the team quietly received.*

> *r/AskAnAmerican — A returning expat compared his Chinese boss, who rarely gave direct praise but repeatedly invited him to family events, with his American boss, who praised him openly in meetings but kept work and personal life firmly separate — and admitted he still isn't sure which one actually respected him more.*

> *InterNations Shanghai — A German manager noted that the biggest operational mistake he made in his first year was routing a disagreement through email to a Chinese senior colleague; resolving it required an in-person conversation initiated through an intermediary, something no onboarding guide had flagged as necessary.*

> *r/cscareerquestions — A US-based employee who joined a Chinese-owned company's American office observed that meetings nominally ran on US norms — open floor, first-name basis — but that real decisions were still made afterward by the most senior person present, making the "flat" culture feel more cosmetic than structural.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving into a Chinese management structure, invest in the relationship before you invest in being right — deference and loyalty are the currency, and results alone won't buy you influence. If you're moving into an American one, stop waiting for permission to speak — silence there is taken at face value, not as a polite placeholder for disagreement you'll raise later. The through-line for both: figure out, in your first month, whether disagreement in your new workplace is supposed to happen in the room or after it — because guessing wrong on that single question will cost you more credibility than almost anything else you could get wrong.
