## Who Shows You the Ropes: Dakar's Godfathers and Accra's Onboarding Decks

**🇸🇳 Senegal · 🇬🇭 Ghana**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Dakar, your first real orientation happens over attaya tea with a manager who has decided, unprompted, to take a personal interest in your career. In Accra, it happens in a shared drive, via a 40-slide onboarding deck with a mentorship-matching form attached to slide 38. Both countries will tell you, sincerely, that mentorship is central to how they do business. They mean two almost entirely different things by the word.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇳 Senegal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet everyone individually each morning, including security and support staff, before discussing work | Walk past colleagues straight to your desk — it reads as cold, not efficient |
| Accept that your assigned mentor may check on your family, not just your workload | Treat a mentor relationship as purely transactional or time-boxed |
| Use formal titles (Monsieur, Madame) until explicitly invited to switch to first names | Assume French fluency alone earns credibility — Wolof carries real social weight in the office |
| Expect instructions to be explicit and detailed from senior staff | Push back on a manager's method in front of other junior staff |
| Build the relationship before asking for anything, including basic favors | Rush a first meeting straight into the agenda |
| Budget real time for the "installation" period — weeks, not days | Expect a written 90-day onboarding plan; the plan is the relationship |

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Complete the formal onboarding paperwork and orientation sessions promptly — they're taken seriously | Assume the formal HR process is a substitute for informally introducing yourself around |
| Seek out a mentor actively; larger firms increasingly run structured matching programs | Wait passively for mentorship to be assigned — it may never come without asking |
| Show deference to older or more senior colleagues, even informally | Mistake Accra's English-language, deck-driven HR style for a Western workplace with no hierarchy |
| Ask direct questions about process — Ghanaian HR teams generally expect and reward it | Skip the well-being and social side of onboarding — team lunches, WhatsApp groups, church or mosque ties often matter for integration |
| Network relentlessly outside formal channels — referrals still open more doors than the portal | Underestimate how competitive entry-level hiring is; treat an offer as low-stakes |

### Senegal: The Godfather System

Senegalese workplaces run on Teranga — hospitality — before they run on process, and mentorship there is less a program than an inherited obligation. According to management-culture guides tracking the country, managers commonly adopt a paternalistic stance toward junior staff, one that extends past task supervision into a genuine, sometimes intrusive, interest in an employee's family and wellbeing (Commisceo Global, Rivermate). Hierarchy is pronounced: instructions from above tend to be explicit and detailed, and employees are expected to execute rather than negotiate the approach, a dynamic consistent with the high power-distance norms Hofstede's framework documents across much of West Africa.

The catch is that this system is not officially onboarded — there is no slide deck explaining it. A new hire either gets absorbed into a senior colleague's orbit, or doesn't, and the difference determines almost everything about how fast they become productive. Meanwhile the labor market underneath this cultural machinery is unforgiving: Senegal's overall unemployment rate hit 22.9% in the first quarter of 2026, up from 21.7% a year earlier, with more than a third of 15-to-24-year-olds neither employed nor in education or training (Ecofin Agency, citing national statistics). A mentor, in that context, isn't a nicety. It's frequently the entire difference between a contract renewal and unemployment.

### Ghana: The Mentorship Deck

Ghana's corporate sector, concentrated in Accra's banking, telecom, and multinational hubs, has moved rapidly toward codified HR practice — onboarding checklists, structured feedback cycles, and, increasingly, formal mentorship-matching programs pitched explicitly as retention tools (SEDAT Consult, Ajna Services). Firms like Ecobank Ghana have built out well-being infrastructure — fitness, mental health support, financial literacy — as part of onboarding rather than an afterthought (SEDAT Consult). On paper, it looks like the deck has replaced the godfather.

In practice, Ghanaians describe a parallel informal economy of relationships running underneath the paperwork: church networks, university alumni ties, and family referrals still do a disproportionate amount of the actual career-building. Ghana's 2021 census put youth unemployment at roughly a third for 15-to-24-year-olds, with unemployment notably higher among urban young women than young men (World Bank/ILO data, Ghana ILO Youth Country Brief). Formal mentorship programs help, but expats and returnees alike report that the fastest onboarding still happens through whoever introduces you around after the HR induction ends — the process just runs quieter, and requires you to ask.

### The Reckoning

The two systems look, from the outside, like a spectrum from "informal" (Senegal) to "formal" (Ghana). The more interesting divergence is who bears the burden of initiating contact. In Senegal, mentorship is largely assigned to you by virtue of hierarchy — a senior person decides you're worth investing in, and you receive it whether or not you asked. In Ghana, the formal structure exists, but activating it is the new hire's job: ask for the mentor, join the program, show up to the social events. Passivity is rewarded in Dakar and punished in Accra.

There's also a currency mismatch. Senegalese mentorship is denominated in time and personal obligation — a manager who has known your father's business for years feels no urgency about a 90-day plan. Ghanaian mentorship, shaped by multinational HR templates, is denominated in milestones and check-ins, even when the underlying relationships that actually matter are just as informal and networked as Dakar's. A new arrival who reads Ghana's polished onboarding deck as the whole story, or Senegal's warmth as an absence of expectations, will misjudge both.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/AskAfrica — A returning diaspora hire in Accra said the hardest adjustment wasn't the work itself but realizing the official onboarding schedule was "a formality that ran in parallel to the real onboarding," which happened entirely through the WhatsApp group her line manager added her to on day three.*

> *Quora — Someone who moved to Accra for a multinational role wrote that getting a mentor assigned through the company's official program felt performative until she separately built a relationship with a senior colleague over shared meals — after that, the "official" mentor relationship became almost irrelevant to her actual day-to-day guidance.*

> *Expat.com (Senegal forum) — A new arrival in Dakar described being taken aback that a business meeting scheduled for 9am effectively started closer to 9:30, after ten minutes of greetings and health inquiries, and that trying to redirect the conversation to the agenda too quickly was read as rude rather than efficient.*

> *Quora — An expat working in Accra noted that job-hunting success had far less to do with the polished application portals multinational employers used and far more to do with who forwarded your CV internally — advice he said he wished someone had given him before he spent weeks on cold applications.*

> *Expat.com (Senegal forum) — A contributor advised that new hires budget for a genuine multi-week "installation" period in Dakar offices, since colleagues generally won't extend real trust, or real mentorship, until they've seen you show up consistently and respectfully for long enough to be worth the investment.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two moves, the practical question isn't "which culture is more hierarchical" — they both are, by most Western benchmarks. It's whether you do better waiting to be chosen or asking directly for what you need. Dakar rewards patience, deference, and a willingness to let a relationship set its own pace; Accra rewards initiative dressed up in a company-branded PDF. Neither will onboard you the way your last job did, and the org chart in your welcome email is, in both cities, a rough sketch of where the actual help lives — not the map to it. My honest advice, over a drink: bring more patience than you think you'll need for Dakar, and more nerve than you think you'll need for Accra.
