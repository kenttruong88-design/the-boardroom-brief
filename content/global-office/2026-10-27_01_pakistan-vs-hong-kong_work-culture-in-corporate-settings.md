**The Boss Walks In, the Room Goes Quiet: A Working Guide to Pakistan and Hong Kong**

**🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong SAR**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Karachi, the unwritten rule is that nobody leaves before the boss does, out of deference. In Hong Kong, nobody leaves before the boss does either, but it has nothing to do with deference and everything to do with a Hong Kong Baptist University survey finding that 38 percent of local employees already work more than 49 hours a week before anyone has to ask them to stay. Two very different cultures, it turns out, can arrive at the identical 9 p.m. desk lamp by entirely different roads.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Stand briefly when a senior executive enters a meeting room, even informally | Skip your manager to escalate directly to their boss — it will be remembered, and not fondly |
| Open business conversations with a few minutes of personal warmth — health, family, the weather — before the agenda | Assume a contracted 9-to-5 means you leave at 5 if your boss is still visibly working |
| Use formal titles ("Sir," "Madam," or a senior colleague's surname) until explicitly told otherwise | Criticize a decision in open meetings — raise concerns privately, afterward |
| Expect decisions to move up a visible chain rather than being made at your desk | Plan for business-as-usual scheduling during Ramadan — office hours and energy both shift |
| Build a personal relationship with your direct boss specifically, since loyalty in Pakistani firms runs to people, not just titles | Expect a written policy to override what your boss actually wants done, informally |
| Treat an invitation to tea or lunch with a client or manager as part of the job, not an interruption to it | Be surprised if "yes" in a meeting means "I heard you," not "I agree" |

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong SAR

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat your contracted hours as a floor, not a ceiling, particularly in finance and trading roles | Be the first to leave the floor while your boss's light is still on |
| Hand over and receive business cards with both hands, giving them a brief, genuine look | Criticize a colleague's work in front of the team — feedback is a private, face-preserving matter |
| Learn to read a "yes" for what it actually means — agreement, acknowledgment, or polite refusal, depending on tone | Expect the flexible or remote-work norms common in Europe or North America — only about one in ten non-government employees here report a flexible schedule |
| Keep communication tight and efficient in meetings — long preambles read as wasting everyone's time | Assume a Chinese-headquartered employer runs decisions the way a local or Western firm would — many key calls still route through the head office |
| Accept that working through lunch, or eating at your desk, is common rather than a sign of a crisis | Be shocked if overtime is unpaid and largely unquestioned, especially below senior levels |
| Show up on time, every time — punctuality reads as basic competence, not an extra virtue | Push back loudly on a superior's instruction in the room — raise it afterward, and quietly |

### Pakistan

Pakistan's workplaces run on a hierarchy that is less an org chart than a small solar system, with the senior-most person as the sun everyone else orbits. Hofstede Insights scores the country at 55 on power distance — a touch above the global midpoint — but the number undersells how much that distance is also personal: subordinates in Pakistani firms often report directly to one authority figure they are expected to please specifically, not to "the company" in the abstract. Individualism, meanwhile, sits at a strikingly low 14, among the most collectivist scores Hofstede records anywhere, which shows up in practice as decisions being made for the good of the department, the family-owned conglomerate, or the boss's reputation, well before anyone asks what's efficient.

The lived texture of this shows up less in HR handbooks than in long-running local complaints about office hours. The blog Pakistaniat has documented, across years of reader engagement, a recurring pattern in which employees — particularly in pharmaceutical, banking, and multinational-adjacent firms — stay at their desks well past any contracted close, sometimes sleeping there to finish projects, with family strain as the quiet cost rather than the headline one. None of this is captured in Pakistan's formal labor statistics, which the ILO records as a standard workweek, because the overtime in question is rarely logged; it is simply what loyalty to a demanding boss looks like in practice; it is cultural pressure rather than payroll policy. On Expat.com's Pakistan forum, newcomers are warned less about the office itself than about everything that surrounds it — commuting, banking, and connectivity frictions that extend the working day before it even starts.

### Hong Kong SAR

Hong Kong's long-hours culture is, by contrast, extremely well measured, which hasn't made it any shorter. A Hong Kong Baptist University survey found 38 percent of employees logging more than 49 hours a week, while The Standard reported separately that roughly 35 percent of HK employees average 50 hours weekly — different surveys landing on a similar, unflattering number. Hong Kong Free Press cited an earlier Regus poll in which a third of local respondents worked a full additional eight-hour day of overtime weekly, more than their counterparts in mainland China, Japan, South Korea, or Singapore. HR consultant So Wai-chung put it simply to local media: an overtime culture "has always existed in Hong Kong," even as younger workers increasingly push back against it.

Hofstede's numbers describe a culture that is less personally hierarchical than Pakistan's (power distance 68, similar in range, but individualism rises to 25 — still collectivist by global standards, but notably higher than Pakistan's 14) and considerably more comfortable with ambiguity, scoring just 29 on uncertainty avoidance against Pakistan's 70. In practice, that shows up as a workplace that tolerates shifting priorities and informal workarounds more easily than it tolerates being seen to leave early. The South China Morning Post's reporting on departures from Chinese-headquartered firms in Hong Kong captured this well: 22-year-old management trainee Calvin Lam left a Chinese bank after two months, telling the paper "the atmosphere became so stiff once the boss stepped into the room" and that staff "didn't really have a say in many things" because decisions routed back to a head office elsewhere — a complaint echoed by recruiters who describe a wider power gap between chief executives and staff at Chinese firms than at many Western ones operating in the same city.

### The Reckoning

Put side by side, Pakistan and Hong Kong look like opposites that somehow produce the same symptom. Pakistan's long hours are a function of personal loyalty to an individual boss in a strongly collectivist, high-deference culture; Hong Kong's are a function of institutional and competitive pressure in a culture that is actually less hierarchically personal and far more tolerant of ambiguity, per Hofstede's own numbers. An employee in Karachi stays late because leaving before the boss would be a quiet act of disrespect to a person; an employee in Hong Kong stays late because the market, the bonus structure, and the general assumption that face-time equals commitment make leaving on time look like a lack of seriousness about the job itself.

The irony that should worry any incoming hire from a flexible-work-friendly market is this: neither culture's version of overtime is likely to be solved by a generous contract. In Pakistan, a clear 9-to-5 clause means little against the informal gravity of a boss who stays until 8; in Hong Kong, with only about one in ten non-government employees reporting genuinely flexible schedules, a negotiated remote-work day is more exception than entitlement. The difference worth knowing before signing anything: Pakistan's system rewards being visibly devoted to a person, while Hong Kong's rewards being visibly devoted to the job — and an expat fluent in only one of those performances will find the other exhausting in a completely new way.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Pakistaniat.com — A Karachi professional writing about his brother's experience at a pharmaceutical multinational described work hours that "stretch to days," including nights spent asleep at the office desk rather than going home to finish a project on time.*

> *Pakistaniat.com — The same writer noted a colleague of his brother's who eventually quit after his family, and particularly his ailing mother, objected to how rarely he came home before midnight — a resignation driven by family pressure rather than the job itself.*

> *Expat.com Pakistan forum — A contributor answering a newcomer's questions about relocating noted that the bigger adjustment isn't the office itself but everything around it: unfamiliar banking processes, unpredictable internet speeds, and even which fuel grade to put in a car, all of which quietly extend an already long working day.*

> *South China Morning Post (named interview) — Calvin Lam, a 22-year-old who left a Chinese-headquartered bank in Hong Kong after two months, said the atmosphere "became so stiff once the boss stepped into the room," and that junior staff had little say because major decisions were made at a head office elsewhere.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two postings on work-life balance alone, read the fine print rather than the job title: in Pakistan, your actual hours will be set by how demanding your specific boss is, not by what's in your contract, while in Hong Kong they'll be set by an entire market's unspoken consensus that presence equals commitment, regardless of who you report to. Both will eat your evenings; they'll just send different kinds of apology notes to your family about it. The honest advice, from someone who has watched both systems up close: in Karachi, invest in the relationship with the one person who controls your schedule; in Hong Kong, invest in not being the person whose desk lamp turns off first.
