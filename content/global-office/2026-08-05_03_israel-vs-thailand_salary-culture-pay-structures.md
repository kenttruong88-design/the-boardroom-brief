# Name Your Price or Save Your Face: Israel and Thailand Take Opposite Approaches to Getting Paid

🇮🇱 Israel · 🇹🇭 Thailand

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Tel Aviv, a candidate who fails to name an aggressive salary figure in the first interview is assumed to be either unprepared or uninterested; in Bangkok, a candidate who names one too early is assumed to be rude. Israel's average monthly wage climbed to roughly 14,344 shekels ($4,882) in February 2026, according to Israel's Central Bureau of Statistics, while Thailand's National Statistical Office puts the national average closer to 15,316 baht (about $440) — a gap north of tenfold that has less to do with productivity than with two entirely different theories of what a salary conversation is even for.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇱 Israel

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Come to the first call with a specific number, not a range | Wait to be asked twice — evasiveness reads as weak, not modest |
| Push back on a lowball offer with data; it signals engagement | Assume a counter-offer will offend anyone |
| Ask directly about the *keren hishtalmut* (study fund) and pension match | Compare only base salary to a US offer — equity and benefits carry real weight |
| Clarify whether quoted pay includes "advance overtime" baked into base | Expect overtime pay on top of a standard contract without checking the terms |
| Negotiate again at each promotion cycle — it's expected, not awkward | Treat a first offer as final; almost nobody does |

#### 🇹🇭 Thailand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Let the employer raise numbers first, especially in traditional firms | Blurt out your target salary before it's asked for |
| Ask about the 13th-month bonus explicitly — it's customary, not guaranteed | Assume a strong headline salary includes the year-end bonus by default |
| Frame a counter-offer around fairness and market data, gently | Push hard in front of others — it costs the other side face, and you the goodwill |
| Confirm whether your visa/work-permit category sets a legal salary floor | Assume all foreign hires are paid on the same scale regardless of nationality |
| Build rapport before any pay conversation — relationship precedes number | Treat a fast, transactional negotiating style as a sign of respect |

### Israel

Israel's salary culture runs on directness that outsiders often mistake for aggression. Israeli workplace communication is famously blunt — "dugri," as it's known locally — and that bluntness extends straight into compensation talk: candidates are expected to name a number early, defend it with evidence, and treat pushback from an interviewer as a sign of interest rather than hostility (Rivermate, Masa Israel cultural guides). Hofstede Insights scores Israel at a strikingly low 13 on power distance — one of the flattest hierarchies measured anywhere — which tracks with a labor market where a junior engineer is expected to argue their own case rather than defer to a manager's opening number.

The number itself has been on a tear. Israeli high-tech salaries reached roughly NIS 39,810 a month in early 2026, about 2.8 times the national average, with AI specialists in LLMs and retrieval-augmented generation pulling in a further nine percent premium over other technical roles (Jerusalem Post reporting, Ravio Israel Salary Benchmarks). The national gender pay gap, meanwhile, narrowed to 7.4 percent in the same period — respectable progress, though the broader economy-wide gap remains closer to 32 percent once senior roles are counted (CBS-derived reporting via Jerusalem Post). What the headline shekel figure often omits is a structural quirk: many Israeli contracts fold "advance overtime" into the quoted base salary, so a worker who negotiates hard on the top-line number can still find they've pre-sold thirty hours of unpaid extra time a month without realizing it.

The upshot is a market where confidence is rewarded and passivity is punished — a fact that keeps the country's tech recruiters unusually busy explaining to foreign hires that "blunt" is not the same as "bad news."

### Thailand

Thailand runs on the opposite operating system. Where Israeli negotiation prizes the direct ask, Thai workplace norms are built around *kreng jai* — a consideration for others' comfort — and saving face, which together discourage the kind of confrontational haggling that reads as normal in Tel Aviv (Chiang Rai Times salary-negotiation guides). Hofstede Insights puts Thailand at 64 on power distance and just 20 on individualism, among the more collectivist and hierarchical profiles in the region, which helps explain why counter-offers tend to arrive through indirect signals and third-party conversations rather than a flat number stated across the table.

Thailand's National Statistical Office and Bangkok Post coverage both show a wage picture shaped as much by geography as by role: the national minimum wage now spans 337 to 400 baht a day depending on province, with a long-promised nationwide 400-baht floor still politically stalled over regional cost-of-living disputes (Bangkok Post). Layered on top is the 13th-month bonus, a near-universal but legally optional custom that has, per Thai Supreme Court precedent, effectively become a contractual obligation for employers who pay it consistently for several years running (joinhorizons.com, relianceconsulting.co.th). For foreign hires the picture gets stranger still: work-permit rules set minimum salary floors that vary by nationality, meaning two candidates doing identical work can be legally required to clear different pay thresholds purely based on passport — a detail that rarely surfaces until the paperwork stage.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the contrast is almost too neat: Israel treats salary negotiation as a form of respect, Thailand treats it as a form of risk. An Israeli manager who receives a confident counter-offer typically reads it as evidence the hire will fight for the team later; a Thai manager who receives the same counter-offer delivered with Israeli-style bluntness may read it as a breach of the relationship the negotiation was supposed to be building. Neither reaction is wrong — they're optimizing for different things, one for individual advocacy, the other for group harmony, and Hofstede's power-distance gap (13 versus 64) all but predicts the outcome before either side opens their mouth.

The deeper irony is what each system hides. Israel's transparent-sounding "just ask for more" culture conceals structural fine print — advance overtime, mandatory study funds — that a blunt negotiator can still miss entirely. Thailand's soft-spoken, relationship-first approach conceals its own hard number: a discretionary bonus that becomes a legal entitlement through repetition, and a nationality-based pay floor that no amount of charm will renegotiate.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

*A Quora respondent fielding recruiter outreach from an Israeli startup described being caught off guard by how hard the interviewer pushed back on every answer, demanding justification for the requested salary figure — until longtime residents in the thread explained that the pushback was the standard "dugri" style at work, not a red flag.*

*Another Quora thread asking why Israeli salaries look low by Western standards drew replies pointing to a structural catch: many quoted shekel figures already have "advance overtime" folded into the base, so the number on the offer letter is not simply the number in the paycheck.*

*A Blind thread comparing engineer compensation across the Israeli offices of the major US tech firms warned newcomers against anchoring purely to the base salary line, since equity and the mandatory pension and study-fund contributions make up enough of the real package that a straight base-to-base comparison with a Silicon Valley offer is close to meaningless.*

*On Reddit's r/expats, an English teacher who moved to Thailand for a fraction of their previous home-country salary reported being genuinely surprised at how far the number stretched once rent, food, and transit were priced in baht — describing living more comfortably on less money than they had anywhere before.*

*A Quora thread on Bangkok salaries for foreign hires surfaced a detail newcomers rarely anticipate: work-permit rules set minimum legal salaries that vary by nationality, so identical roles can carry different statutory pay floors depending purely on the candidate's passport.*

### Conclusion

Neither system is more "honest" than the other — Israel's bluntness just moves the fine print to the negotiating table, while Thailand's discretion moves it to the fine print of the contract and the immigration file. Anyone relocating between the two should do the homework before the first conversation: in Israel, know your number and the structural gotchas hiding inside someone else's; in Thailand, know that the bonus you're counting on is customary right up until a court decides it isn't. The honest takeaway is that both countries will pay you fairly by their own logic — they just disagree, fundamentally, on whether you're supposed to ask for it out loud.
