# Lithuania Works Until the Job Is Done; Norway Works Until Four, Then Sends You Home With a Warning

🇱🇹 Lithuania · 🇳🇴 Norway

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Norway's working week is 37.5 hours and its offices empty at around four o'clock, a time Lithuanians usually reserve for the middle of the afternoon. According to the OECD, the average Norwegian logs about 1,386 hours a year, while the average Lithuanian logs about 1,673, a gap of roughly 287 hours that no amount of hybrid scheduling will conceal. For anyone weighing a move from Vilnius to Oslo, or the other way, the question is not whether Norway is nicer. It is whether you are prepared for a country that regards your enthusiasm for staying late as a mild administrative error.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Lithuania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the contract for the working-time terms; the normal week is 40 hours | Assume a Nordic-style 37.5 hour week |
| Know that overtime is capped at 8 hours a week, or 12 with your consent | Agree to unlimited extra hours because the manager asks nicely |
| Use all 20 working days of annual leave | Treat leave as a favour you owe the team |
| Expect managers to be more distant and decisions to come from the top | Expect first-name chats with the director |
| Keep a record of hours worked if the job drifts above the legal average cap of 48 | Rely on goodwill when the paperwork says otherwise |

### In Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Leave on time; core hours are roughly 9 to 15 and the day usually ends by 15:00 or 16:00 | Stay late to look committed |
| Claim overtime pay, which is at least 40 percent extra, or time in lieu | Work extra hours for free and expect gratitude |
| Take your full holiday, five weeks in most workplaces | Answer work email on a Saturday or cancel a vacation to look keen |
| Expect flat management and gentle, discussion-based feedback | Wait for a director's permission to speak to the director |
| Plan around the July slowdown | Schedule a major July launch and wonder where everyone went |
| Put the arrangement in a written contract | Rely on a handshake |

## Lithuania

Lithuania is an efficient economy with a dutiful working culture, and its hours show it. According to Eurostat's 2024 figures, republished by Via Baltica, the average Lithuanian worker puts in 38.3 actual hours a week, against an EU average of 36.0. The Vilnius University guide to employment terms sets a normal week at 40 hours, caps the average including overtime at 48, and limits overtime to 8 hours a week, or 12 with consent, and 180 hours a year. Annual leave is 20 working days. That is a respectable legal framework, and it contains an unspoken second sentence: these are the limits, and most people drift towards them.

Hofstede's national scores, cited in a 2019 Lithuanian academic paper, give Lithuania a masculinity score of 19 and an indulgence score of 16, a pairing that describes a country that is modest about status and suspicious of fun. Norway's scores, via Cyborlink, are 8 for masculinity, which is among the lowest in the world, and 31 for power distance. In plain terms, neither country is chasing glory. They simply differ in what they do with the evening.

The Fortune magazine piece on Lithuania's work-life reputation notes that only about one percent of Lithuanian employees work very long hours, citing the OECD. This is a good statistic, and it sits awkwardly next to the 287-hour gap with Norway, which tells you that the typical Lithuanian is not overworked so much as fully worked. A couple from Šiauliai who moved to Norway told the union paper Frifagbevegelse that they found Lithuanian managers more distant, and that they had given up a daily drive of an hour and a half. The distance in question is not only geographic.

## Norway

Norway's rules are easy to state and, more importantly, to enforce. According to Tekna, the Norwegian union for engineers and scientists, full-time work is 37.5 hours, overtime pays at least 40 percent extra, and there must be 11 hours of rest between workdays. Most workplaces give five weeks of vacation, though the statutory minimum is four weeks and one day. The Labour Inspection Authority sets the legal ceiling at 40 hours a week and nine in a day, with 37.5 common by contract. InterNations' Expat Insider 2022 found that 77 percent of expats in Norway rate their work-life balance favourably, against a global figure of 62 percent, and ranked the country second for work and leisure.

The culture matches the rulebook. A French lawyer who has worked in Oslo for fifteen years wrote in an essay published by the city that leaving on time is normal, regular overtime is not admired, and nearly half of employees belong to a union. A commenter on Hacker News in Norway reported that many firms nearly shut in July, and that people use their full holidays instead of being nudged to. A 2026 post on X, reported by Business Today, described an Indian professional being called in by his manager for answering email on a Saturday and cancelling a vacation, on the grounds that vacation is mandatory and that juniors would copy him. It is difficult to imagine a more Norwegian disciplinary meeting.

There is a catch, and the Norwegians do not hide it. A Fafo survey from 2021 of 500 Lithuanians and 500 Poles living in Norway found that almost nine in ten had full-time permanent jobs, but that 18 percent of the Lithuanians earning under NOK 300,000 reported doing unpaid work. The generous system does not apply itself. A returned expat on Blind noted that take-home pay was lower than in the United States, that senior roles generally need Norwegian and that local friendships are hard to make. Taxes and language, rather than hours, are the bill.

A note on practicalities, since the legal numbers only matter if the contract reflects them. Norwegian hours are normally set in a written contract, core hours run roughly from nine to three, and the working day usually ends by mid-afternoon, according to the Oslo essay. An engineer arriving from Vilnius should check that overtime pays at least 40 percent extra or is given as time in lieu, and should note that July is a month when many firms nearly shut. In Lithuania the same checks run the other way: the consent requirement for overtime beyond eight hours a week is the clause that matters, and the 180-hour annual cap is the number to keep a record against. Neither system is hard to use. Both require you to read the paper first.

## The Reckoning

On the numbers, this is not close. Norway's annual hours are lower, its overtime pays a premium, its holidays are longer and its managers treat a Saturday email as evidence of poor judgement. Lithuania's rules are not unreasonable, but they set a ceiling that employers can lean on, and the average Lithuanian works about two hours more a week than the EU average. If your priority is a life with the lights off by four o'clock, Oslo wins easily.

What Lithuania offers is something harder to survey. It is cheaper, it is closer to home for a Lithuanian, and its working culture does not require the unspoken fluency in consensus that Norway demands. A Lithuanian who arrives in Norway will find that the workplace is gentler but also that the social world outside it takes years to enter, and the Fafo data suggests that migrants are overrepresented in the jobs where the generous rules are most likely to be bent. Norway gives you the evening. It does not give you somebody to spend it with.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — a commenter living in Norway says many companies nearly close in July, that people take their full holidays and rarely do much overtime, unlike in the US and somewhat the UK, where colleagues had to be nudged to take leave.*

> *Hacker News — another commenter prefers Norway's work-life balance and protections to the UK's, though they argue that strong unions can reduce efficiency and competitiveness, citing a metalworkers' union that would not accept competition at a workplace.*

> *Blind — a returned ex-expat says the Norwegian workday is about eight hours with less pressure and no expectation of replying after hours, and that on-call duty is paid and optional. ## Conclusion

If you are a Lithuanian professional with a transferable skill and a reasonable tolerance for dark Januaries, Norway will return a substantial number of hours to you each year, and will pay you extra for the ones it does take. Check that your contract is written, that overtime is recorded and that someone has explained the union, because the system protects people who use it. Learn some Norwegian before you apply for anything senior.

If you are moving the other way, expect Lithuania to feel more demanding and more direct about hierarchy, and plan your holidays with some care, because the legal ceiling on overtime is more generous than it looks.
