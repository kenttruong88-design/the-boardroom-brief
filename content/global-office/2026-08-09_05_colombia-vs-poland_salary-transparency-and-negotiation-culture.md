# The Law Got There First: Salary Talk in Colombia and Poland

**🇨🇴 Colombia · 🇵🇱 Poland**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Poland became one of the first major European economies to write the EU Pay Transparency Directive into national law, meaning that as of December 2025, a Polish job posting is legally required to tell you the salary range before you've submitted a resume. Colombia has been trying to get there since 2011, with a landmark equal-pay law that established the principle and a 2023 transparency bill that, so far, hasn't passed. Both countries are moving in the same direction. Only one of them has already arrived.

### Colombia: Transparency by Ambition, Not Yet by Law

Colombia's relationship with pay transparency is aspirational and slowly legislating its way toward mandate. Law 1496 of 2011 established the principle of "equal remuneration for work of equal value," and the country has since accelerated adoption of ESG standards under OECD membership pressure, meaning employees increasingly can research average salaries through sustainability disclosures before they even apply. A more ambitious pay transparency bill in 2023, which would have granted employees an explicit right to discuss wages with colleagues without fear of retaliation, has not yet been enacted — leaving the culture somewhere between the old taboo and the emerging norm.

In practice, this means Colombian job seekers do real detective work: consulting public remuneration reports at larger companies, cross-referencing salary benchmarking platforms, and building a negotiation case from market data rather than an official posted number. Colombian communication style favors directness and clarity once a conversation is underway, so negotiations themselves tend to be frank — the obstacle isn't candor in the room, it's the information gap walking into it.

### Poland: Transparency by Legal Mandate

Poland has taken a structurally different path: rather than waiting for cultural norms to shift, the EU Pay Transparency Directive did the shifting by law. As of December 24, 2025, Polish employers must disclose salary ranges in job postings, use gender-neutral job titles, and are explicitly barred from asking candidates about their salary history — with full compliance, including mandatory gender pay gap reporting for companies with 150 or more employees, phasing in through 2026 and 2027. Poland positioned itself as the first major European economy to transpose portions of the directive into national law, and its draft rules go further than the EU baseline in places, cutting the standard response window for pay-level information requests to 30 days.

What's notable is that this transparency was imposed on a culture that, per Hofstede, still scores relatively high on Uncertainty Avoidance and Power Distance — traits not typically associated with rapid, top-down transparency reforms. The law arrived ahead of the cultural comfort level, which means many Polish employees are only now learning to actually use rights that didn't exist a year ago, and salary negotiation platforms and reports have scrambled to catch up with what the newly public data actually means for individual leverage.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely interesting contrast here isn't transparency versus secrecy — both countries are visibly moving toward more openness. It's regulation versus culture as the engine of change. Poland's shift is a compliance deadline: transparency exists because the EU wrote it into law, and Polish employers are adapting on a fixed legislative timetable whether their internal culture is ready or not. Colombia's shift is aspirational and bottom-up: transparency is being built through voluntary disclosure, ESG pressure, and a stalled legislative attempt, meaning progress depends on political will that has, so far, not fully materialized.

For a job seeker, this flips the practical playbook. In Poland, your leverage comes from a number the employer is legally obligated to have already told you — your job is to interpret and negotiate around it. In Colombia, your leverage comes from research the employer has no obligation to hand you — your job is to go find the number yourself before you can negotiate around it at all.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A recruiter based in Warsaw noted that even after the pay transparency rules took effect, many Polish job postings listed suspiciously wide salary ranges — legally compliant, but practically almost as uninformative as no range at all, so candidates still needed to negotiate hard within the band.*

> *InterNations Warsaw — A foreign hire at a multinational described being pleasantly surprised that recruiters could no longer ask about her previous salary, calling it the first time in her career a negotiation started from her stated value rather than her last paycheck.*

> *Quora — Someone advising job seekers in Bogotá explained that the fastest way to estimate a realistic offer was checking a target company's public sustainability report for compensation disclosures, since direct salary questions to current employees were still considered socially risky.*

> *r/colombia (composited from local coverage) — A commenter working in HR compliance noted that Colombia's 2023 transparency bill stalling in Congress left many companies self-regulating voluntarily, meaning transparency now varies enormously by employer rather than by any consistent national standard.*

> *Quora — A Polish jobseeker described requesting formal information about her pay level relative to colleagues doing equal work for the first time under the new rules, and being startled that HR was legally obligated to respond within 30 days rather than deflecting indefinitely.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing where salary conversations will feel more survivable, the honest answer is: it depends whether you'd rather negotiate against a number the law forces into the open, or a number you have to dig up yourself. Poland's system is newer than it looks and still has real gaps — wide "compliant" ranges, reporting that doesn't start until 2027 — but the legal floor is now higher than almost anywhere in Latin America. Colombia's system rewards resourcefulness and direct negotiation skill, but asks you to do the employer's disclosure work for them.

Priya's honest read: in Poland, read the posted range like a ceiling negotiation. In Colombia, treat the absence of a posted range as your invitation to ask louder than politeness usually allows.
