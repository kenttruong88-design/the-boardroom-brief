# Nobody Will Train You in Italy. In South Africa, They'll Just Tell You the Company Has Trust Issues.

## 🇮🇹 Italy · 🇿🇦 South Africa

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Italy, the average new hire learns the org chart the way one learns a family's grudges: slowly, through gossip, over espresso. In South Africa, the org chart is often handed to you on day one, along with a disclaimer that the company is still "building trust" after a history that gives the phrase real weight. Both countries will eventually tell you what your job is. Neither is in a hurry to tell you how to do it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇹 Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask a colleague to translate the "informal" summary after every meeting held in Italian | Assume the onboarding deck you were emailed is the actual onboarding |
| Learn who eats lunch with whom — the seating chart is the real hierarchy | Skip the coffee break to keep working; that's where decisions actually get made |
| Treat your *periodo di prova* as a two-way audition, not a formality | Expect a designated mentor; you'll likely inherit one informally, or not at all |
| Use *Lei* and job titles (Dottore, Ingegnere) until invited to switch to *tu* | Assume merit alone got you hired, or will get you promoted |
| Get comfortable asking the same question three times, politely | Mistake warmth for speed — friendliness and bureaucracy coexist happily |

#### 🇿🇦 South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the formal onboarding pack — companies here tend to actually have one | Assume a good HR deck means a good manager; the gap is where people quit |
| Ask directly whether a mentorship or graduate programme exists — many do, unevenly applied | Wait to be assigned a mentor; senior staff are stretched thin and won't always volunteer |
| Attend the braai or team lunch; relationship-building is not optional | Treat hierarchy as decorative — it is respected even in "flat" org charts |
| Clarify your KPIs in writing within the first two weeks | Assume onboarding will address load-shedding, commute, or safety logistics — ask |
| Ask about probation length and review points up front | Assume every company has the resources of the flagship graduate programmes you read about |

### Section: Italy

Italy has a formal, almost bureaucratic labour code and an almost defiantly informal onboarding culture, and it does not see the contradiction. According to the OECD's Indicators of Employment Protection, Italy sits in the moderately restrictive band for dismissal rules on regular contracts, but far more permissive for temporary ones — a legacy of the Jobs Act reforms of 2014-15 that shortened maximum fixed-term durations while making them easier to issue in the first place. The upshot for a new arrival: your first contract is quite likely to be a *tempo determinato*, renewed a few times, with your actual training left to whoever happens to be free that week.

ISTAT's Survey on Personnel Training in Companies found that in the most recent full data year, 68.9 percent of firms with ten or more employees ran some form of professional training, rising past 90 percent among large employers — but a meaningful share of that is informal, on-the-job instruction rather than structured courses, which in practice means shadowing a colleague until the pattern makes sense. Hofstede Insights scores Italy at 75 on uncertainty avoidance, among the higher readings in Western Europe, which sits oddly alongside a workplace culture famous for improvisation — the resolution is that Italians manage ambiguity through dense personal networks and procedure, rather than by tolerating it outright. Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2026 report clocked European employee engagement at 13 percent, the lowest of any region measured — which tracks with a system where feeling looked after by your employer is not really the point of the arrangement.

### Section: South Africa

South Africa's onboarding infrastructure looks, on paper, considerably more mature than Italy's — the country has a genuine tradition of structured graduate programmes at firms like Sasol and AECOM, built explicitly around mentorship and staged skill development, and professional bodies such as SAICA run formal mentoring schemes for trainees. The strain shows up elsewhere. Statistics South Africa's Quarterly Labour Force Survey for Q1 2026 put youth unemployment (ages 15-24) at 60.9 percent, with the broader 15-34 cohort at 45.8 percent, meaning the pool of people competing for entry-level roles vastly outstrips the number of employers with the bandwidth to properly onboard them. BusinessTech reporting on the country's 2026 skills gap noted roughly 118,500 unfilled ICT vacancies, more than a third of them junior positions sitting open specifically because candidates are seen as not yet work-ready — a mentorship failure disguised as a hiring one.

Gallup's 2026 data showed South African employee engagement had slipped several points year over year, still landing above the anemic European average but trending the wrong way. Academic work published in the South African Journal of Higher Education has found that institutional mentorship materially improves graduate work-readiness, which is a polite way of saying the market has correctly identified the fix and is still not funding it consistently. South African workplaces also carry, per Hofstede's comparison tool, a moderate power-distance score of 49 alongside a fairly high masculinity index of 63 — translating in practice to offices that are socially warm (the braai is not optional) but where hierarchy, once established, is not casually challenged by the new hire.

### The Reckoning

Here is the counterintuitive part: Italy, the country with the thicker rulebook, gives you almost no formal map for your first ninety days, while South Africa, the country still explicitly working to rebuild institutional trust, is more likely to hand you an actual onboarding schedule. The difference is what happens after the schedule ends. In Italy, informal training persists indefinitely because relationships are the infrastructure; you keep learning by being present, for years, at the same lunch table. In South Africa, formal mentorship exists but is unevenly resourced, so its absence is felt sharply and immediately — the graduate programmes are real, but if you land outside one, you may be handed a laptop and a Slack invite and left to reverse-engineer the rest.

Neither system optimizes for the newcomer's first month; both optimize for something else entirely — Italy for continuity of the in-group, South Africa for the appearance of structure amid genuine resource constraints. If you need someone to formally tell you what "good" looks like in week one, South Africa will make more of an effort. If you need someone to actually still be checking in on you in year three, Italy, oddly, does better — provided you were let into the group at all.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Local Italy — A British transplant recounted landing a teaching post near Bologna with no contacts and, on her first day, being warned outright by office staff that the place was "crazy." She found no real orientation: meetings routinely lapsed into rapid Italian, and she had to ask a bilingual colleague afterward for a private summary of what had just been decided.*

> *The Local Italy — The same contributor noted the flip side: chronic disorganisation and a habit of derailing scheduled meetings for lunch was more than offset by a work-life balance she'd never had in London, and she came to see the long midday break not as a delay but as the actual mechanism by which the office functioned.*

> *Quora — A commenter explained that landing an Italian job with no personal connections was seen by locals as faintly miraculous, since an estimated two-thirds of hires at mid-sized firms arrive via referral; newcomers who expect a competitive, CV-driven process are misreading how trust, not merit alone, gets you in the door and gets you shown the ropes once inside.*

> *Quora — Another thread on unwritten office etiquette in Italy singled out the *pausa caffè* as the place where informal hierarchy and real decisions actually surface, well before anything reaches a formal meeting — advice repeated by several respondents: attach yourself to whoever takes the most coffee breaks, not whoever runs the most meetings.*

> *Quora — An expatriate working in South Africa on a foreign salary described colleagues as warm and quick to check in personally, even as the reporting lines stayed rigid and unquestioned; the piece of advice offered to newcomers was to build the relationships early and loudly, because the org chart will not soften just because the small talk is friendly.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two purely on how well someone will hold your hand in week one, South Africa's formal graduate schemes and onboarding packs beat Italy's improvisational approach on paper — but that comparison only holds if you land inside one of the well-resourced programmes, and Statistics South Africa's own unemployment numbers suggest most entrants won't. Italy offers less structure up front and more durability once you're in, provided you're patient enough, and fluent enough, to earn the lunch invitation. Pack accordingly: in Italy, bring patience and a phrasebook; in South Africa, bring a direct question about who, specifically, is supposed to be training you — and get the answer in writing before you need it.
