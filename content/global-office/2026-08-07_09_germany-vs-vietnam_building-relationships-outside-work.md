**Feierabend Means "Not You Anymore." In Vietnam, the Meal After Work Is the Real Meeting.**

🇩🇪 Germany · 🇻🇳 Vietnam

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Germans have a specific word, Feierabend, for the moment the workday ends and colleagues become, functionally, strangers again until tomorrow — a linguistic marker for a culture where private and professional life are treated as separate rooms with a locked door between them (Germany Handbook). In Vietnam, declining an invitation to a shared meal or social gathering with colleagues isn't a neutral scheduling choice — trust itself is built substantially through exactly those informal, after-hours interactions, and skipping them repeatedly can quietly cap how far a working relationship ever develops (Rivermate). One culture treats relationship-building outside work as optional, even slightly suspect. The other treats it as the actual mechanism by which professional trust gets made.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Respect Feierabend — don't message colleagues about work after hours | Assume a colleague's lack of after-work socializing is personal rejection |
| Expect friendships to form slowly, through repeated deliberate contact | Expect an instant, casual "work friend" dynamic in your first weeks |
| Join structured social circles — hobby clubs (Vereine) are the real entry point | Rely solely on the office as your source of a social life |
| Take a genuine German friendship seriously once it forms — it tends to be durable | Mistake initial reserve for coldness; it's a different pace, not a wall |
| Keep small talk brief and let deeper conversation develop over time, not day one | Overshare personal details early — it can read as oversharing, not bonding |

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept invitations to shared meals — they function as real trust-building | Treat after-work meals as optional extras with no professional weight |
| Show personal interest in colleagues' lives — it's part of how rapport forms | Keep interactions strictly transactional and expect strong working trust anyway |
| Expect relationship quality to directly affect how smoothly work gets done | Assume competence alone will carry a working relationship without rapport |
| Notice regional tone — Hanoi more reserved, Ho Chi Minh City more outgoing | Apply one uniform social approach across the whole country |
| Use expat Facebook groups actively — they're the real social infrastructure | Expect InterNations-style formal expat networks to be the primary hub |

---

Germany's separation of work and private life isn't a personality quirk of individual colleagues — it's a structural cultural feature with its own vocabulary and, per Hofstede's framework, statistical grounding: Germany's relatively low power distance promotes flat hierarchies and co-determination at work, but that flatness coexists with a firm compartmentalization outside it (WorkerHero). Proforg's guidance for expats is direct: colleagues remain friendly and professional at work but typically don't socialize extensively outside office hours, and this isn't coldness so much as an active choice to protect personal time (Proforg Global Mobility). The deeper pattern is that Germans build most of their social circles from childhood, university, or local hobby clubs — the workplace is rarely the primary source of a social life at all, which means expecting your German office to double as your social calendar is a category error, not a failed expectation.

Vietnam runs on the opposite operating assumption: relationships are the substrate work gets built on top of, not a separate track. Rivermate's guidance on Vietnamese business culture is explicit that investing time outside formal meetings — socializing, sharing meals, showing personal interest — contributes significantly to the trust that makes collaboration function smoothly (Rivermate). The concept maps loosely onto the broader East Asian idea of guanxi, though scholarship notes it's less formalized and centrally organizing in Vietnam than in China — present, but not as rigidly systematized (academic sourcing via World Scientific). What that means practically is that the informal dinner or drinks invitation isn't a nice-to-have bolt-on to the working relationship; for many Vietnamese colleagues, it functions as the actual proving ground for whether a foreign hire can be trusted with more responsibility, information, or flexibility down the line.

### The Reckoning

The counterintuitive part is that Germany's clear boundaries can actually make trust-building more transparent and predictable, even though it's slower: once a German friendship forms, it tends to be unusually durable precisely because it wasn't manufactured by workplace proximity or obligation. Vietnam's blended approach moves faster on the surface — you can be having dinner with colleagues in your first week — but the relationship carries more implicit weight and expectation, since declining those invitations doesn't just cost you a social evening, it can genuinely cap how far colleagues are willing to extend trust at work. Neither approach is more "genuine" than the other; they've simply built trust through different mechanisms — Germany through selective, deliberate accumulation, Vietnam through consistent, low-friction participation.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *TeamBlind — Someone who moved for a German engineering role wrote that they'd barely made any workplace friends since starting, describing awkward pauses in conversation and no team lunches, and wondering aloud whether something was wrong with them before realizing the pattern was widely shared among other transplants in the same thread.*

> *Quora — A response to a question about working in Vietnam described the culture as hierarchical and relationship-driven, noting that decision-making can appear slow to outsiders specifically because it depends on trust built through informal channels first, not the formal meeting itself.*

> *Mumsnet — A parent who'd relocated for a partner's job in Germany asked directly why German colleagues seemed unwilling to socialize outside work, and the most upvoted replies reframed it not as unfriendliness but as a firmly held cultural boundary that eventually loosens, just on a much longer timeline than UK or US norms.*

> *A recurring theme in expat Facebook groups for Ho Chi Minh City: "Expats in Ho Chi Minh City (Saigon)" functions as the real social infrastructure for newcomers, more so than any formal networking organization, with football, badminton, and yoga groups repeatedly cited as where genuine cross-cultural friendships actually took root.*

> *A common observation among foreign hires in Vietnam: Vietnamese colleagues are frequently eager to practice English and build rapport with a native or fluent speaker, which can accelerate relationship-building for English speakers in ways that don't apply as strongly to other foreign hires.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Germany, don't measure your integration by how many work friends you've made in month one — measure it by whether you've found a Verein or hobby group, because that's where German social life actually happens. If you're moving to Vietnam, treat the after-work dinner invitation as a professional obligation dressed as a social one, and show up consistently, because your willingness to participate is doing real work on how much your colleagues will eventually trust you. The honest tip: in Germany, be patient and let the friendship come to you on its own schedule; in Vietnam, say yes to the dinner — that's the actual meeting.
