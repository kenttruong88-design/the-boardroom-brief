# No Overtime Pay, No Overtime Worked: Ireland and Portugal's Very Different Relationships With the Clock

🇮🇪 Ireland · 🇵🇹 Portugal

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Ireland has no statutory right to overtime pay of any kind, and hardly anyone works it. Portugal has one of the most meticulously tiered overtime-pay schedules in the European Union — 25% here, 37.5% there, 50% on a Sunday — and still ranks fourth in the EU for the share of employees logging 49-hour weeks. If you want to predict how late a country works, do not read its labour code. Watch what time the office lights go off.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Leave when your contracted hours end — nobody is impressed by lingering | Expect statutory overtime pay; none exists, so treat any premium as a favour, not a right |
| Use your full statutory leave (20 days plus 9 public holidays) | Schedule anything serious for Friday after 3pm — the week is already winding down |
| Formally request flexible or remote work under the 2023 Work Life Balance Act | Assume that legal "right to request" means a guaranteed yes — refusals are common and rarely explained |
| Take the tea breaks; they are structural, not slacking | Mistake informality for a lack of seriousness about deadlines |
| Ask directly about actual office hours before accepting an offer — job ads rarely mention them | Sign an "all reasonable overtime included" clause without asking what "reasonable" has meant in practice |

#### 🇵🇹 Portugal
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Track your own overtime hours — the 25%/37.5%/50% pay scale rewards employees who know it better than HR does | Assume the office empties at 6pm because the law caps the week at 40 hours; presenteeism runs deep |
| Expect after-hours silence — contacting staff outside work hours has been a finable offence since 2022 | Rush a residency permit, tax number, or any bureaucratic process; it moves on its own schedule |
| Negotiate salary hard before accepting — national average pay trails most of Western Europe | Read the slower pace as a work ethic problem; it is frequently a different relationship with time, not laziness |
| Budget for a second income stream if you're paid near the minimum wage — it is more common than it should be | Confuse the generous paper leave entitlement with generous take-home pay; the two are not correlated here |
| Ask what "normal" looks like at your specific employer — multinational and legacy-Portuguese firms differ sharply | Assume every company enforces the right-to-disconnect law; firms under ten employees are exempt |

## Ireland

Ireland's Organisation of Working Time Act 1997 caps the working week at an average of 48 hours over four months and mandates 11 consecutive hours of rest daily — solid, unremarkable EU-directive stuff. What it does not do is guarantee a single cent of overtime pay; compensation for extra hours is left entirely to the employer, the contract, or, more often, nothing at all. And yet Ireland was recently ranked the best country in Europe for work-life balance for a second consecutive year, with average weekly hours around 34, according to Remote's Global Life-Work Balance Index. The absence of a legal overtime carrot has not produced a culture of unpaid extra hours; it has produced a culture that mostly declines to ask for them in the first place.

The explanation is cultural enforcement standing in for legal enforcement. Employees on Quora describing office jobs in Dublin note a consistent pattern: managers out the door by four, tea breaks scattered through the day as a matter of course, lunch treated as sacred rather than optional. It is less a policy than a shared assumption — that a 45-hour week is already generous — reinforced from the top down. Ireland's newer legal protections have not kept pace with this reputation, however; Irish national broadcaster RTÉ has described the country's formal right to request remote work as a "toothless tiger," with limited awareness among employees and few real consequences for employers who refuse. The lesson is not that Irish law is generous. It is that Irish norms are, and norms without statutory teeth still manage to hold.

## Portugal

Portugal takes the opposite approach: legislate everything, then watch compliance vary. The Labour Code caps overtime at two hours a day and 150 to 200 hours a year depending on company size, and pays a sliding premium — 25% for the first hour on a weekday, rising to 50% on rest days and public holidays, then jumping again after the 100th annual hour. Since 2022, contacting an employee outside working hours can trigger a fine of up to €9,690 under the country's "duty of absence of contact" law, among the most explicit right-to-disconnect statutes in Europe.

None of this has stopped Portugal from posting one of the EU's worst long-hours figures: 9.1% of employees work 49 hours or more per week, according to Randstad Research data reported by Euronews in April 2026, well above the EU average of 6.5% and behind only Greece, Cyprus, and France. Part of the answer is money. Portugal's average annual salary sits near €22,300 — roughly 30% below neighbouring Spain's — and a Substack analysis from Portugal Decoded describes a workforce that is highly educated but structurally underpaid, with a meaningful share of workers holding a second job simply to keep pace with rising costs. When wages lag this far behind hours logged, staying visible in the office starts to look like a rational strategy rather than a cultural quirk, whatever the Labour Code says about caps.

## The Reckoning

Put side by side, Ireland and Portugal invert the usual assumption that more regulation produces better outcomes. Ireland offers almost no statutory overtime protection and produces some of the shortest actual working weeks in Europe. Portugal offers a genuinely sophisticated overtime-pay architecture, a real right to disconnect with real fines attached, and still can't shake a long-hours problem that outpaces most of the bloc. The law, in both cases, turns out to be a weak predictor of the lived week. What predicts it better is whether the underlying economics make going home financially and socially sustainable.

That is the quieter contrast worth sitting with. Ireland's advantage isn't legislative generosity — it barely has any — it's that wages have kept pace closely enough with cost of living that leaving on time doesn't feel like a gamble. Portugal's problem isn't legislative neglect — its statute book is arguably the more careful of the two — it's that wages have not, so employees hedge with visible hours or a second job instead of trusting the printed rules to protect them. Two very different labour codes, one identical moral: read the payslip before you read the statute.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A respondent describing entry-level office work in Dublin noted that managers were reliably gone by 4pm and visibly protective of their own time off, describing a "working to live" mentality that felt disorienting after US-style 55-to-60-hour weeks.*

> *Blind — Engineers comparing Dublin offers from firms like Workday and Liberty IT described accepting a lower base salary than a US equivalent specifically because reviewers rated the actual work-life balance close to the maximum, treating it as a real trade rather than a recruiting slogan.*

> *Quora — Someone weighing a move to Portugal for work was warned bluntly about the bureaucracy: residency paperwork, tax registration, and permit renewals were described as moving on an entirely separate timeline from any request for urgency, regardless of how the labour code reads.*

> *Substack (Teresa PBG, "Portugal: A Question of Time") — A newcomer initially read Portuguese pacing as slowness or low effort, then reconsidered: delays traced back to understaffing and backlog rather than a relaxed work ethic, concluding Portugal doesn't work less, it just holds a different relationship with time.*

> *Substack (Portugal Decoded) — An analysis of the local labour market described well-educated professionals taking on second jobs or freelance work on the side, not from ambition but because a single salary near the national average no longer stretched far enough on its own.*

## Conclusion

Neither country's brochure is lying, exactly — it's just answering the wrong question. Ireland's brochure should be read literally: the short week is real, protected less by statute than by an employer culture that treats overstaying as slightly odd rather than admirable. Portugal's brochure should be read skeptically: the overtime tables and disconnect fines are real too, but they describe an aspiration that a stagnant wage floor keeps undercutting in practice. Anyone comparing offers between the two should ask less about what the law promises and more about what the last three employees in that specific office actually did at 6pm on a Tuesday.

The honest summary: Ireland enforces work-life balance through wages good enough that nobody feels compelled to test the boundary, while Portugal legislates it in exhaustive detail and then watches the same wage gap quietly overrule the statute.
