# In Japan, the Meeting Was Decided Last Tuesday. In Germany, It's Decided Right Now, Loudly.

🇯🇵 Japan · 🇩🇪 Germany

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

A Japanese meeting is the printing of a result that was computed elsewhere, in hallways, over vending-machine coffee, days before anyone booked the room. A German meeting is the computation itself, performed live, on the record, with the agenda as its proof. Hofstede Insights' country-comparison tool scores Japan 95 and Germany 66 on masculinity, and Japan 92 to Germany's 65 on uncertainty avoidance — two cultures that both hate ambiguity, and have built almost opposite machines for eliminating it. One relocation, two very different ways of finding out you spoke at the wrong moment.

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Line up support informally before the meeting (nemawashi) | Pitch a bold new idea cold in the room and expect a yes on the spot |
| Bring business cards and receive others' with both hands, reading them before putting them away | Pocket a business card immediately or write on it in front of the giver |
| Let silence sit; treat a pause as thinking time, not a cue to jump in | Fill every gap in conversation — it reads as impatience, not enthusiasm |
| Defer visibly to the most senior person present before offering your own view | Respond to a junior colleague's point before the senior one has spoken |
| Follow up in writing to confirm what was actually agreed | Assume a nodding "hai" means agreement rather than "I am listening" |
| Arrive five to ten minutes early | Schedule a hard stop that cuts off the ranking attendee mid-sentence |

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive on time, ideally a couple of minutes early | Wander in "fashionably late," even by five minutes |
| Send a detailed agenda in advance and read everyone else's | Show up unprepared — it wastes the room's time and it shows |
| State disagreement plainly, with reasoning attached | Treat direct criticism of your proposal as a personal attack |
| Use surname and title (Herr/Frau + surname) until invited to switch | Default to first names on day one |
| Stick to the agenda and end when the agenda says to end | Pad the meeting with small talk or open-ended brainstorming |
| Put decisions and action items in writing immediately afterward | Expect a minuted decision to be casually reopened later |

## Japan

The thing nobody tells new hires in Tokyo is that the meeting is not where decisions happen — it is where decisions are ratified. The real work is nemawashi, literally "going around the roots," a round of quiet one-on-one conversations in which a proposal is tested, adjusted, and pre-approved before it ever reaches a conference room. What follows, per analysis from GLOBIS Insights and Japan Intercultural Consulting, is often a formal ringisho document that circulates for signatures up the hierarchy, each stamp confirming what was already settled in private. Foreign staff, expecting the meeting itself to be the forum for debate, tend to find the experience faintly theatrical: everyone nodding toward an outcome nobody appears to be discussing.

The payoff, such as it is, comes after. Because consensus was built before the vote, implementation tends to move fast and meet little internal resistance — the opposite failure mode of a Western-style debate-then-decide meeting, which can produce a clean decision that quietly dies in execution. None of this happens on a leisurely clock, either. The average worker in Japan logged roughly 1,607 hours in 2022, according to OECD data, down from 1,821 in 2000 — genuine progress, though the Japan Institute for Labour Policy and Training's own reporting notes that legal overtime caps (45 hours a month, 360 a year, since the 2018 Work Style Reform Law) exist precisely because plenty of workplaces needed the limit spelled out. The meeting culture didn't get gentler; the paperwork around it got stricter.

## Germany

Walk into a meeting in Germany and the agenda has already told you what will happen, in what order, and by when — sent in advance, expected to be read, followed with something close to liturgical discipline. Destatis, the Federal Statistical Office, reports full-time employees worked an average of 39.9 hours a week in 2025, a modest decline from 40.5 a decade earlier, and the meetings inside those hours are built to justify every minute: structured, punctual, and allergic to drift. Arriving five minutes late is not a quirk to be waved off; multiple workplace-culture guides describe it as a genuine signal of disrespect for colleagues' time.

The directness is the headline feature and the thing that catches people off guard hardest. Feedback lands without much cushioning — the point isn't to soften the message but to solve the problem, on the theory, as several culture guides put it, that clear criticism is more respectful than a comfortable evasion. The Local's reporting on this puts a number to the disorientation: over half of surveyed foreign residents said they experienced German directness as a genuine culture shock, whether in a performance review or at a crosswalk. It tracks with the Hofstede numbers — Germany scores 67 on individualism against Japan's 46 — a culture that expects you to speak for yourself, clearly, and assumes everyone else will do the same.

## The Reckoning

Here is the joke culture built into the data: both countries hate uncertainty roughly equally, and they've engineered near-mirror-image solutions. Japan removes ambiguity by resolving disagreement before the meeting, so the room can be calm; Germany removes it by resolving disagreement inside the meeting, loudly, so nothing lingers afterward. A newcomer who reads a Japanese meeting as consequential will misjudge the real leverage point entirely — it already passed. A newcomer who reads a German meeting as a formality to be endured before the real conversation happens later will find there is no later; the agenda was the whole conversation, and it just adjourned.

The irony is that the supposedly more "efficient" culture, on paper, is the slower one to a decision. Nemawashi can take weeks of quiet lobbying before anything is official — Harvard Business Review's reporting on Japanese consensus-building corroborates the timeline complaints common among foreign staff. German meetings, by contrast, can produce a binding decision in forty minutes flat, agenda item by agenda item, no lobbying required. Which one is actually faster depends entirely on what you're counting: time to decision, or time to a decision that survives contact with the rest of the org chart.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A respondent describing early meetings in a Japanese firm recalled pitching an idea directly in a group setting, only to be quietly told afterward that the "real" conversation should have happened one-on-one with each stakeholder beforehand; the meeting itself had already been a formality he mistook for an opening.*

> *Glassdoor — A review of a major consulting firm's Tokyo office described persistent overtime expectations and a management style employees characterized as closely supervised, alongside praise for the stability and structure of the "traditional Japanese company" experience.*

> *The Local Germany — A contributor described being blindsided the first time a manager critiqued a presentation point-by-point in front of the team with no preamble, then later realizing the same manager treated the exchange as already closed and moved on without any residual tension.*

> *Jobs in Japan (forum) — A foreign employee wrote about initially rushing to fill every pause in meetings, then learning to sit inside the silence instead; colleagues' visible ease with them noticeably improved once they stopped talking over the quiet.*

> *iamexpat.de — A newcomer to Berlin advised that the fastest way to stop taking German bluntness personally was to watch how quickly colleagues moved on after delivering it — the criticism was never meant to linger, only the newcomer's discomfort was.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two on meeting culture alone, choose based on where you'd rather do your real negotiating: in the hallway before the room fills up, or inside the room with the door shut and the agenda open. Japan rewards patience, relationship-building, and a tolerance for finding out where you stand only gradually. Germany rewards preparation, a thick skin, and the ability to say what you mean before someone else says it for you, less gently.

Neither is more honest than the other; they've just moved the honest part to a different stage of the process. Tell a friend over a drink: in Tokyo, learn to read a room that isn't talking; in Berlin, learn to survive a room that won't stop.
