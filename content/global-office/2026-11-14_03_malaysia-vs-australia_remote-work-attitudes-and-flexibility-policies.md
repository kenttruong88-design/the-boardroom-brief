# Malaysia and Australia Both Say Yes to Working From Home, Provided Your Employer Agrees

🇲🇾 Malaysia · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

About 36 per cent of employed Australians usually work from home, according to the Australian Bureau of Statistics, a share that has barely moved in a year. Malaysia, for its part, wrote a right to request flexible work into law in 2023, and in April 2026 told its own civil service to stay home to save fuel. Both countries therefore endorse remote work with enthusiasm. Neither requires your employer to share it. The distinction between a right and a right to ask is where a relocating professional should spend the most attention.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Submit flexible work requests in writing, citing sections 60P and 60Q of the Employment Act 1955 | Assume a request is an entitlement; approval rests with the employer |
| Ask whether the company claims the TalentCorp tax deduction for flexible work, which gives HR a reason to say yes | Expect the government's own WFH order to bind a private employer |
| Agree the home-working terms, including equipment and hours, before you move | Rely on a verbal hybrid promise that a return-to-office memo can erase |
| Plan your commute around Klang Valley traffic if you are office-based | Leave at 6:40am and expect a 20-minute journey |
| Read the 2026 DOSH guide on home workspaces and raise ergonomics early | Treat the guide as binding law; it is advisory |

### In Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Put your work-from-home days in the contract, not a policy that can change | Accept a verbal assurance, since company policy can change at any time |
| Use the Fair Work Act right to request flexible work and keep a paper trail | Assume a refusal is final if the employer skipped the required procedure |
| Check whether your award, such as the Clerks Award, has a working-from-home term | Assume the state-level proposals in Victoria are already settled law |
| Weigh any pay cut offered for remote work against commuting savings | Say yes to a cut before checking what the office alternative would cost you |
| Watch your hours at home; remote workers report more overtime | Let the saved commute quietly become unpaid work |

## Malaysia

Since 1 January 2023, amendments to the Employment Act 1955 have allowed private-sector employees to request changes to their hours, work days or work location, including working from home. According to the Malay Mail's July 2026 report of a parliamentary reply, such requests remain subject to employer approval under sections 60P and 60Q. The state has added a carrot: employers who introduce flexible arrangements can claim a 50 per cent tax deduction on training costs and software purchases, capped at RM500,000, through TalentCorp for assessment years 2025 to 2027.

The government also supplies the evidence. A Ministry of Human Resources study found that 81 per cent of Klang Valley workers reported improved job performance under flexible arrangements; in Johor, 77 per cent said they were more productive when they set their own start and end times; and in Penang, 77 per cent of employers noticed a boost in efficiency. A ministry that champions a policy and then measures it is rarely disappointed, so the figures should be read accordingly. More tangible was the Prime Minister's announcement on 2 April 2026 of work-from-home arrangements for ministries, agencies, statutory bodies and government-linked companies from 15 April, to reduce fuel consumption. The law firm Skrine noted that this was not mandatory for private employers.

Worker appetite is not in doubt. Randstad figures reported by Free Malaysia Today found that 96 per cent of Malaysian workers prefer flexible arrangements and 37 per cent would accept a pay cut to work remotely. The Malaysian Employers Federation conceded that return-to-office orders had caused talent retention problems. Klang Valley commuters, meanwhile, report that a 20-minute journey can take an hour. In practice, Malaysian flexibility is generous in principle and negotiated in detail.

The safety rules are worth knowing. Skrine notes that an employer's duty under the Occupational Safety and Health Act 1994 extends to home working, and the Department of Occupational Safety and Health's 2026 Flexible Working Arrangement Guide, though not legally binding, flags ergonomic risks and psychosocial ones such as stress, fatigue and blurred work-life boundaries. The Malaysian Trades Union Congress, quoted by Free Malaysia Today, has urged employers to create jobs that meet younger workers' demands for flexibility. A sensible employer will therefore ask you to certify your home workspace. A less sensible one will not, and you should notice which you have.

## Australia

The Australian Bureau of Statistics reported in August 2025 that about 36 per cent of employed people usually worked from home, against 36.3 per cent a year earlier and a peak of 40.5 per cent in August 2021. The split is occupational: 59 per cent of managers and professionals, 21 per cent of everyone else. Thirty per cent of employees had an agreement to work flexible hours. One further figure deserves a footnote. While 31 per cent of employed people usually work overtime, the rate is 42 per cent among those who work from home. The commute disappears; the working day appears to expand into the vacancy. The ABS also asked why people work from home: 22.5 per cent cited flexibility or choosing their own hours, and 20.8 per cent said they were catching up on work after hours. Among those who work from home, 47 per cent had a flexible hours agreement, against 22 per cent of those who did not, so the two arrangements tend to arrive together.

The legal position is a right to request, not a right to remote work. According to HFW, in Chandler v Westpac Banking Corporation [2025] FWC 3115 the Fair Work Commission approved a part-time employee's request to work fully remotely after Westpac refused despite years of successful remote work and failed to follow the required procedures. A January 2026 Productivity Commission report found that around 40 per cent of employees work flexibly, with employer agreement. Reform is being proposed rather than delivered: Victoria plans a right to work from home two days a week where a job can reasonably be done remotely, though lawyers doubt a state can legislate this, and a Greens bill is before a Senate inquiry without federal government backing. Proceedings to add a working-from-home term to the Clerks Award were under way in early 2026, so check where they have landed.

Employers have meanwhile grown firmer. Randstad found that 56 per cent of Australians would consider quitting if forced into the office more, according to The New Daily, though Randstad also observed that the balance of power was shifting back to employers as the labour market cooled. The ABS share working from home fell by more than three percentage points between 2021 and 2023. Australia has not abandoned remote work. It has simply stopped treating it as a pandemic concession.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, the countries share a legal architecture: a statutory right to ask, an employer who may decline, and no right to remote work. The differences are in what surrounds it. Malaysia offers incentives, a government that has just demonstrated working from home on itself, and a ministry study with encouraging percentages. Australia offers a regular official statistic, a tribunal that has ruled in an employee's favour, and a political argument about whether to go further. I found no Malaysian equivalent of the ABS series, which means the Malaysian market is harder to read from abroad.

Hofstede Insights offers a cultural footnote. Malaysia scores 100 on power distance and 26 on individualism; Australia scores 36 and 90. A request in Kuala Lumpur travels up a hierarchy and arrives as a favour, while one in Melbourne is an individual's claim against a procedure. Treat national scores as a rough guide, not a verdict. The practical consequence is that Australian flexibility tends to be written down and Malaysian flexibility tends to be understood, and understandings are what return-to-office memos revise.

## Conclusion

If you are weighing the two, assume the law gives you a conversation, not a result. In Malaysia, make the request formally, ask about the tax incentive, and get home-working terms agreed in writing, since the cultural default is that the employer decides. In Australia, secure your remote days in the contract, check your award, and keep a record of every request, because the Fair Work Commission has shown that procedure matters. Test any pay-cut-for-remote trade against what you would otherwise spend on commuting.

Neither country will stop your employer from changing its mind, and Australia, at least, publishes excellent statistics on how often employers do. Both guarantee the right to ask, which is the cheapest right a government can offer.
