# Switzerland Whispers the Salary, Chile Quotes It After Tax

🇨🇭 Switzerland · 🇨🇱 Chile

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Switzerland's median gross monthly wage was CHF 7,024 in 2024, according to Federal Statistical Office figures cited by Newly Swissed, and a union spokesperson once described discussing it as a huge taboo even between friends and spouses. In Chile, INE's income survey put the median monthly wage at 680,000 pesos in 2025, and a new law published in July 2026 requires large employers to put their gender pay gap on their website. One country is rich and silent about it. The other is modest, increasingly obliged to speak, and still in the habit of quoting figures without saying whether they are before or after tax.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Give a narrow range in annual francs and say whether it includes the 13th month | Quote a monthly figure before establishing whether it is paid 12 or 13 times |
| Check the collective labour agreement for your sector, which may set minimum salaries | Expect a national minimum wage; there is none |
| Negotiate on the written offer, with one number and two reasons | Reopen terms after a verbal yes; leverage disappears the moment you accept |
| Ask for a fifth week of vacation, pension plan detail, home-office days or training if the base is fixed | Treat a discretionary bonus as guaranteed pay; it can be zero |
| Benchmark with published data such as the Federal Statistical Office's salary tool | Ask a colleague what they earn over lunch |
| Compare offers on gross annual compensation | Compare monthly figures without allowing for canton and municipality taxes |

### In Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask whether an offer is sueldo bruto or sueldo liquido, gross or net | Assume the figure quoted to you is gross |
| Answer the salary-expectation question with a justified range | Give a vague answer, refuse to answer, or fixate only on base pay |
| Ask whether the legal profit-sharing payment, the gratificacion, is already included in the figure | Compare two offers without checking what each folds in |
| Ask in writing about the September and December bonuses, which are customary at many firms | Assume they are a legal entitlement |
| Consult self-reported salary sites and then check against sector data | Treat one forum figure as the market rate |
| Check whether a collective agreement sets pay in mining, transport or heavy industry | Expect to bargain individually where a union has already done it |

## Switzerland

Swiss pay arrives in a fairly orderly manner. According to swissinfo, gross salary is normally negotiated in the interview before a contract is signed, there is no national minimum wage, and monthly pay usually runs on a 13-month system, with the 13th payment pro-rated in partial years. The Swiss, in other words, negotiate once, quietly, and then stop. Upreer's guide advises giving a narrow annual range backed by a public source such as the Federal Statistical Office's Salarium tool, making a single counter-offer with one number and two reasons, and never reopening terms after saying yes. A Michael Page study reported by swissinfo found that 30 percent of Swiss employees and applicants asked for a raise in the past year, against 40 percent in Germany and France. Only 19 percent of Swiss jobseekers named a specific figure, about half the German rate of 41 percent.

Transparency is where the arrangement thins out. The revised Gender Equality Act, in force since 2020, requires companies with more than 100 employees to carry out a pay equity analysis, but Mercer reports that a 2025 government interim assessment found almost half of the organisations assessed did not comply. In the same Mercer survey, 36 percent of employers do not plan to share pay information at all. A Demoscope survey of 1,004 people for Employes Suisse found that nearly half of employees have no opportunity to discuss their salary, and 54 percent of women do not expect a raise next year, against 37 percent of men. The constitution has guaranteed equal pay since 1981, according to the legal portal Espace Droit, but it also notes that colleagues may have signed salary confidentiality agreements, which make discrimination harder to prove.

The social explanation is less legal than temperamental. A Newly Swissed commentary lists fear of envy, a value placed on privacy, a preference for modesty and trust in labour protections and published pay scales. The Swiss will tell you exactly how cheaply they bought their lunch. They will not tell you what they paid for it with.

The employer side is tidier than the employee side. In the same Mercer survey, 60 percent of Swiss organisations consider themselves prepared for pay transparency, against a European average of 49 percent, and 97 percent cite legal compliance as the main driver. A headhunter at Robert Walters, speaking to swissinfo, adds that in executive search he sees no salary difference between locals and expats at the same level, and that about 80 percent of openings are filled locally. He also warns that anyone moving to Switzerland just for the money often ends up disappointed, since a family-friendly apartment with a home office can cost CHF 6,000 a month.

## Chile

Chile approaches the subject with more noise and, until recently, less data. INE's 2025 income survey, as reported by Diario Financiero, put the median monthly wage at 680,000 pesos, with men at 701,985 and women at 600,253; public-sector employees had a median of 1,006,785 against 700,295 in the private sector. Separately, INE's 2024 survey showed women earning 24.4 percent less than men in their main occupation, according to the Zoom de Genero report covered by La Tercera, with the gap at 33.5 percent in high-skill jobs and 29.2 percent for women living with minors. Equal pay has been law since 2009, when Law 20.348 was passed, and under Article 62 bis of the Labour Code an employee can petition in writing for equal remuneration.

The newer step is Law 21.828, published on 23 July 2026, which Garrigues describes as obliging companies with 200 or more workers, or 50 or more in sectors including mining, finance, energy and construction, to submit an annual gender equity report including pay gap information to the labour authority each March and to publish it on their own website. It is a modest instrument, covering gaps between men and women rather than individual salaries, and it will not tell you what the person at the next desk earns. It is, nonetheless, a requirement that the figures be made public, which is the step Switzerland has so far left to individual firms.

On the practical side, Expat.com's guide says Chileans commonly discuss pay in net terms, and that you must confirm whether a number is gross or net. Expat.cl adds that deductions typically leave about 80 percent of gross, so a 2,000,000 peso offer lands near 1,600,000. Bonuses in September and December are widely expected, according to Expat.com, though not always a legal requirement, and in mining and heavy industry wages are often fixed by collective agreement rather than negotiated. Emol's interview advice, quoting a Trabajando.com expert, is to answer the salary-expectation question with a justified range and to consider the whole package. Negotiation is expected, then, but conducted with a hierarchy and a calculator. Expat.com adds that interviews often run to three to five rounds, sometimes with psychometric tests, that workplaces emphasise titles and formal address, and that the maximum working week fell from 44 to 42 hours in April 2026, with 40 targeted for 2028. The salary conversation, in short, arrives at the end of a long process and is expected to be concluded politely.

## The Reckoning

The two countries fail at transparency in opposite directions. Switzerland has money, rules and a social norm of silence, so salary information flows mostly through recruiters, calculators and sector agreements rather than colleagues. Chile has a median wage that Expat.cl puts at roughly 650 to 700 dollars a month, a pay gap that is measured and published at the aggregate level, and a market where the first job of a newcomer is to find out which number is on the table. The Swiss negotiate rarely and tidily, and the Chilean negotiates in the knowledge that the headline figure may be shrunk by a fifth before it reaches the bank.

Hofstede Insights scores Chile at 63 on power distance and 86 on uncertainty avoidance, against Switzerland's 34 and 58, which squares with a Chilean market that is more hierarchical and cautious about contracts and a Swiss one in which the offer is a reasoned exchange between near equals. Neither country gives the candidate the whole picture unprompted. The difference is that Switzerland expects you to ask about the thirteenth month, and Chile expects you to ask about the tax.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Local Switzerland — a union spokeswoman said a pay-sharing campaign aimed to lay everything bare and create a new culture of talking about wages, while an employers' federation voice replied that Switzerland's gender-neutral pay system works well and each firm should decide whether to disclose.*

> *SwissForum — a newcomer weighing a CHF 120K offer for a pharma management role was told by one poster to negotiate before accepting because leverage vanishes afterwards, by another that asking costs little, and by a third that a fair offer needs no squeezing.*

> *Newly Swissed — a commentator with Swiss roots argues the Swiss talk constantly about what things cost and almost never about what they earn, citing envy, privacy, modesty and trust in published pay scales.*

> *Expat.com Santiago forum — a prospective expat with a Santiago job offer and no clear pay scales online was advised by another poster to sell oneself at maximum when negotiating and to consult tusalario.org for self-reported salaries.*

> *Expat.com Santiago forum — a poster weighing a 1,430,000 peso monthly offer was told it was pretty good money for a single person, if net, and was asked first whether it was gross or net and what benefits were included.*

## Conclusion

If you are moving to Switzerland, do your homework alone and early: find the sector agreement, decide on an annual range, ask about the 13th month and the pension, and make your single counter-offer on the written paper. Do not expect colleagues to tell you whether you did well. The evidence suggests they will talk about the weather first.

If you are moving to Chile, ask whether the number is gross or net before you celebrate, ask whether the profit-sharing payment is included, and ask in writing about the September and December bonuses. The new reporting law will help women in large firms more than it helps you at the interview, so bring your own data. In both countries the salary is real. It is only that in one it is secret, and in the other it is approximate.
