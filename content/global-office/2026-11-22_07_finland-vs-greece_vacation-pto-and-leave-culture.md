# Finland Mandates the Holiday, Greece Merely Mentions It

🇫🇮 Finland · 🇬🇷 Greece

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In July 2024, according to Statistics Finland, a third of employed Finns took at least one full week off, and in the workplaces they left behind, answering an email counted legally as work. In the same year, Eurostat data show Greeks averaged 39.8 hours a week, the longest in the European Union and 3.8 hours above the bloc's 36.0. One country has written rest into the statute book and then built a national cottage industry around using it. The other has a magnificent August and an arrangement in which the rest of the year is, to put it gently, negotiable.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan for a continuous block of at least two weeks in summer; it is the expected shape of the holiday | Schedule anything urgent with Finnish clients between Midsummer and early August |
| Read your contract for the holiday bonus, which collective agreements often set at roughly half of holiday wages | Assume the bonus is a legal right; it comes from the collective agreement, not the statute |
| Start your job before the leave-earning year closes, or negotiate for some leave from day one | Expect to take paid time off in your first months if you start just after April 1 |
| Count Saturdays when you read "30 days"; Finnish law does, so it means five weeks | Plan a six-week trip on the strength of a figure that quietly includes Saturdays |
| Close the laptop; staff are not expected to work during leave | Send a mildly cheerful "quick question" email to a colleague at the cottage |
| Keep to the 8-hour day and the 11 hours of rest between shifts | Treat overtime as a personality trait; it is uncommon and mostly repaid in time |

### In Greece

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether you work a five-day or six-day week; it changes your entitlement from 20 to 24 days in year one | Assume the number on the offer letter is the number everyone gets |
| Ask for leave in writing; the employer must grant it within two months of the request | Rely on a verbal "of course, take what you need" |
| Claim your holiday allowance, capped at half a month's salary | Forget that the allowance is paid at the start of leave, not afterwards |
| Treat August 15 as a hard stop; it is the largest holiday after Orthodox Easter | Try to conduct business with a small shop, or a ministry, in the second week of August |
| Record extra hours yourself and ask how they are compensated | Absorb unpaid overtime quietly because everyone else does |
| Learn which public holidays are mandatory and which are only customary | Presume Clean Monday or Good Friday are guaranteed days off |

## Finland

Finnish holiday law is the sort of thing a civil servant would produce if asked to make leisure bureaucratic and then mildly alarming. According to SAK, the central trade union body, employees earn two days of annual holiday per month worked in the first leave-earning year, which runs from 1 April to 31 March, and 2.5 days per month after that. Over a full year those rates come to 24 and 30 days, counted on a six-day week, so thirty days means five weeks. Collective agreements commonly add a holiday bonus of about half the pay for the holiday itself, a payment the statute does not require but which many employees receive.

The culture does the rest. Statistics Finland reports that July is the most popular holiday month, and Finland.fi notes that the country has 495,145 summer cottages, with owners spending an average of 85 days at them in 2024. A Finnish blog written for foreigners explains that summer leave must include at least one block of two weeks, that staff cannot be expected to work during it, and that replying to a work email counts as work. An American who moved there told CNBC that July offices are very quiet and urgent matters simply wait for August.

The waiting is the catch, and the system has several. A foreign resident who documented the rules found that no leave of any kind can be taken until 1 April has passed during your employment, so a person starting on 2 April faces almost a year with only weekends and public holidays. The full five weeks arrive only after three such April dates. Meanwhile InfoFinland confirms that the working day is capped at eight hours and the week at 40, with at least 11 hours of rest between shifts, and that overtime is uncommon. The generosity is real. It is simply issued in instalments, with paperwork.

Pay during the break is handled with similar thoroughness. SAK notes that salaried employees keep their regular monthly salary on holiday, while hourly-paid staff receive holiday pay of 9 percent of gross earnings, rising to 11.5 percent after a year of employment. Unused leave is compensated when the job ends, and claims can be made for up to two years afterwards. A Finnish employee who is owed money by an employer for a holiday not taken therefore has a longer memory than most employers, which is perhaps the point.

## Greece

Greek law is less theatrical and, on paper, entirely respectable. Payoneer's 2026 guide, which is a vendor publication and should be checked against official sources, puts leave at 20 working days in the first year of a five-day week, rising to 22 from the third year and 25 after ten years with one employer. Nine public holidays are mandatory and paid, and employers must give leave to at least half of eligible staff between 1 May and 30 September. A holiday allowance equal to the pay for the leave is payable, capped at half a month's salary, which at the 2026 minimum wage of 920 euros is 460 euros. Since Law 5239/2025, leave can be split into parts at the employee's written request.

The difficulty is the other 340-odd days. Eurostat's 39.8 hours a week is the highest in the EU, and Greek City Times, citing Eurostat and ELSTAT, reports average hourly pay of 9.30 euros in key sectors against 24.90 euros for the EU, and output of 24.30 euros per hour worked against 53.15. A study by the GSEE Labor Institute, reported by To Vima, found 52 percent of respondents working beyond contracted hours and 49 percent receiving no pay for the extra time. Separate kariera.gr LinkedIn polls, which are explicitly not representative, found 42 percent saying overtime was neither recorded nor compensated. Greeks, in other words, are not lazy. They are merely under-remunerated by a margin the statute is too polite to mention.

Then comes August. To Vima reports that more than one million of greater Athens's 3.6 million residents leave in the month, around the 15 August holiday, and that small shops close, pharmacies rotate their duty shifts, and supermarkets and tourist businesses stay open. Since the 2010 crisis, closures tend to last one or two weeks rather than the full month. The institution survives; the shutters have been shortened.

Daily life has its own rhythm. Expat.com's 2014 guide, which is dated and should be read as such, describes public offices open roughly 8:00 to 14:30 with no lunch break, shops reopening on some evenings from 5 to 8, most businesses closed on Sundays, and strikes that can close roads without much notice. Asking a service provider to work on a holiday can cost roughly double. For a newcomer used to the idea that leave is a clean, bounded event, the Greek version is a negotiation conducted around a siesta of rolling closures, and it is wise to book appointments before 2 p.m.

## The Reckoning

Finland and Greece both guarantee roughly four to five weeks of paid leave, which makes the real contrast one of enforcement and norm rather than entitlement. In Finland, the holiday is a collective obligation: it begins around Midsummer, clients accept it, and the contractual right to be unreachable is respected because everyone else is also unreachable. In Greece, the entitlement exists but competes with a working week that is the EU's longest, an overtime culture that often goes unrecorded, and a single national exodus in August that does the work of an official holiday policy without the paperwork.

Hofstede Insights scores Greece at 100 on uncertainty avoidance against Finland's 59, and at 60 on power distance against Finland's 33, which is a tidy summary of why the Finnish employer says nothing when you leave and the Greek one expects a plan. The Finnish approach is predictable to the point of sedation. The Greek approach is warmer, looser and, for the employee without a collective agreement or a firm view, considerably more expensive in hours.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *CNBC Make It via NBC Chicago — an American who relocated to Finland describes July offices as nearly empty, urgent work pushed to August, and a two-week summer rule that "essentially" forces people to take a break. Her one complaint: Finnish colleagues rarely seek promotion, which sits oddly with her ambition.*

> *Finnwards — a foreigner's guide to working in Finland warns that colleagues and clients can be effectively unreachable for about four weeks in summer, which feels surprising or even annoying at first, and that nobody expects work to be done on leave.*

> *ItsFred blog — a foreign resident says the Finnish leave rules were almost impossible to decode from official English or Swedish sources, that they learned them from Finnish friends and online forums, and that changing employers can cost you the holiday bonus and restart the waiting period.*

> *Al Jazeera — a reporter based in Athens describes the city in August as emptied of the people who run it, with officials unreachable at their desks, banks without queues, and tourists wandering from museum to shade.*

> *Expat.com — an American expat in Athens says that in August many shops and restaurants close for what feels like the whole month, and that newcomers must adopt "Greek time": linger over meals, expect events to start late, and relax.*

## Conclusion

If your priority is predictable, protected rest, Finland is the clearer bet, provided you read the contract for the April rule and negotiate early leave before you sign. Ask your employer in writing how the holiday bonus works, and plan your first year around the waiting period rather than discovering it in March.

If you choose Greece, treat the statutory entitlement as the floor, check whether your week is five days or six, and keep your own record of hours worked, since the data suggest nobody else will. In both countries, the generous part of the calendar is real. The only question is whether it is guaranteed by the system or by your employer, and Finland, with admirable honesty, will simply tell you which.
