**India to Mauritius: Mum Gets Less Leave, Dad Finally Gets Some**

🇮🇳 India · 🇲🇺 Mauritius

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Nearly two-thirds of Mauritius's population traces its ancestry to Indian indentured labourers who arrived through Port Louis between 1834 and 1920 — making it, by some distance, the largest such diaspora the British Empire ever created. More than a century and a half later, the two countries have drifted apart on exactly the question those labourers' descendants might care about most: what happens to your career, and your household, the moment a baby arrives. India offers new mothers a genuinely generous 26 weeks of paid leave and essentially nothing to new fathers. Mauritius offers mothers a shorter, still solid 16 weeks — and hands fathers four full consecutive weeks of their own, by law.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm your employer has 10 or more employees before assuming the Maternity Benefit Act applies to you. | Assume every workplace, especially informal or small-business ones, is legally covered. |
| Check your specific company's paternity policy before you need it — it varies from 5 days to 6 months. | Assume any national paternity standard exists; no such law has actually passed. |
| Plan for a possible multi-year career gap if you leave after your first child. | Assume returning to your previous level and pace is the default outcome — most working mothers report it isn't. |
| Ask directly about remote-work or flexible options during late pregnancy. | Assume a company will offer accommodations unprompted, even a generous one. |
| Keep some paid or freelance work on your résumé during an extended break, if you can. | Disappear from the job market entirely for years without any visible continuity, if you plan to re-enter at a similar level. |
| Know your legal options if you're let go while pregnant — courts have ruled in employees' favour before. | Assume a layoff during pregnancy is simply bad luck with no recourse. |

#### 🇲🇺 Mauritius

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect 16 weeks of fully paid maternity leave as a legal floor, not a negotiated perk. | Assume you need to negotiate basic parental leave — it's statutory. |
| Plan your father's four consecutive weeks of paternity leave within two weeks of the birth. | Delay past the two-week window — the entitlement doesn't carry over indefinitely. |
| Check your tenure — paternity leave is paid only after 12 months of continuous service. | Assume it's paid from day one at a brand-new job; it isn't. |
| Watch for the proposed 2027 reform extending maternity leave toward a full year. | Plan your finances around a benefit that hasn't been enacted into law yet. |
| Research schools and neighbourhoods in person before relocating with children. | Commit to a move based on a listing alone — infrastructure like sidewalks and stroller access varies sharply by area. |
| Budget realistically for the lower cost of domestic help and childcare relative to Western Europe. | Assume Mauritian childcare costs scale the same way UK or French costs do — several expats report it's dramatically cheaper. |

### India

On paper, India's maternity provisions are among the more generous in the world: 26 weeks of fully paid leave for a first or second child under the Maternity Benefit Act, extendable in cases of pregnancy-related illness, with the same term covering surrogacy and six weeks after a miscarriage. The catch is coverage. The Act applies only to establishments with 10 or more employees, which leaves out a large share of India's workforce entirely, and the generosity on the mother's side has no real counterpart for fathers. No national paternity leave law exists; the closest thing, Rule 43-A of the Central Civil Services Rules, grants 15 days and applies only to government employees, while a 2017 bill proposing a private-sector standard has never been passed. Private employers fill the gap entirely on their own judgment, and the range is enormous — five days at some established firms, six months or more at a handful of multinationals, with manufacturing and the unorganised sector offering the least of all.

The career consequences land almost entirely on mothers. ILO data from 2024 found that 53 percent of Indian women outside the labour force cite care responsibilities as the reason, against just 1.1 percent of men — one of the starkest gender gaps the organisation tracks anywhere. A widely cited Ashoka University study put a sharper number on the pattern still: roughly half of working Indian mothers leave their jobs around the time they have children, and only about 27 percent of those who do go on to advance in their careers afterward rather than simply re-entering at a lower rung or exiting for good. Hofstede's data scores India at 77 on power distance alongside a middling 48 on individualism, a profile consistent with a workplace that defers heavily to whatever a senior manager decides is reasonable for a new parent, with little statutory floor to fall back on if that manager decides otherwise.

### Mauritius

Mauritius runs a smaller, more evenly distributed system. The Workers' Rights Act 2019 guarantees 16 weeks of fully paid maternity leave, up to seven of which can be taken before the birth, plus three weeks of paid leave after a miscarriage and a full 16 weeks in the event of a stillbirth. Adoptive parents of a child under 12 months get 14 weeks, provided they've completed a year of service. None of this matches India's 26-week headline number, but it comes with something India's law doesn't: a real, universal paternity entitlement. Fathers get four consecutive weeks, paid in full once they've completed 12 months of continuous service, to be taken within two weeks of the birth or adoption.

A 2026 budget proposal would push maternity leave toward 52 weeks — 26 at full pay, 26 at half — and extend paternity to six weeks, though neither change is law yet and should be treated as a direction of travel rather than a current entitlement. The standard workweek runs 45 hours with time-and-a-half overtime, and the 2019 reforms added a right to request flexible or compressed scheduling, though an employer can still decline on operational grounds. For a certain kind of remote professional, the calculus is less about the statutory leave and more about everyday cost: one British journalist who relocated to be a stay-at-home parent found nursery care there would run roughly £150 a month, a fraction of what she'd have paid in London, alongside a mortgage-sized rent for a house with a pool and a garden rather than a studio flat.

### The Reckoning

The real irony, set side by side, is that the country with the more generous headline maternity number treats fatherhood as entirely optional, while the country with the shorter maternity number treats fatherhood as a genuine legal entitlement. India's 26 weeks sound better in a brochure; Mauritius's four guaranteed weeks for dad do more, in practice, to normalise a father actually being home and visibly present for a newborn, rather than back at his desk within days because nothing legally obliges otherwise.

Both systems share one real weakness: coverage gaps that bite hardest at the edges. India's threshold excludes small employers and much of the informal economy outright; Mauritius's 12-month service requirement for paid paternity leave means a father who recently changed jobs gets nothing but the unpaid version, right when the entitlement would matter most. Generosity on paper, in both countries, depends heavily on which employer you happen to have and how long you've already been there.

The diaspora history makes the comparison sharper rather than softer. The great-great-grandchildren of indentured labourers who left India with nothing now live under a parental-leave regime their ancestors' home country still hasn't matched for fathers, more than a century on — a reminder that colonial-era migration can scramble a country's social contract in ways that outlast the empire that caused it by a very long time.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — An Indian professional who was laid off while roughly halfway through pregnancy asked how employers would view a pregnant job candidate and when to disclose it; one commenter reassured them that "gaps after pregnancy are very much acceptable," while another suggested a writ petition for continued pay was a real, if drastic, legal option worth discussing with an advocate.*

> *Blind — A new parent planning to return to work after quitting to care for a newborn asked how long a break could run before becoming unemployable; a Cisco-based commenter relayed that one friend took a full two-year break and returned a level down, from manager to lead, while others kept freelance gigs listed on their résumés specifically to cover the gap.*

> *expat.com (Mauritius forum) — A parent who had relocated from Switzerland twenty months earlier praised the balance of local and expat children at their son's small international school, but warned bluntly that pushing a stroller around outside a shopping mall is "next to impossible" given the lack of sidewalks and cycle paths.*

> *Syndicated UK press — A British freelance journalist who moved to Mauritius specifically to avoid day care described nursery costs there as roughly £150 a month, well under the UK average, and said of the move: "I don't see how we could have a good lifestyle in the UK" on the same income.*

### Conclusion

If the plan is to start a family while working across these two countries, the honest comparison isn't maternity-leave-length against maternity-leave-length; it's "generous but conditional" against "modest but universal." India will, on paper, give a mother more time than almost anywhere in the world — if her employer is large enough to be covered, and her partner's employer feels like matching the gesture at all. Mauritius gives less to the mother but guarantees something to the father, consistently, by law, regardless of how generous any individual employer feels that quarter.

Neither country will hand new parents an easy year. One just makes sure both parents are legally allowed to be tired together.
