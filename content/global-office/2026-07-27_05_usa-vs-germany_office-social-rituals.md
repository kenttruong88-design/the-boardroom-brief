**"How Are You?" Is Not a Question in Germany: Office Rituals in the USA vs. Germany**

**🇺🇸 USA · 🇩🇪 Germany**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

An American asking a German colleague "how are you?" in the elevator is, functionally, asking nothing — it is a script, and the correct response is "good, you?" regardless of the actual answer. A German hearing the same question interprets it literally, as a genuine invitation to report on their actual state of being, and can find the ensuing script deeply confusing or even insincere. According to ezCater's workplace survey, 68 percent of American workers now say they'd prefer to socialize with coworkers during the workday rather than off the clock — a preference for bonding on the clock that would strike most German offices, where the Feierabend line between work and life is treated as close to sacred, as a mild category error.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Engage in light small talk before meetings — weather, weekend plans, sports | Skip straight to business without any social warm-up; it can read as cold |
| Say yes to the occasional team happy hour, even briefly | Assume attendance is fully optional with no social cost to skipping every one |
| Keep "how are you" responses brief and positive by default | Answer literally with real problems in a casual hallway greeting |
| Treat lunch as a flexible, often social, sometimes working occasion | Assume everyone wants an hour-long sit-down lunch; grab-and-go is common and fine |
| Compliment colleagues' work openly and often | Assume enthusiasm is insincere just because it's frequent — it's usually genuine, just stylistically loud |

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say "Schönen Feierabend" and actually disconnect once you leave | Message colleagues after hours expecting a quick reply; the boundary is real, not performative |
| Accept an invitation to a Feierabendbier (after-work beer) if offered — it's a genuine bonding ritual | Turn every after-work invite into a networking opportunity; it's meant to be low-pressure |
| Bring cake or treats for your team during your first week or two (Einstand) | Skip the newcomer gesture; it's a small custom that meaningfully eases your first impression |
| Keep personal calls, social media, and idle chat out of work hours | Treat the office as a place for casual socializing throughout the day |
| Ask "how are you" only if you actually want to know | Use "how are you" as a scripted greeting — Germans may answer it honestly, and expect you to too |

**The USA's** office social culture treats bonding as an active, ongoing project — something to be engineered through happy hours, team lunches, and icebreakers, not left to develop passively. Hofstede Insights scores the US at 91 on individualism, the highest in the world, which sounds like it should predict less workplace socializing, not more — but American individualism pairs with an equally strong belief that relationships, including professional ones, are built through visible effort. Recent workplace research bears this out at scale: teams that socialize together show measurably higher productivity, and the majority of employees now say they'd rather fold that socializing into the workday itself, at lunch specifically, than treat it as an after-hours obligation competing with personal time.

**Germany's** office social rituals are fewer, smaller, and far more clearly bounded. Feierabend — literally "celebration evening," the daily transition from work to private life — is not just a word but a near-legal expectation, reinforced by strict statutory rules on rest periods that make "always-on" culture genuinely difficult to sustain. Within the workday itself, water-cooler chat, extended coffee breaks, and casual social media checking are, per multiple guides to German workplace ethics, actively frowned upon — the expectation is full focus on tasks, full stop. Socializing does happen, but it's compressed into specific, recognizable rituals: the Feierabendbier immediately after work, where hierarchy visibly softens for twenty minutes, and the Einstand, where a new hire brings cake for the team in their first week or two as a structured, low-effort way of breaking the ice.

### The Reckoning

The counterintuitive finding here is that the more individualistic culture (the USA) invests more deliberate effort into group bonding, while the more collectivist-leaning workplace culture (Germany, per its comparatively lower individualism score of 67) treats work relationships with more reserve. The explanation is structural: American at-will employment and higher job turnover create an incentive to build rapport quickly, because tenure isn't guaranteed and social capital needs to be built fast to be useful. German employment, with stronger job protections and typically longer tenures, allows trust to accumulate slowly over years — there's no urgency to manufacture closeness, because the relationship has time to develop on its own terms.

The other trap is mistaking Germany's social reserve for coldness. It isn't. It's a preference for depth over frequency — fewer, more genuine interactions instead of many performed ones. Americans transplanted to Germany often describe an early loneliness that later resolves into some of the most durable professional friendships they've had, once they stop expecting the volume of interaction they're used to.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone answering "How is the German separation of work and personal life?" said colleagues who are friendly and helpful all day at work can go weeks without any contact outside the office, and that this isn't coldness, it's simply a different definition of where a coworker relationship is supposed to live.*

> *Quora — A respondent defending strict work/life separation wrote that colleagues who got annoyed when they declined to share weekend plans were projecting an American norm onto a boundary that, in their view, was completely reasonable to hold.*

> *Blind — A thread simply titled "german coworkers" collected a long list of adjustment stories from people newly working with German teams, with a recurring theme: initial interactions felt clipped or unfriendly, but many reported the relationships becoming genuinely warm once trust was established over months rather than days.*

> *Blind — A "cringey office talk" thread turned into an unplanned cross-cultural comparison, with several posters noting that scripted American workplace enthusiasm — forced icebreakers, manufactured positivity — reads as bizarre or insincere to colleagues from more reserved office cultures, even when the intent behind it is genuinely friendly.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to a US office, lean into the small talk and the happy hours even if they feel performative at first — the volume of low-stakes social contact is the mechanism, not a distraction from the real work. If you're moving to a German office, don't panic when the first few weeks feel socially quiet; say yes to the Feierabendbier when it's offered, respect the boundary once people say "Feierabend," and trust that the relationship is being built at a slower, sturdier pace than you're used to. My honest line for a friend over drinks: American office friendliness is wide and fast; German office friendliness is narrow and slow. Neither is fake — they're just running on different clocks.
