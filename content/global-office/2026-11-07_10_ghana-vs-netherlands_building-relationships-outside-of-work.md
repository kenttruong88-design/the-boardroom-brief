**In Ghana, You're Family by Friday. In the Netherlands, You're on the Agenda for March.**

🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Accra, a newcomer can expect to be invited to a wedding, a funeral, and at least one christening before the paperwork for a work permit clears. In Amsterdam, a newcomer can expect to wait rather longer for a dinner invitation — long enough that, per one 2020 survey of expatriates, 51 percent had simply stopped trying and built an entire social life inside the expat bubble instead. Both countries describe themselves as friendly. Both, strictly speaking, are telling the truth. The trouble is that "friendly" turns out to mean two almost opposite things, and nobody tells you which one you've signed up for until you're standing in someone's kitchen wondering whether you've just made a friend or merely been fed. The work-life-balance guide to these two countries has already been written elsewhere; this one is about what happens once the laptop closes and you have to find a human being who isn't paid to talk to you.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet each person individually — including the security guard — before stating your business | Don't launch into a request without first asking after someone's family |
| Accept food or drink when it's offered, even a token taste | Don't eat in front of others without offering to share |
| Give, receive, and eat with your right hand, or both hands together | Don't hand anything over, or beckon, using only your left hand |
| Attend funerals you're invited to — they double as the country's real networking events | Don't treat a funeral invitation as optional; a no-show without reason is noted |
| Expect colleagues to become a second family, calls and weekend visits included | Don't assume a warm coworker is only a coworker — that relationship carries obligations |
| Let relationships build through small, repeated exchanges with neighbours and traders | Don't expect European-style privacy; curiosity about your life is affection, not nosiness |

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a club — sports, rowing, a choir — it's the actual mechanism by which Dutch adults make friends | Don't expect friendship to grow out of casual small talk at the office |
| Say what you think plainly; directness reads as respect, not aggression | Don't take blunt feedback personally, and don't read politeness as warmth |
| Treat the first six months as groundwork, not failure | Don't expect a spontaneous follow-up invite after one good conversation |
| Learn some Dutch — even badly — as a visible sign of commitment | Don't assume a group switching to English for you means you've been let in |
| Host something yourself, with a fixed date, booked weeks ahead | Don't drop by unannounced, however normal that felt back home |
| Get used to "agenda culture" — social plans are scheduled, not improvised | Don't be offended when someone checks their calendar before agreeing to see you |

Ghana's sociability is not a personality trait so much as an operating system. Hofstede Insights scores the country's individualism at just 15 out of 100, against the Netherlands' 80 — one of the widest gaps the index records between any two countries actively recruiting the same pool of expatriate talent. In practice this means relationships are not a pleasant addition to daily logistics; they are the logistics. Housing, hiring, credit, and childcare all tend to route through family, church, mosque, or hometown association rather than through institutions, which is precisely why a new arrival gets folded in so fast — the system runs on having people, and it needs you to have some too. A foreign hire who shows up expecting to network the way one would in London or Rotterdam, through conferences and cautious LinkedIn messages, will instead find themselves recruited into someone's wedding planning committee before their second week is out, and will generally be better off for it.

The same density of social obligation that makes Ghana feel instantly warm, however, comes itemised. Ghanaian family and community life, as cultural researchers and local commentators consistently describe it, is explicitly reciprocal: the funeral you attend, the colleague's cousin's wedding contribution, the open-door expectation at your own house, are not generosity in one direction but a running account. Newcomers who mistake the warmth for a one-way gift tend to be the same ones blindsided, a year in, by how much of their calendar and bank balance now belongs to people they met at work.

The Netherlands runs the opposite system, and its own statistics office is admirably clinical about the cost. CBS reported in 2019 that 9 percent of Dutch adults were frequently lonely and 12 percent experienced severe social isolation — modest-sounding numbers for a country that also ranks among the world's five happiest, until you notice that "happy" and "well-supplied with friends" are measuring different things entirely. Dutch social life isn't absent; it's booked. Close friendships, as one Amsterdam-based columnist for the English-language outlet DutchNews has argued, mostly form in childhood and university everywhere, and the Netherlands is simply unapologetic about not generating many new slots for adults afterward. The upside, rarely advertised, is that a Dutch household running at 80 on the individualism index is also one where nobody expects you to attend their cousin's engagement party, lend them rent money, or host their visiting relatives for a week — a trade-off that takes most newcomers roughly a year to appreciate rather than resent.

For the newcomer, this produces a specific and well-documented frustration. InterNations' 2020 Expat Insider survey ranked the Netherlands 50th of 58 countries for "Finding Friends": 52 percent of expats there called it hard to befriend locals, against a 38 percent global average, and 51 percent said their friendship circle was mostly other expats — more than double the 11 percent who said it was mostly Dutch. A separate study by Amsterdam's own Expatcenter found 58 percent of internationals dissatisfied with their friend count and 57 percent feeling no real part of the city's culture, despite rating Amsterdam's quality of life highly. The Dutch aren't hiding anything. They've simply built a society where belonging is opt-in, scheduled, and slow to activate — which is a very different proposition from a society where belonging is the default setting the moment you arrive.

Laid side by side, the two countries invert the usual assumption that fast friendliness and deep friendship are the same curve. Ghana front-loads belonging and back-loads cost: you are included almost immediately, and the bill — in time, money, and mutual obligation — arrives steadily thereafter, for as long as you stay. The Netherlands back-loads belonging and keeps the cost nearly invisible: you are excluded for months, sometimes years, but a friendship that does form tends to ask little of you beyond showing up to the thing you already agreed to attend eight weeks ago. Neither country is lying when it calls itself welcoming; they are simply answering a different question than the one the newcomer is asking.

There is a crueler irony underneath. The individualism that makes the Dutch slow to let you in is the same trait that, once you're in, keeps the friendship low-drama and remarkably durable — nobody needs anything from you, which is its own kind of relief. The collectivism that makes Ghana feel like home within a week is the same trait that can make a foreigner feel, eventually, like a line item in everyone else's mutual-aid network. The two cultures aren't offering different amounts of friendliness. They're offering it on different payment plans, and the surveys on both sides are oddly honest about this: Ghanaians rate their own hospitality as a point of national pride, and the Dutch rate their own directness the same way, each treating the trait outsiders find hardest to navigate as the one most worth keeping.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — a long-running expat thread on the Netherlands described Dutch social circles as settling into small, rigid cliques that trace back to childhood or university, a pattern one commenter tied to the historical "pillarisation" of Dutch society into separate religious and political blocs — meaning the coldness newcomers sense isn't personal rejection so much as a group that closed its membership decades ago.*

> *InterNations (Expat Insider survey respondents) — expats quoted from Eindhoven and Maastricht separately described the Dutch as pleasant in conversation but closed to forming real friendships with non-Dutch people, with the language switch to English cited as a quiet signal of distance rather than inclusion.*

> *Expat.com forum (Ghana board) — a longtime member advised newcomers in Accra to stop waiting for an organic connection and instead seek out structured groups, pointing specifically to monthly meetups for people new to the country run by a local newcomers' association.*

> *ExpatExchange forum (Accra) — one contributor described the city's expatriate and retiree social scene as unexpectedly full, citing regular cultural events, language classes that doubled as a way into local circles, and a steady rotation of both local and international dining and nightlife.*

> *DutchNews.nl columnist — writing from a dinner party of mostly internationals, the columnist argued that most people's deepest friendships form in childhood everywhere, not uniquely in the Netherlands, and that the only real remedy for a newcomer is unglamorous: be reliably friendly to neighbours and acquaintances and let a slow thing be slow.*

### Conclusion

If you need to feel like you belong somewhere within the first month, Ghana will deliver that feeling faster and more sincerely than almost anywhere else — and will then, with equal sincerity, expect you to act like family for as long as you stay. If you can tolerate feeling like a stranger for the better part of a year in exchange for a friendship that, once formed, makes almost no further claims on you, the Netherlands will eventually deliver that too, provided you join the rowing club and show up every Tuesday without fail. Neither arrangement is better; they're simply denominated in different currencies, one in obligation and one in patience, and the research on both sides is consistent enough that the choice is really a question about which currency you'd rather be spending five years from now.

The practical test is this: ask yourself whether you'd rather be lonely for a while and then rarely again, or never lonely at all but perpetually on the hook. There is no third option on offer in either country, and pretending otherwise is how people end up resentful of a culture that never promised them anything different. The honest version I'd give a friend over a drink: in Ghana you're never lonely but you're never quite off the hook, and in the Netherlands you're off the hook immediately but you might be lonely for a while first — so pick the discomfort you'd rather schedule.
