**118th in the World vs. a Law That Mandates the Boardroom Be 40% Female by 2029**

**🇯🇵 Japan · 🇫🇷 France**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Japan ranked 118th out of 146 countries on the World Economic Forum's 2024 Gender Gap Index, with women holding just 13% of management positions nationally — compared to 36% in France. France, meanwhile, passed the Rixain law in 2021, mandating that large companies hit 30% female executives by March 2026 and 40% by March 2029, with real penalties attached. Neither country has solved the underlying pay gap — Japan's sits at 22.1%, France's at 12% by the narrower measure and a startling 30% over a lifetime once part-time work is factored in — but one country is legislating its way toward the fix while the other is still debating whether "womenomics" was ever more than a slogan.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly whether a role is *sogo shoku* (career track) or *ippan shoku* (clerical/non-career track) before accepting | Assume "general track" hiring language is gender-neutral in practice — women fill roughly 95% of ippan shoku roles |
| Expect the M-curve: many women exit the workforce in their 30s and return in their 40s or 50s | Assume a female colleague who left after having a child intends to stay out of the workforce permanently |
| Support the 2022 pay-gap disclosure requirement by asking employers directly for their published numbers | Assume disclosure laws alone have closed the gap — the unexplained gap persists even after controlling for role and tenure |
| Take maternity harassment (*matahara*) seriously as a named, documented legal issue, not a rare anecdote | Assume current legal protections carry real teeth — advocates note the law lacks penalties for violations |

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect generous, legally guaranteed parental leave with job protection on return | Assume equal leave access has closed the pay gap — part-time work still explains roughly 80% of France's lifetime earnings gap |
| Ask how your employer is tracking toward Rixain law's 2026 (30%) and 2029 (40%) executive quotas, if it's a large firm | Assume quotas apply below the 1,000-employee threshold — many mid-sized firms are exempt |
| Note that IT roles report France's smallest sector pay gap (around 5.5%) — a useful benchmark if you're in tech | Assume management-level pay gaps match entry-level ones — the gap widens notably at senior levels (14%) |
| Recognize the cultural framing of France as family-friendly for working mothers — it's grounded in real policy | Assume "family-friendly" policy has eliminated informal bias — the gap is legal-adjacent, not solved |

### Japan: Two Tracks, One Intended for Women

Japan's gender gap in management is structural before it's cultural. Many large firms still hire through a dual-track system: *sogo shoku*, the career track with promotion potential, and *ippan shoku*, general clerical work with little upward mobility — and women fill only around 5% of sogo shoku positions, per employment-system research. The result compounds through a career: Japan's overall female management share sits at 13%, against 41% in the US and 36% in France, and the raw gender wage gap of 22.1% barely narrows — to 24.3% unexplained — even after controlling for age, education, tenure, occupation, and position, according to structural wage-gap analysis.

A 2022 law now requires Japanese companies to publicly disclose gender pay gaps, a genuine step toward transparency. But advocates working on maternity discrimination (*matahara*) note the accompanying legal protections carry no real penalties for violation, and the "M-curve" employment pattern — high participation in the 20s, a sharp drop in the 30s coinciding with childbirth, partial re-entry in the 40s and 50s — remains a defining, well-documented feature of Japanese women's careers rather than a historical relic.

### France: Legislating the Boardroom, Not Yet the Household

France's approach leans hard on statute. The Rixain law, passed in 2021, sets binding executive-gender quotas for large companies — 30% by March 2026, 40% by March 2029 — with real compliance consequences, a legislative tool Japan has not adopted. France's headline gender pay gap of 12% (2022, full-time basis) also compares favorably against Japan's, and parental leave in France comes with strong job-protection guarantees that make returning to a career after childbirth structurally easier than in Japan's M-curve system.

The catch is that France's lifetime earnings picture is less rosy than the headline number suggests. Rising part-time work among women now explains close to 80% of France's lifetime gender earnings gap, according to CEPR research, meaning the same care-work pattern that shapes Japan's M-curve shows up in France too — just filtered through part-time employment status rather than full exit and re-entry. Sector variation is real and worth knowing: IT reports France's smallest gap (5.5%), while management-level roles show a considerably wider one (14%), per APEC data reported by The Local.

### The Reckoning

Both countries have identified the same underlying mechanism — that childbearing years correlate with a measurable, lasting career penalty for women — and chosen almost opposite remedies. France has reached for hard legal quotas at the top of organizations, betting that mandated representation in the boardroom will eventually reshape norms further down. Japan has reached for disclosure and voluntary "womenomics" messaging, a softer approach that produces headline participation gains — Japan's overall female labor force participation rate has, at points, exceeded that of the US — without meaningfully denting management representation or the pay gap underneath it.

Hofstede's masculinity dimension score for Japan (95, among the highest recorded globally) versus France's considerably lower score helps explain the difference in remedy: a culture that prizes achievement-and-competition norms as strongly as Japan's tends to resist quota-based intervention as an affront to meritocracy, even when the "meritocracy" in question funnels 95% of women into non-promotable roles from day one.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone answering how Japanese women are treated in the workforce described the sogo shoku/ippan shoku divide as the single most important thing a foreign hire could learn before accepting a Japanese job offer, since the track determines almost everything about long-term career trajectory.*

> *Japan Today — A feature collecting workplace sexism accounts included one advertising professional's experience of her manager visiting her home to encourage her to retire after learning she was pregnant, framing the choice explicitly as "career or baby" — an account the piece noted was far from unique.*

> *The Local France — Coverage of France's APEC salary-gap study noted that women with identical skill sets to male peers still earned 8.5% less on average, with the gap widening to 14% specifically at management level, undercutting France's reputation as more gender-equal than the numbers at senior levels actually support.*

> *Quora — A respondent explaining France's comparatively high fertility rate described it as "the easiest country for a woman to have children and also keep her career," crediting guaranteed paid leave and strong job-protection law as the deciding factor, rather than any particular cultural attitude toward motherhood.*

> *PBS NewsHour — A segment on Japanese career women challenging cultural norms featured professionals describing the psychological toll of the M-curve pattern — the expectation, often unstated, that a promotion track effectively pauses the moment a woman has a child, regardless of her stated ambitions.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Japan as a woman building a career, ask about sogo shoku status before anything else — it will determine more about your trajectory than your resume will. If you're moving to France, take real comfort in the legal scaffolding around parental leave and executive quotas, but don't mistake the headline pay-gap number for the full picture — ask specifically about part-time patterns and management-level gaps in your sector. The honest version, over a drink: Japan will tell you which track you're on and expect you to accept it; France will pass a law about the boardroom and still leave the daycare pickup unresolved.
