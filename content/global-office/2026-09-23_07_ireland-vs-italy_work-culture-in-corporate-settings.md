# The Flat Org Chart and the Pyramid: Corporate Culture in Ireland and Italy

**🇮🇪 Ireland · 🇮🇹 Italy**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

![Ireland vs Italy — Work culture in corporate settings](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/global-office/2026-09-23-07-ireland-vs-italy-work-culture-in-corporate-settings-hero)
*Photo: Anna Shvets via Pexels*

In Dublin, you'll be on a first-name basis with your VP by lunchtime on day one, and you'll be expected to disagree with them in the next meeting if you have a valid point. In Rome or Milan, you'll call that same-level executive "Dottore" for considerably longer than feels natural to an Irish colleague, and disagreement — while not forbidden — travels up a chain that has more rungs than you'd guess from the org chart PDF. Both countries are EU members with famously warm, relationship-oriented reputations. Only one of them actually organizes its offices that way.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Move to first names quickly — Irish workplaces are described consistently as informal and relaxed even at senior levels | Assume informality means low expectations — 84% of employees at Ireland's "Best Workplaces" report willing to give real extra effort, well above the 59% typical-workplace baseline |
| Speak up with ideas and pushback in meetings — participation from everyone is genuinely expected, not just tolerated | Mistake friendliness for lack of seriousness about deadlines and outcomes — Irish teams are collaborative but still results-focused |
| Socialize with colleagues outside work — it's common and genuinely helps you integrate into the team | Assume every Irish workplace matches the flat, egalitarian reputation equally — multinational tech offices and traditional Irish firms can differ meaningfully |
| Respect the max-48-hour legal working week as a real reference point, not just a formality | Expect the low 12.5% corporate tax rate and multinational presence to mean American-style intensity everywhere — Irish workplace pace and American workplace pace are not the same thing |
| Bring a valid point confidently to a disagreement with a manager — it's expected, not risky, in most Irish firms | Overdo formality with titles or excessive deference — it can read as oddly distant in a culture that prizes approachability |

#### 🇮🇹 Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use last names and professional titles (Dottore, Ingegnere, Avvocato) until explicitly invited to use first names | Assume decision-making happens where the meeting is — real authority sits with senior management, and meetings can be more discussion than decision |
| Invest real time in the long lunch — it's genuine relationship-building and professional networking, not a break from work | Rush a first business relationship toward transactional efficiency — Italian trust-building takes real time and resists shortcuts |
| Learn functional Italian — language skill is read directly as respect and commitment, and unlocks informal channels where real business happens | Expect informal-seeming conversation (jokes, warmth, first-name-adjacent friendliness) to mean flattened hierarchy — warmth and rank coexist here |
| Dress elegantly and consistently — appearance is treated as a visible marker of seriousness about the job | Plan for business as usual in August — the well-known August shutdown genuinely slows much of the country's business activity |
| Expect regional variation — northern offices run more formal than southern ones — and calibrate accordingly | Disagree with a superior without a genuinely well-reasoned point — it's tolerated, even respected, but it isn't casual pushback either |

### Ireland

Irish corporate culture presents, by multiple measures, as one of Europe's flattest and most consensus-oriented. Hofstede scores Ireland at just 28 on power distance — a strikingly egalitarian figure — paired with a high individualism score of 70, a combination that shows up daily as quick first-naming, genuine openness to junior pushback, and managers who are expected to bridge hierarchy rather than enforce it. Multiple business-culture guides describe Irish organizations as favoring flat structures with high accessibility between levels, and negotiations as involving real discussion where participation is expected from everyone rather than deferred to seniority.

This isn't the same as low standards. Data from Ireland's "Best Workplaces" surveys shows a sharp internal contrast: 84 percent of employees at top-ranked workplaces report willingness to give extra effort, against just 59 percent at the typical Irish workplace — a reminder that "flat and friendly" as a national stereotype coexists with real variation in how demanding individual employers actually are. Ireland's legal working week caps at 48 hours, and Dublin's dense multinational tech presence (Google, Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, and others, drawn partly by a 12.5 percent corporate tax rate) means a newcomer's actual experience often depends more on which specific multinational they've joined than on any single "Irish" cultural baseline.

### Italy

Italian corporate culture runs on a genuinely different structural logic: a pronounced, pyramid-shaped hierarchy where decision-making concentrates at the top, even as day-to-day interpersonal style stays warm and informal. Hofstede scores Italy at 50 on power distance — meaningfully higher than Ireland's 28 — reflecting real, if not extreme, comfort with hierarchical authority. Titles matter in a way that would feel excessive in Dublin: colleagues with a university degree and managerial role are addressed as "Dottore," and using last names until explicitly invited otherwise is treated as basic professional respect rather than stiffness.

What makes Italy distinctive is the coexistence of that formality with genuinely relaxed, discussion-heavy meetings and — per multiple expat guides — real tolerance for respectful disagreement with a superior, provided the point is well-reasoned. The long lunch functions similarly to Chile's, as real relationship-building time rather than downtime, and language skill is treated as a direct signal of commitment: expat guides note that missing Italian fluency can mean missing the informal channels — the bar conversation, the 45-minute coffee break — where a meaningful share of actual business relationship-building happens. Regional variation is real and material: northern Italian offices run more formal than southern ones, meaning "Italian work culture" understates real internal range.

### The Reckoning

The clearest contrast is in where authority visibly sits. In Ireland, hierarchy is real but deliberately made invisible in daily interaction — the org chart exists, but the culture actively works to minimize how much it's felt day to day. In Italy, hierarchy is structurally real and kept visible on purpose — through titles, through decision-making concentration, through a general expectation that seniority is acknowledged even amid warmth. Neither approach is more or less "hierarchical" in outcome so much as different in how openly the hierarchy is displayed.

The other divergence is what earns trust. In Ireland, trust is earned relatively quickly through direct, confident participation — showing up with a good idea in your second week is rewarded, not seen as presumptuous. In Italy, trust is earned more slowly and more relationally — through the long lunch, the language effort, the accumulated small gestures of respect for rank — and a newcomer who tries to move at Irish speed risks reading as presumptuous rather than confident.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone describing what it's like to work in Dublin noted the culture as international and easygoing, observing that colleagues from India and elsewhere fit in "pretty easily," and crediting the flat, humor-friendly office atmosphere as one of the more pleasant surprises of the move.*

> *Blind — A thread comparing Meta and Google's Dublin offices found sharply divided opinions: one contributor described their new-grad experience at Meta Dublin as genuinely difficult, while another called the engineering culture there "one of the best" they'd worked in — both agreed the outcome was heavily team-dependent rather than uniformly Irish.*

> *Expat.com forum ("Understanding corporate culture in Milan" guide) — A contributor described Milanese colleagues taking notably long lunch breaks specifically to "cultivate their address book," reframing what initially read as inefficiency into a genuine, and effective, relationship-building practice once the writer understood the local logic.*

> *Quora — Someone asked broadly what working in Dublin's corporate sector was like and got a consistent answer across several replies: rule-following, punctuality, and a certain perfectionism were more defining of the day-to-day office feel than the flat-hierarchy stereotype alone would suggest.*

> *A language-and-culture blog (Migaku) covering Italian work norms described the August shutdown as a genuine structural feature newcomers routinely underestimate — entire business relationships and projects can pause for weeks, and planning around it, rather than fighting it, was the advice repeated by several long-term expats the writer interviewed.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two moves, the practical adjustment is really about default assumptions. In Ireland, assume access and speak up faster than feels natural if you're coming from a more hierarchical culture — reticence reads as underengagement, not respect. In Italy, assume a longer runway before informality is earned, and treat the title, the last name, and the long lunch as investments rather than friction. Priya's line for a friend deciding between them: in Dublin, you'll be trusted with an opinion before you've earned it; in Milan, you'll earn the opinion's right to be heard — pack your patience accordingly.
