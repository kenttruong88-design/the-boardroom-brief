**[The Office Doesn't Close at Five: A Working Guide to Socializing on the Clock in Nigeria and Togo]**

**🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇹🇬 Togo**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Lagos, the Gallup-measured daily stress rate among employees is 55 percent — fifteen points above the global figure — and yet the thing that will actually wear you down in your first month is not the workload. It is the unofficial second job of staying socially current: the lunch contribution you forgot to make, the colleague's wedding you skipped, the after-work round you didn't realize was mandatory. Six hundred miles east in Lomé, the stakes are lower but the etiquette is stricter, built on tea, patience, and small talk that must run its full course before anyone mentions business. Both cities will tell you, cheerfully, that they take work-life balance seriously. Neither means it the way a recruiter in Toronto means it. Pack accordingly, and bring an appetite for either loud camaraderie or long pleasantries, because you will not be excused from either.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Contribute promptly when a colleague collects money for office lunch, a send-off, or a collection — hesitating reads as stingy, not frugal | Skip a colleague's wedding, burial, or naming ceremony without a genuine excuse and a gift — attendance is quietly tracked |
| Expect "owambe"-style send-offs and dress for them; bring small cash for spraying if you're pulled onto the dance floor | Eat lunch alone at your desk while colleagues gather communally — it reads as anti-social rather than efficient |
| Greet seniors formally each morning, even if the afternoon turns casual over jollof rice | Treat a Friday happy-hour invitation from your manager as optional — it rarely is |
| Learn who the office's informal lunch coordinator is and use them — every Nigerian office has one | Assume a loud, joking colleague is unserious about work; energy and output are not opposites here |
| Accept that long days are normal and plan your evening social life around a 7 or 8pm finish | Complain openly about long hours in your first weeks — it reads as a work-ethic flag before anyone knows you |

#### 🇹🇬 Togo

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive close to the stated time — Lomé's tolerance for lateness is real but narrower than Lagos's | Launch straight into business over lunch — let small talk and tea run their course first |
| Learn basic French greetings at minimum; corporate Togo runs on French, with Ewe and Kabiyè common outside the office | Expect frequent, spontaneous office parties — social events here are occasion-based, not weekly |
| Accept an invitation to a colleague's home or family event if one comes — it carries more weight than a work drinks night | Mistake formal, reserved manners for coldness; warmth shows up in small repeated gestures, not big nights out |
| Build a social life beyond any expat circle early — isolation sets in fast if you don't | Assume a slow "yes" from a manager means no — approvals just take longer, so follow up politely |
| Use the genuine freedom of Lomé's streets and festivals; locals are described as unusually welcoming to newcomers | Show up to a first meeting without a handshake and a proper greeting — it is noticed |

Nigeria's office culture does not so much blur the line between work and socializing as refuse to draw one. A Lagos-based start-up coach, interviewed by How We Made It In Africa, put it plainly: there is no separation between work and pleasure in the city, because nearly anyone you meet at dinner, a club, or a wedding is a potential business contact. That intensity shows up in the numbers. The ILO estimates Nigerians work roughly 39.5 hours a week on average, and Gallup's State of the Global Workplace finds only 17 percent of Nigerian employees engaged at work, against 55 percent reporting stress the previous day — well above both the Sub-Saharan African and global averages.

What keeps that pressure from curdling entirely is a dense layer of informal ritual. Great Place to Work Nigeria's wellbeing survey finds 53 percent of employees say colleagues show real concern and support for one another — the single highest-scoring measure in the report, even as formal work-life balance policies score lowest at 46 percent positive. In practice, this plays out through food: someone in nearly every office, per Zikoko's survey of workplace archetypes, takes on the unofficial role of collecting lunch orders and coordinating delivery, treated as genuinely essential labor rather than a chore. Team retreats — beach resorts, escape rooms, picnic games — are common enough that HR platforms publish planning guides for them, though attendance is rarely as optional as the invitation suggests. Hofstede's data helps explain the texture: Nigeria scores high on power distance, meaning deference to seniority is real, but the socializing that happens around and despite that hierarchy is loud, frequent, and largely non-negotiable.

The same Zikoko survey of workplace horror stories supplies the counterweight to all that warmth: a contributor described nightly team calls running until nine, including Sundays, and a manager who assigned a same-day task the one time she phoned in sick. Another described colleagues openly commenting on her lunch choices, to the point that she grew afraid to eat at her desk. The lesson for a new arrival is not that Nigerian office culture is uniquely harsh, but that its warmth and its intrusiveness share the same engine — nobody in a Lagos office treats you as a stranger, for better and for worse, and your lunch, your weekend, and your dating life are all considered fair conversational territory by week two.

Togo runs on a quieter clock. The country has no bespoke Hofstede culture score — it is typically folded into a broader West Africa regional estimate rather than measured on its own — but Safeguard Global's workplace guide describes a corporate culture that is formal, French-dominant, and still visibly hierarchical, where senior staff make the calls and relationships are built over tea and lunch rather than loud group outings. Meetings open with a courteous greeting and some small talk before anyone gets to the agenda, direct refusals are rare — expect "I will try" rather than "no" — and messaging apps handle the quick exchanges while anything formal still travels by email. The ILO's weekly-hours estimate for Togo, at roughly 37.9 hours, sits below Nigeria's, and the social rituals match the lower intensity: fewer office parties, more small talk before meetings, and approvals that take their time, so a polite follow-up is standard practice rather than an imposition.

A diplomatic spouse's account on Talesmag of life in Lomé captures the trade-off: expat morale could sour quickly from the slow pace and limited entertainment, and she advised newcomers to build an independent social circle fast rather than wait for the office to provide one. That said, the formality has limits. A longtime resident posting on an Expat.com Togo forum thread described surprising freedom and warmth after hours — safe late-night streets, welcoming locals, and village festivals that ran until dawn — suggesting the real social life in Togo happens off the org chart entirely, in a register the workday itself never quite reaches.

**The Reckoning**

The irony is that both countries lean on the same underlying currency — food, patience, and reciprocity — at wildly different speeds. Nigeria compresses socializing into the workday itself, making lunch contributions and after-work attendance closer to job requirements than perks; Togo spreads the same obligations out, demanding small talk before each transaction but rarely a long evening of it. A new arrival who reads Nigeria's volume as warmth and Togo's formality as distance will misjudge both: Nigerian camaraderie coexists with genuinely high stress and middling engagement, while Togolese reserve coexists with hospitality that, once off the clock, can be considerably more generous than the office ever lets on.

Hierarchy, too, survives the border crossing in a disguise. Nigerian offices are loud about seniority — formal morning greetings, visible deference — and then get informal fast once the hierarchy has been acknowledged. Togolese offices are quiet about seniority throughout, maintaining the same even, careful register in the boardroom and at the lunch table alike. Mistake either for the absence of rank and you will find out otherwise, usually at the worst possible moment: a rushed decision in Lagos, or an unreturned "I will try" in Lomé that was, in fact, a no.

### Conclusion

The honest trade is this: Nigeria will fold you into its social life whether you asked for it or not, at a pace that produces real camaraderie alongside real exhaustion — fitting for a workplace culture where only 17 percent of employees report feeling engaged, yet the majority still say colleagues have their back. Togo asks less of your calendar and more of your patience, rewarding anyone willing to sit through the small talk with a warmth that rarely shows up in the office itself, and the two extra hours a week Togolese employees are statistically not working, compared with their Nigerian counterparts, go a long way toward explaining the difference in temperature. If you want colleagues who become your social life by default, choose Lagos. If you want colleagues who stay colleagues until they decide, slowly, to become something more, choose Lomé.

Neither option is the relaxed, zero-stakes posting the brochure implies, and anyone choosing between them purely on the strength of a company's "vibrant culture" slide deck should ask what that deck is not showing. Either way, bring cash for the lunch collection and don't ask why the meeting started twenty minutes late — in one capital that's a scandal, in the other, Tuesday.
