# Thailand Grants You Six Days of Leave and Thirteen Holidays; Australia Grants You Four Weeks and Guilt

🇹🇭 Thailand · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Australia hands every full-time employee four weeks of paid annual leave from the first day of the job, and its workers have responded by banking roughly sixteen unused days each. Thailand offers a statutory six days after a full year, and its workers have responded by feeling guilty about using them. Both countries have therefore solved the problem of rest by arranging for nobody to take it. The method differs, as does the paperwork.

## Do's & Don'ts

**Thailand**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read your contract for annual leave, since the legal minimum of six days is often exceeded in practice | Assume the statutory minimum is what your employer actually offers |
| Count the paid public holidays, which are at least 13 a year | Plan trips around a holiday calendar you have not checked, as extra days are added around Songkran and New Year |
| Use paid sick leave, which the law caps at 30 days a year, and bring a medical certificate if you are off for more than three days | Skip the certificate and then dispute the pay |
| Agree leave dates with your manager early and politely, because the employer fixes them | Announce your dates in the manner of a person with rights |
| Ask whether unused days carry forward, because the courts have held that bans on carry-forward violate the law | Let days lapse quietly |

**Australia**

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take your four weeks, which accrue from day one and roll over year to year | Sit on a balance until the employer is entitled to direct you to take it |
| Check whether your award or contract includes a 17.5 percent leave loading, because it is not a national entitlement | Assume the loading is automatic |
| Use sick and carer's leave, with 10 days a year that accrue without expiry | Apologise for a sick day as though it were a confession |
| Ask about long service leave, which in most states is 8.67 weeks after 10 years | Forget that the rules vary by state |
| Decline a request to work a public holiday if the request is unreasonable | Assume you must say yes |

## Thailand

The Thai law is plain and economical. According to Tilleke & Gibbins, an employee who has worked continuously for one year is entitled to not less than six working days of annual leave under the Labour Protection Act. Rivermate adds that market practice often runs to 10 to 15 days, that employers must provide at least 13 paid traditional holidays, and that the gazetted calendar can reach 16 to 19 days depending on the year. Sick leave is paid for up to 30 days a year, with a medical certificate for absences beyond three days. The employer fixes the leave dates and does not need your consent, though the courts have held that rules barring carry-forward violate the law.

The ground-level reality is more complicated. The Nation Thailand, citing the ILO, reported in October 2025 that Thailand has the third longest working hours in the world, with 46.7 percent of workers exceeding 48 hours a week. In the same report, 80 percent of employees said they deserve more leave but avoid taking it for fear of burdening their team, 74 percent had cancelled a holiday because of workload, and between 35 and 48 percent admitted to working while ill. The report does not name the survey behind the leave figures, so treat them as indicative. Hofstede Insights offers a useful translation: Thailand scores 64 for power distance and 20 for individualism, against Australia's 36 and 90, which suggests that the group tends to outrank the individual in the decision to leave the office.

Reform is pending, which is the traditional status of Thai labour reform. The Thaiger reported that on 24 September 2025 the House passed at first reading a bill that would give employees 10 days of annual leave after 120 days of work and cap the working week at 40 hours. A companion bill would add family care leave and menstrual leave, according to Tilleke & Gibbins, with up to 15 working days of family caregiving leave. Both went to special committees and had not become law at the time of the reports, so the six-day minimum remains the number to plan around. Prospective arrivals should also note that a Thai employer who fails to fix annual leave can be fined and must pay holiday wages for the unused days, according to Tilleke & Gibbins, so the days are more enforceable than they are fashionable.

## Australia

Australia states its rules generously and in writing. The Fair Work Ombudsman confirms that full-time and part-time employees get four weeks of paid annual leave, that it accrues from the first day including during probation, and that unused leave carries over. Boundless adds that personal and carer's leave is 10 days a year and does not expire, that long service leave is governed by state law and is typically 8.67 weeks after 10 years in most states, and that leave loading of 17.5 percent applies only if an award, agreement or contract provides it. Employers can generally direct leave when a balance is excessive, which Boundless puts at more than eight weeks.

Entitlement and practice, however, travel separately. The Australian HR Institute, reporting the ELMO Employee Sentiment Index for the third quarter of 2024, found that the average employee carried 15.7 days of accrued annual leave and that 22 percent had four weeks or more banked. The same report put the national total at 160 million days and noted that nearly half of Australian workers report feeling burned out. Reasons offered for hoarding include cost-of-living pressure, a workplace culture in which long hours are admired, short staffing, and the difficulty of switching off. Australia has built the world's most carefully drafted holiday and then queued outside it.

Still, the system protects the person who uses it. A public holiday that falls during annual leave does not reduce your balance, according to Boundless, and an employer asking you to work on one must make a genuine request that you can refuse if it is unreasonable. Intentional underpayment of leave became a criminal offence from 1 January 2025. Thai enforcement is described by one forum participant as weak in rural areas. Australian enforcement is described by a federal statute.

## The Reckoning

On paper this is not close. Australia offers 20 days of annual leave and 10 of personal leave with a statutory enforcement apparatus. Thailand offers six days of annual leave, a 30-day sick-leave allowance and at least 13 public holidays, with enforcement that varies with the employer. Even counting generously, the Thai worker has fewer weeks to spend and less recourse if they are not allowed to spend them. The possible reforms to 10 days are welcome, though a bill in committee is a promise rather than an entitlement.

But the paper tells less than half of it. In both countries a large share of workers fail to use what they have, and for similar reasons: a workload that nobody else will cover and a suspicion that absence is read as disloyalty. The difference is in the consequences. An Australian who hoards leave eventually has a liability on the employer's books and a legal route to cash it out or be directed to take it. A Thai worker who hoards leave has, at best, a carry-forward claim and a relationship to protect.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — paraphrased experience: Employees discussing an Australian software company's work-from-anywhere policy said that time abroad is generally excluded from vacation days, that enforcement depends heavily on the manager, and that a long stay in Thailand would draw far more scrutiny than a month in the mountains near Seattle.*

> *Expat.com — paraphrased experience: A foreigner asking about hours and leave in Thailand got no firm answer on whether expatriates and locals are treated differently. Others said terms are mostly set by the individual contract, that private schools often ask for more hours, and that enforcement of labour law is weak, particularly outside the cities.*

> *Yahoo Finance Australia — paraphrased experience: A Seattle-born photographer living in Perth said her biggest culture shock was that taking a sick day was a non-event, whereas back in the United States she felt guilty about leave even when it was paid. She added that hospitality, healthcare and teaching workers still have it hardest.*

> *The Nation Thailand — paraphrased experience: A Bangkok-based expatriate columnist argued that foreigners who grumble about Thai public holidays should count their own, noting that under full compliance with the labour law Thai employees get about 74 days off a year, and that expatriates tend to skip work on their home-country holidays as well.*

## Conclusion

If the plan is to maximise paid time away, Australia is the plain winner, with four weeks, rolling balances, 10 days of personal leave and a regulator that answers the phone. Negotiate the loading, learn the state's long service rules, and take the leave before the balance starts to look like a savings plan. If you accept Thailand, negotiate leave in the contract rather than relying on the six-day floor, count the public holidays, and use the paid sick leave without apology.

Before accepting either offer, ask the employer two plain questions: how many days did the last person in this role actually take, and what happens to the rest? The answer is more reliable than any handbook, and a pause before replying is itself information. In both places, the gap between the policy and the person is cultural, and no statute fixes that. You may find that the hardest part of relocating is not obtaining the days off, but being the first person in the office to take them.
