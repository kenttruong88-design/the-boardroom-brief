# Pakistan Will Judge You by How Formal You Look. The UAE Will Judge You by How Formal You Look, Then Fine You If You Get It Wrong.

**🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Karachi office might let you show up in jeans and a polo if your boss is relaxed about it, provided you can produce a suit within the hour a client meeting is announced. A Dubai office operates on the same basic logic, except the dress code is partly a matter of municipal decency law, and "within the hour" is replaced by "before you leave the house." Both countries take formality seriously. Only one of them can write you a fine for forgetting.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep a suit at the office for client meetings, even if daily wear is casual | Assume your department's casual norm applies company-wide |
| Dress conservatively in public-facing roles — shalwar kameez or a suit both read as professional | Wear shorts or sleeveless tops to client-facing work events |
| Expect formality to escalate sharply the more senior the meeting | Show up underdressed to a meeting with someone above your boss's level |
| Match your dress to your city — Karachi and Lahore skew more fashion-forward than smaller cities | Assume Islamabad's more conservative norms apply in Karachi, or vice versa |
| Budget time to change clothes if a meeting gets added last minute | Be caught in casualwear when a client meeting is called with no notice |

#### 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep shoulders, knees, and cleavage covered in both office and public settings | Wear anything sleeveless or above-the-knee to the office |
| Wear a dark, well-tailored suit for formal meetings | Treat "business casual" as an invitation to dress down significantly |
| Dress more conservatively in Abu Dhabi and the Northern Emirates than in Dubai | Assume Dubai's relatively relaxed norms apply nationwide |
| Dress more modestly during Ramadan, even if not fasting yourself | Ignore seasonal shifts in formality expectations around Ramadan |

### Pakistan

Pakistani office dress code is genuinely negotiable at the department level and genuinely non-negotiable the moment a client or a more senior executive enters the room. One contributor to the Foreign Service community's Tales Mag Real Post Report summed up the daily reality bluntly: "whatever you work out with your boss — some people wear jeans and a polo, some wear suits" — but the same source immediately adds that "coat and tie are often required for meetings," and that newcomers should pack several regardless of how casual their actual desk job looks day to day. In public-facing or diplomatic contexts the bar rises further still, with guidance to dress "more conservatively and more formally than Americans" and women advised to follow a shoulder-to-ankle rule with a shawl on hand even over long sleeves.

What formality signals in Pakistan goes beyond fabric. A widely read Pakistaniat essay on corporate culture argues that visible presence — staying late, being seen at your desk after hours — functions as its own kind of uniform, describing bosses who "like to sit late in the offices just to show how efficient and effective they" are, with subordinates matching the hours to avoid standing out. The essay's own framing is severe — "I want to call it exploitation... like a bullock in fields" — but the underlying point about formality as visible performance, not just clothing, holds regardless of how one feels about the hours themselves.

### United Arab Emirates

In the UAE, dress code formality is backed by actual public decency standards, not just office custom. Guidance from UAE business-culture resources is specific down to the garment: suits in navy, charcoal, or black for men, with light-colored shirts, ties expected in formal settings; knee-length or longer dresses and sleeved blouses for women, shoulders and cleavage covered as a baseline rather than an aspiration. Tales Mag's own Dubai entries track this consistently across more than a decade of resident reports — "business casual at the office, and formal for meetings" in one, "suits and ties at the office" in another — with the throughline that business attire is the default assumption unless you're explicitly told otherwise.

The formality also isn't uniform across the country, which trips up people who learn Dubai's relatively relaxed reputation and assume it travels. Abu Dhabi runs more conservative and more formal by default, suits and ties expected without exception; the Northern Emirates lean more traditional still. And formality tightens seasonally — dress gets more modest during Ramadan even for employees who aren't observing the fast, a courtesy expectation rather than a legal one, but a real one nonetheless.

### The Reckoning

The two systems converge on an unexpected point: in both countries, national dress is a mark of respect rather than a requirement for outsiders. Shalwar kameez functions in Pakistan the way a kandura or abaya functions for a non-Emirati in the UAE — wearing it signals goodwill at a formal occasion, but nobody expects it of a foreign employee on an ordinary Tuesday. Where the two diverge is enforcement: Pakistan's formality is social, policed by hierarchy and context, while the UAE's is partly civic, backed by public decency norms that apply in malls and streets as much as in meetings. Hofstede Insights data tracks the underlying hierarchy gap too — UAE's published power-distance score of 90 sits well above Pakistan's 55, meaning the UAE's formality is less about personal judgment and more about an expectation that rank should be visibly, unmistakably legible at all times.

> *Pakistaniat — A Pakistani essayist's blunt framing of corporate visibility culture doubled as an unintentional dress-code insight: because staying late and "looking the part" of a dedicated employee carries real social weight, formality in Pakistani offices often has less to do with the clothes themselves than with being seen to take the job seriously at all.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Pakistan, the real lesson isn't about the clothes you own — it's about having a formal option ready at all times, because the gap between a relaxed Tuesday and a suit-required client meeting can close with almost no warning. If you're moving to the UAE, the lesson is that "business casual" is doing far less work than you'd expect, and that the decency standard you'll be held to in the office is the same one posted on the wall of the mall downstairs. Pack more formalwear than either country's day-to-day reputation would suggest you need, and you'll be fine in both.
