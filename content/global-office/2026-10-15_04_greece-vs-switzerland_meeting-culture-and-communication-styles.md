**Fifteen Minutes Late vs. Fifteen Minutes Early: Greece and Switzerland Walk Into a Meeting**

**🇬🇷 Greece · 🇨🇭 Switzerland**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Athens, a 9:00am meeting starting at 9:20 is not a delay, it's a rounding error. In Zurich, arriving at 9:00 sharp and finding the agenda already distributed, read, and annotated is not overachievement, it's Tuesday. Both countries will tell you their way makes meetings more productive. Both are, infuriatingly, correct.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇷 Greece

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build 15-30 minutes of flex time into any meeting's start — arriving exactly on time is fine, but don't expect your counterpart to match it | Mistake overlapping speech and interruptions for rudeness; talking over each other is normal engagement, not a breakdown in order |
| Expect and welcome personal small talk (family, weekend, your opinion of the food) before business starts | Rush straight into the agenda; skipping the relationship-building step reads as cold, not efficient |
| Identify the most senior person in the room beforehand — they typically drive the real decision | Expect a strict written agenda to be followed to the letter; informal meetings especially tend to wander, productively |
| Treat a signed written contract as genuinely binding and expect it to be honoured strictly | Assume a fast "yes" in the room means a fast decision; Greece's very high uncertainty avoidance (100 on Hofstede's scale) means real decisions get examined from every angle before they're final |
| Enjoy the extended business lunch — it's where a meaningful share of the actual relationship-building happens | Raise sensitive historical or political topics (the debt crisis, Cyprus, Greek-Turkish relations) unless your host brings them up first |

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive exactly on time, not fashionably early or even two minutes late — punctuality is treated as a basic competence signal | Interrupt a colleague mid-sentence; wait for a pause, even an uncomfortably long one |
| Come with a full agenda, data, and documentation prepared in advance | Improvise, exaggerate, or lean on superlatives in a pitch; understated, evidence-based framing lands far better |
| Use surnames with formal titles (Herr/Frau, Monsieur/Madame) until a colleague explicitly invites first names | Assume silence after you make a point means disagreement or confusion — long pauses are normal, reflective, not awkward |
| Treat an unprompted compliment as rare and meaningful; a lack of visible praise doesn't mean a lack of approval | Send a casual first-name email greeting to someone you haven't met in person; formal greetings are the default until a face-to-face meeting happens |
| Expect decisions to move through a slow, methodical, multi-layer process — and plan your timeline around that reality | Push for an immediate answer; being rushed is read as a red flag, not urgency |

**Greece: The Agenda Is a Suggestion, the Relationship Is the Point**

Hofstede Insights scores Greece at a striking 100 on uncertainty avoidance — tied for the highest in the world — a number that sounds like it should produce rigid, rule-bound meetings but in practice produces the opposite: a deep need to examine every angle of a decision before committing to it, which slows things down and makes formal agendas feel almost beside the point. BusinessCulture.org's etiquette guide confirms the pattern directly: informal meetings often have no fixed agenda at all, overlapping speech and frequent interruptions are completely normal, and open debate is treated as "stimulating and essential for the correct decisions to be taken" rather than as a loss of control.

What holds the room together instead is hierarchy and relationship. The most senior person present tends to dominate discussion and own the real decision, personal questions are a genuine prerequisite to doing business (not small talk filler), and once a written contract is finally signed, it is expected to be followed strictly — the informality is front-loaded, not permanent. Marco, an international worker who moved to Athens for an office job, told Yobbers.com that starting in an office specifically gave him "a team, a routine and people to ask questions to," and that daily coffee with colleagues — an unstructured, recurring social ritual — did more to make the move feel permanent than any formal onboarding process did.

**Switzerland: The Agenda Is the Point, and So Is the Silence**

Switzerland's meeting culture reads almost like Greece's photographic negative. Hofstede's figures put Switzerland's long-term orientation at 74 (versus Greece's 45) alongside moderate uncertainty avoidance (58) — a profile that favours methodical process over passionate improvisation. iamexpat.ch's rundown of Swiss-versus-American work culture captures the texture precisely: long pauses in meetings are read as thoughtful reflection rather than awkward silence, interruptions are rare, and feedback is deliberately understated — praise may simply never arrive, with added responsibility serving as the real signal of approval instead.

SwissInfo.ch's reporting on foreigners adjusting to Swiss offices adds the formality layer: Margaret Oertig-Davidson, a Scottish-born consultant based in Basel, noted that "it is unusual for someone to give an immediate reaction to something without the full information," which can frustrate newcomers who treat their own priorities as automatically urgent. Valeria Coloiera Harlay, an Argentina-born consultant in Vaud, described a cultural emphasis on doing things "properly" where "process is as important as the product." One anonymous reader learned the formality rule the hard way, assuming a first-name email greeting would be fine, only to be corrected by their boss — a small, specific lesson in just how long formal address is expected to persist before an actual in-person meeting unlocks first names.

Language adds a layer most comparisons skip. Switzerland runs on three working languages (German, French, Italian) plus English in many international firms, and Cyborlink's business etiquette notes that communication style actually shifts by region — German-speaking Switzerland reads as more directly factual, French-speaking Switzerland as somewhat softer and more diplomatic — meaning "Swiss meeting culture" is really at least two adjacent dialects of the same underlying formality. Greece is linguistically simpler (Greek, with widely spoken English in business contexts) but BusinessCulture.org's etiquette guide flags a different translation risk: confirming your message actually landed matters more than confirming an interpreter is present, since fluent English can still carry very different assumptions about directness underneath it.

**The Reckoning**

The genuinely funny part is that both cultures think the other one is being inefficient. A Swiss colleague watching a Greek meeting sees unstructured chaos — no agenda, people talking over each other, decisions stretching across multiple extended lunches. A Greek colleague watching a Swiss meeting sees something closer to theatre: everyone silently reading a pre-circulated document, nobody interrupting, approval expressed only through the gift of more work. Both are optimising for the same thing — a decision everyone can actually live with — just through opposite tempos.

The real risk for a newcomer isn't picking the "wrong" style, it's applying your home country's tempo to the wrong room: pushing a Swiss team for an immediate answer reads as a reliability problem, and showing up to a Greek meeting with a rigid, uninterruptible agenda reads as someone who skipped the part where you're supposed to actually get to know the room first.

### Conclusion

If you're deciding which meeting culture to bet your sanity on, ask yourself which kind of slowness you can tolerate better: Greece's slowness is social — you'll spend real time on relationship and debate before anything closes, but once it closes, it holds. Switzerland's slowness is procedural — you'll spend real time on documentation and sequential sign-off, but once the process completes, nobody revisits it either.

My honest advice to a friend: if you thrive on reading a room and improvising, Greece will reward you and Switzerland will quietly exhaust you. If you want to know exactly what's expected of you before you walk in, Switzerland will reward you and Greece will feel like showing up to a play without a script. Neither meeting style is broken. You're just not used to how either one measures "productive" yet.
