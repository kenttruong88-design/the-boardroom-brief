**Educated, Employed, and Still Outnumbered: Gender at Work in Sri Lanka and Canada**

**🇱🇰 Sri Lanka · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

More than 60% of Sri Lankan university graduates are women, and yet only 34.6% of the workforce is female and barely 8.5% of boardroom seats belong to them — a gap one Sri Lankan governance advocate summarised in nine blunt words: "We are educating women, but we are not putting them to work." Canada, for its part, has mostly solved the putting-to-work part. The paying-fairly part remains, politely, a work in progress.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇱🇰 Sri Lanka

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research a prospective employer's actual track record on promoting women, not just its stated policy | Assume a workplace that is majority-female in headcount is majority-female in power — it rarely is |
| Document any inappropriate conduct in writing and in real time, given documented institutional reluctance to act | Expect HR to automatically act as a neutral or protective party; several documented cases show the opposite |
| Look specifically at sales and senior-management tracks when evaluating real mobility, not just HR/marketing/comms hiring | Take a title or department assignment as gender-neutral; role segregation by gender is well documented here |
| Seek out organisations publicly participating in board-readiness or gender-parity initiatives | Assume maternity or travel requirements won't factor into a hiring or promotion decision, even unofficially |
| Ask directly about flexible-work and childcare support before accepting a role, since it's inconsistent across employers | Underestimate how much career attrition happens quietly, through resignation, rather than through visible firing |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for transparent, level-by-level compensation bands before negotiating, since the gap widens specifically at the management and C-suite tiers | Assume a stated commitment to pay equity means the gap has actually closed; it hasn't, even in tech |
| Negotiate bonus and equity components explicitly, not just base salary | Treat high reported job satisfaction as proof compensation is fair — researchers explicitly warn against this inference |
| Benchmark your pay against public sector-wide reports if you suspect a gap | Assume the wage gap is uniform; racialized and Indigenous women face a measurably larger one than the national average |
| Use formal HR and legal channels; Canadian workplaces generally have more developed, tested complaint infrastructure | Assume "diversity hiring" numbers alone indicate real progress on compensation or promotion equity |
| Look at actual board and executive composition at a prospective employer, not just its stated values statement | Expect every province or sector to have moved at the same pace; disparities vary considerably |

### Sri Lanka

The architecture of the problem in Sri Lanka has been described, in print, as a "neo-feudal" corporate system — one where women cluster heavily in administrative and support roles (the Personal Assistant position remains almost entirely female-staffed) and in marketing, HR, and communications, while sales leadership and senior management stay overwhelmingly male, framed locally as requiring an aggressive competitiveness considered unsuitable for women. The country's Global Gender Gap ranking fell 14 places to 116th out of 156 countries in the most recent comparative assessment, even as its women out-educate its men: well over 60% of university graduates are female.

CA Sri Lanka, the national accounting body, has tried to close the gap directly with a Board-Ready Directory of qualified female chartered accountants for audit-committee roles, and its own governance council has moved from zero women to three of sixteen seats. The economic case is not subtle — companies with more women on boards show a 36% better return on equity, and the IMF estimates Sri Lanka could grow GDP by up to 20% through full gender parity — yet documented reporting from inside corporate Sri Lanka describes women in management facing disproportionate pressure to outperform male peers simply to be seen as credible, alongside widespread, often unaddressed, harassment where HR has in some documented cases actively discouraged women from speaking up to protect a perpetrator's standing rather than the complainant's safety.

### Canada

Canada's version of this problem is quieter and better-measured, which is its own kind of tell. Statistics Canada's own data shows real, if slow, progress: women aged 15 and over earned 88 cents for every dollar men earned in 2025, up from 82 cents in 1997, with women in their prime working years (25-54) at 89 cents against 81 cents three decades earlier. But that national average flattens a sharper story underneath it — racialized women earn roughly 78 cents on the same dollar, and Indigenous women about 79 cents, gaps considerably wider than the headline number suggests.

Tech, the sector that likes to describe itself as the most meritocratic, tells an even blunter version. A recent report on Canadian tech leadership found women earning roughly 78 cents to men's dollar overall — a 22% gap in median base salary — widening to 27% specifically at the lead-and-manager level, with men also receiving bonuses at a higher rate (67% versus 53%) and larger median bonus amounts ($40,000 versus $25,000). Women hold only about 25% of Canadian tech jobs in the first place. The report's own researchers added a pointed methodological warning worth repeating to any employer citing survey satisfaction as proof of fairness: "satisfaction scores don't absolve you of inequity." Hofstede's research scores Canada at 80 on individualism, among the highest in the world, which tracks with a culture that frames the fix as an individual negotiation — ask for the raise, benchmark your own pay — rather than, as in Sri Lanka, a structural, institution-level reform project; Sri Lanka itself was never part of Hofstede's original research sample, so no comparable country-level score exists for it.

### The Reckoning

The uncomfortable symmetry here is that both countries have, in their own register, figured out how to measure the problem precisely without actually closing it. Sri Lanka can tell you its boardroom representation to one decimal point (8.5%) and still can't get that number past single digits; Canada can tell you exactly how much smaller a woman's bonus is, broken out by seniority level and equity participation, and the gap, in the report's own words, "never hits zero" at any level measured.

The practical difference for someone arriving as an employee is in what's broken: in Sri Lanka, hiring and promotion themselves are the bottleneck, shaped by assumptions about marriage, pregnancy, and travel before a woman is even considered for the role; in Canada, the bottleneck has largely moved downstream, into compensation and executive advancement after a woman is already in the building and doing the job. One is a problem of access. The other is a problem of arithmetic that nobody has been willing to actually correct.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Groundviews — A woman recounting her own experience in a Sri Lankan corporate role described sustained harassment from a supervisor that institutional HR chose to manage by protecting his reputation rather than hers, ultimately leading to her resignation; she noted the particular irony that this happened in a workplace where women made up the majority of staff but held almost none of the actual decision-making power.*

> *The Morning (Sri Lanka) — Aruni Rajakarier, speaking about CA Sri Lanka's own gender-parity push, offered the line that most efficiently diagnoses the whole system: "We are educating women, but we are not putting them to work" — a direct rebuttal to the idea that an education gap, rather than a structural one, is what's holding women back.*

> *Blind — In a thread debating whether aggregate pay-gap statistics match individual experience, one tech worker described being paid less than a male colleague a full level below them at a company with an entirely female HR department, while another argued that women are frequently "held to higher hiring standards than men for the same role" — a complaint that complicates any employer's claim that fair pay is simply a pipeline problem.*

> *Groundviews — Reporting on corporate Sri Lanka's internal culture noted that senior sales roles are specifically gatekept as unsuitable for women on the stated grounds that they require an "aggressive" competitiveness — a justification the same reporting notes has no basis in the actual sales performance data from companies that have broken the pattern.*

### Conclusion

If you're a woman weighing these two postings, the honest framing is that you're choosing which failure mode you'd rather manage. Sri Lanka will make the ceiling visible early — in hiring, in role assignment, in whose name ends up on the sales target — which is clarifying, if bleak. Canada will let you get further before the ceiling appears, dressed in the more palatable language of bonus pools and equity grants, which can make it easier to miss until you're the one doing the arithmetic on your own offer letter.

Ask for the number either way. Sri Lanka's system is unlikely to volunteer it, and Canada's system, for all its spreadsheets, has shown it will happily let you not ask.
