# Croatia Has a Boss; Austria Has a Boss Who Also Has a Doctorate

🇭🇷 Croatia · 🇦🇹 Austria

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Hofstede Insights scores Austria at 11 on power distance, one of the lowest numbers in the world, and Croatia at 73. A 62-point gap is the sort of difference that usually divides continents, and here it divides two countries whose capitals are about four hours apart by train. The surprise is that Austria, with its famously flat score, is also a country where you are expected to address a colleague as Doctor. Hierarchy, it turns out, is a measure of what people say and not of what people call each other.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Croatia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect major decisions to be made at the top, and relay them as though you were consulted | Assume your opinion will be sought in the meeting |
| Correct someone in private, starting with a compliment before the specific behaviour | Criticise a colleague or subordinate in public |
| Read feedback in context; it is often indirect | Take a lack of complaint as approval |
| Budget time for personal relationships; they drive workplace dynamics | Treat the first meeting as a purely transactional one |
| Check whether you are working in a tech firm or startup, where things are flatter | Assume the same rules apply in every sector |

### In Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Open with Herr or Frau plus the title, such as Magister or Doktor, until you are invited to use first names | Use first names on the first email |
| Ask early whether the company has an informal "du" policy | Assume that "du" is universal because the startup downstairs uses it |
| Expect senior people to hold the final say while leaving you latitude on method | Wait for instructions on how to do the work |
| Leave when your hours end; working late is read as poor time management | Treat overtime as a sign of loyalty |
| Write formal, structured emails | Send a casual message to a senior contact |

## Croatia

The reasonably consistent account of Croatian management is that authority sits at the top and politely stays there. CommiSceo Global's management guide calls Croatia a fairly hierarchical culture in which decisions are often made by the person in charge, advises against criticising staff in public, and notes that nepotism has been common but is becoming less acceptable. Babel Group, a consultancy, is slightly kinder, describing Croatia as moderately hierarchical, flatter in technology and startups, and given to indirect feedback. The two can both be right: a Zagreb software firm and a municipal office will present different bosses.

Hofstede's figures give Croatia a power distance of 73 and uncertainty avoidance of 80, which suggests a workplace that prefers clear rules and clear authority and is mildly alarmed by improvisation. Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2025 supplies the morale: 7 percent of Croatian employees are engaged, 77 percent are not engaged and 15 percent are actively disengaged. The European average is 12 percent engaged and the global average is 20 percent. Gallup does not attribute this to managers, and we shall not either, though a workforce that mostly does what it is told is not obliged to enjoy it.

There is also a note on working hours. Eurofound's European Working Conditions Survey for 2024 reports that 29 percent of Croatian workers say they work six or seven days a week, which gives the top-down model a practical consequence. The boss decides and the employee absorbs the schedule. A Croatian commenter in a 2024 Reddit thread described colleagues as friendly and social, but also as makers of promises that do not always arrive, and as treating outsiders as outside "the tribe". That is one voice, but it matches the guides: relationships do the work that formal process does elsewhere, and a newcomer has to be admitted before the system opens.

## Austria

Austria is where the numbers and the experience start to disagree. Eurofound's 2024 survey finds that in Austria, teams decide the division of tasks in 75 percent or more of cases, against an EU average of 64 percent. More than 60 percent of staff in teamwork set their own timetable, against 49 percent across the EU, and team members choose the team head in 37 percent or more of cases, against 27 percent. A French freelance writer who spent nearly eight years in Vienna told Welcome to the Jungle that she called her boss by his first name, that employees were credited for ideas whatever their seniority, and that managers set the goals and left the "how" to staff. She was sent to a European working group within six months.

Then there is Expatica's account of the same country: structure that "remains traditional", a "pervading hierarchy", senior management that makes most of the decisions and academic titles that carry real weight. You use Frau or Herr and the title until someone invites you to stop. Expatarrivals adds that senior managers can be less open to group decision-making and may give direct orders, while rarely being tyrannical. Working late, Expatica says, is read as poor time management and not as ambition, which will be news to anyone who arrived from a consultancy.

These accounts fit together once you separate formality from power. Austrian hierarchy is a set of manners layered over a system that gives people considerable independence. You will be addressed correctly, you will be given a task and you will be left to do it, and nobody will feel the need to supervise you into excellence. Austria's uncertainty avoidance score of 70, against Croatia's 80, suggests that both countries like rules, but Austria likes them written down and then left alone.

The practical consequences differ in a few small ways. CommiSceo notes that older Croatian staff may blame external factors rather than admit mistakes, which means a manager new to the country should expect problems to be reported late and framed as circumstances, and should ask open questions in private. In Austria, a coworking provider's guide to local office culture says authority is exercised with quiet professionalism, that silence in meetings may signal disagreement and that decision-making is slower but thorough. Qualifications count in both places, but in Austria they are printed on the door: if you hold a degree, expect it to be used, and if you do not, expect colleagues to notice who does.

## The Reckoning

If you want to know which country has the flatter workplace, the answer is Austria by a wide margin on paper and a narrower one in the corridor. Croatia concentrates decisions at the top, makes feedback indirect and treats trust as something earned through personal contact. Austria spreads responsibility across the team, lets employees organise their own time and surrounds all of it with formal address and academic titles. A Croatian arriving in Vienna may be startled by how much autonomy comes with so much ceremony; an Austrian arriving in Zagreb may be startled by how little of either is on offer unless someone senior has already said yes.

The sector matters more than the border in both places. A startup in Zagreb and a research employer in Vienna will both feel flatter than the surveys, while a traditional Austrian firm and a Croatian public body will feel exactly as formal as the guidebooks warn. Ask, before accepting an offer, who makes decisions, how feedback is given and what the staff call the director. The answer is a more reliable guide than a national average.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (r/AskEurope, read via a mirror) — a Croatian commenter describes Austrian colleagues as arrogant, quick to give unrequested advice and hard to understand because of the dialect. The same commenter says Croatian colleagues are friendly and social but make promises they do not keep, and treat outsiders as outside "the tribe".*

> *Welcome to the Jungle — a French freelance writer recalls nearly eight years in Vienna. She found hierarchy far looser than in France, called her boss by his first name, was credited for ideas whatever her seniority, and was sent to a European working group within six months.*

> *ExpatExchange (expat Q&A, general rather than workplace-specific) — an expat in Zagreb names the language barrier, slow bureaucracy, a looser attitude to punctuality and Croatians who seem reserved with strangers as the hardest parts of settling in.*

## Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two, ask what kind of independence you want. Austria offers the room to run your own work in exchange for good manners, correct titles and a refusal to look overworked. Croatia offers warmth and personal loyalty once you have been accepted, in exchange for decisions you may not be consulted on and feedback you may have to decode.

Read the room in both. Whatever the organisation chart says, the person who matters is the one whose door is open when you have a problem, and in both countries that is a matter of manners, not rank. Learn the title first and the first name later.
