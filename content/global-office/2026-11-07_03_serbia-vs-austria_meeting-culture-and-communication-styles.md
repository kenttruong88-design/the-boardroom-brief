**Serbia Will Tell You Exactly What It Thinks of Your Proposal; Austria Will Schedule a Follow-Up to Discuss It**

🇷🇸 Serbia · 🇦🇹 Austria

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Fly two hours north from Belgrade to Vienna and you cross one of the sharpest meeting-culture borders in Europe: a country where, according to Hofstede Insights' country-comparison data, power distance is among the highest on the continent, into one where it is among the lowest anywhere on earth. Which is a formal way of saying that in one capital your boss's opinion is a law of physics, and in the other your boss is, technically, just a colleague with a better parking spot who will still expect you to challenge his spreadsheet politely, on the record, before lunch. Both countries will give you a calendar invite. Only one of them means it literally.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇷🇸 Serbia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the coffee or rakija before the agenda starts — it is the actual opening item | Pull out your laptop and start the slide deck cold |
| Treat the loudest, most animated voice in the room as engaged, not angry | Mistake volume and interruption for breakdown in negotiations |
| Assume the real decision gets made in a side conversation after the meeting | Expect the person with the fanciest title to necessarily be the one who signs off |
| Build the relationship before you push for the signature | Email a contract to someone you've never had coffee with |
| Expect the meeting to run 20–40 minutes past its slot, and plan your day accordingly | Book back-to-back meetings on a Belgrade afternoon |
| Clarify action items in writing afterward, since verbal commitment is warmer than it is binding | Leave a meeting assuming a verbal "yes" is a signed deliverable date |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive five minutes early, not five minutes late, not exactly on time | Wander in at the scheduled start time expecting that to count as punctual |
| Use "Sie" and the person's academic title (Dr., Mag., Ing.) until told otherwise | Default to first names and "du" because it worked at your last job in Austin |
| Bring a structured agenda and circulate it beforehand | Propose "let's just brainstorm live" as an opening move |
| Expect the meeting to end exactly when scheduled, and leave when it does | Schedule a "quick follow-up call" assuming anyone will treat it as quick |
| Take flat, understated criticism of your work at face value, not as a prelude to something worse | Read a monotone objection as a sign the relationship is in trouble |
| Respect the formal hierarchy in the room while knowing consensus is often built quietly beforehand | Assume the most senior person present is the one who actually decided the outcome |

Serbia's working culture is, by the numbers, considerably more intense than its laid-back café-terrace reputation suggests. Eurostat's labour force data for late 2025 put the average Serbian working week at 40.9 hours, 5.3 hours above the EU average of 35.6, trailing only Turkey in the wider European comparison. That extra time, however, is not obviously spent on process. Meetings in Serbia tend to function as a parallel, more honest channel to the org chart: hierarchy exists on paper, but influence travels through personal relationships, family connections, and who you had coffee with last week, a dynamic widely documented in cross-cultural literature on Southeast European business behavior. Hofstede's power-distance score for Serbia sits among the highest in Europe, yet that formal deference coexists with a meeting room that is, in practice, loud, interruption-tolerant, and more persuadable by charm than by PowerPoint.

Engagement data complicates the stereotype further. Gallup's 2025 State of the Global Workplace reports that 15% of Serbian employees are engaged at work, above both the European regional average of 12% and most neighbouring economies, while 31% report significant daily stress, below the European average of 39%. Read generously, Serbian workplaces have found a way to combine long formal hours with comparatively lower reported burnout, possibly because so much of the actual work of persuasion happens outside the meeting itself, over food, where nobody is billing hours.

That informality has a cost, and it falls disproportionately on the newly arrived. A meeting called for 10 a.m. is a statement of intent rather than a contract, the agenda is a suggestion rather than a sequence, and the person who speaks last and loudest is not necessarily disagreeing with you so much as finally getting to the point. Academic work on Serbian organisational behaviour, cited in regional cross-cultural journals, consistently finds this pattern: high formal hierarchy on the org chart, low actual distance in how people argue once the door is closed. For a newcomer used to treating a meeting as the decision-making venue, the adjustment is less about learning new vocabulary and more about learning to notice which conversations in the room actually mattered.

Austria sits at the other temperamental pole, and the numbers are, in their own dry way, more alarming. The same Gallup survey finds only 9% of Austrian employees engaged at work, among the lowest figures recorded in Europe, alongside a job-satisfaction rate that fell five points year-on-year to 46%, according to Gallup analyst Marco Nink's estimate reported by Vienna's Vol.at, which pegs the resulting productivity loss at roughly €51.7 billion, about 11% of economic output. None of this shows up in the meeting room itself, which, per multiple business-etiquette guides and corroborated by forum accounts, remains a model of punctuality, formality, and agendas honoured to the minute. Hofstede's data puts Austria's power-distance score at 11, among the lowest ever recorded globally, which theoretically means your opinion counts as much as your director's. In practice it means everyone still uses "Sie," waits to be invited onto first-name terms, and delivers disagreement so evenly that newcomers sometimes miss that they have just been told no.

Structure, in other words, is not the same thing as warmth, and Austria keeps the two scrupulously separate. Flexible scheduling arrangements such as Gleitzeit exist precisely because the workday is treated as a measurable quantity rather than a mood, and the same instinct governs meetings: start time, end time, agenda item, decision, minutes circulated by someone whose job it specifically is to circulate them. The reward for all this precision is a workplace that is, by multiple satisfaction measures, quietly miserable, which suggests that Austrian order was never designed to make anyone happy. It was designed to make things predictable, and on that narrower promise, it delivers.

The Reckoning, then, is less about who is "nicer" and more about where the real meeting happens. In Serbia the scheduled meeting is theatre; the negotiation occurs informally, before and after, and punctuality is a polite fiction everyone quietly agrees to ignore. In Austria the scheduled meeting is the actual event, structured and time-boxed, and the informal layer is almost nonexistent, which is precisely why foreign managers mistake Austrian directness for coldness, when it is simply a low-context culture doing exactly what it says it will do, at the time it said it would do it.

The irony is that Austria's famous order coexists with workforce dissatisfaction, while Serbia's famous informality coexists with comparatively higher reported engagement — suggesting that structure and happiness at work are not the same project at all, whatever either country's brochure implies. And Vienna, which Mercer's Quality of Living index has repeatedly ranked among the most liveable cities on earth, is populated by employees who, per Gallup, are among the least engaged with their jobs anywhere in Europe: the city works beautifully; what happens inside the office, evidently, is a separate matter entirely.

There is a second, quieter divergence worth naming: what each culture considers rude. In Vienna, arriving unprepared to a meeting is the transgression; emotional volume in the room would be read as a loss of control. In Belgrade, the transgression is closer to the opposite: treating the meeting as purely transactional, skipping the coffee, declining to argue. A flat, agenda-only style that reads as competent in Austria can read as cold and untrustworthy in Serbia, and a passionate, interruption-heavy style that reads as engaged in Serbia can read as unprofessional in Austria. Neither side is wrong. They are simply answering a different question about what a meeting is for.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — a commenter describing Austrian tech firms noted that promotion and respect track almost entirely with formal titles and degrees, so an individual contributor without the right credential can do excellent work for years and still be read as junior in the room.*

> *Hacker News — another commenter called the Austrian civil service "enormous, inefficient," yet noted locals treat its slowness as the price of stability, something newcomers initially mistake for simple dysfunction before realizing nobody wants it to move faster.*

> *A Belgrade tech-worker forum (Beobuild.rs) — a poster who had joined an international firm in the city said colleagues read as reserved and even "cold" in the first weeks, since warmth in Serbian offices is extended gradually and through repeated informal contact, not assumed on day one.*

> *Expat.com's Serbia forum — a long-term resident advised newcomers job-hunting in Belgrade to specifically target companies paying in foreign currency from abroad, since domestic pay scales and negotiating norms run on a completely different logic than Western colleagues expect.*

> *Tales from the Foreign Service, a Belgrade post report — a diplomatic household described Serbian colleagues as unexpectedly warm and fluent in English even outside the capital, the opposite of what the formal paperwork and visa process had led them to brace for.*

### Conclusion

If the choice comes down to where the meeting itself will tell you the truth, pick Austria: the agenda is the agenda, the end time is the end time, and the criticism, however flatly delivered, means exactly what it says. If the choice comes down to where you'd rather actually enjoy the eight-plus hours a day you're there, the Gallup numbers suggest Serbia, somewhat perversely, has the edge, structural chaos and all.

The one number that should matter most to an actual relocating professional is not punctuality or politeness but where influence physically lives: in Austria, it lives in the room, on time; in Serbia, it lives in the coffee afterward, whenever that happens to start. Pick the country whose version of trust you're better equipped to earn, not the one whose office hours sound more civilised on paper. Over drinks, the honest version is this: Austria will never waste your time, and Serbia will never waste your relationships — choose your tolerance for ambiguity accordingly, and in either country, bring a translator for the parts nobody bothers to write down.
