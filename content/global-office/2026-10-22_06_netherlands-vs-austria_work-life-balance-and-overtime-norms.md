**The Netherlands Clocks Out at Five. Austria Just Stops Counting the Extra Hours.**

**🇳🇱 Netherlands · 🇦🇹 Austria**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In the Netherlands, the average worker clocks 32.1 hours a week, the shortest in the European Union, according to Eurostat. In Austria, the average worker clocks about 34, and last year supplied 42.3 million overtime hours that nobody paid for, according to Austria's Chamber of Labour (AK). Both countries will tell you, cheerfully, that they have excellent work-life balance. Only one of them can show you the invoice.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Leave at 5pm without apologising — your colleagues already have | Don't send "quick" Slack messages after 6pm expecting a same-evening reply |
| Negotiate a four- or four-and-a-half-day week; it's a routine request, not a red flag | Don't assume part-time hours signal low ambition — close to half the workforce works part-time |
| Come to meetings having read the agenda; Dutch colleagues expect you to have an opinion ready | Don't schedule "quick syncs" at 8am or after 5pm — they will be declined, politely and without drama |
| Use your statutory holiday allowance in full (minimum 20 days, often more by contract) | Don't offer unpaid overtime as a show of commitment — it buys you nothing; hours are hours |
| Ask about *vakantiegeld* (holiday allowance, roughly 8% of annual salary) when negotiating pay | Don't mistake blunt feedback in meetings for hostility — it's efficiency, not an attack |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read your collective agreement (*Kollektivvertrag*) before signing an "all-in" salary | Don't assume overtime gets paid automatically — about a quarter of recorded overtime hours currently don't |
| Ask whether your office runs on *Gleitzeit* (flexible banked hours) — many do | Don't book anything for Sunday; shops are shut and colleagues will be with family |
| Use "Sie" with managers and strangers until invited to switch to "du" | Don't skip the after-work drinks entirely — rapport is built there, not in meetings |
| Know your legal right to refuse overtime beyond 10 hours a day or 50 a week, no reason required | Don't treat a four-day week as standard — it's debated, not default, outside a few sectors |
| Clarify your rest-period entitlement (11 uninterrupted hours between shifts) | Don't confuse "flexible hours" with "fewer hours" — Austrians still average close to 34 a week |

### Clocking out in the Netherlands

The Dutch have spent half a century normalizing not working very much, and they did it on purpose. The OECD has flagged for over a decade that roughly half the Dutch workforce, and more than seven in ten Dutch women, work part-time — a structural choice, not a labour-market accident, dating to the 1980s "Dutch model" of trading wage growth for shorter hours. OECD Secretary-General Yves Leterme once grumbled that this restrains productivity; the Dutch, by and large, have declined to feel bad about it.

The payoff shows in the numbers. Eurostat puts the Dutch average working week at 32.1 hours in 2024, the lowest of any EU member state and nearly four hours below the EU average of 36.0. The InterNations Expat Insider survey, reported by IamExpat.nl, found 79% of international workers rate their hours positively and 78% rate their overall work-life balance favourably — the best-reviewed part of an otherwise uneven expat experience (housing and healthcare drag the Netherlands down to 21st of roughly fifty countries overall, even as it ranks 6th for "Working Abroad" specifically).

What makes this durable rather than decorative is the social contract behind it: leaving on time isn't tolerated, it's expected, and a colleague who stays late is more likely to be quietly pitied than praised. There is no cultural reward for looking busy. There is, increasingly, a legal push to formalize a right to disconnect — codifying a norm that mostly already exists.

### Clocking out, eventually, in Austria

Austria's Working Time Act looks, on paper, like one of the more generous overtime regimes in Europe. The standard week is eight hours a day or 40 a week, though most collective agreements set 38.5. Overtime is paid at 1.5 times the normal rate, a 50% surcharge; employees can refuse any request that would push them past 10 hours a day or 50 a week, and don't need a reason. Austrians, per Eurostat, average around 34 hours a week — a fairly short week by global standards, tied with Germany and Denmark.

The trouble is what happens between the law and the payslip. The AK and the Austrian Trade Union Federation (ÖGB) reported that in 2024, 42.3 million overtime and additional hours — about a quarter of all overtime worked — went unpaid, up from roughly 14% in 2020. AK President Renate Anderl called the practice "wage theft," noting that the state alone loses an estimated €960 million in income tax and €330 million in social security contributions. Earlier research from Austria's WIFO institute found the pattern is oldest among the most qualified workers, and that women are considerably more likely than men to have their overtime go uncompensated — nearly one in three hours worked by women versus 23% for men.

The explanation is mostly contractual: many Austrian salaries are structured as "all-in" packages assumed to already cover a certain amount of overtime, a convention that looks tidy in a contract and considerably less tidy once the actual hours are counted.

### The Reckoning

Here is the irony the brochure skips: Austria has the stronger paper protections — a statutory surcharge, a refusal right, a capped week — and the Netherlands mostly runs on an informal expectation that nobody has bothered to write down because nobody needs to. According to Hofstede Insights' cultural-dimension data, Austria scores one of the lowest Power Distance ratings in the world (11, against the Netherlands' 38), meaning Austrians should, in theory, find it just as easy as the Dutch to tell a boss "no." In practice, a quarter of Austrian overtime vanishes into unpaid "all-in" contracts anyway, while a lower-individualism, higher-hierarchy-tolerant Netherlands (Hofstede's Individualism score: 80 versus Austria's 55) somehow produces workers who leave at five without asking.

The lesson for anyone choosing between the two isn't which country has better labour law — it's which country's norms get enforced by habit rather than by statute. The Dutch workweek is short because almost nobody, including management, considers it normal to extend it. The Austrian workweek is short on paper and occasionally much longer in practice, with the difference quietly absorbed into a salary line nobody renegotiates.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/Netherlands (via DutchReview) — A Dutch employee was called into a meeting by his American manager over his "commitment to the team," because he logged off Slack at 5pm and stayed offline until 9am. He argued that needing extra hours to finish work "doesn't mean you are dedicated, it means you are inefficient or understaffed." When he brought it to HR, the Dutch HR rep reportedly laughed and told him to ignore his manager entirely.*

> *teamblind.com — An employee reviewing Booking.com's Amsterdam office described the work-life balance as so strong it becomes its own career hazard: "WLB is good; in fact too good in [the] sense it hinders career growth," with most staff clearing out of the office by around 4pm.*

> *Expatsi.com — American expats in the Netherlands describe an adjustment period before the lack of after-hours email starts to feel normal rather than suspicious; some report feeling guilty, at first, for not checking messages on a Sunday, before realizing "they are not" doing anything wrong by ignoring them.*

> *Octagon People (relocation consultancy blog) — New arrivals are advised that leaving work exactly on time "is not considered rude, it is expected," and that the operative principle in Dutch offices is "efficiency is the currency here, not hours clocked" — advice the author wishes had been given before the first awkward exit at 5:01pm.*

> *The Local Austria — Employees moving to Austria are reminded that overtime refusal is a genuine legal right, not just a theoretical one: workers can decline any request that would take them beyond 10 hours a day or 50 a week, no justification required, though contracts and collective agreements vary enough that it's worth checking the specifics before agreeing to anything.*

### Conclusion

If the deciding factor is which country will actually let you log off, the Netherlands wins on both the statistics and the lived testimony: a shorter average week, a culture that treats a 5pm exit as unremarkable, and expat surveys that consistently rate working hours as the best part of the deal. Austria offers the stronger-looking legal scaffolding — real overtime premiums, a genuine refusal right — but a system where a quarter of overtime hours are, by the labour movement's own account, simply not paid, usually because an "all-in" contract was signed without reading the fine print.

So read the fine print. In the Netherlands, the thing to negotiate is which four days you work. In Austria, the thing to negotiate is which collective agreement covers you, because that document, not the Working Time Act, decides whether your overtime is compensated or just assumed. Either way: ask before you sign, because the hours you don't notice signing away are the ones nobody will offer to give back.
