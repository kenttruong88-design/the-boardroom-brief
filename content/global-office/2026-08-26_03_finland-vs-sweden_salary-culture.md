# Finland vs Sweden: Everyone's Salary Is Public Record, Yours Is Still Non-Negotiable

### 🇫🇮 Finland · 🇸🇪 Sweden

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Every November, Finland publishes the taxable income of its entire population in a ritual so beloved it has earned the nickname National Jealousy Day. In Sweden, you need not even wait for a calendar date — under the principle of public access, anyone can phone the Tax Agency and ask what a named stranger earns, no subpoena required. Two countries, in other words, where your salary is effectively a matter of public record before you have even cashed the first paycheck. And yet, in both, the number itself is something you will do remarkably little to shape, because it was mostly decided months ago by people in a negotiating room you were never invited to.

#### 🇫🇮 Finland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check which sector collective agreement (TES) covers your role before the first interview — it sets the real floor. | Don't assume the number on the offer letter is a starting bid; many entry points are contractually fixed. |
| Do treat colleagues' curiosity about your salary as normal — refusing to answer reads as odder than answering. | Don't be surprised when a coworker, or a journalist, can look up your taxable income every autumn. |
| Do ask HR for the union pay scale bracket for your grade, not just "the range for similar roles." | Don't lowball your own ask assuming foreigners have less standing — the scale generally applies regardless of passport. |
| Do expect raises tied to sector agreement upgrades and tenure steps. | Don't expect an annual, US-style merit negotiation cycle — it mostly isn't how raises happen here. |
| Do read your payslip closely for the itemized holiday bonus (lomaraha) — it's separate from base pay. | Don't fold it mentally into your monthly salary when comparing offers; it distorts the real number. |

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Do learn your union's "märket" benchmark before any raise conversation — it's the reference point everyone negotiates around. | Don't treat a written offer as the final word; a counter is expected, not rude. |
| Do ask about the full package — pension top-ups, wellness allowance (friskvårdsbidrag), parental-leave supplements. | Don't fixate on the base monthly figure; it's deliberately only part of the story. |
| Do accept that pay bands are compressed — a ten-year veteran and a two-year hire may sit closer together than expected. | Don't assume seniority alone will pry the gap wider; title changes move pay more than tenure does. |
| Do remember that, legally, anyone can call Skatteverket and learn your income. | Don't treat your salary as a private matter — it functionally isn't one. |
| Do negotiate hard at the offer stage. | Don't accept a low starting salary assuming it "de-risks" you for a raise later — many report waiting years for one that never lands. |

### Finland

Finland does not have a statutory minimum wage. Instead, wages are set almost entirely through TES — sector-level collective agreements between unions and employer federations — which means an individual's negotiating leverage is smaller than expats often assume. According to the OECD, the average Finnish worker earned roughly €55,462 in 2025, translating to a 2026 monthly gross average near €4,140, though the figure that actually matters to a newcomer is the sector floor, not the national mean. Third-country professionals applying for a residence permit in 2026 need a monthly gross salary of at least €3,638 — a number set by immigration policy rather than market rate, and one recruiters will quote before discussing anything else.

What Finland lacks in individual negotiation room, it makes up for in radical, almost cheerful transparency. Public information on individual income taxation, published annually by the Finnish Tax Administration (Vero.fi), means anyone's earnings from the previous year are a phone call — or a newspaper database — away. Some employers have taken the logic further: Vincit and other Finnish firms have adopted fully open internal salary models, on the theory, as the professional association TEK has argued, that a number nobody can see becomes mysterious, and mystery breeds resentment. The result is a workplace culture where asking a colleague's salary outright is less taboo than refusing to answer.

### Sweden

Sweden runs on the same collective-bargaining chassis — about 90 percent of the workforce is covered by sectoral agreements, per The Local Sweden's reporting, with no statutory minimum wage to fall back on — but its choreography is different. Rather than one blanket sector rate, Swedish unions and employer bodies negotiate an annual or biennial benchmark increase, known as "märket," which functions less as a hard rule than as the gravitational center every subsequent workplace negotiation orbits. Statistics Sweden (SCB) puts the average gross monthly wage at roughly SEK 38,000 in 2026, with median and mean sitting close together — a signature of a genuinely compressed pay distribution rather than a statistical fluke.

Sweden's transparency mechanism is more direct than Finland's: under offentlighetsprincipen, the principle of public access, tax and income records are open on request, no annual publication cycle required. This openness does not, however, translate into aggressive individual negotiation. The Local Sweden has reported a recurring pattern among foreign hires: many start with a modest salary on the theory that it reduces perceived risk to the employer, then wait for a merit increase that, absent a title change, rarely arrives — at which point they begin doubting themselves, their employer, or Sweden generally. The more durable advice, echoed across expat guidance, is to negotiate the full package upfront — pension contributions, wellness stipends, parental top-ups — since base salary alone was never designed to move much after the fact.

### The Reckoning

Put side by side, the two systems reveal a shared premise wearing different clothes. Finland's transparency is retrospective and annual — a single loud publication event each November — while Sweden's is quiet and perpetual, available on demand year-round. Finland's collective bargaining operates mostly at sector level, producing a hard floor with little local variance; Sweden's operates with a national benchmark that individual workplaces and unions then negotiate on top of, giving marginally more room to maneuver, at least in theory. Hofstede Insights' comparison of the two countries places Sweden further toward the "feminine," consensus-oriented end of its cultural spectrum than Finland — a data point that lines up neatly with Sweden's preference for negotiated total packages over blunt figures, and Finland's more procedural, scale-driven approach to the same problem.

The counterintuitive part is that the country with the more theatrical transparency ritual — Finland's tax-data reveal — is also the one where individual salary negotiation is, in practice, more constrained by fixed sector scales. Sweden, which lets you learn a stranger's income with a single phone call any day of the year, is the one where employees are more actively encouraged to negotiate the shape of their package, if not always its headline number. Radical openness, it turns out, is not the same thing as radical flexibility.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A foreign front-end developer asked what salary to request from a Finnish employer, having no reference point beyond his home country's pay scale. The responses converged on a range noticeably lower than what he'd assumed a "European tech salary" should look like, forcing a recalibration before the first interview even happened.*

> *Quora — Someone with a decade of engineering experience and a salary benchmark of 26 lakh rupees per year back home asked whether a Swedish offer of 45,000 SEK a month, plus a lunch card, counted as good money. The thread's answer, in effect, was that Swedish pay compression makes home-country prestige an unreliable yardstick — the number that looks modest converts into a comfortable, tax-and-benefit-supported life once local costs and the welfare package are priced in.*

> *Teamblind — An engineer comparing notes on Swedish tech compensation noted that total pay trails the US market substantially, with signing bonuses and equity grants largely absent; base salary is, for practical purposes, the entire offer, not an opening figure to be layered with extras later.*

> *The Local Sweden — Foreign hires described a familiar arc: accepting a lower starting salary in the belief that it would make them a safer, lower-risk bet for the employer, then waiting years for a raise that never appeared absent a change in title, eventually souring not just on the company but on the country itself.*

> *Hacker News — A commenter working in Finnish web technology described salaries landing in a comfortable, if unspectacular, band once benefits and work-life balance were factored in — less dramatic than expected, but also less stressful than the number alone would suggest to an outsider.*

### Conclusion

Neither country will reward an American-style hardball negotiation, and neither will punish you for asking direct questions about pay — the taboo, where it exists at all, runs the opposite direction. The practical move is the same in both places: learn the collective agreement or benchmark that actually governs your role before you say a number out loud, then negotiate the package around it rather than the base figure alone, because the base figure was largely decided without you. Finland rewards those who show up with the sector scale already memorized; Sweden rewards those who know which stipends are still on the table after the salary line is settled.

The honest summary is that both countries will tell you, publicly and on request, exactly what everyone else makes — and neither will make it any easier to argue for more.
