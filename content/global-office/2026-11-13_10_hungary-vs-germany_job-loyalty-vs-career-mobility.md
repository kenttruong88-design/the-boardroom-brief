# Hungary Wants Your Loyalty at Thirty Days' Notice; Germany Wants It for Three Months, in Writing

🇭🇺 Hungary · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Germany, 41.7 percent of employees aged 25 and over had been with their current employer for at least ten years in 2023, according to the Federal Statistical Office. In Hungary, 47 percent of respondents in an Erste Bank survey said they did not see themselves in their current job and wanted out within two years. One country treats the employer as a long-term relationship with paperwork. The other treats it as a venue.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Hungary

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your contract for a notice period longer than the default 30 days; up to six months can be agreed in writing | Sign without reading the notice clause and assume 30 days |
| Treat probation as two-way; either side can end it immediately without giving a reason | Assume probation is a formality |
| Remember severance applies to employer-initiated dismissal after three years of service | Expect severance if you are the one resigning |
| Expect an employer's dismissal to state its reasons in writing | Accept a vague termination letter without asking what it rests on |
| If you hold a non-EU work permit, confirm how it transfers before you resign | Resign first and ask the immigration question afterwards |

### Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Resign on paper with a signature and a documented delivery | Email your resignation; electronic termination is not valid |
| Read the notice clause; three months is common, and the statutory four weeks is only a floor | Assume you can leave in four weeks because the law says so |
| Ask for your Arbeitszeugnis, the reference letter, once you have given notice | Leave without it; it is the document the next employer expects |
| Line up the next job before quitting, because a voluntary exit can trigger a three-month benefits block | Resign into a gap and plan to claim unemployment benefit |
| Aim for several years in a mid-career role | String together several stints of under six months |

## Hungary

Hungary's labour code is, on its face, quite flexible. According to Forvis Mazars, an employee's notice period is 30 days regardless of tenure, though up to six months can be agreed in writing. An employer must give 30 days rising to 90 after 20 years, and employees with at least three years of service get severance if the employer ends the contract, from one month of pay at three years to six months at 25 years. Probation allows immediate termination by either side without giving a reason. Eurofound adds that employers must justify dismissals and that workers need not give reasons for resigning.

The data suggests plenty of people are using that flexibility, or contemplating it. Erste Bank found that 47 percent wanted to move within two years and 49 percent saw no realistic chance of career growth where they work, according to Portfolio. Profession.hu reported in early 2025 that nearly half of employees were looking or open to a new job, with 12 percent actively applying, and that the share of active job seekers who already have jobs rose to 25 percent, from 16 percent a year earlier, according to the Budapest Chamber of Commerce. Deloitte Hungary found 57 percent of respondents were considering a change, with two in three of those who had under two years of tenure among them.

The reasons are practical rather than philosophical. Pay comes first, then development, then, in Deloitte's findings, rigid office rules: nearly half would consider changing jobs if mandatory office days increased. Daily News Hungary reported in 2026 that 54 percent of career changers said a higher salary would have kept them in their previous job. Finding the next job is not the obstacle it once was. Profession.hu's March figures, as reported by Daily News Hungary, put the average job search at three months and 22 days, down from just over five months a year earlier, and half of surveyed Hungarians have changed careers at least once. Deloitte also found that 40 percent of respondents would accept a pay cut to choose where they work, and that 94 percent want at least one remote day a week. Mobility, in other words, is as much about lifestyle as salary. Loyalty, in short, is available at a market price, and the market price has been moving. A study reported by Trade Magazin said the share of employees with very strong loyalty to their employer fell from 59 percent to 47 percent in three years, while 51 percent of employers still believed their staff were very loyal, which is the sort of gap in perception that keeps HR consultants employed.

## Germany

Germany reverses the emphasis. The legal baseline is modest: four weeks to the 15th or the end of a month, according to Germany Trade & Invest, and two weeks in a probation period of up to six months. But the employer's side climbs to seven months after 20 years, and dismissal protection applies to companies with more than ten employees once the employee has passed six months. The Local, citing the Institute for Employment Research, reported an average tenure of about 11 years, up from 10.3 two decades earlier, and the researcher said job hopping "is not a phenomenon that affects the majority of employers." The same researchers noted that fixed-term contracts had doubled over those two decades, so stability is not universal: the long-tenure majority coexists with a flexible fringe, and newcomers often start on the fringe. That figure dates from 2012, but Destatis's 2023 data points the same way.

The cultural rules sit on top of the legal ones. Karrierebibel advises roughly one to two years per job at entry level, three to five in mid-career and five to ten at senior level, and describes recruiters becoming suspicious at two or three jobs in a year or several stays under six months. All About Berlin notes that a three-month notice period is described as common in contracts, and The Local points out that quitting voluntarily typically triggers a three-month Sperrzeit, a block on unemployment benefits. Germany does not forbid leaving. It simply makes leaving a project with a timeline.

Hofstede Insights scores Germany at 65 on uncertainty avoidance, against 82 for Hungary, and 66 on masculinity against 88. Both are cautious cultures. Germany's caution is channelled into process and institutions, while Hungary's shows up as an assumption that the next arrangement will probably be better, or at least different.

## The Reckoning

The two countries sell different guarantees. Germany offers the stable career: long notice, formal protection and a CV culture that rewards staying. Hungary offers the quick exit: short notice, a probation period with no reason required and an employee population that, by its own account, is willing to leave when the pay or progression fails. The German figure of 41.7 percent with ten or more years at one employer describes a system that rewards staying put, while the Hungarian finding that 49 percent see no realistic career growth where they work describes one that rewards leaving, or at least threatening to. Neither is wrong. Neither is a good fit for someone who wants both.

For the foreign worker, the question is which risk is worse: being held in place or being let go without much ceremony. In Germany you will probably stay longer than you planned, which is useful if you want residency and a reference. In Hungary you may move more freely but, as the Hungarian data suggests, so will your colleagues, and the person who trained you may not be there to sign off your next promotion.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — a commenter explains that three-month notice is standard in Germany so that employers have time to hand off work and onboard a replacement. The statutory notice an employer owes grows with tenure, while the employee's contractual period often stays fixed.*

> *Hacker News — a commenter notes that German law provides notice periods but no statutory severance, that employees generally must keep working during notice and cannot start another job, and that mutual termination agreements are where severance usually appears.*

> *Hacker News — a commenter in Western Europe says notice periods soften layoffs, with German employees usually getting about three months, and that this is why they would not work for an American company.*

> *Expat.com — a non-EU worker in Budapest wants to leave for a better offer but their work permit is tied to their present employer. They ask whether it can be transferred or whether they must reapply, and the only reply offered a private message.*

> *Expat.com — a foreign worker with a year left on a two-year Hungarian contract asks whether they can apply to another employer directly, how long they may stay while looking, and whether the current employer must permit it. The thread did not settle any of it.*

## Conclusion

If you value a predictable career and a reference letter that opens doors, Germany suits you, provided you can live with three months of notice and a quitting process that requires paper and a stamp. If you are mobile and comfortable with short horizons, Hungary offers more freedom, but you should read the contract for notice length and, if you are not an EU citizen, check what your permit allows before you hand in a letter.

Either way, the loyalty on offer is contractual, not emotional. Both countries will hold you to the paperwork, which is a form of honesty.
