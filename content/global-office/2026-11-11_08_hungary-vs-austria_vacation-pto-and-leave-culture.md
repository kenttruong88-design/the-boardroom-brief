# Hungary Grants You Twenty Days and Counts Your Birthdays; Austria Grants Twenty-Five and Counts the Bridge Days

🇭🇺 Hungary · 🇦🇹 Austria

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The Hungarian employee is entitled to a base of 20 working days of paid annual leave and works, on average, more than 1,800 collectively agreed hours a year, according to Eurofound figures reported by Daily News Hungary. The Austrian employee is entitled to five weeks, or 25 working days, and, according to OECD data, actually works about 1,435 hours. The two capitals are separated by roughly two hundred kilometres and the Danube. They are separated in the matter of leisure by something closer to three weeks of the working year. This is the sort of arithmetic that tends to be presented in relocation brochures as a rounding error, and experienced by the relocated as a personality.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Hungary

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Count your age-based extra days; they begin at age 25 and rise to +10 days at 45 | Assume your 20-day base is the whole entitlement if you are over 25 |
| Declare children under 16, who add 2 days for one child, 4 for two and 7 for three or more | Forget that this requires paperwork rather than goodwill |
| Get a doctor's certificate for any sick day, even a single one | Treat a one-day cold as something to report by text |
| Check the annual list of shifted working days before booking a long weekend | Assume a bridge day is free; it may be repaid with a Saturday shift |
| Confirm how many of your 11 public holidays fall on weekdays | Assume a public holiday on a Sunday is compensated |
| Plan around the 44 working days of parental leave per parent, with only 10 percent employer pay | Plan family finances around the leave as though it were a salary |

### In Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use your five weeks; the entitlement is statutory and colleagues will use theirs | Apologise for being absent in August |
| Learn the term Zeitausgleich, since time off in lieu is a normal arrangement | Work late as a substitute for asking |
| Note that new hires accrue vacation gradually in the first year | Book three weeks in your second month without checking |
| Map the 13 public holidays against weekends and plan long weekends early | Expect Good Friday to be a public holiday |
| Check your payslip for the extra salary payments that make up the 14-pay year | Assume 12 equal instalments is the rule |
| Report illness promptly and provide medical confirmation when asked | Disappear for a week and explain afterwards |

## Hungary

The Hungarian system is generous in construction and modest in headline. The base entitlement is 20 working days, according to the CXC Global hiring guide, but the law then adds days for age: one extra at 25, rising in steps to ten extra at 45, with further days for employees with children under 16. A 45-year-old with two children, on those figures, is entitled to 34 working days, which is more than a 24-year-old Austrian would be. There are 11 public holidays in 2026, and they do not reduce the annual leave entitlement. The generosity is real. It is also distributed by the calendar of your life rather than by the length of your service, so that the newcomer in his twenties learns the system's most important lesson: the benefit goes to the patient.

Public holidays are protected in a practical way: working on one is permitted only in limited circumstances and normally carries a supplement of at least 100 percent of ordinary hourly pay, according to the same guide. An employee who is asked to give up a holiday is therefore expensive to ask, which is the most reliable form of workplace protection yet devised. Time off for illness is where the system becomes administrative. Employees need a doctor's certificate even for a single day, the employer pays 70 percent of absence pay for up to 15 days a year, and social-security benefits take over afterwards. Each parent with a year of service may take up to 44 working days of parental leave before the child turns three, at 10 percent of normal earnings from the employer. This is protection of a kind, though one that assumes a household with other income. The picture on hours is less restful. Daily News Hungary, citing Eurofound's 2024 report, put Hungarian collectively agreed working time at 1,832 hours a year, third highest in the European Union and about 110 hours above the EU average of 1,722, and cited Eurostat for an average hourly labour cost of 14.1 euros against an EU average of 33.5. The paper noted that the legal minimum paid vacation of 20 days compares with 25 to 30 days in France and Germany. The newspaper's own chart caption and text disagree about whether holidays are included, which is a reminder that the data on this subject is itself somewhat on leave.

Hofstede's published country scores suggest the temperament behind this. Hungary scores 88 on masculinity and 82 on uncertainty avoidance, against Austria's 79 and 70, and 46 on power distance against Austria's 11. A hierarchical, achievement-oriented, risk-averse workplace is one in which the person who takes every available day is making a statement, whether or not one was intended. Leave is a right. Using it conspicuously is a negotiation.

## Austria

Austria's leave regime is easier to describe and harder to criticise. The GloRoots guide lists five weeks of paid vacation, rising to six after 25 years of service, with service at earlier employers counting within legal limits, and 13 statutory public holidays that employees must be paid for. Good Friday is not among them. Sick pay scales with tenure: full pay for six weeks in the first year, eight weeks from the second year, ten from the sixteenth and twelve from the twenty-sixth, each followed by four weeks at half pay before the health insurance fund takes over. Anyone required to work on a public holiday receives a premium there as well, so the calendar is respected on both banks of the Danube, if at different volumes. The Hungarian sick day requires a certificate and a certain amount of optimism. The Austrian sick week requires a certificate and a pulse.

The hours match the leave. OECD data, compiled by TheGlobalEconomy.com, puts Austrian annual hours worked per worker at about 1,435 in 2023, against roughly 1,679 for Hungary on the same basis. That is a difference of 244 hours, or roughly six working weeks, which is a considerable amount of attention to be paying to an espresso. Austria's culture, by the same measure, combines a high masculinity score of 79 with a power distance of 11, one of the lowest anywhere: the workplace is competitive in ambition and informal in hierarchy, which means that taking five weeks off is neither a challenge to authority nor a career statement. It is simply the arithmetic of the contract.

Expatriates tend to discover this by comparison. Expat.com notes that Austria sits among the European countries offering 25 days, against most others at 20 to 22 before public holidays, and that people moving from a generous system to a stingy one find the adjustment difficult. The reverse adjustment, from a stingy system to Austria's, is described by newcomers as pleasant and slightly suspicious. The suspicion tends to lift at the first August when the office empties without comment.

## The Reckoning

On paper, Austria wins on the headline: 25 days against 20, 13 public holidays against 11, and a sick-pay ladder that ends in months rather than a fortnight. In practice, Hungary closes the gap for workers over 35 with children, for whom the age and child supplements can bring the entitlement near or above the Austrian base. What Hungary cannot close is the hours. A Hungarian employee with 34 days of leave still works longer in the remaining weeks than an Austrian does in the whole year, and is employed, per Eurostat, at an hourly labour cost of roughly 42 percent of the EU average.

The deeper difference is how the benefit is held. In Austria, leave is something the employee consumes, colleagues expect it and the manager schedules around it. In Hungary it is something the employee accumulates, by age and by family, and uses within a system that runs hotter on hours and colder on pay. Both countries are generous by the standards of the world's laggards. Only one of them is generous in a way a 28-year-old single newcomer will notice in the first year.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — commenters in a 2012 discussion praised Austria for paying 14 salaries a year, with lower tax on the extra two, and for 25 days of paid vacation. One who had worked in California described almost never getting consecutive days off and called the Austrian arrangement a dream, while others noted that in American workplaces doctor visits and funerals often come out of the same leave.*

> *ExpatExchange — an American who had worked two years in Austria, and was weighing a job near Heidelberg, described Austrian employment as 14 payments a year and 25 paid vacation days, and asked whether flextime, known as Zeitausgleich, is common in Germany. The post drew no replies.*

> *Expat.com forum — one poster, recalling Hungarian leave as starting at around 20 days and varying with age and the number of children, joked that Hungary sits roughly halfway between the United States and France on vacation. Public holidays are paid, they added, but can fall on weekends.*

> *Expat.com forum — in a thread on after-work life in Hungary, a retiree said they were tired of weekend and late-day requests from work and expected to be glad to stop in about two and a half years, while others described a workplace drinking culture that began earlier in the day than the term after-work suggests.*

> *Expat.com forum — a resident asking about an upcoming Hungarian long weekend explained that people whose work or school follows Hungarian holidays would have time to spare, while another reply said they would simply be working on the Monday because they needed the income.*

## Conclusion

Austria is the safer choice for anyone who values predictable time off and a workplace that treats it as ordinary. Hungary is workable, even rewarding, for the employee who is older, has children, or is employed by a company that interprets the statutory minimum generously. The money and the hours are the harder part. A Hungarian salary buys fewer days of rest per year than an Austrian one, and the days it does buy arrive on a schedule set by your birth certificate.

The practical advice is to ask, before accepting an offer, for the leave entitlement in working days, the year of accrual and the treatment of public holidays in writing, since these three numbers explain most of what follows. Whichever country you choose, read the contract for the number, then watch the office in August for the practice. The statute describes the holiday. The colleagues, as ever, decide whether you are allowed to have it.
