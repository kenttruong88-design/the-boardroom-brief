# Thailand and Japan Have Both Mastered the Suit, and Both Would Like You to Stop Sweating Through It

🇹🇭 Thailand · 🇯🇵 Japan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Tokyo, the government has recently invited its own civil servants to come to work in shorts, sneakers and T-shirts, which in a country where the dark suit is practically a national uniform counts as a constitutional reform. In Bangkok, meanwhile, a business dress guide still describes the expected look as men in suits and ties and women in skirts, in a climate that treats both as an act of faith. Two Asian economies, two humid summers, and one shared conclusion: clothing is a statement about respect, and respect is not negotiable merely because it is 34 degrees.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Thailand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a jacket and tie to formal business meetings, even if the visit before it was casual | Assume a relaxed client visit means the next meeting will be relaxed too |
| Keep clothes pressed, clean and neat; appearance is read as effort | Turn up wrinkled and call it tropical style |
| Greet seniors with a wai, once, and follow it with a spoken greeting | Wai everyone, including juniors, in an enthusiastic frenzy |
| Give feedback privately and indirectly | Correct a senior colleague in front of the team |
| Accept after-work invitations, which are part of the job | Treat the office and the dinner as separate jurisdictions |
| Stay calm when a proposal is met with silence | Show anger; silence is already your answer |

### Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a dark suit to interviews and client meetings | Dress like a founder of a start-up you have not yet founded |
| Receive business cards with both hands and place them on the table | Pocket a card you have just been handed |
| Address colleagues by title or with -san | Use first names because you read it was friendly |
| Follow Cool Biz dates and your team's lead on jackets | Remove your tie on 1 May because you are an individual |
| Join the nomikai, even without drinking | Take the seat near the head of the table |
| Raise disagreement privately | Contradict your manager in a meeting |

## Thailand

Business attire in Thailand is, according to Expat.com's guide to the Bangkok work environment, "quite conservative": men typically wear suits and ties, women dresses or skirts, and appearance matters because Thais are fashion-conscious. That guide also advises entering an office with a wai to everyone, a degree of ceremony that would exhaust a Western open-plan floor by 9:15.

The expat forums are less rigid and more useful. A long-term resident on Expat.com says customer-facing visits can be casual but business meetings still call for trousers, jacket and tie. Another describes everyday office dress as close to Western norms, with ties becoming rarer. In other words, the written rule is formal and the lived rule is "formal when it counts," which is the sort of ambiguity that rewards the observant and punishes the confident.

The harder formality in Thailand is social rather than sartorial. The Thaiger notes that respect for hierarchy is essential, that decisions travel from the top down, that public criticism is frowned on, and that employees may nod when they have not understood. Hofstede Insights scores Thailand at 64 on power distance, against 54 for Japan, and at 20 on individualism, against 46. Translated: the Thai office will forgive a tieless afternoon far sooner than it forgives a challenged boss.

Manners are the other half of the uniform. Expat.com's guide says Thais are reserved with newcomers and do not usually ask personal questions, and that relationships come before business. Forum residents add that handshakes are uncommon, that a smile is the standard sign of goodwill, and that the wai is given once and not demanded of foreigners. The practical effect is an office where nobody will tell you that your shirt is wrong, and where everybody will notice. Politeness here is not the absence of judgement. It is judgement delivered at a lower volume.

## Japan

Japan has turned the weather into policy. The Japan Times describes how, between May and September, government staff and conservative office workers swap dark suits for polo shirts and linen, a programme branded Cool Biz and described, with commendable restraint, as a glass-half-full description of what is essentially a hot office. Thermostats are set to 28°C from 1 May, and ties return around October whether or not the weather agrees.

The 2026 sequel is bolder. According to Business Today, the Tokyo Metropolitan Government is now encouraging shorts, polo shirts, T-shirts and sneakers, with Governor Yuriko Koike citing a challenging outlook for electricity supply. Cool Biz began in 2005 and originally meant only skipping the jacket and necktie. A marketing employee quoted in the report praised the flexibility, while a realtor welcomed dropping the jacket and tie but considered shorts too casual for an office. That is Japan in one sentence: reform arrives, and a committee of one immediately forms to feel uneasy about it.

Away from the heatwave, Expat.com's Tokyo guide says a suit with a jacket is expected for men and women at most interviews and client meetings, with IT and design firms more relaxed. Business cards are a requirement, colleagues are addressed by title, and at a nomikai the leader takes the best seat while junior staff sit near the door. Formality here is not a dress code. It is a seating plan with trousers.

The heat is also not the only reason Japanese dress rules make headlines. In 2019, according to Expat.com's guide on adapting to dress codes, Labour Minister Takumi Nemoto defended heel requirements for women as tradition, and the backlash produced the #KuToo movement. The same guide notes that salarymen are recognisable by light shirts and dark suits, and that Japanese firms tend to judge French-style office wear as too flashy. Expat Japan's workplace guide adds that tattoos should be covered, heavy perfume avoided, and that "that might be difficult" generally means no. Newcomers should note that the dress code is only the first layer of a language in which the word no is considered a failure of manners.

## The Reckoning

On dress alone, the two countries are closer than their reputations suggest. Both expect a suit when the stakes are real, both relax it when the thermometer insists, and both read neatness as a form of manners. Japan, however, has the advantage of a published calendar: Cool Biz tells you when the rules loosen, by roughly how much, and who announced it. Thailand offers no such memo. You learn the rule by watching which colleague gets the quiet look.

The deeper difference is where the formality lives. In Japan it lives in procedure, in cards, titles, seating and slow, process-driven decisions. In Thailand it lives in relationships, in deference, face and the careful avoidance of confrontation. A newcomer who dresses correctly in either country has solved perhaps a fifth of the problem, which is more than most manage on day one.

There is a further asymmetry. Japan's formality is written down, so a foreigner can learn it from a guide and be forgiven for the remainder. Thailand's formality is relational, so a foreigner can be scrupulously correct on paper and still miss the point, because the point is how a senior colleague feels about you. The Hofstede gap on power distance, ten points, is smaller than it sounds. The gap in how the rules are communicated is considerably larger.

## Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two on dress alone, pack the same suit for both and add a linen jacket for Tokyo in July. Japan will give you a formal rulebook and a seasonal exemption. Thailand will give you a flexible rulebook and no one to ask. Pick the one whose ambiguity you can live with, and the one whose after-work dinners you can survive.

In either country the tie is optional right up until the moment it is not, and nobody will tell you which moment that is.
