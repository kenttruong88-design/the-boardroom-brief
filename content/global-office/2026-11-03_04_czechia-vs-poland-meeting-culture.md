# Czechia Will Smile and Say Nothing. Poland Will Not Smile and Say Everything.

🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇵🇱 Poland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Gallup's latest *State of the Global Workplace* report puts employee engagement at 16 percent in Czechia and 7 percent in Poland, which is the kind of statistic that should make a newly arrived manager nervous for entirely different reasons in each country. In Prague, the low number sits underneath meetings that run smoothly, end on time, and produce consensus that may or may not exist. In Warsaw, the number sits underneath meetings where someone will, in fact, tell you your plan has a flaw in it, loudly, and apparently without having lost any engagement at all. The two countries share a border, a post-1989 economic trajectory, and a healthy mutual suspicion of each other's football teams. They do not share a theory of what a meeting is for.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Send a written agenda before any meeting, even a ten-minute one | Propose a Friday-afternoon meeting, especially during summer "cucumber season" |
| Arrive five minutes early; punctuality reads as competence | Push for a yes-or-no answer on the spot — decisions tend to follow the meeting, not happen in it |
| Use formal titles and surnames until a colleague invites first names | Read a nodding, agreeable room as a room that agrees with you |
| Join colleagues for a sit-down lunch before noon | Schedule a breakfast meeting — it will register as an eccentric American habit |
| Follow up anything important by email, since Czechs favor a paper trail | Stay late to "wrap things up" — it signals poor planning, not dedication |
| Learn a few words of Czech for small talk before the agenda starts | Confront a disagreement head-on in front of the group; raise it privately afterward |

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring data and a prepared case before raising a problem in a meeting | Mistake a blunt critique — "this won't work because…" — for personal hostility |
| Ask early who actually holds sign-off authority on a decision | Assume a flat-looking org chart means no one outranks anyone informally |
| Share materials in advance, especially for colleagues working in a second language | Let a meeting run past its scheduled end time — punctuality on the close matters as much as the start |
| Mirror the formality level the other person opened with | Read curt, imperative emails ("Fix line 42") as rude — it is often just efficient phrasing |
| Treat a pushback or challenge in a meeting as due diligence, not obstruction | Expect warmth or small talk to arrive before competence has been demonstrated |
| Confirm next steps in writing immediately after the meeting ends | Assume Warsaw-style directness is universal — regional and generational variation is significant |

---

Czechia's meeting culture rewards patience over confrontation, and the data backs up what that patience is covering for. Gallup's 2026 report places Czech employee engagement at 16 percent on a three-year rolling average, unchanged from the period before — a workforce that shows up, nods, and does not necessarily mean it. Up Benefity's 2026 Employee Barometer, reported by Expats.cz, found that nearly half of Czech employees withhold direct critical feedback from their managers, preferring to vent to colleagues, stay quiet, or use anonymous channels instead. About 40 percent believe management filters information to suit itself, and only around one in ten say they trust leadership almost completely. When something goes wrong at a company level, roughly two-thirds of staff learn about it through official channels — the rest hear it secondhand, or not at all.

None of this means Czech colleagues are being evasive for sport. Hofstede Insights scores Czechia's uncertainty avoidance at 74 out of 100, a preference for structure and established procedure over improvisation, and its power distance at a moderate 57 — hierarchy exists, but it is softened rather than announced. The reluctance to challenge a boss openly in a meeting has a specific historical throughline: under communist-era management, the safe move was never to be the one who made the mistake, and that calculus did not evaporate with the regime. The practical result for a foreign manager is that a Czech team's silence in a meeting is not an absence of opinion — it is an opinion being held for a safer venue, usually a private one-on-one, usually afterward.

Poland runs the experiment in the opposite direction. A Talaera survey of 300 foreign professionals working in Poland found interactions split 42 percent direct, 33 percent indirect, and 26 percent neutral — a country that leans toward naming the problem first and skipping the preamble. Hofstede's power distance score for Poland sits at 68, noticeably higher than Czechia's, meaning that hierarchy is more explicit even when the org chart is drawn flat: senior approval is routinely required for decisions that other countries' offices would delegate, and seniority or technical expertise carries real informal authority. Yet directness and hierarchy coexist with something that looks, on paper, like thinner skin than the bluntness implies — a Statista survey from April 2024 found that 90 percent of Polish respondents said they had never been publicly criticized or humiliated at work in the past five years, suggesting the critique culture operates inside clear, if unwritten, bounds about where and how it's delivered.

The practical texture of a Polish meeting, per the Talaera research, treats the session as a working meeting rather than a performance: preparation and supporting evidence are expected, and a colleague who challenges your numbers is doing due diligence, not picking a fight. One American working at a U.S. manufacturer's European headquarters in Poland — in an account shared on the Expat.com forum — described deliberately building a culture where "the employee is able to thrive," contrasting it with what he called the more common Polish management habit of pushing staff hard through pressure. He noted that his boss's conversations with other business owners suggested younger leadership is slowly adopting a friendlier style, while older owners are not in a hurry to change. Hierarchy, in other words, is real in Poland, but which direction it points is shifting by generation and by company, not fixed by national character.

### The Reckoning

Here is the inversion that should worry anyone leaning on stereotypes: the quieter country is not the more content one. Czechia's engagement figure, low as it is, still runs more than double Poland's 7 percent — meaning the nation famous for never telling its boss a hard truth in the room is, by Gallup's own measure, less disengaged than the one famous for telling the boss exactly what's wrong with the plan. Directness, it turns out, is not a proxy for job satisfaction, and politeness is not a proxy for buy-in. A Czech team can sit through a meeting in total, harmonious silence and leave with less actual commitment to the decision than a Polish team that just spent twenty minutes arguing about it.

The second irony is about where the real decision happens. In Czechia, the meeting is theater and the decision is made afterward, in private, through channels a newcomer can't see — so trying to win the room is a waste of effort. In Poland, the meeting is often where the decision actually gets contested and shaped, but who has final sign-off may still require a separate, invisible climb up the approval chain you weren't told about. Both countries, in short, have a hidden layer of authority operating underneath the visible one. Czechia hides it behind manners. Poland hides it behind an org chart that looks flatter than it behaves.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (via third-party mirror) — a commenter describing Polish corporate culture called it consistently "top-down" and cited unpaid internships at a major multinational's Polish operation as evidence that back-office roles there get treated as disposable, regardless of how the parent company behaves elsewhere.*

> *Expat.com forum — an American relocated to run a U.S. manufacturer's Polish EU office said he built his team around making employees feel they could thrive, explicitly pushing back against what he called the more typical local habit of motivating staff through pressure and hard deadlines.*

> *ExpatExchange.com — a longtime expat in Prague described a lingering mistrust of officials and businesspeople rooted in the country's communist past, and recalled finding that almost no one in a Czech visa office was willing to personally make a decision, forcing every request through an employer or a hired attorney instead.*

> *Personal expat blog (doucuji.eu) — an American who moved to Czechia wrote that her habit of smiling and greeting strangers made locals visibly stare, and that she eventually adopted a neutral, more reserved public expression to stop standing out — a small social recalibration that maps directly onto how little small talk opens a Czech meeting.*

> *Expat.com forum — a UK-born poster of Polish descent who took a factory job in Poland said his fluent English counted for little against his weaker Polish and lack of local qualifications, and wished he'd known beforehand that language and paperwork, not goodwill, determine how seriously a Polish employer takes you.*

### Conclusion

If there is one thing worth carrying into your first week, it's this: in Czechia, assume the real answer is being withheld until it's safe to give, and build in a private follow-up after every meeting where you actually want to know what people think. In Poland, assume the blunt answer you just got in the room is the real one, and that a colleague arguing with your numbers is doing you a professional courtesy, not picking a fight — then go find out, separately, who actually has to sign off before anything moves. Mixing up the two styles is the fastest way to either miss a quiet veto in Prague or pick an unnecessary fight in Warsaw.

The honest version, the one I'd give a friend over a drink, is that neither country is being difficult on purpose — they've just optimized meetings for different kinds of safety, one social and one procedural, and Gallup's numbers suggest neither approach is making anyone particularly happy to be there.
