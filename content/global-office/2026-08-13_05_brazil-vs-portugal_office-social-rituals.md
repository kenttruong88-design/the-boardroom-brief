**The Coffee Pot Never Stops Moving: Office Rituals in Brazil vs. Portugal**

**🇧🇷 Brazil · 🇵🇹 Portugal**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries connected by language, colonial history, and an apparently genetic devotion to small, strong coffee — and yet one treats the lunch table as the actual site of business, while the other treats it as a break from business that happens to be where relationships also get built. Brazilian lunches routinely stretch past 90 minutes; Portuguese workers are legally entitled to one hour and often take two anyway. Neither country is in a hurry, exactly, but they're not in a hurry for different reasons.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the mid-morning cafezinho when it's offered — declining reads as standoffish | Treat the coffee round as optional small talk you can skip |
| Budget a real hour-plus for lunch, ideally with colleagues | Eat at your desk as a default — it's noticed and read as antisocial |
| Expect physical warmth — hugs, cheek kisses, close conversational distance | Recoil from physical greetings or keep exaggerated personal space |
| Say yes to the occasional weekend barbecue invite from coworkers | Treat after-work socializing as strictly optional networking |
| Build in slack for meetings starting later than scheduled | Show up irritated when an 8am meeting doesn't start at 8am |

#### 🇵🇹 Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join colleagues for the daily *prato do dia* at a local *tasca* | Eat at your desk every day — it's one of the fastest ways to stay an outsider |
| Treat the mid-morning *bica* (espresso) as office currency, not caffeine | Skip coffee breaks to look busy — it can read as unfriendly |
| Keep business and social conversation separate unless the host raises work | Pitch business over a purely social lunch uninvited |
| Use appropriate titles and show deference to seniority in group settings | Assume Portuguese hierarchy is as flat as it might feel over lunch |
| Expect deadlines and meeting times to flex somewhat | Panic if a meeting starts a few minutes late |

---

Brazil's office rituals are built around the idea that trust precedes output. The cafezinho — small, strong, sweet, and offered everywhere from construction sites to boardrooms — functions as a recurring social punctuation mark through the day, and lunch, typically an hour or more even in busy offices, is treated as close to sacred. Brazilian work culture is explicitly relationship-based, according to cross-cultural business guides, meaning personal connection often takes precedence over strict deadline management, and it's genuinely common for colleagues to extend the relationship past the office into weekend barbecues. The flip side, familiar to anyone who's sat through a Brazilian 8am meeting that started at 8:20, is a looser relationship with scheduled time — not carelessness, but a cultural priority ordering where the person in front of you outranks the clock.

Portugal runs on a similar coffee-and-lunch rhythm but layers in more formality. The standard workweek is 40 hours, typically 9am to 6pm with an hour for lunch — though that hour "can often take longer, especially if you go to a restaurant," and eating at your desk daily is, per multiple workplace guides for expats, one of the fastest ways to mark yourself as not fully integrated. Where Brazil's warmth is loose and physical, Portugal's is more structured: workplaces tend to be hierarchical, with clear deference to seniority and titles, and communication is generally polite and indirect, with criticism delivered gently and open confrontation avoided even in casual settings. Socializing after work — drinks, long dinners — is genuinely important for building relationships, but it operates alongside, not instead of, a fairly traditional chain of command.

The Reckoning: Brazil's rituals flatten hierarchy through physical warmth and shared time — the boss drinks the same cafezinho, sits at the same lunch table, gets hugged same as anyone. Portugal's rituals build relationship on top of hierarchy rather than through it — you'll share the bica and the long lunch, but you'll still address your manager with appropriate formality doing it. Both cultures would be baffled by a culture that eats lunch at a desk in eleven minutes, but they'd be baffled for different reasons: Brazil would read it as a rejection of connection, Portugal would read it as a rejection of a shared daily rhythm that has nothing to do with rank. If you're used to Anglo directness, both will feel slow; the difference is that in Brazil, slow is intimate, and in Portugal, slow is simply correct.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone answering what Brazilian work culture looks like described it as warm and informal, where conversations start with small talk and real relationships get built over casual lunches — adding that colleagues become close much faster than the respondent was used to elsewhere.*

> *Quora — A separate respondent listing what foreigners find strange about Brazil noted the physicality of workplace interaction: frequent touching, cheek kisses on meeting someone of the opposite sex, and hugging, alongside a willingness to ask and share personal details early in a relationship.*

> *Quora — Someone describing Brazilian punctuality culture said flatly that people are always late — to 8am meetings, to 7pm dinners — but that once they arrive, the mood is warm enough that the wait stops feeling like a slight.*

> *teamblind.com — Employees discussing Nubank, one of Brazil's largest tech employers, described its "cool" and optional office attendance policy as a genuine outlier — reviewers explicitly noted this kind of flexible, come-if-you-want office culture is not the norm across most Brazilian companies, where physical presence and the shared rituals around it still matter.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Brazil, the practical adjustment is emotional rather than logistical: show up for the coffee, the lunch, and the hug, because skipping them is read as a statement about you, not just a scheduling choice. If you're moving to Portugal, the adjustment is to enjoy the same slow rhythm while still tracking who outranks whom in the room — the long lunch is real, but so is the org chart underneath it. Priya's honest take: neither country is going to rush its coffee for your quarterly targets, and the sooner you stop expecting them to, the sooner you'll actually enjoy the cafezinho.
