# Lunch Is the Meeting: How Mexico and Saudi Arabia Actually Get Business Done

**🇲🇽 Mexico · 🇸🇦 Saudi Arabia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Somewhere in a global onboarding deck, a newcomer to either country reads the phrase "relationship-driven business culture" and nods without absorbing what it costs in actual hours. In Mexico, the comida can run from two in the afternoon to five, with the first hour reserved entirely for soccer and family updates before a single number gets mentioned. In Saudi Arabia, a meeting doesn't start until the gahwa has been poured, sipped, and refilled at least once. Neither country considers this inefficiency. Both consider it the actual mechanism by which anything gets accomplished at all.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Block your calendar for a full two hours for any comida invitation — treating it as a quick 45-minute working lunch will read as rushed and slightly rude | Don't lead with business the moment you sit down; the first stretch of any meal is reserved for family, weekend plans, and small talk, and skipping it undermines the relationship-building purpose of the meal itself |
| Show up to Friday office botanas (snacks) and birthday cake celebrations in the breakroom, even briefly | Don't skip these casual gatherings repeatedly — colleagues read consistent absence as a signal you don't like them, which quietly makes it harder to get help on projects later |
| Ask about a colleague's family, recent trip, or weekend before any work topic, even in a rushed week | Don't assume a warm, personal lunch conversation means the business relationship is settled — genuine trust here is built over months of repeated meals, not one good conversation |
| Accept dinner or after-work invitations from Mexican colleagues when they come — they're a meaningful extension of the same relationship-building logic as the comida | Don't treat "let's grab a coffee sometime" as a throwaway pleasantry; in Mexican business culture it's frequently a genuine, expected follow-up |

#### 🇸🇦 Saudi Arabia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept gahwa (Arabic coffee) when it's offered at the start of any meeting or office visit — declining is read as declining the relationship, not just the drink | Don't rush past the coffee-and-small-talk opening of a meeting to get to the agenda; the ritual is the beginning of the business, not a delay of it |
| Learn basic majlis etiquette if you're invited to one — it's a genuine and common venue for relationship-building outside the office | Don't schedule anything that requires strict punctuality immediately after a business lunch; these meals routinely run long and cutting one short damages rapport |
| Invest specifically in personal relationships with Saudi colleagues and managers — credentials matter, but who knows and vouches for you carries comparable weight | Don't expect quick professional integration in your first months; expats consistently describe the initial period as one of deliberate, patient relationship-building rather than fast productivity |
| Respect gender-segregation norms and conservative dress expectations in shared social and dining spaces without needing repeated reminders | Don't assume compound-based expat social life (gyms, pools, international schools) substitutes for building actual relationships with Saudi colleagues — it's a comfort layer, not a shortcut to integration |

### Mexico: The Comida Is the Real Meeting

In Mexican business culture, per the Mexico Handbook and multiple workplace guides, the comida — the main meal of the day, typically eaten between 2pm and 4pm and often stretching well past that — functions as the actual site where trust gets built, not a break from the real work. InterExchange's comparison of Mexican and American work culture puts it plainly: small talk in Mexico "is the actual meeting," and jumping straight into deliverables is considered rude rather than efficient. Business only surfaces, typically, over dessert or the coffee that follows — after an hour or more of genuinely social conversation about soccer, politics, and family.

The social calendar inside the office matters just as much as the external one. TACNA's guide to Mexican workplace culture notes that skipping Friday botanas or a colleague's birthday cake in the breakroom isn't a neutral choice — colleagues read repeated absence as a signal of personal dislike, which has real downstream costs when you later need help on a deadline. The through-line across both settings is the same: Mexican workplace relationships are built cumulatively, meal by meal and gathering by gathering, and there is no efficient shortcut that skips the accumulation.

### Saudi Arabia: Coffee First, Then Everything Else

Saudi Arabia's version of the same principle runs through gahwa — Arabic coffee brewed in a long-necked dallah and served in small finjan cups — which Esquire Middle East describes as "about presence," meant to be lingered over rather than rushed through. CrossCulture2Go's guide to Saudi working style confirms the pattern holds structurally: meetings "usually begin with coffee or tea, as well as a period of small talk and socializing before diving into business matters," and the majlis — the traditional gathering space — remains a genuine, active venue where professional relationships develop outside formal office hours.

What distinguishes the Saudi version from Mexico's is the degree to which personal reputation and relationship carry direct professional weight. CurrencyTransfer's guide on working in Saudi Arabia notes that "your credentials are just as important as who you are and who you know" — a framing that makes the social rituals feel less like relationship-building on top of the job and more like an inseparable part of how the job itself gets evaluated and assigned. Compound living, common among expats, provides a comfortable parallel social world of gyms and international schools, but per multiple guides, genuinely integrating with Saudi colleagues still requires the deliberate, unhurried social investment the culture expects.

### The Reckoning

Both cultures treat the meal or the coffee as the meeting rather than a preamble to it, which is the single biggest adjustment for anyone arriving from a culture where lunch is something you eat at your desk between calls. The difference is in the shape of the social obligation: Mexico's runs through recurring, frequent, low-stakes group gatherings — the Friday snacks, the birthday cake, the long comida — that add up over time into trust. Saudi Arabia's runs through a smaller number of higher-ritual, higher-formality occasions — the gahwa-opened meeting, the majlis invitation — where the social signaling is denser per occasion but the occasions themselves are less frequent.

The trap for a newcomer is applying the wrong cadence to the wrong country: treating Mexican social obligations as occasional big events (and therefore skippable most weeks) misses how much weight the small, frequent gatherings carry, while treating Saudi ritual occasions as casual and interchangeable badly underestimates how much a single gahwa-opened meeting or majlis evening is doing for your standing.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering "What is business culture like in Mexico?", one contributor described their first comida with a client running nearly three hours, with the actual contract discussion taking up less than fifteen minutes at the very end — and noted that trying to speed up the process in later meetings visibly cooled the relationship rather than being appreciated as efficient.*

> *Quora — A contributor to "How is the life of an expat in Mexico?" described work culture shock not from the pace of business itself but from realizing that repeated invitations to casual lunches and after-work coffees were not social pleasantries to be politely deflected, but genuine expectations that, left unanswered, quietly closed doors later.*

> *Expat.com, Saudi Arabia forum ("The work culture in Saudi Arabia") — A contributor working in Riyadh described the first several months on the job as feeling unproductive by Western standards, since so much time went into relationship-building meetings before real project work began — a frustration they said resolved once they understood the coffee-and-conversation phase wasn't separate from being productive, it was the productive work.*

> *CurrencyTransfer, Saudi working-in guide — The piece specifically warns newcomers that scheduling anything requiring strict punctuality right after a business lunch is a common early mistake, since these meals routinely extend well past their expected length and cutting one short to make another appointment reads as a rebuff to the host.*

### Conclusion

The honest advice for either country is the same, even though the rituals look nothing alike: stop treating the social layer as overhead sitting on top of "real" work, because in both Mexico and Saudi Arabia, it functionally is the work. Clear your calendar generously, show up to the small recurring things in Mexico and the higher-formality occasions in Saudi Arabia, and resist every instinct to speed either one up. The colleague who treats the two-hour comida or the gahwa-opened meeting as an obstacle between them and the agenda will get through the agenda slower, not faster, than the one who simply accepts that in both places, lunch was never really about lunch.
