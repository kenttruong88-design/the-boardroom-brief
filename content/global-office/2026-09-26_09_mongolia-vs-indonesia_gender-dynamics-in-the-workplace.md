# Educate Them Thoroughly, Promote Them Sparingly: Gender Dynamics in Mongolia and Indonesia

**🇲🇳 Mongolia · 🇮🇩 Indonesia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Mongolia and Indonesia have almost nothing in common on paper — a landlocked Central Asian steppe nation of 3.4 million versus the world's largest Muslim-majority archipelago of over 270 million — and yet their workplaces have independently arrived at the identical arrangement. In Mongolia, over 60% of university graduates are women, and women still lead just 6% of the country's top 100 listed firms. In Indonesia, women make up 59% of tertiary graduates, and only 15% of enterprises have a female CEO. Educate them thoroughly, promote them sparingly: it turns out to be a remarkably portable system.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇳 Mongolia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Recognize that a female colleague is statistically more likely to hold a university degree than a male one | Assume educational credentials predict seniority the way they might elsewhere |
| Note that women dominate the civil service (roughly 60%) even where private-sector leadership lags | Assume public-sector gender balance means the private sector has caught up |
| Expect real, if slow, structural change — political candidate quotas are rising toward 40% by 2028 | Assume current representation numbers reflect where the country is headed, rather than where it's starting from |
| Ask directly about a company's specific leadership pipeline if advancement matters to you | Assume a mid-sized, women-friendly employer is representative of larger firms — female leadership drops sharply in bigger companies |
| Account for unpaid household labor — Mongolian women report roughly double the domestic workload of men | Assume equal formal employment access means equal capacity to pursue it |

#### 🇮🇩 Indonesia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clarify dress code expectations explicitly in writing, especially around religious attire like the hijab or niqab | Assume an unwritten norm will be applied consistently across offices or industries |
| Expect women to be well-represented in finance and HR functions specifically | Assume that representation extends evenly into strategic, P&L, or R&D-facing roles — it usually doesn't |
| Take workplace harassment reporting seriously — rates are meaningfully high, especially in manufacturing | Assume "high female workforce participation" numbers mean a fully safe or equitable environment |
| Look for the 22% of enterprises with women in top executive roles as the more realistic benchmark than the 15% CEO figure | Expect the average enterprise's board to be gender-balanced — only 8% currently are |
| Recognize that most companies now say gender diversity helps their business (77% in one survey) | Assume that stated belief has already translated into changed hiring or promotion practice |

Mongolia's gender story runs almost entirely backwards from the stereotype of a traditional, male-dominated frontier economy. Girls complete school at higher rates than boys, and women make up the clear majority of university graduates and roughly 60% of civil servants. And yet the country's own Labor Minister, in a 2024 interview, laid out the arc honestly: "In 1992, just one in 20 legislators in our parliament were women. Today, it is one in six" — real progress, but still a minority a full generation later. Private-sector leadership tells a starker story: women hold around 30% of middle-management roles but just 15% of senior management, and lead only about 6% of the country's top 100 listed firms — a figure that has actually *declined* from historical levels. Perhaps most striking, the gender gap in overall labor force participation more than doubled between 1996 and 2015, from 4.8 to 12.6 percentage points, moving in the wrong direction even as education outcomes improved. Mongolia isn't in Hofstede's original scored dataset, so no official power-distance number exists for comparison — but a pattern like this, worsening even as credentials rise, points toward something structural rather than a simple pipeline problem that will fix itself with time.

Indonesia's numbers describe nearly the same shape with different specifics. Women's share of tertiary graduates rose from 16% in 1993 to 59% by 2018, and women now dominate vocational and technical programs at 65% of graduates. Formal labor force participation sits at 51% for women against 78% for men — already a meaningful gap — but the real bottleneck appears higher up: only 15% of Indonesian enterprises have a female CEO, just 18% of board chairs are women, and a mere 8% of boards hit genuine gender balance. Researchers describe the mechanism as "glass walls" rather than a glass ceiling — women aren't just blocked from the top, they're channeled sideways into finance and HR rather than the strategic, profit-and-loss or R&D-facing roles that tend to lead there. Indonesia's Hofstede power-distance score sits at 78, notably high, and workplace hierarchy shapes how visibly any of this gets challenged from below. Practical, everyday friction shows up in dress code, too: Indonesian law doesn't ban face coverings like the niqab in private workplaces, but many companies handle the question reactively, without a written policy, which forum discussions describe creating real ambiguity and occasional workplace tension over communication and security norms.

**The Reckoning:** The genuinely striking part isn't that either country has a gender gap — plenty do — it's that both gaps sit at almost the exact same pinch point: the transition from "highly credentialed" to "actually running things." Mongolia's women outpace men in the classroom and in the civil service, and still lead a single-digit share of major companies. Indonesia's women dominate tertiary education and still hold the CEO seat in only 15% of enterprises. Neither country has an education problem to solve — they've already solved it, twice over. What both have is a promotion problem, and a Labor Minister's honest 32-year timeline (one in 20 legislators to one in six) is as good an estimate as any of how slowly that second problem tends to move even after the first one is fixed.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A recurring question asking about the general experience of doing business in Mongolia draws answers that consistently emphasize how relationship-driven and personally negotiated deals still are, a dynamic that several respondents connected to why formal credentials alone don't automatically translate into decision-making authority.*

> *IFC interview — Solongo Batbekh, a Mongolian business leader, said she personally hadn't faced major gender-specific obstacles thanks to her organization's balanced governance, but was candid that "there might be significant challenges for women in leadership roles" at smaller firms or organizations without similar support structures.*

> *Expat Indo Forum (expatindo.org) — A thread on dress code policy revealed how unresolved these questions still are in practice: one contributor noted that a full face covering made building security and CCTV compliance genuinely difficult, while another admitted that "trying to converse with someone with full facial covering is awkward" — friction that emerged only because no written policy existed to resolve it in advance.*

> *The Diplomat (interview with Mongolia's Labor Minister) — Asked about the pace of change, she cited hard numbers rather than sentiment: political candidate quotas rising from 30% to 40% by 2028, and a civil service already 60% female, framing the private-sector gap as the next, harder-to-legislate frontier.*

### Conclusion

If you're a woman weighing a move to either country for work, the useful question isn't "how educated are women here" — both places have already answered that emphatically — it's "how far up does that education actually carry you." Mongolia and Indonesia have each built genuinely strong pipelines of qualified women and then narrowed them sharply somewhere between middle management and the boardroom. Ask any employer you're considering, directly, how many women sit above the director level right now — the honest answer will tell you more about your actual ceiling than any national graduation statistic will.
