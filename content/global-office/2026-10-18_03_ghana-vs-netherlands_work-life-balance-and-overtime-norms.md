# Ghana vs. the Netherlands: One Clocks Out at Noon, the Other Never Quite Clocks In

🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇳🇱 Netherlands

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In the Netherlands, the average worker logged roughly 32.1 hours a week in 2024, according to Eurostat — the shortest standard workweek in Europe, propped up by a culture that treats "part-time" as a lifestyle choice rather than a career setback. In Ghana, the Labour Act caps the workweek at 40 hours and sets no statutory overtime rate at all, leaving the actual number negotiated case by case, invoice by invoice, "yes" by "yes." One country has engineered its way out of overtime through decades of deliberate scheduling. The other never quite engineered its way into enforcing the concept, and has instead developed an entire social vocabulary — "Ghana Man Time" chief among it — for living around the gap. Both will tell you, sincerely, that they have a healthy relationship with work. Neither is lying. They just mean two very different things by "healthy."

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build 30–60 minutes of flex into every meeting invite; a 9am start often means 9:30 or later | Don't schedule back-to-back meetings assuming each will start or end on time |
| Get overtime pay terms in writing before you need them — there is no statutory rate to fall back on | Don't treat a verbal "yes" as a signed commitment; follow up in writing or it may quietly evaporate |
| Keep mobile money (MoMo) loaded for transport and small payments; banking queues can eat a morning | Don't assume unhurried pace means low output — deadlines still exist, just with more patience attached |
| Confirm who is withholding tax on any overtime pay; employers can and do get this wrong | Don't skip the National Labour Commission if unpaid overtime becomes a pattern — it has real enforcement power |
| Learn basic greetings in the local language of your region (Twi, Ga, Ewe); relationships open doors contracts don't | Don't expect overtime pay without a prior agreement or collective bargaining clause specifying it |

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate your contracted hours upfront — 32, 36, and 40-hour weeks are all standard job postings | Don't email colleagues after 6pm expecting a same-day reply; it will arrive tomorrow, unapologetically |
| Put personal commitments directly in your shared calendar; colleagues plan around visible blocks without asking why | Don't interpret an early finish or part-time Friday as a lack of ambition |
| Leave at your contracted end time daily, without a justifying speech | Don't skip the agenda in meetings; unscheduled rambling reads as unprepared, not passionate |
| Confirm your statutory leave (minimum four times your weekly hours) and actually take it | Don't assume unpaid overtime is routine; extra hours are usually time-banked, not simply absorbed |
| Expect blunt, direct feedback as a style choice, not a personal attack | Don't be surprised when the "part-time" colleague outranks the "full-time" one |

### Ghana: Flexible by Necessity, Informal by Design

Ghana's Labour Act, 2013 (Act 651) sets the standard workweek at eight hours a day or 40 hours a week, with overtime triggered beyond that threshold. What it conspicuously does not do is fix an overtime rate. Employers set their own policy, or a union negotiates one through collective bargaining; in practice, Ghana Law Hub notes that 150 percent of the hourly rate on weekdays and 200 percent on weekends and public holidays has become informal standard practice, not law. The effect is a labor market where your overtime premium is less a right than a result of how good your contract — or your union — happens to be. The International Labour Organization's statistics on the country lean toward informal employment as the norm rather than the exception, which means a large share of the workforce operates entirely outside any overtime framework, written or otherwise.

What this produces, anecdotally and statistically, is less a culture of long hours than a culture of loosely bounded ones. The workday has official edges; whether anyone observes them is a separate negotiation, conducted daily, with patience as the primary currency. Meetings start when enough people have arrived. Deadlines are real, but the path to them tolerates more detours than a Dutch project manager would find comfortable. Hofstede Insights' country-comparison framework pegs Ghana with notably high power distance, which tracks with workplaces where hours and overtime expectations often flow from what a superior wants rather than from a clause in a contract.

Much of this plays out below the radar of any ministry. The International Labour Organization's statistics on Ghana lean heavily toward informal employment as the norm rather than the exception, which means a large share of the workforce — street vendors, drivers, small traders, informal-sector tradespeople — operates entirely outside any overtime framework, written or otherwise. For salaried staff in formal firms, the 2026 minimum daily wage of GHS 21.77, agreed through the National Tripartite Committee, sets a wage floor but still leaves overtime pay to employer policy or union muscle. A Gallup World Poll snapshot of workplace engagement across West Africa suggests Ghanaian workers report comparatively high day-to-day satisfaction despite this informality — a reminder that an unenforced boundary is not automatically an unhappy one.

### Netherlands: Scheduled, Short, and Suspicious of Overtime

The Netherlands has spent decades optimizing itself out of the long workweek. Eurostat's 2024 figures put the average Dutch worker at roughly 32.1 hours a week — the shortest in Europe, ahead of Austria, Germany, and Denmark, all of which still average closer to 34. The OECD has long tracked the Netherlands among the lowest annual-hours economies in its membership, a position built less on any single law than on a thoroughly normalized culture of part-time work: a large share of the Dutch workforce, including a majority of women, works part-time by choice, not by economic necessity, and the country adopted what sociologists call a "one-and-a-half earner" household model decades ago.

The InterNations Expat Insider survey backs this up from the newcomer's seat: 79 percent of expats rated their working hours positively and 78 percent rated their work-life balance positively, with flexible hours and the ability to work remotely cited as standout perks, and 76 percent saying that "New Work" — self-fulfilment and autonomy on the job — plays a meaningful role in Dutch business culture. Hofstede Insights scores the Netherlands remarkably low on its masculinity dimension — one of the lowest in the world — which in practice means a culture that prizes quality of life and consensus over competitive, hours-as-status signaling. Dutch labor law reinforces the instinct rather than merely reflecting it: employees have a statutory right to request a reduction in contracted hours, and the legal minimum paid leave is four times the weekly contracted hours, meaning a standard 36-hour-a-week employee banks at least 144 hours of vacation a year as a floor, not a favor. Staying late here does not read as commitment. It reads as poor planning.

### The Reckoning

Here is the trick: both countries will insist, correctly, that they respect work-life balance — they simply built the respect into opposite ends of the system. The Netherlands legislates and schedules its way to fewer hours, down to the half-hour, with contracts that specify exactly how part-time you'll be. Ghana has no such machinery; its flexibility is social rather than statutory, which means the same looseness that spares you a rigid 9-to-5 also spares your employer from having to pay you precisely for the extra hour you just worked.

The irony cuts both ways. A Dutch employee technically has stronger overtime protections on paper, yet overtime itself is rare enough that the protections rarely get tested — the system prevents the problem rather than remedying it. A Ghanaian employee has far weaker statutory backing, yet the informal culture around punctuality means fewer people are quietly furious about being asked to stay until 7pm, because 7pm was never a hard boundary anyone expected to hold in the first place. Short hours by design versus loose hours by custom: look similar in a brochure, feel completely different in your second month.

There is also a tax wrinkle worth knowing before you sign anything. Ghana actually taxes overtime pay at a reduced, simplified rate for lower-paid staff — five percent up to half of monthly basic salary, ten percent above it — which is arguably a more overtime-friendly tax regime than exists in the Netherlands, where additional earnings simply stack onto the regular progressive brackets. So the country with no statutory overtime rate taxes the overtime it does pay more gently than the country that has all but engineered overtime out of existence. File that under things nobody mentions in the relocation packet.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — a poster describing work-life balance at Booking.com's Amsterdam office called it genuinely strong: flexible hours, no real expectation of unpaid overtime, and a short bike commute that made the whole day feel less like a grind.*

> *Expat.com forum — a longtime resident of Accra described a 9am meeting routinely starting after 10, sometimes later, and warned that a spoken "yes" in a business conversation is sometimes just politeness rather than an actual commitment, so confirmations need to be chased in writing.*

> *A volunteer's blog account of a placement in Ghana (Verge Magazine) named "Ghana Man Time" as the hardest adjustment of the first weeks — a taxi once arrived three hours late with no warning — but came to see the slower rhythm as what made deeper relationships with colleagues and neighbors possible.*

> *An interview published on ioplus.nl with a Romanian professional in the Netherlands described the relief of a workplace built on "freedom within a framework," where being told explicitly not to take on too much work was a genuinely new experience after years in a more competitive outsourcing job back home.*

> *An interview with a Canadian who built a business in Ghana (Expat Arrivals) offered the advice that mattered most in hindsight: sort out your tax compliance properly, because that paperwork — not the pace of the workday — is what determines whether you get to stay.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between these two on work-life balance alone, the honest answer is that the Netherlands will protect your time better and Ghana will demand less emotional investment in protecting it yourself. Dutch structure means you rarely have to fight for your evenings, because the system already assumes you'll have them; Ghanaian flexibility means you may have to fight occasionally, but the fights are smaller and the culture doesn't moralize about ambition the way some do. Neither is objectively better — they are solving for different failure modes, one through regulation and scheduling, the other through social tolerance and informal reciprocity. The practical test is which failure mode you'd rather manage: a calendar that enforces boundaries you never had to ask for, or a culture that will forgive you for missing one, provided you extend the same forgiveness back.

What I'd actually tell a friend over a drink: go to the Netherlands if you want your calendar to do the enforcing for you, and go to Ghana if you're prepared to do your own enforcing but don't mind, most days, if nobody's really keeping score.
