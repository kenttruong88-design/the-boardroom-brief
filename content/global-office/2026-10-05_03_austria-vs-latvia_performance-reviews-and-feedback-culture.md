**Granteln vs. the Group Chat: How Austria and Latvia Actually Tell You You're Doing a Bad Job**

**🇦🇹 Austria · 🇱🇻 Latvia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Austria will deliver your performance review wrapped in enough qualifiers and coffee-and-cake small talk that you may not notice you've been criticized until the following Tuesday. Latvia will deliver it in roughly the same number of words a weather report uses, with about the same emotional investment. Both countries would tell you, if asked, that they're simply being honest. They mean two very different things by it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Listen for vague phrasing as the real signal — "let's see…" is often a soft no, and criticism arrives wrapped, not blunt | Expect your manager to say "this is wrong" outright; read between the diplomatic lines instead |
| Use professional titles and academic credentials (Mag., Dr., Ing.) until told otherwise — Austrians track qualifications closely | Treat frequent Kaffee und Kuchen breaks as idle time; relationship-building happens there, feedback context included |
| Leave work on time and expect your colleagues to do the same — staying late reads as poor planning, not dedication | Push back loudly in a group setting if feedback stings; raise it quietly, one-on-one, afterward |
| Prepare thoroughly before a review — "reliability, preparation, accuracy, structure" are the values being judged | Assume silence in a meeting means agreement; it frequently means the opposite |
| Expect decisions and evaluations to move top-down, however diplomatically delivered | Confuse indirectness with softness — accuracy and deadlines are still non-negotiable underneath the politeness |

#### 🇱🇻 Latvia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect feedback stripped to its content — no greeting, no cushioning, just the point | Take blunt phrasing personally; it's the local operating system, not a judgment of you specifically |
| Get comfortable with minimal small talk in work emails and reviews — brevity signals efficiency, not hostility | Expect a "Dear [Name], hope you're well" before the actual feedback; it usually isn't coming |
| Let your work speak for itself — Latvians tend to compartmentalize job performance from personal warmth | Mistake privacy and reserve for coldness; earned familiarity, not instant friendliness, is the local default |
| Push for structured calibration processes if your company has them — only about a quarter currently use them, and they measurably help | Assume informal chumminess from a manager (outside a handful of industries) means the review will be gentler |
| Learn a few words of Latvian; language effort is read as respect in ways that outrank material success | Expect diversity and inclusion criteria to be a default part of your review — survey data shows most companies don't yet weight them |

### Austria: The Criticism Is in There Somewhere

Austrian feedback culture runs on what Workaround's guide to Austrian office norms calls indirect communication: "'No' might sound like 'Let's see…'" and critical feedback arrives "wrapped in vague phrasing," with direct confrontation genuinely rare. Silence in a meeting can mean disagreement rather than consent — a fact that trips up plenty of newcomers who read a quiet room as a green light. None of this is a failure to have standards; the same guide describes Austrian workplace values as "reliability, preparation, accuracy, structure," with deadlines respected and quality treated as non-negotiable. The diplomacy is in the delivery, not the bar.

Hierarchy shapes how that delivery lands. Titles and academic credentials — Mag., Dr., Ing. — aren't vanity; The Local's reporting on Austrian work culture quotes one foreign hire noting "everyone loves a title here and I've been quizzed by colleagues about my qualifications," a reminder that status markers do real work in how seriously your input (and your review) gets taken. Work-life boundaries, by contrast, run looser than the hierarchy might suggest: a British designer interviewed by The Local described a culture where "you work the hours needed and no more… it's frowned upon when you stay late," with Fridays often ending early and long, actively encouraged parental leave treated as normal rather than a career risk. A former British diplomat posted to Vienna described the city's particular social texture as "Wiener Schmäh" — a blend of charm and a kind of ritualized complaining ("granteln") that outsiders initially misread as negativity but that locals treat as a national pastime, not a mood.

### Latvia: Content Over Cushioning

Latvia's feedback style is, by most firsthand accounts, close to the opposite of Austria's: minimal warmth, maximum content. One long-time resident's account of workplace communication in Riga put it plainly — colleagues asked her friend "but why, why do you smile all the time?" — and emails commonly skip greetings entirely in favor of terse subject-line efficiency. The trade-off, as the same account notes, is genuine: "we do get a heck of a lot done," with time that other cultures spend on pleasantries redirected into actual output.

But the formal machinery behind that bluntness has real gaps. A study from the Stockholm School of Economics in Riga found that while 87% of surveyed organizations consider performance evaluation integral to meeting strategic goals, roughly 25% admit their processes don't actually achieve that, and about half say current practices fail to attract, retain, and motivate employees. Only 26% of surveyed companies use calibration committees — a structured safeguard against reviewer bias — and the same research flagged that nearly two-thirds of respondents don't weight diversity and inclusion in evaluations at all, which researchers linked to lingering conservative social norms. Latvia's reported gender pay gap in identical roles sits at a comparatively modest 4.7%, suggesting the directness cuts both ways: less padding, but also less formal structure catching what the bluntness misses. A Latvian cultural commentator summed up the deeper pattern well: outsiders "often misinterpret Latvian reserve as coldness," when it reflects a preference for earned intimacy over instant familiarity — including, it turns out, at review time.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely counterintuitive part: Austria, a German-speaking country with a reputation (largely inherited from its neighbor) for blunt directness, actually delivers feedback indirectly and diplomatically — while Latvia, a reserved Baltic state with a quieter international reputation, delivers it with almost no cushioning at all. If you walked in assuming "Germanic efficiency" would predict Austrian review culture, you'd be backwards; if you assumed Baltic reserve meant gentle, conflict-averse managers in Latvia, you'd be backwards there too.

The second divergence is structural rather than stylistic. Austria's feedback is wrapped but the underlying system — titled hierarchy, credential-checking, top-down evaluation — is unmistakably rigorous. Latvia's feedback is unwrapped, but the surrounding system, per the SSE Riga data, is still catching up: good at saying the quiet part out loud, less consistent at building the calibration and inclusion infrastructure to make that bluntness fair across the board.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Local (Austria) — A British designer working in Vienna described the office norm plainly: "you work the hours needed and no more, which is great, and it's frowned upon when you stay late" — a practical, pleasant surprise for anyone arriving from a culture that treats long hours as a loyalty signal.*

> *The Local (Austria) — The same reporting captured a cultural misread in the making: a foreign hire noted being "quizzed by colleagues about my qualifications" almost as a matter of course, a reminder that credentials function as a quiet form of ongoing performance review in Austrian offices, independent of the official one.*

> *FCDO diplomat's blog, Vienna — A former British diplomat posted to Austria described "Wiener Schmäh" — a local blend of charm and ritualized complaining ("granteln") — as a communication style outsiders often mistake for genuine negativity before realizing it's closer to a civic sport.*

> *Independent author blog ("How to Piss Off a Latvian") — The author's practical advice for newcomers doubled as a warning about reading Latvian reserve correctly: "surnames are rarely given during introductions, and you can spend years not knowing your neighbours' names," and what outsiders read as coldness is better understood as a culture that extends warmth only once it's earned — including, by extension, at review time.*

> *Life in Riga (personal blog) — A long-term resident described the daily reality of Latvian workplace emails bluntly: colleagues write terse notes like "FYA" with no greeting at all, which initially read as rude before the author concluded the trade-off was fair — "we do get a heck of a lot done" — once she stopped expecting warmth that wasn't coming.*

### Conclusion

If you need your feedback cushioned so you can hear it without flinching, Austria will do that for you — just budget extra time to decode what was actually said. If you'd rather know exactly where you stand in the first sentence, even if nobody asks how your weekend was first, Latvia will get there faster, though you may be managing the structural gaps (bias checks, inclusion criteria) yourself. The honest version, for either: the volume of the delivery tells you nothing about the rigor behind it. Austria is diplomatically strict. Latvia is bluntly inconsistent. Neither one is being rude on purpose — they're just optimizing for different things, and neither of them is optimizing for your comfort.
