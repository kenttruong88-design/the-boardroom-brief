**The Compliment Sandwich Doesn't Cross Customs: Performance Reviews in the USA vs. Israel**

**🇺🇸 USA · 🇮🇱 Israel**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In America, a manager might spend four sentences softening the fifth one, which is the actual point. In Israel, the fifth sentence comes first, arrives without warning, and is often followed by an argument you're expected to win if your idea is actually good. Only 14% of American employees say their performance reviews inspire them to improve, according to Gallup — which raises the question of what, exactly, all that padding is for.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Document your wins year-round for the annual review | Assume your manager remembers your Q1 accomplishments in Q4 |
| Ask directly for more frequent, informal feedback | Wait passively for the once-a-year sit-down to learn where you stand |
| Treat self-assessments as persuasive writing, not confession | Undersell yourself out of modesty — no one else will sell you up |
| Expect feedback wrapped in context and next steps | Expect blunt, unqualified criticism without a path forward |
| Use the review to negotiate scope, title, or comp | Treat the review as purely retrospective |

#### 🇮🇱 Israel

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Argue back when you disagree — it's expected, not insubordinate | Take a blunt critique of your idea as a critique of you |
| Get comfortable with feedback arriving in real time, not on a schedule | Wait for a formal quarterly review to raise a concern |
| Speak up regardless of your title or tenure | Assume seniority buys you deference in a meeting |
| Read directness as engagement — people don't bother arguing with work they don't respect | Mistake informality for a lack of high standards |
| Treat a manager's "suggestion" as a probable instruction the second time it's repeated | Ignore a repeated request because it wasn't phrased as an order |

Formal, annual, and increasingly distrusted describes the American performance review about as well as anything. Gallup reports that 76% of U.S. organizations still evaluate employees once a year, even though 45% of HR leaders privately don't think that cadence produces an accurate picture of anyone's work. Only 29% of employees strongly agree their reviews are fair, and just 26% think they're accurate. The instinct to soften bad news — the so-called compliment sandwich — is a symptom of a system that treats feedback as a formal event requiring the right packaging, delivered by a manager who has, per Gallup, roughly one shot a year to get the framing right. The upside: when it's done well, American reviews tend to come with actual scaffolding — development plans, calibration against peers, a clear line from evaluation to raise. The downside is that a full year can pass before an employee learns that something they've been doing since March isn't working.

Israel skips the packaging. Cultural-intelligence researchers at Hofstede Insights score the country at 13 on power distance — one of the lowest in the world — which is the academic way of saying an intern can tell a VP their plan has a hole in it, in the same meeting, without anyone treating it as a career risk. The concept locals call *dugri* (roughly, "straight talk") frames bluntness as a form of respect: if a colleague argues with your idea, it's because they think it's worth arguing with. Feedback in Israeli workplaces tends to be frequent, informal, and unscheduled rather than confined to an annual ritual — a habit reinforced, according to workplace guides like Route 38's Israel briefing, by a culture where flat hierarchies and improvisational, fast-pivot norms (traced partly to a national service system where 20-year-olds run operations under pressure) make waiting a year to say something feel almost absurd.

The Reckoning is where the two systems' actual incentives show. American reviews are built to protect against liability and inconsistency — hence the calibration meetings, the documentation, the HR involvement — which makes them slow, political, and, per Gallup's own data, only modestly effective at changing behavior. Israeli feedback is built to protect the idea, not the person delivering it or receiving it, which makes it fast and unfiltered but offers no formal record when review season (if there is one) actually arrives. The irony is that the culture famous for gentleness produces less real-time information, while the culture famous for bluntness produces almost no paper trail. Neither one has solved the actual problem, which is that people want to know how they're doing without waiting for a form.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *teamblind.com — An American employee who joined a fast-growing Israeli-founded company's U.S. arm described the feedback aimed at their team as "harsh and cut-throat" compared to what they were used to, and said the tone took real adjustment before it stopped feeling personal.*

> *teamblind.com — In a thread from someone about to work under a manager with an Israeli background, longtime commenters advised arguing back rather than deferring, on the theory that the best idea wins the room regardless of who's speaking — and warned that the humor that goes with it can land as mean to American ears before it starts landing as funny.*

> *Quora — Someone comparing the two systems noted that Israeli employees often initially treat a manager's request as more of a strong suggestion than a directive, and only fully comply once the manager repeats it — a pattern that reads as insubordination to American eyes but is closer to a negotiating reflex.*

> *Quora — A respondent fielding recruiter outreach for U.S.-based roles at Israeli startups described the pace as "get-shit-done," with rapid pivots and multitasking that some found stressful and others found more honest than the deliberate, meeting-heavy cadence of American corporate life.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving from the U.S. to Israel, the adjustment isn't learning to take criticism — it's learning that criticism isn't a verdict on your standing, just a data point in an ongoing argument you're allowed to win. If you're moving the other way, the adjustment is patience: American systems ask you to wait for the formal moment, then trust that the formal moment actually reflects the work. Neither habit is objectively healthier; they're just optimized for different fears — Israel's system is built by people unafraid of conflict, America's by people trying to avoid a lawsuit. The honest advice, over a drink: figure out which fear you'd rather live inside, because you'll be living there for a while.
