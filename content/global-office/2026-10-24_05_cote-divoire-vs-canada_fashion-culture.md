# The Suit Is Optional in Toronto. In Abidjan, It Is a Statement.

🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Canadian engineer relocating to Abidjan will likely pack fewer blazers than the one relocating to Toronto, on the theory that West Africa is hot and informal. This is the most expensive wardrobe assumption either could make. In Abidjan, a pressed shirt and clean shoes are a baseline sign of self-respect, read even by the man selling phone credit on the corner; in Toronto, roughly three in four accounting and finance departments now call their dress code "somewhat" or "very" casual, per a Robert Half survey of 270 Canadian CFOs. One country treats looking sharp as a civic duty. The other treats it as a personality trait it would rather not have.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in at least two well-tailored outfits before you arrive; a good local tailor is cheap but takes time to find | Assume heat is an excuse for flip-flops and gym shorts at the office — it isn't |
| Buy natural fibers (cotton, linen) over synthetics; Abidjan humidity punishes polyester | Wear your most delicate fabrics on day one — sweat and street dust will ruin them fast |
| Keep one formal outfit ready for unannounced events — weddings, funerals, and client visits happen on short notice | Show up underdressed to a first meeting with a government office or bank; shoes and ironing are noticed |
| Expect colleagues to dress up, not down, as seniority increases | Treat pagne (wax-print wrap fabric) as costume; it's worn seriously, including in some offices on Fridays |
| Get shoes resoled rather than replaced; a well-kept pair signals more than a new cheap one | Over-pack formal wear for leisure time — weekends and markets are far more relaxed |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy one genuinely warm coat before your first winter, even if it costs more than you'd like | Rely on fashion-forward thin coats bought for looks; frostbite doesn't care about tailoring |
| Default to "business casual" unless told otherwise, and watch what your specific office actually wears in week one | Assume "casual" means no floor at all; most offices still expect clean, intentional clothing |
| Layer — thermal base, mid layer, shell — rather than one heavy item | Wear cotton as a base layer in deep cold; it holds sweat and gets you colder faster |
| Shop end-of-season sales or secondhand (Winners, Marshalls, Kijiji) for winter gear; quality outerwear is expensive new | Feel obligated to buy a premium status parka to fit in; plenty of senior staff wear basic gear |
| Keep one smart outfit for client-facing days, even in a casual-coded industry | Show up in pyjama-adjacent clothing just because a coworker did; some workplaces still quietly judge it |

Côte d'Ivoire's relationship to dress is best understood through economics, not vanity. The Institut National de la Statistique estimates that roughly 93 percent of the active population works informally, a sector contributing up to 60 percent of GDP — meaning appearance is rarely subsidized by a corporate casual-Friday memo. It is a personal investment, made heavier by the fact that the country is one of Africa's largest cotton producers, with a textile and garment economy the World Bank has tracked for decades as a potential engine of formal job creation. Clothing here is a livelihood, an export ambition, and a daily performance of dignity at once, where looking precarious can cost the sale or the loan officer's attention.

That performance has an increasingly international audience: Ivorian designer Loza Maleombho built an Abidjan-based label that found its way, via Instagram, into the wardrobe of Beyoncé's stylist — proof Ivorian fashion runs confidently opposite "developing-economy modesty." Pagne, the wax-print wrap fabric associated with West Africa generally, is not a tourist souvenir here; it is formalwear, sometimes office wear, and a live political subject regionally — neighboring Gabon's 2024 decree requiring civil servants to wear African dress on set days, and the debate over who pays for the fabric, shows how seriously these governments take sartorial nationalism. An expatriate who treats Ivorian formality as theater, rather than the social contract it is, gets quietly recategorized as not worth taking seriously.

Canada's dress culture has moved the opposite way for reasons that are structural, not aesthetic. Indeed's Hiring Lab found casual-attire mentions in Canadian job postings peaked at 6.7 percent in mid-2022, settling around 5.3 percent since — Quebec leads provinces at 6.9 percent, while childcare, sports, and cleaning roles far outpace tech (software postings sat at 2.2 percent). Research from McMaster University's DeGroote School of Business links the shift less to generational rebellion than to cost: a separate "work wardrobe" is expensive, dry cleaning a recurring tax, and hybrid work blurred the line between a video call and a grocery run. Hofstede Insights, which rates Canada far higher on individualism than Côte d'Ivoire's West Africa cultural cluster, offers a tidy footnote: where comfort outranks conformity, "dress for yourself" wins by default — even when that self prefers fleece.

### The Reckoning

The country with less disposable income spends more effort and money looking presentable; the wealthier one has spent a decade engineering its workers out of looking presentable at all, and calling it progress. In Abidjan, dressing well is a hedge against being underestimated in a system with few formal safety nets — you dress for the bank, the client, the ceremony, because the alternative is being filtered out before you speak. In Toronto or Montreal, the hedge runs the other way: dressing too formally can read as try-hard, insecure, or — worse in Canadian workplace mythology — American.

Both cultures, in their own idiom, remain rule-bound about clothing; neither is actually "relaxed" once examined closely. Canada's casualness has its own unwritten floor — jeans yes, visible pyjamas generally no — policed by raised eyebrows as rigorously as Abidjan's ceiling. The newcomer who thinks one country has rules about clothes and the other doesn't simply hasn't been told the rules yet.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Talesmag Real Post Reports (Abidjan) — a respondent described Ivorian colleagues as formally dressed to a degree that surprised newly arrived diplomats, in suits or elaborate traditional outfits despite the humidity, and advised sticking to natural fibers since sweat and street conditions ruin synthetics fast.*

> *r/MovingToCanada — a newcomer asked what to actually buy for their first Canadian winter, and the thread's top advice was to skip fashionable thin coats entirely for a genuine knee-length parka, with several commenters calling premium brands like Canada Goose overpriced status items rather than a functional necessity.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — a tech worker recounted that an insurance employer let its engineers stop wearing ties, framed as a perk, while noting the job itself paid roughly a third less than their current formal-dress-code role — a reminder that "casual" and "well-compensated" don't reliably travel together.*

> *pvtistes.net — a francophone community for workers on Canadian working-holiday visas warned newcomers not to wait until arrival to buy winter gear, noting a reliable new coat in Canada starts around 250 Canadian dollars, with secondhand options via Kijiji available for a fraction of that if bought early.*

### Conclusion

The practical difference that matters is not formality versus casualness — it is who absorbs the cost of getting it wrong. In Côte d'Ivoire, under-dressing costs you immediately, in a meeting or at a bank counter; a couple of good outfits is cheap insurance. In Canada, the cost is seasonal and literal — a bad coat is a health risk before a fashion failure — while over-dressing socially just earns raised eyebrows and a nickname. Pack for the floor that actually exists, not the one the stereotype suggests: Abidjan's is higher than the heat implies, Toronto's casual ceiling lower than the fleece vests suggest.

If a friend asked me over a drink: in Abidjan, buy the tailor's phone number before you buy anything else; in Toronto, buy the coat before you buy anything else — and in both cities, resist the urge to dress for the climate you expected rather than the one, and the office, you actually got.
