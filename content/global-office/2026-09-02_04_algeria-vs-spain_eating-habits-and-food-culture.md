# Two Ways to Never Finish Your Workday Before the Meal Does: Algeria vs. Spain

🇩🇿 Algeria · 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Algeria, the state imports roughly two-thirds of the wheat its population eats and still manages to serve a stranger the best cut of meat on the plate; in Spain, the workday famously stops for two hours at lunch, and yet Spaniards clock some of the longest working hours in the European Union. Both countries have built entire economies of time around the table. Neither, it turns out, is doing what the postcard implies.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇿 Algeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the first glass of tea or coffee offered in a meeting | Decline hospitality outright, even to save time |
| Eat from a shared dish with your right hand | Reach into a communal plate with your left hand |
| Treat 1–3pm as the day's real main event and plan around it | Schedule back-to-back meetings straight through lunch |
| Step away and eat privately during Ramadan working hours | Eat or drink visibly at your desk in front of fasting colleagues |
| Praise the couscous, generously and often | Ask whether it's "better than Moroccan food" |
| Stock up early on imported staples you can't live without | Assume jarred sauces, liquid milk or chicken sausage will be on the shelf |

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Block out 90 minutes to two hours for lunch outside big corporate offices | Expect to schedule a client call through the 2–4pm window |
| Linger for *sobremesa*, the conversation after the plates are cleared | Stand up and leave the moment the meal ends |
| Push dinner reservations to 9 or 10pm | Expect restaurants to be lively — or even open — before 8:30pm |
| Order the *menú del día* for a proper multi-course lunch deal | Assume a heavy lunch means a light dinner |
| Let coffee arrive before steering the conversation to business | Open with the agenda before the coffee does |
| Pace your week around a long day, not a short one | Mistake the long lunch for a short workday — the OECD says otherwise |

### Algeria: A Feast Built on Someone Else's Wheat

Algeria's table runs on couscous, semolina and bread, and by the numbers it runs on borrowed grain: wheat supplies roughly 60% of the average Algerian's caloric intake and about 70% of dietary protein, according to USDA GAIN Grain and Feed reporting compiled from FAO-aligned data, and the country now imports the majority of that wheat — recent marketing years have seen imports climb past 8 million metric tons, with domestic production covering only a fraction of the roughly 220 kg per person Algerians consume annually. Food-security analysts put Algeria's cereal-import dependency at around 70%. None of this arithmetic is visible at the lunch table, which is precisely the point: lunch, served between 1 and 3pm as shops close and families reconvene, remains the largest meal of the day, and hospitality norms treat a guest's plate as sacred regardless of what the balance sheet says.

That hospitality extends deliberately into the workplace. Business meetings open with tea or coffee, and declining it is rarely read as neutral — a curt "no thank you" can register as a rejection of the relationship, not just the drink, according to guidance compiled by Commisceo Global and Rivermate on Algerian business etiquette. During Ramadan the etiquette inverts: non-fasting colleagues are expected to eat out of sight rather than at their desks, a norm several expat guides describe as unenforced by law but firmly enforced by disapproval. The overall effect is a food culture organized less around efficiency than around not, ever, appearing stingy — with imported grain or with time.

### Spain: The Two-Hour Lunch That Isn't Laziness

Spain's long lunch gets blamed on the siesta, but the siesta itself has mostly left the office; what remains is the schedule the siesta built. Lunch at 2–3pm, historically followed by a break through the hottest hours and a return to work until sundown, still shapes how restaurants and, in many sectors, offices run — the government once mandated an affordable multi-course *menú del día* specifically to feed workers through that stretch. Dinner then lands at 10pm, with restaurants barely filling before 9. According to OECD data, Spaniards work among the longest hours in the EU; the midday gap doesn't shorten the day so much as move dinner to where other countries would put a nightcap.

The health data complicates the stereotype further. OECD Health at a Glance 2025 puts Spain's self-reported obesity rate at 15%, below the OECD average of 19%, and life expectancy at 84 years, nearly three years above the OECD average — figures that sit awkwardly next to reporting on the Mediterranean diet's decline and rising ultra-processed food consumption in Spanish households. Whatever Spain is doing with its schedule, it has not yet caught up to it nutritionally in the way outsiders assume; the postcard version of the Mediterranean diet is aging out even as the postcard version of the lunch break survives.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the irony sharpens. Algeria's workday breaks for a home-cooked, generosity-soaked lunch built substantially on wheat the country doesn't grow; Spain's workday breaks for a leisurely restaurant lunch that outsiders read as evidence of a shorter week, when OECD figures say the opposite. Each country's eating habits have been misdiagnosed by the same lazy shorthand — Algeria as scarcity-driven, Spain as indolence-driven — and each shorthand collapses on contact with the actual numbers.

The more useful comparison isn't abundance versus leisure but what each culture protects with its mealtime rules. Algeria protects the guest's plate and the fasting colleague's dignity; Spain protects the *sobremesa* and the late dinner as non-negotiable social infrastructure. Neither is optimizing for a lunch break that gets you back to your desk fast — which is, this being The Global Office, precisely the metric most newcomers arrive expecting to matter.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Medium (personal travel blog) — A newly arrived expat in Algiers described weeks of culinary defeat: no liquid milk that wouldn't curdle in coffee, no chicken sausage, no jarred sauces, and an eventual surrender to street-food stalls for daily meals instead of the home-cooked dinners she'd grown used to.*

> *Quora — A respondent describing early business meetings in Algeria recounted declining an offered glass of tea to save time on a tight schedule, then sensing the room cool; a colleague later explained that turning down hospitality reads as turning down the relationship, not the drink.*

> *Expat Exchange — Guidance compiled from residents in Algeria advised newcomers to quietly step into a private office or break room to eat during Ramadan working hours, since eating visibly in front of fasting coworkers, while not against the law, is taken as a provocation.*

> *UD Abroad (study-abroad blog) — A student living with a host family in Spain described the disorientation of lunch at 2pm and dinner at 10pm, saying it took weeks before her hunger stopped arriving hours ahead of the "correct" Spanish mealtime.*

> *Quora — A long-term resident answering a question on why Spaniards eat so late wrote that the two-hour lunches that once felt like lost productivity became, after moving away, the single part of Spanish life they missed most.*

### Conclusion

The practical advice is almost embarrassingly simple: in Algeria, don't schedule anything important through 1–3pm and don't say no to the tea; in Spain, don't schedule anything at all through 2–4pm and don't expect dinner reservations before nine. Bring an appetite and, more usefully, bring patience for a system that has already decided what time you'll eat regardless of your calendar. The honest summary is that both countries have simply decided the workday can wait for the meal — Algeria because refusing food is an insult, Spain because the meal is the actual point of the day, and the work is what happens in between.
