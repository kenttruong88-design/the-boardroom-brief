**Jollof at Your Desk, Pints After Six: What "Team Bonding" Actually Costs You in Lagos and London**

**🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Lagos, a junior staffer who skips the office birthday contribution is the office villain by Friday; in London, a junior staffer who skips the office birthday contribution is simply at their desk, unbothered, while everyone else argues about a cake fund over Slack. Both offices call this "team culture." Only one of them means it as a threat.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet everyone individually each morning — skipping it reads as rude, not efficient | Walk past a superior's office without a greeting, even if you're late |
| Contribute to the lunch/birthday/send-off pool when it's passed around | Opt out of group contributions without a polite, specific excuse |
| Accept food offered at a colleague's desk, even a token taste | Refuse food outright — a small portion is the graceful move |
| Expect the driver or office assistant to run the lunch errand for senior staff | Assume this is laziness rather than how status and support roles work |
| Treat the office generator noise and power cuts as normal small talk, not a complaint session | Vent publicly about infrastructure to someone senior on day one |
| Show up to the send-off party, even briefly, when a colleague leaves | Send regrets by text and expect no social cost |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go to the pub after work once in a while, even for one soft drink | Make your absence a running theme — occasional no-shows are fine, total absence is noted |
| Let colleagues order first if you're unsure of the tab etiquette | Assume a work drink is ever truly optional if your manager is buying the round |
| Eat lunch at your desk without apology — it's the norm, not a faux pas | Expect a leisurely hour-long lunch; the average is 34 minutes and shrinking |
| Use understatement; "not bad" can mean genuinely good | Take enthusiasm at face value, or the lack of it as hostility |
| RSVP honestly to the Christmas party and leave at a sensible hour | Overshare personal or political views once the second round arrives |
| Treat hybrid schedules as real — check which days the team is actually in | Show up on your contracted office day assuming everyone else will too |

### The Sign-Off You Can't Skip

In Lagos, the question is never whether your colleagues matter — in a labour market where the Nigeria Bureau of Statistics puts informal employment at 93% of total employment and self-employment at 85.6% of the working class (Nairametrics, citing NBS Q2 2024 figures), simply having a formal office job already places you in a visible, relatively small club. That club runs on obligation. Mornings open with individual greetings down the corridor — a ritual that, as one British expat recalled to Expat Arrivals, took real getting used to, since asking after everyone's health and evening "can delay getting to your desk" by a meaningful margin. Seniority is performed daily: Hofstede Insights scores Nigeria at 80 on power distance against the UK's 35, and an assistant fetching a director's lunch is not an imposition, it is the organisational chart made edible. Gallup's 2026 State of the Global Workplace data puts Nigerian employee engagement at just 17%, with 55% of workers reporting a lot of stress the previous day — numbers that make the birthday cake fund and the Friday send-off look less like fun and more like the glue holding a stressed system together.

The emotional register is also calibrated differently: Nigeria scores 84 on Hofstede's indulgence dimension, among the highest in the world, which tracks with the sheer enthusiasm Nigerian offices bring to celebration — the leaving party, the promotion lunch, the "owambe" energy applied to a Tuesday afternoon. FM Magazine's business-culture briefing for Lagos newcomers puts it plainly: earning personal trust is what makes business happen at all, and that trust is built in person, over food, not over email, because email and the phone network cannot always be relied on to work.

Cross the Hofstede line into the UK and the warmth doesn't disappear, it just gets rationed and scheduled. Individualism sits at 89 against Nigeria's 30, and the signature ritual — the after-work pint — has quietly become optional in theory and fraught in practice. Nigel Frank International's workforce survey found 22% of UK employees feel pressured to socialise with colleagues outside work, rising to 29% in London and hitting 56% among women who report that pressure; separately, a CEBR report commissioned by Heineken claims after-work socialising adds £28.9bn to the UK economy and that 72% of workers believe relationships are best built outside the office — a claim worth reading with the brewer's commercial interest in mind, though the underlying behavioural pattern tracks with other, less self-interested survey data.

Lunch, meanwhile, has nearly vanished as a social event. Workthere's survey of 2,000 British workers, reported by HRreview, found the average UK lunch break down to 34 minutes, with 52% of workers skipping it altogether and most eating at their desks four days out of five. And the office itself is a part-time stage: ONS figures show hybrid working rose from roughly 10% of staff in early 2021 to 28% by the first quarter of 2025, while Indeed's UK hiring data shows employers quietly tightening the screws, with 85% of hybrid job postings in 2025 requiring at least two office days, up from 65% in 2022. The ritual survives; the room it happens in is increasingly conditional.

### The Reckoning

Here is the irony: Nigeria, scored as the more collectivist, high-power-distance culture, makes its office social rituals explicit, compulsory, and warm — you know exactly what's expected, and the expectation is attendance. The UK, scored as one of the most individualist cultures on the planet, makes its rituals implicit, "optional," and quietly load-bearing for your career — nobody will ever tell you the pub round matters, and then you'll notice who gets mentioned for the promotion.

Put differently, Lagos asks for your time and gives you clear rules in return. London asks for nothing and tests you on whether you correctly inferred the rules anyway. A Nigerian transplant who tells colleagues "I don't drink" gets a shrug in Lagos and a seat at the table regardless; the same sentence in a London office, per Ask a Manager's archive of workplace letters, can trigger a genuine crisis of belonging for the person saying it, even when every manager insists, on the record, that nobody is required to drink.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — A commenter recalling 1980s and '90s British software jobs described Friday as effectively a half-day: the team went to the pub at lunchtime and, as the running joke put it, came back to the office mainly "to log off and collect my coat." They note the habit faded by the late 1990s as computerisation, longer commutes and stricter drink-driving enforcement took hold, and that younger colleagues now drink far less and socialise elsewhere.*

> *Ask a Manager — A university intern on a UK work placement described a team that went to the pub several nights a week, sometimes with the boss buying shots, and worried that declining — which they did, going to bed early instead — would read as aloof or uncommitted ahead of a reference and a hoped-for return offer. The advice back was that genuine pressure to drink is rare and a red flag, and that showing up occasionally, sober, for an hour, is enough.*

> *Lemmy (a Reddit-alternative discussion forum) — A UK-based commenter noted that drinking at lunchtime in Britain now reads as a problem rather than normal, tying this to a culture where people tend to drink heavily in one sitting rather than socially across the day; another recalled their own office's drinking moving from lunchtime pub visits in 2000, to after-work-only, to effectively nothing by their later career.*

> *Zikoko's Abroad Life — A Nigerian software developer who relocated to a UK tech job and doesn't drink said he skipped pub culture entirely and instead invited colleagues to his home and cooked for them, calling it a deliberate effort to bond on his own terms; he also recalled messaging his manager at 2 a.m. after finishing a task and getting back only, "Oh, thank you. Please get some sleep," a response he contrasted with founders in Nigerian startups who treated constant availability as the baseline.*

> *Expat Arrivals — A British expat in Nigeria said Nigerian colleagues' habit of greeting each other warmly and individually every morning took adjustment, since it visibly delays the start of the workday, and recommended that new arrivals join InterNations or a local social club rather than expect the office alone to supply their social life.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between these two postings on the strength of office social life, choose on honesty, not warmth. Nigeria will tell you, loudly and in advance, exactly what participation costs and exactly what it buys you — status, goodwill, a functioning support network in a system that Gallup's own numbers describe as stressed and under-engaged. The UK will insist none of it is compulsory while quietly keeping score, and the real cost shows up later, in who got remembered when it mattered.

Pack a reasonably thick skin for Lagos mornings and a decent excuse for London evenings, and you'll be fine in both. And if a friend asked me over a drink which one actually tells the truth about what it wants from you: Lagos wants your attendance. London wants your fluency in not being asked.
