# Marble Ceilings and Polite Ones: Gender at Work in Italy and the UK

🇮🇹 Italy · 🇬🇧 UK

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Italy has one of the narrower headline gender pay gaps in the OECD, a fact usually presented as a triumph. It is not: the gap looks small largely because so few women are in paid work to begin with, giving Italy the lowest female employment rate in the European Union alongside a board quota law strict enough to dissolve a company's entire board for non-compliance. Britain has a wider pay gap, considerably more women in employment, and a culture that prefers letting market forces sort things out to legislating anyone into a boardroom seat. Both countries will tell you, sincerely, that they are the more enlightened one.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇹 Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect early, direct questions about whether you plan to have children — have a neutral deflection ready | Assume the five months of paid maternity leave means an easy flexible return afterward |
| Ask employers about their actual board-quota compliance numbers in interviews | Mistake compliments on appearance for informality — redirect firmly, expect repeats |
| Dress deliberately; presentation reads as seriousness, and women are judged on it more | Wait for mentorship to appear organically in family-run firms — sponsors are chosen |
| Learn who owns the company — most Italian firms run on kinship logic, not org charts | Assume a warm office means informal promotion rules — the ladder is still there |
| Ask female colleagues privately how leave affected their careers before planning your own | Cite the small pay-gap statistic with pride — colleagues know why it looks small |

#### 🇬🇧 UK

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask how hybrid or remote work affects promotion before accepting it | Skip after-work socialising entirely — sponsorship often happens there |
| Use the formal channels — shared parental leave, menopause policy, grievance procedures | Assume a small early-career pay gap holds — it roughly triples by your fifties |
| Push for visibility deliberately if you work flexibly | Take politeness as the whole message — feedback on gender issues is often coded |
| Check whether your employer publishes mandatory gender pay gap data before accepting an offer | Expect representation in meetings to equal influence over decisions |
| Build a peer network early to offset weaker senior sponsorship for remote workers | Assume office "banter" is harmless — it doubles as an information channel you may be missing |

### Italy

The paradox starts with the numbers. The OECD puts Italy's gender wage gap near the narrow end among rich economies, a statistic officials cite often. What it obscures is that Italy also has the EU's lowest female labour force participation rate, around 41%, and a motherhood penalty so steep that employment among mothers (57.2%) trails fathers (86.3%) by nearly thirty points. Most inactive mothers cite family responsibilities as the reason; almost no fathers do. A pay gap measured only among the women who remain employed is a survivorship-biased number.

Where Italy has moved decisively is at the top. The 2011 "Golfo-Mosca" law, tightened in 2020, requires 40% of listed-company board seats to go to the underrepresented sex, backed by fines up to €1 million and, in theory, board forfeiture. It worked: women held roughly 5% of board seats in 2010, over a third a decade later. Hofstede Insights scores Italy 70 on Masculinity, a culture oriented around competition and visible success, which makes the quota's speed more notable, not less — it was imposed on a culture that did not especially want it, and complied, at least in the boardroom. The unglamorous middle of the org chart has moved far less.

### UK

Britain's headline numbers are less flattering and more honest. The ONS puts the 2025 gender pay gap at 12.8% across all employees and 6.9% full-time — both falling slowly, with structure: the full-time gap is under 1% for workers in their twenties and roughly 12–13% by their fifties. That widening tracks the arrival of children and the flexible arrangements that follow. McKinsey and LeanIn.Org find only 37% of women working remotely more than three days a week were promoted in the past two years, against 49% of men on the same pattern — flexibility, meant as an accommodation, functions as a promotion tax paid mostly by women.

The UK compensates with paperwork Italy largely lacks: mandatory gender pay gap disclosure for firms above 250 employees, statutory shared parental leave, and an expanding menopause-policy norm. Deloitte's Women @ Work 2024 report, which surveyed UK respondents, still found rising stress and disproportionate domestic loads despite this scaffolding — policy and lived experience are not the same instrument. Hofstede scores the UK 66 on Masculinity, nearly as high as Italy's 70, complicating the idea that Britain is simply the gentler culture; it is a competitive culture that has mostly declined to legislate outcomes.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the contrast inverts expectations. Italy, culturally comfortable with hierarchy, imposed one of Europe's most aggressive board quotas and hit its target within a decade, while tolerating one of the currency union's lowest female employment rates below the boardroom. Britain, allergic to quotas, has more women in work overall and stronger formal leave and disclosure regimes, but a pay gap that compounds visibly with age and a flexible-work culture that punishes the accommodation it advertises.

The honest summary: Italy gets a woman into the room by law, then leaves her largely alone to navigate the rest informally. The UK lets almost any woman into the workforce, then quietly narrows her trajectory the longer she stays. Neither is the fix the other's brochure implies.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — a woman who relocated to Italy for work described genuine warmth from colleagues and being mentored early by older staff, but noted the surprised reaction, more than once, when she said she intended to keep working full time after having children.*

> *TheLocal.it — a contributor working through Italy's maternity system found the entitlement itself generous on paper, five months at a high percentage of salary, but ran into the opposite problem afterward: almost no employer in her sector offered the part-time schedule she needed to return, turning the leave into a countdown to an ultimatum.*

> *Medium (Expat Minds) — a foreign professional in an Italian office wrote that an understated communication style, which read as competent at home, was initially mistaken for hesitancy in meetings dominated by more assertive colleagues, requiring a conscious recalibration of tone without abandoning it.*

> *Blind, Women in Tech channel — a UK-based tech worker described being the only woman on her team and missing the informal information exchange that happened at post-work drinks and one-on-ones held outside the office, not through deliberate exclusion but because nobody thought to extend the invitation twice.*

> *Jessica Spiegel travel blog — a longtime American resident of Italy summed up her adjustment as holding her professional ground firmly while accepting that certain customs, compliments on appearance chief among them, were not going to disappear, and that picking her battles mattered more than winning every one.*

### Conclusion

If the choice is between a country that mandates representation at the top and neglects the middle, or one that welcomes broad participation and lets the gap widen quietly with seniority, know which trade-off you are making before signing the contract. Ask an Italian employer about their board numbers and their part-time policy in the same conversation; ask a British one about their published pay gap and how it correlates with who works from home. Neither country has solved this so much as chosen, with admirable national consistency, which half of the problem to ignore.
