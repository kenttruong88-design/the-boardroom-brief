# The Salary Is Confidential, the Directive Is Late: Hungary and Ireland Discuss Not Discussing Pay

🇭🇺 Hungary · 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Under an EU directive due on 7 June 2026, employers in both countries are meant to tell candidates what a job pays before they negotiate for it. According to RTÉ, Ireland reached mid-May with no draft legislation and a bill absent from the government's priority list. Hungary, according to law firm WTS Klient, had not implemented the directive when the deadline passed. Two nations with very different temperaments have therefore reached the same position by different routes: the law is coming, and the conversation about money remains private until further notice.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Hungary

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask the employer to define exactly what the quoted figure means, gross or net, and which contributions are already deducted | Assume a number in an offer letter is what reaches your account |
| Negotiate the whole package: allowances, benefits, relocation support, working arrangements | Fixate on the headline salary and leave the rest unexamined |
| Research local market pay before you name a figure, because Budapest rates differ sharply from London or Dallas | Anchor on your home-country salary and expect the employer to match it |
| Ask politely and in writing; asking costs little, according to expat forum posters | Wave an inflated number about, since a very high salary was reported to cause friction with local colleagues |
| Take tax advice if you are an American citizen or on a secondment | Treat tax as a detail for later |

### Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for the budgeted range early, and counter with an overlapping range of your own | Name a figure first if you can avoid it |
| Prepare market data from several sources before the first call | Disclose your current salary if you can decline gracefully |
| Look at the whole package: bonus, pension, leave, health cover, flexibility | Accept a base figure without learning whether a bonus is included |
| Treat a recruiter's first number as an opening position | Ask about pay in the very first interview with no context, which some Irish posters say reads as desperation |
| Walk away politely from roles that will not state a range at all | Spend three interview rounds on a role without confirming the budget |

## Ireland: A Nation That Will Discuss Anything Except This

Ireland is a country of famously fluent conversation, which makes its reticence about pay more conspicuous. The Irish Times noted in December 2025 that asking about pay has long been a social taboo, with people sometimes unaware of what close relatives earn. The practical result is visible in job adverts. Indeed data reported by ThinkBusiness.ie put the share of Irish postings with any pay information at about 39 percent in March 2026, behind the UK at 56 percent, the Netherlands at 48 and France at 43, though ahead of Spain at 17 and Germany at 12. Among adverts that do list pay, the median range is 19 percent wide, which is more precise than a shrug but not by a great deal.

Candidates, for their part, would like to know. In the same Indeed survey, 82 percent of Irish respondents said they would be more likely to apply if a range were shown, the highest share among the countries surveyed. The government appears to share the sentiment. The Irish Times reported that it seemed set to go further than many member states by requiring pay levels or ranges in adverts, while the directive itself requires only that pay information reach candidates before interview. Ibec, the employers' group, has meanwhile asked that provisions beyond pre-employment transparency should not become law until 7 June 2027 at the earliest. The Department of Children, Disability and Equality has said implementation will be phased and that employers will not be penalised for failing to complete every element by June 2026. Awareness is thin: an HRLocker survey cited by RTÉ found that only 14 percent of respondents strongly understand the directive, while 70 percent named legal compliance as their biggest worry. Ireland does already require gender pay gap reporting, and the threshold fell last June to employers with 50 or more staff, so the machinery for embarrassment exists even if the machinery for advertising has yet to arrive.

None of this abolishes negotiation. SD Worx Ireland points out that the directive does not ban individual salary negotiation; employers may offer a range and bargain within it, provided differences can be justified by objective criteria. Indeed economist Lisa Feist, quoted by ThinkBusiness.ie, observes that employers have historically changed their posting habits when a specific law takes effect rather than in anticipation, which describes Irish human resources departments rather well. The newcomer's task is therefore unchanged and slightly easier: find the range, justify the position within it, and do so in a country where saying the number aloud still feels faintly like a confession.

## Hungary: Secrecy by Habit, Transparency by Directive

Hungary approaches the matter from a different angle. A joint survey by Profession.hu and PwC Hungary, reported by Trade Magazin, found that 51 percent of employees oppose making wages public, citing malicious gossip and a worse atmosphere. The same survey found that more than 75 percent say pay information in a job advert affects whether they apply. Hungarians, in short, would like to read the price tag but would prefer that the neighbours not read theirs. It is a coherent position, and it also happens to be what the directive mostly asks for: transparency of pay structure and criteria rather than a public list of individual wages.

The gender pay gap gives the policy its urgency. Daily News Hungary reported in October 2025 that women in Hungary earn on average 17 percent less than men, against 13 percent across the EU. Eurostat's own reference year is older, 2022, when the EU figure stood at 12.7 percent. WTS Klient adds that from 7 June 2026 Hungarian courts must interpret employment and discrimination law consistently with the directive's objectives even without national legislation, so the absence of a statute does not entirely excuse an employer. The directive also bars employers from asking candidates about previous pay and, according to WTS Klient, applies to all employers regardless of size, which will interest the many Hungarian recruiters who have long treated the question about current salary as a conversational opener. Hofstede Insights could not be consulted for this article, as its figures could not be retrieved, and none are invented here.

## The Reckoning

Ireland has the stronger case for a head start in practice and the weaker one in manners. Its candidates are accustomed to recruiters, agency intermediaries and a labour market where the range can sometimes be extracted by asking twice. A 2013 Expat.com thread suggests the habit is old: employers state an expected salary in the advert, and an exceptional background might earn more, though the poster called that very rare. Its adverts remain reticent, but the government appears inclined to legislate ahead of the directive, and its negotiating etiquette is well documented: do not name a number first, ask for the range, counter with an overlapping one. Boards.ie posters were advising precisely this a decade ago, which suggests the etiquette is stable even if the statute is not.

Hungary offers less visible pay information today, and a workforce that is openly ambivalent about publishing it. The compensation, for a mover, is that Budapest rewards preparation. A newcomer who defines gross against net, researches local rates and asks calmly for the whole package will do well in either country. The difference is that in Dublin the awkwardness is social and in Budapest it is largely arithmetic.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Boards.ie, a poster in a 2016 thread said Irish interviewers usually have a budget but avoid revealing it, and advised deflecting the salary question or naming the highest figure one could say with a straight face.*

> *Boards.ie, another poster in the same thread said they would not pursue roles without a stated range, since a low offer after several interviews wastes everyone's time.*

> *Boards.ie, a poster in a 2016 salary thread advised not naming a figure first and, if pressed, giving a range that begins at your minimum acceptable pay, while weighing holiday, flexibility and healthcare alongside it.*

> *Expat.com, a member of the Budapest forum said asking for a high salary costs little and that no known cap exists, while another warned that a very large package could cause friction with local coworkers.*

> *Expat.com, a long-standing member of the Hungary forum said the real money for Western expats lay in Budapest, and that they avoided Hungarian employers altogether by contracting to firms in Western Europe.*

## Conclusion

Neither country will hand you a transparent salary on arrival, and both will, eventually, be obliged to. Until then the practical rule is the same in Dublin and Budapest: learn the range, define the terms of the figure, and negotiate the package rather than the number. Check the current state of each country's legislation before you sign, because the reporting available at the time of writing dates from May and June 2026, and statutes move more slowly than directives.

The practical advice is to treat the law as a tailwind rather than a plan. Both governments have promised that pay will be discussed openly, which in two countries this polite is likely to be achieved by a form, a deadline and a lengthy silence.
