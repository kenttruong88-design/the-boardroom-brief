# Czechia Wants a Written Agreement, Canada Wants You in the Building: The Home Office Tour

🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Working from home was meant to be the pandemic's permanent gift, and two countries have since taken opposite views of what to do with it. In Canada, the provincial and federal governments, which set an example for everyone else, have been sending staff back to desks for years. In Czechia, the Labour Code now says that telework requires a written agreement, and that either side may cancel it on 15 days' notice. Neither arrangement resembles the freedom advertised in 2020.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get any home-office arrangement in a written telework agreement | Rely on a verbal understanding with your manager |
| Settle cost reimbursement in the agreement: actual costs, a lump sum, or none | Assume the employer will cover electricity and internet automatically |
| Ask what the termination terms are, since 15 days' notice is the default | Treat a home-office arrangement as permanent |
| Ask about hybrid options at international firms, where they are more common | Expect a traditional local employer to offer them readily |
| Note whether you qualify for the right to a justified refusal, such as caring for a child under 9 | Assume there is a general statutory right to work from home |
| Work out which days you are expected on site | Disappear from view; some employers still assume visible staff are working |

### In Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask which province's or employer's office-attendance rule applies to you | Assume the whole country works the same way |
| Get the number of required office days into the offer letter | Accept "flexible" without a number |
| Check whether the job is federal, provincial or private, since the rules differ | Assume a hybrid arrangement will survive a new policy memo |
| Ask how the commute is treated, as many cities have long ones | Choose housing before you know your office days |
| Keep evidence of your productivity if you work remotely | Rely on goodwill alone when a mandate arrives |
| Compare the province's public-sector policy with private-sector norms | Treat the previous year's policy as a guide to next year's |

## Czechia

Czech employees want to work from home more than Czech employers have allowed. An IDEA study from the CERGE-EI think tank found that after the pandemic ended in 2023, about one in ten employees still worked from home, against about one in twenty before, and that most of them did so for fewer than half their hours. The practice is concentrated: information and telecommunications leads, with half of employees working at least partly from home, and about a quarter of university graduates do so. Yet in January 2023, 66 percent of men and 70 percent of women said they would like to work from home several days a week. For context, Eurostat's figure for the whole European Union, reported by Euronews, was 8.8 percent of workers usually working from home in 2025, so Czechia sits roughly at the Union's level on this evidence, though the two figures come from different sources and no Czech figure for the same year was found. It was not always so. A Czech Radio report citing Eurostat, undated but evidently from before the pandemic, put regular home working in Czechia under 4 percent against an EU average of 5 percent, with women (5 percent) well ahead of men (3 percent).

The law is permissive in a cautious way. According to Grant Thornton, an amendment to the Labour Code that took effect on 1 October 2023 requires a written telework agreement. Employees have no general statutory right to telework, although some, such as pregnant employees and those caring for a child under nine, can require the employer to justify a refusal. Employers can reimburse actual proven costs, pay a flat amount set by ministerial decree, or agree with the employee that no costs will be reimbursed at all. A Czech Daily report on the drafting shows how that last option arrived: the original draft would have obliged employers to reimburse costs, a new paragraph made reimbursement optional, and unions and business groups disagreed over whether this was a loss for workers or a win for common sense. Either party may end a telework agreement on 15 days' notice unless they agree otherwise.

Attitudes, on the evidence available, are changing slowly. A Czech Radio report, undated but apparently from before the pandemic, quoted an industry confederation official saying that some employers are conservative and assume only visible staff are working. Expat.com's guide to Prague work culture says home-office options are often offered as a benefit and that hybrid arrangements are common in international companies. Hofstede's scores, 57 for power distance and 74 for uncertainty avoidance, describe a country that likes its arrangements documented, which is precisely what the Labour Code now demands.

## Canada

Canada's picture is less a policy than a tug of war. Statistics Canada figures reported by Global News showed that in May 2025, 17.4 percent of employed Canadians mostly worked from home, down from 18.7 percent a year earlier, while 77.6 percent worked only outside the home and 5.1 percent were hybrid. Commuting had risen for a fourth straight year. By May 2026, according to the IRPP, 11.4 percent of employed Canadians worked exclusively from home and 9.8 percent had a hybrid arrangement, so fully remote work continues to shrink and the hybrid share looks larger, although the two years' figures were reported by different outlets and may not be strictly comparable. The same report notes that in February 2024, 47.4 percent of public administration workers worked from home at least some of the time, which gives some idea of how much there was to take back.

The public sector sets the pace. The IRPP reports that the federal public service moved from a hybrid model of two to three days in 2023 to three days for most employees and four for executives in 2024, then tightened to four days for most employees and five for executives in 2026. Ontario, Alberta, Nova Scotia and Saskatchewan are listed as requiring full-time or near-full-time attendance, while British Columbia, Manitoba, New Brunswick and Quebec have standardised hybrid rules. Private employers follow their own course; Amazon moved its corporate staff to five days in the office, which CBC reported affected about 8,000 employees in Canada. Hofstede's scores for Canada, 39 for power distance and 48 for uncertainty avoidance, describe a low-hierarchy culture that tolerates ambiguity, a trait the mandates have been testing.

Workers say they want something else. An Angus Reid Institute survey, reported by CBC in July 2025, found that three in five Canadians would prefer to spend most of their time working from home, that 79 percent want a schedule that allows some remote work, that 51 percent of employees ordered into the office more often were upset or very upset, and that a quarter of remote workers would quit if forced back.

## The Reckoning

Czechia has a more modest remote work culture and a clearer legal framework: a written agreement, a menu of reimbursement options, and a short exit. Canada has a larger remote population, shrinking, and no national framework to speak of, only the preferences of the federal government, the provinces and the employer. A worker in Prague can read the rules in a single statute. A worker in Canada has to read the memo.

For the arriving professional, the practical difference is predictability against volatility. In Czechia, the arrangement is whatever the agreement says, and it can end quickly, but at least it exists on paper. In Canada, it is whatever the employer's current policy says, and the direction of travel, across the public sector and several large firms, is towards the building. Neither is a safe harbour. The Czech one is simply easier to audit.

Workers' preferences, meanwhile, point the same way in both countries. Czech surveys show most employees would like several days a week at home, and the Canadian survey shows an even larger appetite. In both, the employer holds the pen. The difference is that Canadian employers have increasingly used it to write the same sentence, while Czech employers have mostly been asked to write something down at all.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (via blogTO) — Toronto-area users reacting to Ontario's five-day office order split between those who plan to boycott downtown businesses and those who call that pointless, with some noting that remote jobs have largely disappeared and that lower-paid workers have never had the choice.*

> *Daily Hive (federal workers) — Public servants and their unions called the three-day federal mandate ludicrous, describing cramped offices, crowded commutes and video calls taken in spaces with no room for them.*

> *CBC News (Amazon employees) — A cloud engineer said his team was upset, that no data had been offered to support the claim that office work improves collaboration, and that 523 colleagues had signed a letter to the company's chief executive.*

> *Czech Radio — A programmer said she works from home one or two days a week because the calm suits her, while an industry representative conceded that some employers still assume only visible staff are working.*

> *Czech Daily — A trade union leader was disappointed that the home-working cost rule departed from the original agreement, while a chamber of commerce representative called the softer version a victory for common sense.*

## Conclusion

If you value a legal framework, Czechia is the simpler place to negotiate remote work: ask for the telework agreement, the cost clause and the termination terms. If you value a larger market with more remote roles, Canada has the numbers, but the trend is against you, and public-sector offers should be assumed to come with a building attached.

In either country, get the arrangement written down before you sign, and assume it can change. Flexibility, like most benefits, is generous until someone checks what it costs.
