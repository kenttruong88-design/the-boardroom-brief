**Free Healthcare, Different Catches: Living Costs in Brazil vs. Canada**

**🇧🇷 Brazil · 🇨🇦 Canada**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Both countries offer free, universal public healthcare, and in both countries a large share of the population quietly opts out of it anyway. In Brazil, over 70% of expats and a substantial share of locals pay for private *plano de saúde* to skip SUS wait times that can exceed 180 days for a specialist. In Canada, the public system stays free at the point of use, but average national rent has climbed past $2,027 a month, and Toronto or Vancouver tech salaries — per widely shared comparisons — buy noticeably less house than the same job in an American city with a higher headline paycheck. Universal coverage turns out to be the easy part; the actual cost of living is where each country hides its real trade-off.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for SUS immediately (with a CPF) for free emergency and routine care | Rely on SUS alone for specialist care if you can budget for a private plan |
| Budget R$200–R$1,200+/month for private health insurance depending on age and tier | Assume Brazilian healthcare pricing resembles U.S. private insurance costs |
| Start the daycare search early and consider private options if budget allows | Assume a public creche spot will be available — 70% of under-3s aren't enrolled |
| Compare cost of living by city — São Paulo/Rio run far higher than secondary cities | Budget for Rio-level costs if you're actually relocating to a smaller city |
| Expect healthcare costs to rise sharply with age on private plans | Wait until later in life to lock in a private health plan — enroll early |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for provincial Medicare immediately — most essential care is free | Assume Medicare covers dental or vision — it typically doesn't without separate plans |
| Research provincial childcare subsidies — some provinces now offer $10/day daycare | Assume childcare costs are uniform nationally — they vary hugely by province |
| Budget rent as your largest cost — national average is now $2,027/month | Assume Toronto or Vancouver rents reflect the national average |
| Check Canada Child Benefit eligibility if relocating with children | Assume tech-sector compensation matches U.S. levels — it generally runs lower |
| Compare real take-home pay after Canada's higher income and sales tax | Budget based on gross salary alone without accounting for the tax difference |

---

Brazil's system is built around a genuinely universal floor with a widely-used private ceiling above it. SUS guarantees free care to anyone in the country, citizens and foreigners alike, and handles vaccinations, surgeries, and emergencies at real scale — but wait times for elective procedures and specialists can stretch past 180 days in major cities, which is why more than 70% of expats and a large share of the middle class carry private plans, ranging from a couple hundred reais a month for young, healthy entrants to several thousand for comprehensive senior coverage. Childcare tells a similarly split story: the constitution mandates free public daycare, but demand vastly outstrips supply, leaving 70% of children under three without any enrolled program at all, and parents who can afford the roughly $1,000-per-child-per-month private alternative simply buy their way past the waitlist. Overall living costs remain genuinely low by global standards — Numbeo ranks Brazil 125th out of 151 countries on its cost-of-living index — but that affordability masks real regional variation, with São Paulo and Rio running two to three times the cost of secondary cities.

Canada's system inverts the trade-off: strong universal coverage for core healthcare, but a housing and childcare cost structure that's become a genuine national anxiety. Medicare, funded through taxation, keeps essential medical services free for citizens and permanent residents, and newer additions like the Canadian Dental Care Plan extend coverage to households under $90,000. Childcare, long a pain point, has improved dramatically through federal-provincial deals — British Columbia's $10-a-day centers, Ontario's fee cap near $22 a day, and the Canada Child Benefit paying up to $8,500 a year per child under six for lower-income families. But housing has moved the opposite direction: national average asking rent hit $2,027 a month in April 2026, detached homes in Toronto and Vancouver have surged past $1 million CAD, and multiple tech-sector compensation threads describe total comp running meaningfully below equivalent U.S. roles even as rent approaches San Francisco-level pricing in some neighborhoods.

The Reckoning: Brazil solves healthcare and childcare access unevenly but keeps overall cost of living genuinely low, meaning the system punishes patience (long waits) more than wallets. Canada solves healthcare and, increasingly, childcare quite well at the policy level, but the housing market has become the system's actual failure point, punishing wallets (especially in Toronto and Vancouver) more than patience. If you're the kind of person who can tolerate uncertainty and budget for private backstops, Brazil's overall lower cost of living may leave you better off in absolute terms; if you value predictable, subsidized childcare and healthcare and can stomach a serious rent line item, Canada's welfare architecture does more of the work for you, at a real ongoing price.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *teamblind.com — In a thread comparing Toronto and Vancouver cost of living for tech workers, posters noted that total compensation in Canada runs "way lower" than equivalent U.S. roles, while housing costs in Toronto were compared directly to San Francisco — with one commenter summarizing that the salary is considered good by Canadian standards, but that saving meaningfully on it is difficult once rent and Canada's higher income and sales tax are factored in.*

> *Quora — Someone detailing the cost of moving to Canada described real sticker shock on arrival, citing basement apartments in outer Toronto neighborhoods running $1,000–$1,400 a month, and warned that first-year costs run especially high due to currency conversion fees and the expense of switching over financial accounts and services.*

> *Quora — A respondent describing healthcare as an American living in Brazil said SUS access is free with proper visa status but variable in quality by location, and that most expats end up buying a private plan anyway — with premiums climbing sharply for anyone over 60, sometimes running into the thousands of reais monthly for major hospital network coverage.*

> *Quora — Someone answering what it costs to live comfortably in São Paulo or Rio put a mid-range single expat lifestyle at $1,200–$2,500 a month all-in, and noted that dropping to a smaller provincial city can cut that closer to $600–$1,200 — a spread newcomers said they hadn't appreciated until they'd actually compared neighborhoods.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Brazil, budget for a private health plan and a private daycare spot from day one rather than assuming the public systems will come through on your timeline — the overall lower cost of living gives you the room to do it. If you're moving to Canada, budget rent as your dominant line item and treat the strong childcare and healthcare subsidies as the offset, not a bonus — the welfare state is real, but so is the rent. Priya's honest take: Brazil will make you pay for speed, and Canada will make you pay for square footage — figure out which shortage bothers you more before you sign a lease in either country.
