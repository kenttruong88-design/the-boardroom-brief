# The Philippines Offers 105 Days and a Relative; Britain Offers 39 Weeks of Pay and a Nursery Invoice

🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Filipina mother is entitled to 105 days of paid maternity leave, a British mother to up to 39 weeks of statutory pay, and on leave alone the United Kingdom appears to win by a distance. Then the nursery invoice arrives. According to OECD figures reported by the World Economic Forum, net childcare costs for a UK couple on average pay amount to about a quarter of household income, while in the Philippines, as the World Bank notes, more than 95 percent of survey respondents say childcare should be provided by family. One country has a market for childcare and a bill to match. The other has a grandmother and a labour-force participation problem.

## Philippines: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check that you have made at least three monthly Social Security System contributions in the 12 months before the birth semester | Assume the 105 days apply without the contributions and the notice to your employer |
| Ask your employer how the salary difference is handled; the employer pays, the SSS reimburses | Let the payment run past the 30 days the employer is supposed to take |
| Give 45 days' notice if you want the optional 30 unpaid extra days | Expect the extension to be paid, or to be granted without notice |
| Expect the father's leave to be seven days, plus up to seven transferred from the mother | Plan a long paternity leave on the strength of the statute alone |
| Budget for a nanny or household help if relatives are not nearby | Assume a formal daycare place exists near your home |
| Ask about flexible hours directly, since reduced hours after leave are only proposed | Treat the government's proposals for reduced post-leave hours as law |

## United Kingdom: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Work out your Statutory Maternity Pay: 90 percent of earnings for six weeks, then a flat weekly rate or 90 percent if lower | Assume the flat rate replaces your salary |
| Ask whether your employer enhances the statutory scheme | Treat the statutory minimum as the ceiling |
| Apply early for the childcare code for the free 30 hours in England | Wait until the month you need a nursery place |
| Join nursery waiting lists as soon as you know you are expecting | Assume places for under-twos exist on demand |
| Check whether your visa gives you access to public funds before counting on subsidies | Assume that being employed and taxed makes you eligible |
| Use paternity leave, now a day-one right for children born on or after 6 April 2026 | Expect the reform to have changed the pay |

## Philippines: 105 Days and the Extended Family

Republic Act 11210, in force since March 2019 according to Mercer, grants up to 105 days of paid maternity leave per pregnancy, with 15 further paid days for solo parents and an optional 30 unpaid days for any mother. The Philippine Commission on Women adds that seven of the 105 days can be assigned to the child's father, in addition to the existing seven days of paternity leave, and that the law covers private and public employees as well as those in the informal economy. Employers advance the payment and the Social Security System reimburses them. At 15 weeks, the entitlement clears the 14-week benchmark that the International Labour Organization has long used, a benchmark which its 2014 report found only a handful of Asian countries meeting.

The leave is generous. What surrounds it is thin. The World Bank found in 2022 that female labour force participation in the Philippines was 49 percent in 2019, against 76 percent for men, and that being married with a child aged zero to two lowers a woman's chance of working by seven to fourteen percentage points. Access to domestic help softens that effect, which tells you what the real childcare system is. BusinessWorld reported in March 2026 that Filipino women spend about 3.2 hours a day on unpaid care and domestic work, against 1.7 hours for men, according to the Philippine Commission on Women, and that the government hopes to raise female participation from 53.7 percent in 2025 to 59 percent by 2034.

The state is aware of the problem. The Trabaho Para Sa Bayan plan, reported by the Inquirer in May 2025, proposes reduced working hours after maternity leave and a parental leave open to all genders. The same BusinessWorld report quotes the Federation of Free Workers as saying 17 million Filipino women aged 15 and over are outside the labour force, mostly because of unpaid care work, and a labour department official citing 99.9 percent compliance with women-protection laws among monitored establishments. Both statistics may be accurate, which is the more interesting possibility. Meanwhile an opinion writer in the Manila Bulletin calls the absence of accessible childcare a systemic failure, and argues for neighbourhood daycare in every barangay. A reasonable reader will conclude that the Philippines has legislated the first three months well and left the following fifteen years to the family.

## United Kingdom: Generous Leave, Punishing Invoices

British statutory maternity pay, according to GOV.UK, is 90 percent of average weekly earnings for six weeks and then £194.32 a week, or 90 percent if lower, up to 39 weeks. A UNICEF ranking reported by Nursery World placed the UK 34th of 41 rich countries on paid leave, noting 13 further unpaid weeks. Fathers receive two weeks of paternity leave, and under the Employment Rights Act 2025 that has been a day-one right since 6 April 2026 for children born on or after that date, though law firms note that no matching pay entitlement came with it. Shared parental leave exists, but UNICEF noted that when it was introduced the government expected only 2 to 8 percent of eligible fathers to use it, and that take-up remained low. On leave, then, Britain is long and thinly paid.

The childcare is where the arithmetic bites. GOV.UK offers 30 free hours a week, for 38 weeks a year, for children from nine months to four in England, provided each parent earns at least £203.36 a week and under £100,000 a year, and holds the right immigration status for public funds. Outside that window, nursery fees apply. Access is another matter. The Hong Konger, citing the campaign group Pregnant Then Screwed, reports that only 11 percent of parents had obtained a code when applications opened for the expanded hours, and cites research that just 17 percent of nurseries could offer them, owing to staffing shortages. Both figures are second-hand and should be read as such. The OECD figure of roughly a quarter of household income for a couple on average pay is the context, with the United States, at 32 percent, named by the World Economic Forum as the highest.

Then there is the career. The Office for National Statistics found, in a study published in October 2025, that five years after a first birth mothers in England earned on average 42 percent, or £1,051 a month, less than the year before, a cumulative loss of £65,618 over the period. Part of the fall came from working fewer hours. The ONS called the reduction substantial and long-lasting, which is a restrained description of £65,618.

## The Reckoning

Both countries have an unresolved argument about who raises the child while the mother is employed. The Philippines answers it with the family and the household helper, which keeps the cost low and the participation rate low with it. Britain answers it with a market and a subsidy, which keeps the participation rate higher and the invoice visible. Hofstede Insights scores the United Kingdom at 89 for individualism and the Philippines at 32, which is a tidy summary of why one country expects a nursery and the other expects an aunt.

For an expatriate, the difference is practical. A newcomer to Manila with a salary and no relatives can substitute money for family, and the World Bank finding suggests that is exactly what women with domestic help do. A newcomer to Britain with no relatives and a visa that excludes public funds substitutes money for money, at the full rate, with a waiting list. The first system is cheaper and more dependent on goodwill. The second is better documented, and the documentation includes the price.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Modern Filipina — paraphrased experience: A woman working in business process outsourcing says she had only 15 days of leave before and after her due date in an earlier job, relied on a nanny because family lived far away, pumped milk at work and stored it in a restaurant fridge. She later moved to night shifts and wakes in the afternoon to spend time with her son before work.*

> *Modern Filipina — paraphrased experience: A veterinarian who returned to work when her daughter was eight months old pumps at least three times a day, often works through lunch to make up the time, and sometimes brings a babysitter along to multi-day trainings. She says the unsolicited advice about breastfeeding has been the harder part.*

> *Hacker News — paraphrased experience: A UK-based commenter wrote in 2021 that six months of shared parental leave has become normal at their workplace, and that returning parents report feeling more fulfilled. They add that childcare costs approach the cost of rent, and that some colleagues left the country after a second child because of them.*

> *Hacker News — paraphrased experience: A commenter in early 2024 with two young children said they would likely turn down a job offer in Cambridge because nursery fees there exceed a thousand dollars per child per month, a level they describe as typical of comfortable families in the developed world.*

> *The Hong Konger — paraphrased experience: A Hong Kong-born writer in London, close to her due date, reports full-time nursery places for under-threes at about £2,000 a month, four local nurseries with no under-two places until the following year, and a non-refundable waiting-list fee. She notes that holders of her visa type are generally excluded from the childcare subsidies, and says she does not regret the move.*

## Conclusion

If you are weighing these two for family reasons, ask the question the brochures avoid: who is looking after the child at 9 a.m. on a Tuesday? In the Philippines the answer is usually a relative or a paid helper, so the decision turns on whether you have access to either, and on whether you are covered by Social Security contributions before the birth. In the United Kingdom the answer is a nursery, so it turns on your income, your visa status, your waiting-list diligence and the generosity of your employer, which can exceed the statute and often does.

On career, neither country is kind to mothers: Filipino women are pushed out of the labour force by unpaid care, and British mothers see earnings fall by two-fifths. The difference is that Britain will quantify the loss for you in a statistical bulletin. The Philippines will merely send a cousin.
