# Ghana Hands You a Mentor Whether You Asked or Not; Canada Hands You a Laptop and Wishes You Luck

🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Hofstede Insights scores Ghana at 80 on power distance and Canada at 39, a gap that explains most of what happens in a new employee's first fortnight. In Ghana, the arrival of a newcomer activates a chain of seniority, titles and informal guardianship that few handbooks mention because nobody needs to write it down. In Canada, a survey of 835 workers found that 47 percent began their careers without a peer to guide them. One country over-supplies supervision. The other treats it as an optional extra, like a standing desk.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet colleagues properly, asking after health and family before any business | Rush into the task list on day one; it reads as rude, not efficient |
| Use Sir, Madam, Mr or Mrs and professional titles until invited to use first names | Assume first names are fine because the company intranet says so |
| Treat the three or six months of probation as a long, observed audition | Imagine probation is a formality that ends quietly |
| Ask a senior colleague for guidance and expect it to be given generously | Contradict or correct a senior in front of others; maintaining face matters |
| Request regular check-ins with your supervisor and agree goals in writing | Wait for a structured training calendar to arrive |
| Leave buffers around meetings, which often start late or overrun | Schedule back-to-back onboarding sessions and expect them to hold |

### In Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for a named point of contact in the first week, since one may not be assigned | Assume silence means someone has you in hand |
| Book coffee chats with people in your field and treat networking as part of the job | Wait for a referral or a formal mentor to appear |
| Use first names from the start, including with executives | Insist on titles; it will be received as stiffness |
| Confirm in writing that paid training is paid, and note your start date | Work an unpaid trial day on the promise of future goodwill |
| Join a newcomer mentorship programme if your employer or a local agency offers one | Rely on the company alone to build your professional network |
| Ask for a 30-60-90 day plan and a manager check-in cadence | Wait for feedback to arrive unprompted at the annual review |

## Ghana

Ghanaian workplaces run on hierarchy, and onboarding follows it. Expat Arrivals describes business culture as hierarchical, with respect determined by age, experience and position, and with titles such as Madam and Sir expected until a colleague invites otherwise. Relationships come first: a proper greeting includes enquiries about health and family, and first meetings may involve no business at all. Hofstede Insights supports the picture, scoring Ghana 80 on power distance and 9 on individualism, which is to say that the group, and the person at its head, outrank the individual. Uncertainty avoidance is a moderately high 65, so rules and a clear chain of command are reassuring rather than irksome.

What this means for a newcomer is that the formal onboarding programme may be modest, while the informal one is thorough. A Graphic Online column by a former Unilever Ghana executive recalls a succession of senior colleagues who gave career advice, pushed him towards the management trainee route and, in one case, reportedly risked their own standing to keep him in a department he wanted. The mentors combined high standards with personal investment, and one of them is remembered as demanding and tough in the same breath as devoted. It reads like fond memoir, but it describes a recognisable pattern: seniority carries a duty of care.

The structured part is the probation period. Jobberman, a Ghanaian jobs platform, notes that an appointment letter usually includes three or six months of probation, and its advice reads like a survival guide: hold regular check-ins with your supervisor, agree goals, avoid overcommitting and stay clear of gossip. The labour market explains the stakes. The Ghana Statistical Service reported annual average unemployment of 13.6 percent in 2024, with youth unemployment at 32 percent for ages 15 to 24, and the Government Statistician himself said too many jobs remain informal and insecure. A permanent formal post is worth guarding. The same release put the working population aged 15 and over at 14.2 million, with services employing an average of 5.5 million, agriculture 4.8 million and industry 2.1 million, so the offices that run formal onboarding employ a minority of the people who work.

Daily etiquette reinforces the arrangement. Expat Arrivals advises that timekeeping is flexible and meetings often start late, that communication is somewhat indirect, and that embarrassing a colleague in front of others is a serious error. For a newcomer this means questions are best asked privately, and feedback should be expected the same way. Formal dress is generally expected in corporate settings, a small point that signals the newcomer has read the room before reading the handbook.

## Canada

Canada's problem is not hierarchy but absence. Robert Half's survey of 835 Canadian workers found that 47 percent lacked a workplace peer to guide them early on, 37 percent felt unprepared owing to insufficient training, and 32 percent could not apply their skills immediately. A Robert Half director attributed the gap to leaner teams, retirements and thinner learning departments. The employer, in other words, has not become cruel, merely understaffed, and has expressed this by leaving the new hire alone with a login. Internships, the article adds, give employers roughly four months to judge fit, a probation by another name, though Canadian probation is usually less theatrical than the Ghanaian version.

Hofstede Insights scores Canada 39 on power distance and 72 on individualism, which fits the pattern. The newcomer is expected to be self-directing, to ask, to network and to build their own support. The same MoneySense article quotes a Concordia career adviser recommending coffee chats and early use of paid internships, advice that amounts to organising your own mentor.

Where mentorship does exist, the evidence is striking. In a survey by Accenture and ALLIES of 292 immigrant mentees, unemployment fell from 73 percent to 19 percent after twelve months, the share working in their field of expertise rose from 27 to 71 percent, and average full-time earnings climbed 62 percent. Robert Half's separate survey of more than 1,050 Canadian hiring managers found only 34 percent offer paid internships to students or recent graduates, and 32 percent recruit on campuses, so the formal on-ramps are narrower than the optimism of the careers pages suggests. The mentorship programme is not standard issue. It is something an immigrant must find, usually through a settlement agency, and then use with some energy.

## The Reckoning

On paper the two systems are mirror images. Ghana supplies relationships and withholds structure; Canada supplies structure, where it supplies anything, and withholds relationships. A Ghanaian newcomer is unlikely to be ignored, and equally unlikely to be told in writing what the goals are, although Jobberman advises asking. A Canadian newcomer will be told the goals in a document and then may not hear from anyone for a week.

The practical difference is who does the work of connection. In Ghana the senior person is expected to take an interest and the junior person to defer. In Canada the junior person is expected to manufacture the interest, politely, over coffee. Both arrangements function, and the Canadian one is the more forgiving of a stumble, because nobody is watching closely enough to remember it. Ghana's version is warmer and slower to forgive an unguarded remark made in front of the wrong audience.

There is also a question of what the guidance is for. Ghanaian mentorship tends to be a sponsorship of the whole person, including career moves, advice and occasionally a lift to work. Canadian mentorship, where it exists, is usually bounded: a defined programme, a defined year and a measurable outcome, such as the ALLIES employment figures. The Ghanaian kind is harder to apply for and harder to leave. Both also punish the person who has misjudged which one they are in: the deferential Canadian hire is invisible, and the first-name Ghanaian hire is faintly alarming.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/askTO — A Toronto worker was dismissed after a single day of training and was unpaid for it. Commenters agreed that training is work, called the non-payment wage theft, and pointed to the Ministry of Labour complaint process.*

> *Hacker News — One team lead, tired of repeating setup steps for each hire, wrote a checklist and an onboarding guide, and new starters then needed almost no live training. A former sailor said moving to a new unit meant shadowing whoever held the role and learning local habits that differ from ship to ship.*

> *Expat.com (Ghana forum) — Expats posting about settling in Accra recommended learning Twi or Ga, described locals as welcoming to foreigners, and suggested that the growing market, construction in particular, offers openings. None described a formal onboarding process.*

> *ExpatExchange (Accra) — One expat said locals tend to say what they think you want to hear, which makes it hard to know whom to trust. In an office, that is worth remembering when a colleague assures you something will be done.*

> *Graphic Online — A Ghanaian executive recalled that his early bosses gave him demanding standards alongside personal sponsorship, including backing a move between departments. The mentor was not optional, and nor, evidently, was the pressure.*

## Conclusion

If you are moving to Ghana, bring formality, patience and a willingness to be looked after, which sometimes feels like being supervised. Learn the titles, ask for goals in writing and treat the probation period as the real interview. If you are moving to Canada, assume nobody is coming to rescue you, and organise your own rescue in the first week: a named contact, a plan, a coffee chat and, if you are an immigrant, an enrolled mentorship programme.

A practical note for the sceptical: in Canada, ask in the interview who will train you and how long it takes, because the answer is informative even when it is vague. In Ghana, ask who your supervisor is and how they like to receive questions, because the answer is a map of the office. Neither question is rude, and both signal that you have understood the local mechanism.

Neither system is superior, though each has a recognisable failure mode: being managed too much, or not at all. Choose according to which of these you could survive on your first Monday.
