**Blazer or Bata Sandals: What Zimbabwe and the United Kingdom Actually Expect You to Wear to Work**

🇿🇼 Zimbabwe · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Consider two facts that have nothing to do with each other until you put them side by side. According to the Zimbabwe National Statistics Agency, 86.8 percent of employed Zimbabweans worked in the informal economy in the final quarter of 2023 — meaning the overwhelming majority of the country's workforce has no dress code to violate in the first place. Meanwhile, according to Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2023 report, only 10 percent of British employees say they are actually engaged at work, which raises the question of why so many of them still iron a shirt for it. One country mostly doesn't have offices. The other has offices and, increasingly, opinions about whether you need to wear a tie to sit in one feeling nothing. Welcome to the global office's most unexpectedly fraught subject: what to put on your body before 9 a.m. It sounds like a trivial logistics question — pack the suit or pack the trainers — until you realise it is actually a proxy for a much larger one, which is how hierarchical, formal, and self-serious the workplace you are walking into intends to be.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇿🇼 Zimbabwe

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a jacket to any first meeting with a bank, law firm, or government ministry — conservative sectors still expect it | Assume the informal sector's relaxed dress norms apply once you cross into formal employment |
| Keep a lightweight blazer on hand year-round; Harare's altitude means mild winters, not an excuse to dress down | Pack heavy wool suits expecting British winter conditions — you will overheat and overpack |
| Default to closed-toe shoes and polished leather for client-facing roles | Wear sandals or flip-flops into any office setting, even on casual Fridays |
| Ask local colleagues directly what "smart" means at your specific employer — it varies sharply by sector | Assume a multinational's Harare office follows the same casual norms as its London or Nairobi branch |
| Invest in well-tailored basics over a large wardrobe; quality and fit read as more senior than quantity | Treat denim as safe business casual — it is read as too informal in most office settings |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Overdress on day one, then recalibrate once you see what colleagues actually wear | Wear shorts to the office even in a heatwave — it remains one of the most policed items in UK offices |
| Default to "smart casual" if no dress code is stated; it is now the preference of 57 percent of UK workers | Assume "business casual" means the same thing at a City bank and a Shoreditch start-up |
| Keep one dark suit in rotation for client meetings, interviews, and unspecified formal occasions | Take remote-work casualness as licence to show up scruffy on the days you are physically in the building |
| Notice the regional and sector gap — financial and legal London still expects more formality than most of the country | Mistake London's visible formality for a national standard; the rest of the UK skews markedly more casual |
| Check whether "dress for your day" policies (common in hybrid offices) apply before client meetings | Over-rely on jeans-everywhere tech-sector lore — several sectors never adopted it |

Zimbabwe's workplace formality is best understood as two economies wearing two different outfits. In the formal sector — banks, law firms, telecoms, multinationals, and the civil service — dress remains notably conservative; local coverage from The Herald describes business formal as the norm in banking, law, and senior roles, with tailored dark suits and closed-toe shoes still the expectation rather than the exception. But that formal sector is, numerically, the minority experience. ZimStat's own labour force data puts 86.8 percent of employed Zimbabweans in informal work, where a dress code is a theoretical concept rather than a disciplinary one. The practical result for an arriving professional is that Zimbabwe does not have a single national dress culture so much as a narrow formal lane running through a vast informal highway, and almost all expatriate hiring happens on that lane. The Herald's own advice column for office newcomers runs through the categories deliberately — casual, smart casual, business casual, business formal — as though dress were a language with grammar to be learned, which, functionally, it is: get the register wrong in a conservative sector and colleagues will read it as a signal about how seriously you take the job, not just the wardrobe.

What Hofstede Insights' cultural dimensions data can offer here is limited and worth stating plainly: Zimbabwe does not have an individually scored Hofstede profile, so comparisons rely on the broader Sub-Saharan African regional pattern — generally high power distance and strong collectivism — rather than a precise national figure. That regional lean toward hierarchy tracks with what shows up in practice: deference to seniority and formality with superiors in Zimbabwean offices runs noticeably deeper than the informality of the wider economy would suggest, and dressing respectfully for one's position in the hierarchy still matters more than personal style.

The United Kingdom, by contrast, has spent the last five years quietly abandoning the dress code it spent the previous century enforcing. Indeed's Hiring Lab UK found that in July 2025, 3.3 percent of UK job postings explicitly referenced casual dress — more than ten times the pre-pandemic share, though down from a 2023 peak of roughly 4 percent. The driver is not egalitarianism so much as demographics and remote work: Millennials, now the UK's largest working generation, grew up without ties, and hybrid schedules make daily formality harder to justify for a role performed partly from a kitchen table. YouGov polling backs this up from the employee side: 57 percent of UK adults say their ideal workplace dress code is smart casual, against just 18 percent who want something stricter, with the preference noticeably stronger among women (66 percent) than men (47 percent), who are more likely to still want a formal standard to point to.

But formality in the UK has not disappeared evenly. London's financial and legal sectors remain the conspicuous holdouts — Indeed's data shows London posting the lowest casual-dress mentions of any UK region, likely because its finance and corporate-law employers never got the memo everyone else received. A new arrival landing a role at a City law firm should expect the traditional uniform of dark suit, ironed shirt, and conservative shoes; a new arrival landing almost anything outside London, or inside most tech and creative firms, should expect something closer to what one would wear to a nice dinner, and nothing closer to that. Hybrid working has added its own wrinkle: a growing number of UK employers now run informal "dress for your day" policies, under which the jeans are fine until a client meeting appears on the calendar, at which point the expectation quietly reverts to something closer to the old rules, unannounced.

Here is the part the two countries have in common that neither advertises: both formal dress cultures are shrinking relative to their economies, just for opposite reasons. Zimbabwe's formal-sector dress code is stable but shrinking in relevance, simply because fewer people work inside it. The UK's formal dress code is shrinking by active abandonment, even as the underlying office-based economy stays large. Put differently, a visitor who only read Zimbabwe's business etiquette guides would overestimate how much of the country dresses that way; a visitor who only read pre-2020 descriptions of British workplace culture would overestimate it too, just in the other direction. The more genuinely useful predictor in both countries turns out to be sector and seniority, not nationality — a Harare bank and a London bank have more in common with each other, sartorially, than either has with its own country's informal-sector or tech-sector counterpart.

The irony sharpens further on engagement. Gallup's 90 percent disengagement figure for UK workers sits oddly next to a national mythology of understated professionalism — people are evidently willing to dress the part of a job they have, by their own report, mentally checked out of. Zimbabwe's formal-sector workers, operating in an economy where formal employment itself is scarce and valuable, by most expatriate and local accounts invest more visible pride in the appearance of their roles, precisely because holding one at all is the harder-won outcome. The country with the more casual dress code, in other words, is not obviously the more relaxed one to work in; it is simply the one where fewer people still bother dressing for a role they have quietly stopped believing in.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (r/AskUK) — a new hire starting at a law firm records office asked what to wear on day one; the consensus was to arrive overdressed in a dark suit and tie, since removing a jacket later is easy but recovering from showing up underdressed is not.*

> *Reddit (r/AskBrits) — an American weighing a move to the UK asked what to expect; the one substantive reply noted that, outside specific sectors, the British genuinely don't scrutinise what you wear nearly as much as reputation suggests.*

> *Expat.com forum (England business-etiquette thread) — a newly arrived professional was told that UK office dress varies enormously by employer: formal in London finance, markedly relaxed at a primary school down the road, with no single national standard to rely on.*

> *Talesmag Post Report (Harare) — an expatriate posted that diplomatic workplaces expect full formal dress, ties included, while the broader Zimbabwean business world has quietly dropped the necktie, and that Harare's mild, high-altitude climate means a medium jacket covers nearly the whole year.*

> *Herald (Zimbabwe) advice column on office dress — the recurring piece of guidance for newcomers unsure of a workplace's expectations is simple: when in doubt, dress one notch more formally than you think necessary, and ask directly rather than guess.*

### Conclusion

The single fact worth carrying into either move is this: neither country's dress code is really national, and both are more sector-coded than the guidebooks suggest. In Zimbabwe, the deciding question is whether your job sits inside the narrow formal economy or the vast informal one — if it's the former, dress conservatively and err upward. In the UK, the deciding question is whether your sector or city ever received the casual-dress memo — London finance and law largely didn't, almost everywhere else largely did. Neither answer is really about the country's culture in the abstract; both are about which slice of its economy you've actually landed in. Pack accordingly, ask a local colleague before your first week rather than after it, and treat any guidebook — including this one — as a starting estimate to be corrected by the first person who actually works there.

If a friend asked me over a drink which one to pack for: in Zimbabwe, dress for the job you want people to think you already have; in the UK, dress for the job you have and hope nobody notices you stopped caring about it around 2021.
