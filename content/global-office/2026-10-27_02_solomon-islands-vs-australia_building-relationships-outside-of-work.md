**Everyone Will Know Your Business, and Half of Them Will Be Related to You: Solomon Islands and Australia, Compared**

**🇸🇧 Solomon Islands · 🇦🇺 Australia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Honiara, you can turn up to a colleague's house unannounced on a Sunday and be fed before you've finished explaining why you came. In Sydney, you can work beside someone for three years and still not know their surname, let alone their address — not from unfriendliness, but because Hofstede Insights scores Australia a startling 90 on individualism, among the highest in the world, versus a culture across the water in which almost nothing, least of all friendship, happens outside the context of an obligation to someone else.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇧 Solomon Islands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept that your "wantok" — people who share your language group, village, or clan — come with real, reciprocal obligations, including financial ones | Treat a colleague's extended family network as background noise; it's often the actual decision-making structure |
| Learn basic Solomon Islands Pidgin (Pijin) early — it signals effort and opens doors that English alone won't | Assume quiet, unhurried pacing in a conversation means disinterest — it's often simply the norm |
| Expect to be invited into someone's home relatively quickly, and reciprocate when you can | Expect formal nightlife or Western-style "networking events" — nightlife is limited to a handful of venues |
| Show up to community or church events if invited — they are genuine social infrastructure, not optional extras | Discuss your sexual orientation or relationship status too openly if it departs from local norms — discretion is the safer default |
| Budget time, not just money, for relationship-building; trust is earned slowly, not transactionally | Expect a large pre-existing expat social scene outside Honiara — it is small and tight-knit, not anonymous |
| Lean on your wantok network for introductions — it is how most things, including informal work opportunities, actually happen | Assume a work permit or long-term arrangement will be simple to secure — bureaucracy here moves at its own pace |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say yes to the first few invitations to a barbecue, trivia night, or Friday knock-off drinks — this is where real relationships start | Expect colleagues to invite you into pre-existing friend groups automatically — you may have to ask first |
| Learn to banter — mild, affectionate teasing ("taking the piss") is often a sign of acceptance, not hostility | Take early teasing personally — read it as inclusion, not an insult, unless it clearly isn't |
| Be direct and self-deprecating rather than status-conscious when meeting people | Oversell your achievements or credentials — "tall poppy syndrome" means standing out too much can backfire socially |
| Expect "mateship" to be demonstrated through actions — shared effort, turning up, reliability — more than declarations | Assume a friendly chat at work automatically becomes a friendship outside it — many Australians keep the two separate |
| Make the first move on weekend plans if you want them — waiting to be invited can mean waiting a long time | Underestimate how long-settled friend groups can be; breaking into one takes patience, especially past your twenties |
| Join a sports club, dog park crowd, or hobby group — Australians often build adult friendships through shared activity, not proximity | Assume loneliness is unusual or your fault — immigrant and newcomer forums are full of people describing exactly this |

### Solomon Islands

The wantok system is the single most important fact about Solomon Islands social life, and almost nothing about relationships here makes sense without it. Research from the Australian National University's Development Policy Centre describes wantok ties — built on shared language, village, or kin — as carrying real, enforceable reciprocal obligation: wantok will house you, feed you, and expect the same in return, and this logic extends well beyond family into workplaces, politics, and civic life generally. Formal statistics are thin on the ground for a country of roughly 700,000 people, but the World Bank's broader Pacific household survey work consistently finds that informal kin-based support networks, not formal insurance, welfare, or savings systems, are what most households actually rely on in hard times — which is also, functionally, how most people build their social world.

That same density of obligation shapes how slowly and specifically outsiders are absorbed into it. A TalesMag posting report from a spouse who lived in Honiara for two and a half years described the local population as extremely friendly and communicative, with no reported problems around ethnic, racial, or religious prejudice — but also noted a genuinely small expat community and limited nightlife, meaning social life for a newcomer runs almost entirely through personal relationships rather than public venues. On Expat.com's Solomon Islands forum, one contributor advised newcomers directly not to "limit yourself through language barriers if you are not proficient with pidgin," and described some of the best friendships of their life as having formed with local people specifically — a reminder that the wantok system isn't a wall against outsiders so much as a different, slower door into belonging.

### Australia

Australia's version of belonging looks almost architecturally opposite: Hofstede's individualism score of 90 is among the highest recorded anywhere, alongside a relatively low power-distance score of 36, suggesting a culture where relationships are formed between equals, by choice, rather than inherited through obligation or hierarchy. In practice this produces the stereotype — "mateship" — that is real but also, per repeated immigrant accounts, surprisingly hard to access from outside. Industry guides for newcomers routinely describe the Friday-afternoon drink, the weekend barbecue, and the office trivia night as the actual infrastructure of Australian adult friendship, layered on top of, rather than instead of, professional relationships.

The gap between the stereotype and the lived experience shows up clearly in immigrant-support spaces. On a Beyond Blue Forums thread specifically about new-immigrant isolation, one poster described years in Australia without having built "the friend network" they'd had elsewhere, noting that "no one seems to have a background like me," and described feeling shut out of their own Australian partner's long-established friend group — until, after considerable anxiety, a women's weekend with that group went better than feared and led to further invitations. Another poster on the same thread, who'd returned to Australia as a child with a Canadian accent, remembered that making friends at school was hard precisely because "the list was endless" of small cultural differences locals didn't register as differences at all. The lesson for an adult professional arriving cold: Australian friendliness is genuine and immediate at the surface, but converting it into an actual friend group takes initiative that the culture expects you to supply yourself.

### The Reckoning

The real irony here is that both cultures would tell you, sincerely, that they're easy to make friends in — and both would be lying by omission in exactly the opposite direction. In the Solomon Islands, you are pulled into an existing, obligatory web of relationships almost immediately, whether or not you are ready for the reciprocal demands that come with it; in Australia, you are left pleasantly alone, and the pulling-in has to be done largely by you. One culture's social structure is a slow-moving net you can't avoid; the other's is a wide-open field you have to walk across yourself, repeatedly, often past your own assumption that someone else should do the inviting.

What this means in practice for someone relocating for work: in the Solomon Islands, the discipline is learning to say yes to obligation without resentment, since the same network that asks something of you is the one that will actually support you. In Australia, the discipline is the opposite — learning to generate your own invitations rather than waiting for an organic one, because per that Beyond Blue thread and the dozens like it, waiting is how otherwise sociable, well-adjusted newcomers end up quietly isolated for years in a country famous for being friendly.

It's worth adding that neither failure mode is really about likability. The Solomon Islands newcomer who struggles isn't disliked; they simply haven't yet entered the reciprocal web that makes belonging legible there, and entering it takes time that can't be rushed by being more charming. The Australian newcomer who struggles isn't disliked either, by and large; they've usually just been too polite to invite themselves along, mistaking the absence of an invitation for the absence of interest, when the far more common explanation is simply that everyone else assumed somebody else would ask first.

### Conclusion

If relationship-building is the thing you're actually optimizing for in this move, the honest advice diverges sharply by destination. In the Solomon Islands, surrender to the wantok logic rather than fighting it — the obligations are the relationship, not a tax on it. In Australia, treat the famous friendliness as an opening bid, not a finished transaction, and be the one who organizes the second barbecue. Either way, don't mistake a culture's surface warmth, in either direction, for information about how quickly it will actually let you in.
