# In Singapore, Onboarding Is a Spreadsheet. In Nigeria, It's an Uncle.

🇸🇬 Singapore · 🇳🇬 Nigeria

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Singapore's onboarding orthodoxy runs to ninety days, several PDFs, and an assigned buddy who may never speak to you again after week two. Nigeria's most durable management-training institution has run for over a century, has no PDF, and requires living with your boss for six to eight years. Both countries call this "developing people." Only one hands out a trading business as a graduation gift.

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the onboarding portal cover to cover — HR assumes you did | Assume a manager's "we'll think about it" is a maybe; it's a no |
| Ask explicitly for mentorship time — seniors rarely volunteer it | Interrupt silence in a meeting; it's thinking, not an opening |
| Show up to the welcome lunch, even briefly | Skip it to "get ahead" on task one — it reads as a signal |
| Study the org chart and cc discipline early | Expect your assigned "buddy" to become a real mentor by default |
| Confirm understanding with specific follow-ups | Take a hesitant "yes" at face value |
| Note the gap between the values deck and daily practice, quietly | Point out that gap out loud in your first month |

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn names, titles, and the greeting order in week one | Walk past a senior colleague's office without greeting them |
| Accept informal invitations — lunch, church, a family event | Expect a written manual; attach yourself to a specific person instead |
| Be warm but firm, with humor, when asked for "something small" off the books | Assume the org chart reflects who actually holds influence |
| Show up early and often for after-work gatherings | Treat one meeting as relationship-built; trust compounds over repeats |
| Ask questions freely once rapport is established | Mistake hierarchy for coldness — the two coexist here |
| Bring a notebook to unscheduled explanations; they won't repeat | Wait for a scheduled 1:1 that, culturally, was never coming |

### Singapore: The Portal Remembers Everything Except How You Feel

According to Singapore's Ministry of Manpower, the labour market has professionalized onboarding into near-bureaucratic completeness: tracked job tenure and mobility, formal 90-day integration windows at leading employers, and an HR software industry (BrioHR, Swingvy, and peers) built to ensure no checklist item is missed. On paper, it's the most thoroughly onboarded workforce in the region.

On paper is doing a lot of work in that sentence. The Gallup Singapore Workplace Report 2026 finds people managers are the single biggest lever on engagement — and that leaders themselves admit stated values and daily practice diverge in most organizations, a gap employees notice immediately. Hofstede Insights scores Singapore at 74 on power distance, meaning juniors are culturally disinclined to ask seniors for anything, mentorship included, unless directly invited — which, per Gallup, isn't happening as reliably as the onboarding deck implies. The system is excellent at issuing laptops and access badges, and inconsistent at pairing new hires with someone who actually checks in.

Ask directly for what you need. Nobody will notice you're struggling on your own timeline; the portal already marked you "onboarded."

### Nigeria: No Manual, Considerable Faculty

Nigeria's formal youth labour market is under real strain — the National Bureau of Statistics has tracked youth unemployment and underemployment running into double digits in recent years, with tens of millions of young Nigerians unemployed or underemployed as of 2025, which makes any functioning entry point into paid work fiercely competed for. Formal onboarding does exist: firms like McKinsey run structured apprenticeship-style programs for graduates in Lagos, pairing new analysts with coaching from day one, per the firm's own account of its Young Leaders Programme.

But the country's real signature contribution to workforce development predates any consulting firm's org chart. The Igbo apprenticeship system — Igba Boi, informally the "Nwa Boy" system — has, per BusinessDay NG and academic accounts, trained generations of traders by having a young apprentice live under an established "Oga" for six to eight years, receiving food, shelter, and hands-on mentorship in exchange for labor, before graduating with seed capital to start independently. Hofstede Insights puts Nigeria's power distance at 80, higher than Singapore's — yet that hierarchy runs alongside an aggressively personal style of instruction: proximity, not process, is the training method. TechCabal's coverage of Lagos tech suggests the corporate world has imported some of this instinct, with growth-stage startups leaning on relationship-heavy onboarding even while adding formal HR trappings.

### The Reckoning

Here is the irony neither HR department will volunteer: Singapore, which writes everything down, produces workers who report on Glassdoor and Quora that leadership development is inconsistent and internal politics decide who gets ahead more than any documented mentorship track. Nigeria, which writes almost nothing down formally, has an unofficial training program old enough to have alumni generations deep, some of whom became the country's most prominent traders and property owners.

A higher Hofstede power-distance score should predict the more remote senior colleague. Nigeria's 80 beats Singapore's 74, yet it's Lagos, not Singapore, that turns up more often in first-hand accounts as the place where a boss personally walks a newcomer through the unwritten rules over a shared meal. Formality is not the same thing as attentiveness — Singapore has built the more sophisticated onboarding machine and the more mentorship-starved culture, while Nigeria has built almost no onboarding machine and, informally, a rather effective one.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — a foreign hire in Singapore noted that many firms expect long hours as unspoken norm, the office as a "second home," and that treatment varies sharply between being classed internally as "foreign talent" versus merely a "foreign worker."*

> *Quora — in a thread on why Singaporean employers' Glassdoor reviews skew negative, one respondent pointed to internal political maneuvering and a lack of real leadership development, despite firms' formal talk of career growth and mentoring.*

> *Glassdoor — a reviewer at a Singapore-based firm described onboarding software so outdated that new hires had to repeatedly beg IT for basic system access before doing any actual work.*

> *An expat blog documenting one executive's first two months in Nigeria (via selectglobal.co.in) described the real onboarding as learning "street smartness" fast — reading people and situations on instinct, since no briefing prepares you — and mastering a firm, good-humored no to frequent informal "do you have something for me" requests, with nobody officially warning him this was part of the job.*

> *Quora — a long-term foreign worker in Nigeria described colleagues as far warmer than the formal hierarchy suggested, with senior staff strict on paper but quick to informally absorb newcomers into personal and family networks well beyond what any onboarding process required.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing Singapore against Nigeria for the quality of your first ninety days: neither will hand you a mentor who simply appears. Singapore hands you a beautifully organized portal and expects you to ask, specifically and repeatedly, for the human attention the portal cannot provide. Nigeria skips the portal and most of the paperwork, and instead folds you into somebody's actual life — lunch invitations, family events, instruction over shared meals — on the tacit understanding that this is the real training program and always was.

Neither approach is more "developed"; they're optimized for different failure modes. Singapore's risk is a flawless onboarding that never produces a relationship. Nigeria's risk is a rich relationship that never produces a manual, so take notes fast. If a friend asked me which to pick, I'd tell her: in Singapore, learn to ask for help out loud, because no one is coming to offer it; in Nigeria, learn to say no politely but firmly, because everyone is coming to offer you something, and not all of it is help.
