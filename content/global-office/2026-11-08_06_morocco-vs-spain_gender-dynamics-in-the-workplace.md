# Morocco Has 19% of Its Women in the Workforce. Spain Has a Pay Gap Report That Keeps Getting Worse Anyway.

🇲🇦 Morocco · 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The World Bank's figure for Morocco is blunt enough to need no commentary: roughly 19% of Moroccan women participate in the labor force, a number low enough that the institution has publicly warned it is actively dragging down the country's growth potential. Spain, by contrast, has near-parity labor force participation and a legal framework built to protect it — and yet the EADA Business School and ICSA Grupo's own annual report found the pay gap in senior management widening in 2025 after seven straight years of narrowing, landing at 13%. One country has a presence problem. The other has, after fixing the presence problem, discovered it still has a ceiling problem. Neither brochure mentions either.

## Do's and Don'ts: Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect mixed-gender meetings to be increasingly normal in Casablanca's corporate and finance sectors specifically | Assume the same norms apply uniformly nationwide — rural and smaller-city offices can be far more conservative |
| Support female colleagues' authority explicitly and visibly in meetings, especially with outside clients | Make assumptions about a woman's availability for travel or long hours without asking — family obligations are often used as a gatekeeping excuse by others, not a real limit she's set |
| Take workplace harassment protections seriously — Morocco criminalized sexual harassment with real penalties, and enforcement is a live, discussed issue | Treat harassment concerns as culturally overblown; local surveys report the large majority of Moroccan women face harassment in public and work settings |
| Recognize that women in Moroccan management are often highly credentialed, having had to clear a higher bar to get there | Act surprised that a senior woman exists in the room — it is both rude and, statistically, increasingly common |
| Ask, don't assume, about comfort with physical greetings across genders | Treat the low female labor participation rate as proof women don't want to work — surveys consistently cite barriers, not preference |

## Do's and Don'ts: Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect women at every level, including senior leadership, as unremarkable and standard | Assume formal gender parity has solved informal bias — the management pay gap is real and, as of the latest report, growing |
| Call out casual sexist remarks in meetings; Spanish corporate culture increasingly treats this as a manager's job, not just HR's | Dismiss "micromachismo" as a foreign media exaggeration — it's a term Spaniards themselves use to describe subtle, everyday workplace bias |
| Support flexible parental leave uptake by men as well as women; it's increasingly normalized and encouraged | Assume long Spanish work hours fall evenly on everyone — domestic labor expectations still skew toward women outside the office |
| Treat negotiating pay as acceptable and expected, including for women | Assume a woman's seniority is new or token — Spain has decades of women in professional leadership, just with a stubborn compensation gap on top |
| Raise pay transparency proactively; Spanish law increasingly requires it | Assume Spain's legal gender-equality framework means the culture has fully caught up to the law |

## Morocco: The Participation Problem Comes First

Morocco's gender story in the workplace starts further back than most of its regional peers would like to admit: the World Bank's repeatedly cited figure of roughly 19% female labor force participation places Morocco behind several countries not typically held up as development benchmarks, a comparison Moroccan outlets including Hespress and Le360 have reported on with some discomfort. The causes are structural as much as cultural — childcare infrastructure, transport safety, and informal-sector dominance all suppress the number before any individual workplace's culture even gets a chance to help or hurt.

Where women do enter formal, white-collar employment — disproportionately concentrated in Casablanca's banking, finance, and multinational-corporate sectors — the picture shifts meaningfully. Those women are frequently highly educated relative to male peers, a pattern World Bank labor-market analysis attributes partly to selection: the barriers to entry are high enough that women who clear them tend to be strongly credentialed. Workplace harassment remains a serious, openly discussed problem rather than a settled one; a widely cited Moroccan survey found that a large majority of women report facing harassment in public and professional settings, and Morocco World News has tracked both the legal reforms (criminal penalties for workplace sexual harassment since a 2018 law) and the persistent enforcement gap that follows most such reforms.

The generational split inside Morocco is sharper than the national averages suggest. Younger, urban, university-educated women entering multinational employers in Casablanca or Rabat report workplace environments that look genuinely comparable to European peers in terms of formal respect and advancement opportunity, while women in smaller cities or more traditional sectors describe a far more conservative set of expectations around visibility, travel, and after-hours socializing. A foreign manager transplanted into one of these environments and assuming the other is the single most common cultural misstep international HR consultancies flag when onboarding staff into Moroccan operations.

## Spain: Parity on Paper, a Ceiling That's Climbing Back

Spain presents almost the inverse problem. Female labor force participation sits close to male participation, and decades of EU-aligned equality law have normalized women in professional and even senior roles to a degree that would read as unremarkable in most Western European offices. But the EADA/ICSA Grupo report — an annual, methodologically consistent study that has tracked this specific gap for years — found the gender pay gap in management positions widening again as of its most recent edition, after seven consecutive years of narrowing, settling at 13% in senior leadership specifically.

Spaniards have their own vocabulary for the subtler end of this problem: "micromachismo," a term that has migrated from academic feminist writing into mainstream workplace conversation, describing small, often unconscious behaviors — interrupting, over-explaining, assuming domestic responsibilities fall on the woman in the room — that persist underneath formal equality. Hofstede Insights' comparative data situates Spain closer to the middle of the masculinity-femininity dimension than its Northern European neighbors, consistent with a culture where formal gender equality has advanced faster than some of the informal defaults beneath it. The practical result is a workplace that looks equal in an org chart and still produces a management pay gap that, inconveniently, just ticked up.

Spain's own labor regulators have responded with transparency mandates rather than aspiration alone — pay registries and gender-gap reporting requirements have expanded over the past several years, on the theory that visibility forces correction faster than cultural goodwill does. The EADA/ICSA report's own authors frame the renewed widening as evidence that legal tools alone have a ceiling of their own: once the easiest gains from transparency are banked, the remaining gap turns out to be made of subtler material — informal sponsorship networks, who gets considered "leadership material" early in their career, and who is expected to manage the household alongside the job. None of that shows up on a pay-equity audit until someone goes looking for it specifically.

## The Reckoning

Morocco's challenge is almost entirely about the door: getting women into the formal workforce at a rate that matches their education and ambition, against real structural and safety barriers that no individual company culture can fully offset. Spain's challenge is almost entirely about the room: women are already inside it in large numbers, and the remaining gap lives in subtler mechanisms — who gets promoted faster, who gets paid more for the same title, who gets interrupted in the meeting. These are not versions of the same problem at different stages of a shared timeline; they're genuinely different problems that happen to produce the same headline phrase, "gender dynamics in the workplace."

For anyone relocating, this means the useful advice is almost opposite in each direction. In Morocco, visibly supporting a female colleague's authority is a meaningfully rare and appreciated act, precisely because so few women are in the room to begin with. In Spain, that same gesture can land as mildly patronizing, because the room has long since normalized her presence — what's appreciated there is closer scrutiny of pay, promotion speed, and who's quietly expected to leave early for the school run.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com Morocco forum — paraphrased experience: A foreign professional relocating to Casablanca describes being surprised by how senior and confident the women in her finance-sector office were, after having been warned by friends back home to expect something closer to total exclusion.*

> *Expat Exchange, 'What do expats find most challenging' (Morocco) — paraphrased experience: A contributor notes that harassment in public spaces, rather than in the office itself, was the bigger adjustment, describing the workplace as comparatively more respectful than the commute to it.*

> *Reddit r/askspain (via redlib mirror) — paraphrased experience: A recent immigrant to Spain describes being startled by how openly colleagues joke about "micromachismo" in meetings, treating it as a real, nameable workplace issue rather than something to downplay.*

> *Morocco World News, harassment survey coverage — paraphrased experience: A commentator on the 2024 survey data notes that younger Moroccan professional women increasingly report the legal framework as real but enforcement as inconsistent, varying heavily by employer and city.*

## Conclusion

If you're a woman — or managing one, or hiring one — moving between these two markets, the honest briefing is that Morocco will test your patience with structural barriers before you ever reach the office door, and once inside a modern Casablanca firm, the culture may be more progressive than the country's headline statistics suggest. Spain will hand you formal equality from day one and then quietly make you watch a pay gap report tick upward anyway. Neither country has solved this. They've just solved different halves of it, in the wrong order from each other.
