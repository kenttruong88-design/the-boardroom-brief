**Bhutan Is Losing Its Graduates. Australia Is Losing Its Patience With Them.**

**🇧🇹 Bhutan · 🇦🇺 Australia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In the twenty-seven years since Bhutan became the last country on Earth to legalise television, it has moved fast enough to produce a generation that would rather clean hotel rooms in Canberra than sit the civil service exam at home. Australia, meanwhile, has spent the same period quietly assembling a workforce with five living generations in it, at least two of which disagree, with some bitterness, about whether a "quick call" requires a calendar invite. Both countries will tell you their young people have changed. Only one of them is losing a quarter of its 2024 teacher resignations to the airport.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇹 Bhutan
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear the gho or kira to any government office — it is still effectively mandatory. | Assume "business casual" is a real category outside a handful of Thimphu startups. |
| Treat a civil service posting as the default prestige job, because almost everyone else will too. | Expect that prestige to translate into either high pay or fast promotion. |
| Expect career decisions to run through family obligation — eldest children are often the household's main earner-in-waiting. | Assume a colleague's five-year plan includes staying in Bhutan; for many graduates it genuinely doesn't. |
| Work through rank and seniority deliberately before approaching a manager's manager. | Mistake Bhutanese deference for the absence of office politics — it just moves more quietly. |
| Budget extra time for decisions; consensus-building is the norm, not a delay tactic. | Be shocked when a well-qualified younger colleague resigns with no notice; the "Australia rush" is now routine enough to have its own nickname. |
| Ask directly about vacancy numbers before relocating for a government role. | Assume degree plus civil service exam equals guaranteed placement — the ratio of qualified candidates to posts has been tightening for years. |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect blunt, direct feedback regardless of seniority. | Mistake that bluntness, or the informality that comes with it, for a lack of hierarchy. |
| Raise flexible or hybrid work arrangements in the first conversation, not the exit interview. | Assume "job for life" loyalty exists anywhere outside the public service and a shrinking slice of finance. |
| Take seriously a younger hire's questions about purpose, mentoring, and mental health. | Write those questions off as fragility — Gen Z workers rank culture fit above pay as a reason to stay. |
| Prepare to manage, or be managed by, people two generations removed from you. | Assume younger colleagues are the junior ones by default; some of the fastest promotions now skip a generation. |
| Expect older colleagues to stay working well past 65 — the pension age is now 67. | Expect the corner office to turn over on any predictable schedule. |
| Negotiate salary, leave, and conditions explicitly and early. | Expect tenure alone to buy goodwill; national engagement figures have been sliding for two straight years. |

Bhutan's workforce problem starts with arithmetic. More than a third of the population is under twenty-five, and youth unemployment sat at 17.7 percent in the final quarter of 2024 — nearly six times the national rate of 3.11 percent, and worse still for young urban women, at 19.8 percent against 14.5 percent for young men, according to the National Statistics Bureau's own labour force survey. Into that gap steps the civil service: roughly 25,000 strong, closer to 40,000 counting state corporations, in a country of under 800,000 people — a share of the formal economy that would be considered a nationalisation program anywhere else. For decades this was the plan working as intended: a secure government post was the reward for schooling, and 417 education graduates competing for 182 teaching vacancies, as one domestic commentator noted, was treated less as a crisis than a sign the system had credibility.

The generational break is that fewer young Bhutanese now want that reward, and the ones who still qualify increasingly leave anyway. A World Bank household survey conducted in mid-2024 found that nearly 70 percent of voluntary civil service resignations that year came from just two sectors, education and health, with education alone accounting for roughly a quarter of all outbound migrants. Once in Australia, six in ten of those migrants take low-skilled jobs — cleaning, aged care, hospitality — despite having held professional roles at home; only 3 percent stay in professional work. And yet the economics still make sense to them: most earned under Nu 40,000 a month in Bhutan and now clear Nu 60,000 or more abroad, with over 40 percent topping Nu 220,000 — roughly the income bracket of Bhutan's top one percent of earners. Thirty-eight percent cite inadequate pay as the main reason for leaving; almost half say Bhutan simply has no suitable path to work in their field. The older generation built a civil service to absorb the young. The young have started treating that civil service as the fallback, not the finish line.

Australia's generational story is less about scarcity than arithmetic of a different kind. The 2021 Census found Millennials had all but overtaken Baby Boomers as the country's largest generational bloc — each group over 5.4 million people, separated by fewer than 6,000 individuals nationwide — while Generation Z now makes up 18 percent of the population and the pension-eligibility age climbs toward 67. The practical result, as Australian workplace coverage has noted, is up to five generations sharing an office for the first time, with no obvious consensus on what any of them owe each other. Deloitte's 2025 Global Gen Z and Millennial Survey found both cohorts newly anxious rather than newly entitled: 48 percent of Gen Z and 46 percent of Millennials now report feeling financially insecure, up sharply from 30 and 32 percent the year before, and only 6 percent of Gen Z name reaching senior leadership as a primary career goal. Half say they want a manager who actively mentors them; roughly a third say they're actually getting one. Meanwhile Gallup's global data shows Australia and New Zealand's once-leading job-market optimism dropping twelve points in a single year, hardest among employees ordered back to the office despite remote-capable roles. The stereotype of the demanding young worker survives mainly among the managers who haven't yet read the engagement numbers.

Put the two side by side and the irony sharpens. Bhutan's older generation is accused, loudly and in its own press, of having built an entitlement culture around government jobs; Bhutan's younger generation is responding to that entitlement by leaving the country, taking a demonstrable pay rise, and accepting a demotion in status to get it. Australia's older generation accuses its younger one of the same entitlement — wanting flexibility, purpose, mentoring — while the data suggests the young are simply asking for things that used to be assumed, not escalating their demands. Hofstede Insights, for what it's worth, has no official cultural-dimension score for Bhutan at all; the closest available proxy is the wider South Asian pattern of high power distance and group-first decision-making, a poor match for Australia's firmly documented low power distance and famously individualist 90-point score. The two workplaces aren't just generationally mismatched. They're not even measured on the same instrument.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A tech worker's complaint about senior colleagues refusing to collaborate drew a split response: some commenters backed the "boomers block everything" read, others argued "boomer" has become shorthand for a mindset rather than an age, and suggested the poster's own expectations were part of the friction.*

> *Expat.com Bhutan forum — A Bhutanese member answering a newcomer's etiquette questions described national dress as effectively compulsory in government offices and admitted the country's time-management habits were "poor but improving" — a candid local admission that outsiders rarely get on a tourist itinerary.*

> *The Bhutanese (reader interview, Canberra) — A former Bhutanese journalist described her first Australian job as hotel housekeeping, thirty minutes per room, roughly AUD 22 an hour, "quite challenging" by her own account — and noted that younger arrivals now post the grind openly on TikTok, while older migrants who left office jobs tend to stay quiet about it "to keep up appearances back home."*

> *The Bhutanese (six-youths feature) — A 23-year-old graduate said plainly that Bhutan's pay and opportunities were "too poor," and that work matching her actual skills and qualifications — not just any job — was the one condition that would have kept her from leaving.*

> *The Bhutanese (entitlement-culture commentary) — A domestic columnist argued that Bhutan's brightest consistently choose government security over private-sector risk, and that even the country's own civil service "elite" privately concede the system now rewards status over output.*

### Conclusion

The honest advice for anyone weighing these two postings is to stop asking which country treats its young workers better and start asking which problem you'd rather have. In Bhutan, the generational rupture is structural: an economy too small and too civil-service-heavy to absorb the graduates it produces, so the young leave, take a pay cut in status and a pay rise in cash, and call it rational. In Australia, the rupture is more like a long, mutually irritating negotiation inside an economy that can actually afford to have one — five generations arguing over mentoring, flexibility, and who has to come back to the office, while the underlying anxiety is less about entitlement than about money and AI, in roughly that order.

If you're moving for the work itself, Australia will cost you less certainty about hierarchy and more about job security than the brochure admits; Bhutan will cost you almost the opposite. Either way, ask the people already there what they actually do all day, not what their government or their LinkedIn says they do — and if a Bhutanese colleague mentions Perth more than once in a conversation, don't be surprised when the conversation turns out to have been a goodbye.
