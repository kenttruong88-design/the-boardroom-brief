**The Org Chart and the Open Secret: Generational Change at Work in Georgia and Laos**

**🇬🇪 Georgia · 🇱🇦 Laos**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Georgia, the generational divide at work is loud, measurable, and showing up in national survey data: younger employees are visibly pulling away from the deference their parents showed authority, and the numbers track it year over year. In Laos, the same divide is real but quieter — a "young, modern" NGO office might run on first names and flat structure, while the traditional ministry down the street still runs on rank, and almost nobody says so directly. Both countries are changing. Only one of them is arguing about it out loud.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇪 Georgia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a real generational split in how authority is treated — younger Georgians show measurably less automatic deference than older colleagues | Assume "Georgian hierarchy" is one uniform culture; the data shows it's actively diverging by age cohort |
| Take Gen Z's stated priorities seriously: flexible conditions and autonomy rank well above pay for many young job-seekers | Offer only a bigger salary to retain a young employee who's actually asking for flexibility or professional freedom |
| Recognize entrepreneurship as a genuinely popular alternative to traditional employment among people in their 20s | Assume every ambitious young Georgian wants a conventional corporate ladder; many are actively opting out of it |
| Expect real churn risk — a large share of young people are actively weighing leaving the country for work or study | Treat a young hire's loyalty as guaranteed just because the local job market looks tight on paper |
| Pay older employees the loyalty and deference Commisceo's research describes, while coaching younger hires through collaborative, team-based processes they may not have been trained in | Manage both generations identically; the research describes genuinely different starting points for each |

#### 🇱🇦 Laos

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect age and seniority to function as the default basis of authority almost everywhere, with real cross-generational uniformity | Assume a "modern" job title or a foreign employer automatically flattens the hierarchy; it often doesn't |
| Notice the real exception: younger, foreign-linked, or NGO-style offices can run noticeably flatter than traditional domestic organizations | Assume every younger-staffed office in Vientiane works the same way — it varies sharply by organization type, not just by employee age |
| Learn to read indirect signals of disagreement rather than waiting for someone to say "no" outright, regardless of their age | Push a younger colleague to openly contradict a senior figure in a meeting; it risks "losing face" for everyone involved, generational attitudes aside |
| Budget real time for hand-processed bureaucracy and a long, genuinely social lunch — both cut across generations here | Expect younger staff to privately resent the slower pace; the available evidence doesn't show a generational revolt against it |
| Watch the demographic pressure build: nearly a third of the population is under 25, and youth un- and under-employment is a real, documented problem | Assume Laos's young population automatically means a youth-driven culture shift at work; deference to elders remains the default even among the young |

### Georgia: The Numbers Are Starting to Show the Gap

Georgia's generational story isn't speculation — it's increasingly a documented trend. Research published in the Caucasus Analytical Digest found that younger Georgians (18-35) have grown markedly more skeptical of institutions than their elders over the past decade: institutional trust in the fairness of elections dropped roughly 35 points among 18-35-year-olds between 2013 and 2024, versus about 20 points for older cohorts, while support for non-electoral activism rose 22 points among youth even as actual protest turnout fell across all age groups. The same research found younger Georgians showing both stronger pro-Western orientation and more negative views of the Soviet era than older Georgians — a values gap that doesn't stay neatly outside the office.

Commisceo Global's management guide makes the workplace version of that split explicit, in a section headed, bluntly, "Generational Divide": the older generation, it notes, tends to "display loyalty and deference to authority but show detachment from company objectives," while the younger generation tends to "demonstrate eagerness to embrace market opportunities and actively participate in teams," though it "requires coaching in collaborative processes" that older hierarchical workplaces never taught them. A Tbilisi-focused research brief adds hard numbers to the picture: 55% of young respondents rank professional freedom and flexible conditions as their top priority at work, against just 28% who prioritize compensation — a reversal of the traditional loyalty-for-security bargain older Georgian employees grew up with. The same research found 40% of 2023 entrants to the "Produce in Georgia" startup program were aged 20-30, and over 60% of participants in the 2024 "Silicon Valley Tbilisi" program were Gen Z — alongside a less comfortable number: 34% youth unemployment for ages 20-24 in 2023, and nearly 48% of young Georgians surveyed saying they're actively considering leaving the country for work or education.

### Laos: The Hierarchy Holds, With One Real Exception

Laos's generational picture reads almost the opposite way: not a loud divide, but a quiet, mostly uniform deference to age and seniority that the available evidence doesn't show breaking down by cohort. A cultural overview from Northern Illinois University's Southeast Asia program describes authority in Laos as resting substantially on age and seniority, with near-unquestioning deference to seniors built into ordinary interaction, disagreement communicated indirectly rather than stated outright, and "individual initiative" generally discouraged in favor of a more authoritative, if considerate, leadership style — a description that, notably, doesn't carve out a separate norm for younger employees.

But one genuinely useful firsthand account complicates the uniform picture. A foreign worker's personal blog about office life in Laos observed that "the sense of hierarchy is also firmer here than back in the UK," before adding a specific and telling qualifier: it's "less prominent in the young, modern NPA where I work," even though "it can be quite rigid in more traditional organisations." That's a real generational-adjacent split in Laos — but it tracks organization type (a modern, foreign-linked NGO versus a traditional domestic institution) at least as much as it tracks employee age. Verge Magazine's account of adapting to Lao office culture backs up the traditional side of that split in vivid detail: hand-signed, hand-delivered bureaucracy for routine approvals, prominently displayed org charts reinforcing rank visually, and a 90-minute shared lunch functioning as essential relationship glue rather than downtime — none of it described as generationally contested. Underneath both pictures sits a genuine demographic pressure point: UNDP reporting found nearly a third of Laos's population is aged 10-24, among the youngest profiles in Southeast Asia, with 39% of youth not in employment, education, or training — the highest rate in the region — meaning the workforce is getting younger fast, even if the culture inside most workplaces hasn't visibly shifted to match yet.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't whether these cultures are changing — it's whether the change is legible. Georgia's generational shift shows up in survey data, named sections of management guides, and startup-program enrollment numbers; it's a divide people openly discuss and measure. Laos's generational story is harder to see precisely because the dominant norm — deference to age and seniority — is still the norm for young and old alike in most organizations; where a real split exists, it's organizational (a modern NGO versus a traditional ministry) rather than a clean cross-society youth-versus-elders story.

The irony worth sitting with: Georgia's youth, by the numbers, are simultaneously more entrepreneurial, more mobile, and more likely to leave the workforce's traditional track entirely than any prior generation — a visible and somewhat destabilizing shift for employers used to the older deference model. Laos's youth are, demographically, the largest and fastest-growing share of the workforce the country has ever had, and yet the cultural operating system they're stepping into has not, on the available evidence, bent much to meet them. One country's young workers are reshaping the rules in real time; the other's are mostly still being asked to follow them.

> *BTU AI research brief (Tbilisi, Georgia) — A striking reversal of priorities among young Georgian workers: 55% rank professional freedom and flexible conditions above pay, with only 28% naming compensation as their top concern — a quiet rejection of the loyalty-for-security bargain their parents' generation generally accepted.*

> *Personal blog, Laos — A foreign NGO worker's firsthand read on the local hierarchy landed somewhere between the stereotype and its exception: "the sense of hierarchy is also firmer here than back in the UK," though "it's less prominent in the young, modern NPA where I work" compared with "more traditional organisations" — a reminder that in Laos, the office you land in may matter more than the generation you belong to.*

> *UNDP Lao PDR press release — A blunt demographic fact with workplace implications nobody should ignore: 39% of Lao youth are not in education, employment, or training, "the highest rate in the region," even as nearly a third of the population is under 25 — a young country whose institutions, this reporting suggests, have not yet caught up to its own age structure.*

### Conclusion

If you're managing or joining a Georgian team, come prepared for a workplace actively negotiating its own generational contract — don't assume loyalty is automatic from anyone, and don't assume flexibility alone will retain talent that might just as easily start its own company or leave the country altogether. If you're heading to Laos, come prepared for deference to age and seniority as the durable default nearly everywhere, and look specifically at the kind of organization you're joining — a modern, foreign-linked office may genuinely run flatter — rather than assuming your own age, or your colleagues', tells you much about how formal the room will be.
