# The Handshake Round and the 17th Meeting: Kenya vs. US

**🇰🇪 Kenya · 🇺🇸 United States**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

A Kenyan meeting formally opens with every single person standing up to introduce themselves, and genuinely asking after each other's families before the agenda gets a word in. An American meeting, the average worker now attends more than 17 times a week, and privately believes about 30% of them — roughly five hours — accomplish nothing at all. One culture spends the first ten minutes building the relationship the meeting needs. The other has stopped expecting the meeting to need one.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇪 Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm an appointment two or three days before it happens, after booking it weeks ahead | Treat a scheduled meeting time as fixed the moment it's calendared and forget to confirm |
| Ask after a colleague's family and wellbeing before any agenda item — it's expected, not filler | Rush straight into business; attempts to speed past greetings often lengthen the meeting instead |
| Let everyone contribute before expecting a decision, especially in private-sector settings | Expect a quick, single-voice decision in a room where contribution from everyone is the norm |
| Build in buffer time for meetings starting later than scheduled ("Swahili time" in casual use) | Show visible irritation at a meeting starting roughly an hour late without notice |
| Use professional or academic titles with surnames until told otherwise | Switch to first names before your Kenyan counterpart signals it's welcome |

#### 🇺🇸 United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Come with a clear agenda and expect the meeting to end close to on time | Assume relationship small talk is expected before getting to the point — it's often skipped entirely |
| Push back on a daily standup if it's genuinely excessive — roughly 60% of Blind-surveyed workers in one era wanted them less frequent | Accept 17+ meetings a week as simply normal without ever questioning which ones need you there |
| Decline a meeting invite you're not needed for, directly and without lengthy justification | Over-explain a scheduling conflict the way more indirect cultures might expect you to |
| Expect a manager asking for status updates to also report their own, if you push for parity | Assume a lack of small talk means a lack of warmth — it's usually just efficiency, not coldness |
| Treat meeting time as a real cost — survey estimates put annual "wasted" meeting spend over $25,000 per employee at some firms | Schedule a meeting as the default way to communicate something an email or message could handle |

### Kenya: The Meeting as a Relationship, Not an Interruption to One

Kenya has no individually published Hofstede country score; it's covered only within the broader East African regional cluster, a gap worth stating honestly rather than inventing a precise national figure that doesn't exist. What cultural guides do document in granular, almost procedural detail is how a Kenyan meeting actually unfolds: appointments are booked roughly two weeks out and reconfirmed two or three days before, meetings open with every participant standing to introduce themselves, seating often follows hierarchy, and greetings — genuine questions about a person's wellbeing, family, and business — are treated as mandatory content rather than throat-clearing before the real agenda. Cultural Atlas notes plainly that "attempts to rush a meeting may have the adverse result of lengthening discussions," which is a remarkably direct way of saying that skipping the relationship-building step doesn't save time, it costs it.

The same communication style extends into how feedback and dissent move through a room. A feature essay on Kenyan work culture published by K24 Digital describes the texture well: colleagues share food, know each other's family names, and show up for each other with a sincerity "other cultures spend money trying to create" — but that same warmth comes bundled with real obligation. Deference to bosses discourages honest upward feedback, speaking up takes careful calculation, and the diplomacy required to navigate hierarchy is, in the essay's own words, genuinely draining over time even when it produces a warmer room than the alternative. An expat forum thread describing work at a Nairobi media company backs up the practical texture: office hours commonly run 8 to 5 with a full lunch hour, Fridays run quieter, and "a little bit of office politics and bureaucracy" exists everywhere, which the poster found unremarkable compared to other markets they'd worked in.

Pace itself varies within the country in ways a visiting manager should expect rather than find confusing. Business in Nairobi runs noticeably faster than in coastal Mombasa, and decision-making speed shifts by sector as much as by city: public-sector meetings tend to funnel ideas upward for a senior figure to decide, while private-sector teams more often generate options collectively before a superior makes the final call. Either way, the room's patience for a rushed agenda is low, and a foreign manager who treats the greeting round as skippable overhead rather than the actual mechanism by which trust gets built will find every subsequent meeting slower, not faster, for having tried to shortcut the first one.

### The United States: The Meeting as Pure Overhead

Hofstede's US profile sits at the opposite pole on nearly every relevant dimension — individualism at 91, among the highest readings globally — and American meeting culture reflects that directly: the meeting exists to transmit information efficiently between individuals, not to maintain a relationship between them, which is exactly why it's so easy for the format to spiral into pure overhead with nobody noticing until the math gets done. Survey research on meeting load found workers and managers averaging over 17 meetings a week, more than 3.5 a day, with respondents estimating more than five of those as genuinely unnecessary — close to 30% of total meeting time — and nearly six hours a week lost to meetings that, by the attendees' own admission, didn't need to happen. The same research put an eyebrow-raising estimate on the cost: more than $25,000 per employee annually in "wasted" meeting investment at firms with senior, well-compensated staff, scaling to over $100 million a year in potential waste at a 5,000-person organization.

The daily standup — a format meant to solve exactly this problem by keeping status updates short — has generated its own dedicated genre of complaint. A widely upvoted Blind thread from engineers at eBay, Microsoft, Cisco, and Intel captured the frustration precisely: one poster calculated five hours a week lost to a daily 9:30-to-10:30 stand-up plus code review, another objected that managers who demand daily status updates rarely report their own, and a third, more bluntly, wrote that the format "makes me hate my life everyday." The common thread across every complaint is the same: Americans aren't rejecting meetings because they dislike talking to colleagues, they're rejecting a format that's stopped efficiently transmitting information, which is the one job American meeting culture actually expects it to do.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely useful reframe here is that neither culture's meeting style is "better" at communication — they're optimizing for different outputs entirely. Kenya's meetings are built to produce and maintain relationship capital, with information exchange as a secondary, slower-moving byproduct; American meetings are built to produce decisions and status updates as efficiently as possible, with relationship-building treated as something that happens elsewhere, if it happens at all. A Kenyan employee transferred to a US office can find American meetings jarringly transactional, information delivered with none of the social scaffolding that makes a decision feel legitimate back home. An American manager transferred to Nairobi can find the handshake rounds and family check-ins an exasperating tax on a 30-minute slot that was supposed to just confirm next week's deadline — right up until they notice how much smoother the actual decision goes once the relationship work is done first.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *teamblind.com — An engineer at a Cisco subsidiary calculated their daily stand-up plus code review at roughly five hours a week during one particularly meeting-heavy stretch, adding dryly that a junior colleague kept interrupting their allotted turn to speak: "It is my 2 to 3 minutes dude."*

> *teamblind.com — Another contributor, describing a different team's management style, objected specifically to managers who collect everyone's status update without ever reporting their own — a small structural complaint that captures a lot of what Americans actually resent about excess meetings: not the talking itself, but talking that isn't reciprocal.*

> *expat.com — One forum member who ran a small business in Nairobi said they struggled to find local staff they considered highly reliable, observing that people were "good at talking" in meetings but didn't always follow through afterward — an outside perspective worth weighing against the K24 essay's much warmer account of the same relational communication style.*

> *k24.digital — The essay's sharpest piece of advice for navigating Kenyan workplace hierarchy was to treat it "as a communication style rather than a fixed cage" — meaning respectful, well-timed pushback is genuinely possible, it just requires reading the room far more carefully than a blunt American-style objection would.*

> *Advice worth having before you land: in Kenya, never skip the greeting round to save time — it doesn't save any, and it costs you the goodwill the rest of the meeting runs on. In the US, don't assume silence means agreement in a meeting; ask directly, because the room may simply be waiting for the meeting to end rather than withholding an objection.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two for which culture will actually respect your time, the honest answer is neither does, exactly — Kenya spends your time on relationship maintenance you may not have budgeted for, and the US spends it on meetings a third of attendees privately consider pointless. The thing I'd tell a friend over a drink: in Nairobi, budget an extra fifteen minutes for the handshakes and mean the questions about someone's family; in the US, if a meeting could have been a two-line message, say so — roughly a third of your colleagues already agree with you, they're just also in the meeting.
