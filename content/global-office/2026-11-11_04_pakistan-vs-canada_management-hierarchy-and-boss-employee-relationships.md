# Pakistan Salutes the Boss, Canada Asks for His First Name

🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Canada has retired the honorific, the corner office and, in many firms, the pretence that the manager knows more than you do. Gallup's latest country data nonetheless find only 21 percent of Canadian employees engaged at work, against 31 percent across the United States and Canada combined, which suggests that calling the boss by his first name is not, in itself, a cure. Pakistan has kept the honorific, the owner's chair and a considerable respect for the organisation chart, though not always for what the chart says. This guide compares how authority is exercised, questioned and survived in each country, for the reader contemplating a move in either direction.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address seniors by title and surname until invited to do otherwise | Assume first names are welcome because the office is modern |
| Greet the most senior or oldest person first in any room | Walk past the owner on the way to your desk and call it efficiency |
| Work out who actually decides, which is often one person with no title on the door | Treat the organisation chart as a description of power |
| Arrive on time, since managers measure commitment by hours at the desk | Blame the traffic more than once |
| Raise disagreement privately and gently, ideally after you have built some goodwill | Contradict a superior in a meeting in front of his own team |
| Keep eye contact modest with seniors | Stare at a superior to signal confidence |

### In Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use your manager's first name once introduced, and ask if unsure | Say sir as a reflex; it reads as distance, not respect |
| Offer ideas, concerns and questions unprompted in one-to-ones | Mistake silence in a meeting for agreement or approval |
| Read soft feedback closely, since criticism often arrives between two compliments | Hear the compliments and miss the criticism |
| Use titles for owners and senior executives until they say otherwise | Assume a title entitles anyone to obedience, yours or theirs |
| Take your holiday and disconnect after hours | Send midnight emails as proof of devotion |
| Ask directly when something is unclear | Wait to be told, hoping rank will do the explaining |

## Pakistan

Pakistani business, according to Expat Arrivals, assigns great importance to hierarchy. Colleagues are addressed by title and surname, and the oldest or highest-ranked person is greeted first. This is the visible part. The working part, as Dawn's Faisal Bari described in March 2026, is the owner. In the owner-centred workplaces he recalls, hiring, salaries and promotions depended on personal connections, and the approval of owners and senior managers was the main measure of success, which bred uncertainty, infighting and suspicion. He also records a counter-example: a firm that interviewed him after a twenty-minute conversation, with no sifarish, the local term for a helpful introduction. It was, he implies, a remarkable thing to remember.

The pattern has a structural footing. A study by the Pakistan Institute of Development Economics, reported in The Express Tribune in December 2021, profiled 573 directors of KSE-100 companies and found that about one in four sat on boards through family ties alone, while 227 came from state institutions. The author linked this to a colonial-era seth leadership style marked by paternalism, seniority over competence and arbitrary decisions. Employees have little collective counterweight: the International Trade Union Confederation gave Pakistan its lowest rating, 5, in its 2020 index, meaning no guarantee of rights. The Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, in its 2024-25 survey, put unemployment at 7.1 percent, with 46.8 percent of all employment in the informal non-agricultural sector. Leverage, in short, lives upstairs.

Daily life reflects it. Muhammad Ibtesam Mazahir, writing in The Express Tribune in October 2025, argued that private-sector Pakistani offices equate commitment with hours at a desk and penalise late arrivals in a city where roads make punctuality a lottery. The system is not without comforts: a paternalistic boss is at least a boss who knows your name, your family and, on a good day, your rent. Whether that is protection or surveillance depends largely on the boss.

The cost of a boss who is not benevolent is documented with some candour in the local press. A ProPakistani column on work culture describes a strict, top-down style, known as danda, that leaves little room for discussion; employees who fear for their jobs do the minimum and decline to take ownership of their work. Mr Bari offers a quieter example from the banking sector, where a classmate with stronger credentials lost a post to a senior bureaucrat's son, and the bank acquired a reputation accordingly. Average monthly income in the 2024-25 survey was Rs39,042, which does not leave much room for a principled resignation.

## Canada

Canadian workplaces are, by design, less theatrical. Newcomer guidance published for immigrants says managers and employees have different roles but are expected to treat each other as equals, that first names are usually accepted after introductions, and that employers want ideas and concerns shared in one-to-ones, staff meetings and anonymous surveys. Karima-Catherine Goundiam, a Canadian founder writing in The Globe and Mail, put the newcomer's version plainly: hierarchies are flatter, titles do not command blind trust, and criticism tends to be softened between two positives. She adds that an attitude of entitlement works against the newcomer, and that some newcomers meet judgment of their accent or their lack of Canadian experience.

The egalitarian manager, however, presides over a tired team. Gallup's 2025 figures show 21 percent of Canadian employees engaged, 65 percent not engaged and 15 percent actively disengaged, with 58 percent reporting daily stress and only 50 percent thriving in life overall. The share saying it is a good time to find a job locally fell 16 points to 51 percent. Informality has not purchased enthusiasm, and the reader should not expect that a first-name basis will substitute for a good manager.

Nor is the employee's voice collective by default. Statistics Canada reports that 30.4 percent of workers were covered by a collective agreement in 2023, but only 15.5 percent in the private sector against 76.7 percent in the public sector. The International Trade Union Confederation rated Canada 3 in 2025, describing regular interference with collective rights. In most private firms, then, an employee's protection is a mixture of statute and manners, which works well until the manners fail.

Structure matters as much as manners. Immigration guidance for newcomers distinguishes between vertical organisations, common in large employers, where authority is clear but decisions can be slower, and flat ones, common in smaller firms, where managers oversee more people and each receives less close support. The advice is practical: check the organisation chart, ask your manager who you report to, and be brief with senior leaders and detailed with your supervisor. A forty-hour week is described as typical, and disconnecting after hours as normal. The equality is genuine, but it comes with a corollary that Pakistani arrivals sometimes find disorienting, namely that nobody is going to look after your career on your behalf.

## The Reckoning

Hofstede Insights offers the neatest summary. Its power-distance scores are 55 for Pakistan and 39 for Canada, a gap of sixteen points that will surprise anyone who expects the contrast to be dramatic. The wider gulf is individualism, 14 against 80. A Pakistani employer is, in effect, a patron to whom loyalty is owed and from whom protection is expected. A Canadian employer is a counterparty to a contract, polite and bounded. The first relationship reaches into your hiring, your hours and occasionally your marriage prospects; the second ends, with some ceremony, at five o'clock. Uncertainty avoidance, 70 in Pakistan and 48 in Canada, completes the picture: a hierarchy that dislikes surprises tends to manage them by deciding centrally.

Each has a characteristic hazard. In Pakistan it is arbitrariness: the rule is whatever the owner decides this quarter, and an unwritten rule is difficult to appeal. In Canada it is ambiguity: nobody shouts, nobody explains, and the verdict is delivered in a compliment. The newcomer from Pakistan will find that deference is read as passivity and will be asked, repeatedly and kindly, for opinions. The newcomer to Pakistan from Canada will find that directness is read as insolence and that the question being asked is rarely the question that matters.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/developersPak — a first-time intern at a Pakistani services agency described a two-person team led by someone he considered unqualified, who committed secrets to the code repository and defended it. Commenters offered sympathy, advised raising concerns with a manager, and suggested that foreign or product companies were the way out. He could not change teams.*

> *Blind — a young engineer at a Pakistani startup asked where to relocate. Replies were blunt: one said Pakistan was a poor place to build a software career and that leaving in 2008 was the best decision of his life; another noted that moving to Canada generally required working for a company with offices in both countries.*

> *ProPakistani comments — one reader said youth unemployment is so severe that workers feel lucky to have any job and therefore do not challenge conditions. Another told of a telecom engineer pressured to finish an impossible number of sites in a day, as if being reachable at all hours were a job description.*

> *ProPakistani Lens — a Canadian-raised woman who moved to Karachi to join her husband said her interviews dwelt on housing, in-laws and household chores rather than her seven years of experience. She had never encountered such overt sexism in Canada, though she granted that bias exists there too. One reader called her account exaggerated.*

> *The Globe and Mail — a Canadian founder who arrived as a newcomer wrote that she found criticism softened between compliments, learned to ask direct questions, and was surprised that a title earned no automatic trust. She also described isolation and bias, and advised newcomers to find mentors and build networks.*

## Conclusion

Before accepting either job, ask who really decides, and how you will find out what they think of you. In Pakistan, that means identifying the owner's circle and treating the relationship as the contract. In Canada, it means asking for the one-to-one cadence in writing, reading feedback for what lies between the compliments, and using your first-name privileges for something other than small talk. In both countries, document your agreements, since goodwill is a poor employment contract. Those weighing Canada should also budget for the slower business of being believed: newcomers' accounts, including Ms Goundiam's, suggest that early months are spent proving that a foreign title means what it says. Those weighing Pakistan should budget for the opposite problem, in which a title is believed rather too readily and attached to responsibilities that nobody has written down.

A last observation for the undecided. In Pakistan, the boss is always right. In Canada, he is merely always polite, which is a good deal harder to appeal.
