**Who Actually Watches the Baby: A Transcontinental Comparison**

**🇿🇼 Zimbabwe · 🇦🇺 Australia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Zimbabwe's answer to "who watches the baby while you work" is almost always a person: a grandmother, an aunt, sometimes a child sent home to a village entirely. Australia's answer is almost always a number: 120 days of government-funded leave, a subsidy percentage, a waitlist. Both countries will tell you they support working parents. Only one of them means it includes actual, continuous labor from someone who loves the child for free.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇿🇼 Zimbabwe

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect 98 days (about 14 weeks) of fully paid maternity leave by law, with a medical certificate | Assume paternity leave is covered; Zimbabwe's labour code is largely silent on it |
| Plan your leave window carefully — it can start no earlier than 45 days and no later than 21 days before the due date | Assume formal daycare is the default solution; private crèches are frequently described as expensive and inconsistent in quality |
| Build your actual childcare plan around extended family from day one, not as a backup | Expect to "opt out" of supporting relatives who take on childcare; the arrangement runs on reciprocal, ongoing financial support |
| Ask directly whether the 2023 reform removing the old 24-month/three-pregnancy limit applies at your specific employer; implementation has lagged the law | Underestimate the strain on grandparents already raising other children; many are stretched well before a new grandchild arrives |
| Treat sending a child to live with grandparents as a mainstream, respectable option, not a sign of failure | Assume a remote arrangement means disengaged parenting; many parents describe sending money and staying involved as the whole point |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check current entitlements directly — the government scheme has been expanding (120 days now, 130 from July 2026) and includes superannuation since mid-2025 | Assume "paid parental leave" means your old salary; the government rate is paid at the national minimum wage, not your prior pay |
| Document everything around your pregnancy announcement and leave dates | Assume discrimination is rare; a national survey found 49% of mothers reported it around pregnancy, leave, or return to work |
| Negotiate your return role and expectations before you leave, not after | Expect a full recovery of lost earning trajectory quickly; wage growth after return has been measured at roughly 7-12% lower for several years running |
| Use the "use it or lose it" partner days if you have a second parent; they can't be transferred and are designed to be used | Assume men taking leave is the norm; only about 12% of paid parental leave nationally is taken by men |
| Ask your employer directly whether they offer anything beyond the government scheme | Expect automatic reinstatement to protect you from redundancy; "restructuring" during leave remains a documented pattern, even if rare |

### Zimbabwe

The statutory baseline looks generous on paper: 98 days of fully paid maternity leave, and since a 2023 reform, that entitlement no longer caps out after 24 months or three pregnancies with one employer (wageindicator.org). What the law doesn't cover is almost everything that happens after those 14 weeks end, which is where the real cultural system takes over. Zimbabwean mothers interviewed by VOA, many working across the border in South Africa, describe formal daycare as both unaffordable and unreliable — one mother, Cynthia Ncube, said crèches were "very expensive" and described children getting sick under care that in some documented cases included unauthorized sedation, prompting many mothers to pull their kids out entirely (voazimbabwe.com).

The actual safety net is extended family. Siziwe Dube, another mother interviewed, put the core problem plainly: "you don't have anybody to leave the kids with while you are at work" — so children get sent home to grandparents in Zimbabwe, who gain a connection to their culture and extended family, while parents keep working and sending money (voazimbabwe.com). It's a functioning system, but not a free one: a third voice in the same reporting, Nondumiso Moyo, warned that some parents quietly stop supporting the grandparents once the children are out of sight, leaving already-stretched relatives — some already raising other children — to absorb the real cost alone.

### Australia

Australia's government-funded scheme has genuinely expanded: 120 days currently, rising to 130 from July 2026, with a 12% superannuation contribution added on top since mid-2025 — a real acknowledgment that time off work was quietly costing mothers their retirement savings too (anz.peoplemattersglobal.com). The catch is the payment rate: Parental Leave Pay runs at the national minimum wage, not a percentage of your actual salary, which is why the scheme is still, by the trade union movement's own description, "one of the least-generous paid parental leave schemes in the developed world" relative to OECD peers averaging 50 weeks (sbs.com.au).

The bigger issue isn't the leave itself but what happens on return. A Human Rights Commission survey of 2,000 mothers found 49% reported discrimination tied to pregnancy, leave, or returning to work, and separate wage data found women's pay growth lagging by roughly 7 to 12% for several years after coming back — a gap that, in some studies, took a full decade to close (aifs.gov.au). Outcomes vary sharply by employer: Nudzejma Avdic, promoted and given a raise a month before giving birth, called herself "one of the lucky ones" after returning "to an even better position" than she'd left; Keren Wallace, made redundant while on leave at a different company, said only that she "would have liked for there to be a bit more humanity in the process" (sbs.com.au). Only about 12% of paid leave nationally is taken by men, which, advocates argue, is precisely why employers keep treating mothers as the structural risk.

### The Reckoning

Hofstede Insights has never published an individual score for Zimbabwe, and it isn't covered by any of the research's regional clusters either — a genuine data gap worth naming rather than papering over. Australia's individualism score of 90 is one of the highest recorded anywhere, and it shows directly in how the two systems distribute the cost of raising a child: Australia formalizes the cost into government payments, superannuation contributions, and individual legal rights, then leaves each family to privately absorb whatever the system doesn't cover. Zimbabwe skips most of the formal infrastructure and distributes the cost across a kinship network instead — grandparents, aunts, siblings — which is more resilient in some ways and more exploitable in others.

The uncomfortable parallel is that both systems ultimately run on unpaid or underpaid female labor. Australia's unpaid childcare has been valued at roughly $345 billion, nearly three times the financial and insurance sector combined, performed disproportionately by mothers. Zimbabwe's version just has a job title: grandmother.

### Conclusion

If you're planning a family across either of these systems, the honest framing is this: Zimbabwe gives you less legal protection but tells you upfront, clearly, who's actually going to help — go build that relationship with your family early, because the state isn't going to. Australia gives you more legal protection on paper and then quietly tests whether your specific employer will honor the spirit of it once you're actually gone. The thing I'd tell a friend: ask your Australian employer what happened to the last three women who took leave before you decide whether the number on the policy means anything at all.
