**Three in Five Young Irish People Are Thinking About Leaving the Country Entirely. In America, One in Five Bosses Are Just Thinking About Quitting on Gen Z Instead.**

**🇮🇪 Ireland · 🇺🇸 United States**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

A National Youth Council of Ireland-commissioned poll found three in five Irish adults under 25 are considering emigrating, with nearly 95% of students citing housing as a major source of financial strain and two-thirds believing they'd simply have a better quality of life elsewhere. The generational tension in the United States looks different but runs just as hot: a survey of 1,000 managers found 18% had considered quitting specifically over the stress of managing Gen Z employees, and 51% reported outright frustration doing it. Ireland's youngest workers are responding to generational strain by planning to leave the country. America's managers are responding to it by planning to leave the job. Both countries have a real generational problem on their hands. Neither one is actually about laziness, whichever side is doing the complaining.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect younger colleagues to ask "why" about instructions as a norm, not a challenge to authority | Read a Gen Z employee's questions about a task as insubordination |
| Factor the housing crisis into any conversation about relocation, pay, or long-term retention | Assume a young employee's hesitation about commuting or location is about commitment |
| Offer real coaching and development, not just task assignment | Assume "guidance and support" means more supervision rather than more mentorship |
| Recognize that purpose and meaning genuinely drive retention for Irish Gen Z and Millennials alike | Treat "purpose" talk as generational softness rather than a real retention lever |
| Expect younger staff to build new skills proactively, often weekly | Assume visible effort looks like it did a generation ago — reward initiative, not face time |

#### 🇺🇸 United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Give frequent, specific feedback rather than relying on an annual review | Assume younger employees will intuit expectations without explicit, regular check-ins |
| Build real cross-generational contact into team structure | Assume avoiding friction by keeping age groups separate solves anything |
| Recognize that disengagement, not laziness, explains a lot of what reads as low effort | Default to the "Gen Z has poor work ethic" framing without examining the actual job design |
| Treat flexible, outcome-based work arrangements as a genuine retention lever, not a concession | Assume returning to rigid, traditional structures will automatically restore engagement |
| Invest specifically in managers who are struggling, rather than just managing around them | Let frustrated managers quietly disengage from the problem instead of supporting them through it |

### Ireland: Purpose-Driven, Priced Out

Ireland's generational story right now is inseparable from its housing numbers. Deloitte's global Gen Z and Millennial survey found that around three in five Irish Gen Z workers live paycheque to paycheque, and separate Red C polling found housing is cited as a major financial strain by nearly 95% of students, with 85% saying the crisis disproportionately affects young people specifically. NYCI policy director Kathryn Walsh summarized the mood bluntly: young people feel "Ireland is no longer a place where they can build a future." That backdrop reframes a lot of what looks, from a manager's chair, like generational pickiness. When 87% of Irish Gen Z say a sense of purpose matters to job satisfaction, and 66% say they're building new skills weekly to get ahead, it reads less like entitlement and more like people trying to actively out-earn a cost-of-living problem the previous generation didn't face at the same scale.

What younger Irish workers specifically want from management is well documented and fairly concrete: 56% want real guidance and support from their manager, and only about a third say they currently get it. HR consultant Merriman, writing about the gap, argued managers need to shed admin and firefighting to actually mentor people, framing the ideal manager as something closer to a coach — and noting that younger staff simply expect explanations for instructions as standard practice: "Asking why is just their norm." That's a genuinely different expectation than the one many Irish managers were trained under, where instructions from above were followed with less explicit justification required. It isn't defiance. It's a different default about what a reasonable working relationship includes.

### United States: Boomers Keep the Work-Ethic Crown, Nobody's Talking to Each Other

America's generational workplace conversation has, by multiple recent surveys, curdled into something close to open resentment. Zety's Generational Leadership Report found just 9% of respondents named Gen Z as having the strongest work ethic of any generation, compared with 46% for Baby Boomers, 26% for Gen X, and 19% for Millennials — Gen Z finishing dead last by a wide margin in its own contemporaries' eyes. The management side of that resentment has real documented costs: in the Intelligent.com survey of 1,000 managers, 47% said Gen Z has poor work ethic, 75% said they communicate inappropriately, and 65% said they'd changed their own management style in response — mostly toward more frequent feedback, but 38% toward outright micromanaging.

The deeper data suggests something less like laziness and more like a genuine disengagement spiral. Gallup found nearly half of American workers fit the "quiet quitting" profile, and among workers under 35 specifically, the share who strongly agreed someone at work encourages their development dropped by double digits between 2019 and 2022 — with young fully remote workers seeing a 12-point drop in that same measure, and fewer than four in ten clearly knowing what's expected of them. A LinkedIn study found roughly one in five Gen Z workers had no direct conversation with a colleague over 50 in the entire past year — the two groups aren't so much clashing as simply not interacting. Sociologist Disha Daswaney put the mutual caricature plainly: "Gen Z often sees Boomers as outdated, while Boomers often see Gen Z as lazy." Maya Vertigans, a Gen Z founder who deliberately built an age-mixed team, offered a counterpoint from inside an actual working example: "Our over 50s team member has more work and life experience than the Gen Z team members" — a mundane, obvious-sounding observation that apparently still needs saying out loud. Hofstede Insights scores both countries similarly high on individualism (roughly 91 for the US, 70 for Ireland), which may partly explain why the generational conflict shows up as individual disengagement and individual manager burnout in both places, rather than as an organized, collective negotiation between age cohorts.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't that Irish Gen Z is more sympathetic than American Gen Z, or that American managers are more combative than Irish ones — it's that Ireland's generational strain has an obvious, named, external cause everyone agrees on, while America's has splintered into a fog of dueling surveys about who's lazier than whom. Nobody in Ireland is seriously debating whether housing costs are real; the debate is about what to do about them, and the generational friction sits downstream of that shared diagnosis. In the US, the Gen Z–versus-everyone-else conflict has become almost entirely a battle of competing perception surveys — work ethic rankings, communication-style complaints, productivity anxieties — with comparatively little shared agreement on what's actually driving the disengagement underneath all of it.

That distinction matters for anyone managing across these two cultures. An American manager relocating to Ireland who assumes Gen Z's "why" questions are a symptom of the same work-ethic deficit the Zety survey complains about will miss that Irish Gen Z's frustrations are substantially economic, not attitudinal — solvable, in part, by things like clearer paths to ownership and stability, not just better feedback cadence. An Irish manager relocating to the US who assumes open generational friction will resolve itself the way a shared economic grievance might will miss that the American conflict is more diffuse, more perception-driven, and arguably harder to fix precisely because there's no single Red C poll everyone already agrees on.

### Conclusion

If you're relocating for work and trying to read the generational temperature correctly, look past the "lazy young people" and "clueless old management" headlines in both countries, because neither fully explains what's happening. In Ireland, the generational friction is a symptom of a housing and cost-of-living crisis that's reshaping what young workers need from an employer to stay at all. In the US, it's a symptom of a disengagement spiral that's gotten tangled up in generational blame because that's an easier story to tell than naming the actual job-design and management failures underneath it. Ask what problem the complaint is actually standing in for. In both countries, it's rarely the one in the headline.
