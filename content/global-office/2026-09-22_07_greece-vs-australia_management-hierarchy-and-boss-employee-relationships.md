# The Boss Who Looks After You vs. The Boss Who Sits Next to You: Greece and Australia on Hierarchy

**🇬🇷 Greece · 🇦🇺 Australia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Greece, a good boss is a patron: someone who expects loyalty and, in exchange, genuinely looks after your interests, the way a well-run family looks after its own. In Australia, a good boss is barely a boss at all in the visible sense — first name, open-plan desk next to yours, team lunch attendance mandatory in spirit if not in policy. Both cultures will tell you their system produces more trust than the alternative. Both are right, for reasons that have almost nothing to do with each other.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇷 Greece

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use titles and surnames until your manager explicitly invites first-name terms | Push for informality early — moving to first names is the senior person's call to make |
| Expect decisions to come from the top and flow down, even after extensive discussion | Assume a lively, opinionated meeting means the final call is up for grabs |
| Build a genuine personal relationship with your manager — loyalty runs both directions here | Treat the boss-employee relationship as purely transactional; paternalism cuts both ways |
| Prioritize harmony in working relationships, even when it costs some speed | Rush a decision at the expense of the relationship — Greek business culture reads that as costly, not efficient |
| Lean on family and personal networks when job-hunting or building your own team | Assume promotions run on merit alone, disconnected from relationships and trust |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Call your manager by first name and disagree with them politely in a meeting | Assume a casual tone means low standards or that hierarchy has genuinely disappeared |
| Stay modest about individual wins, even real ones | Self-promote too visibly, too early — tall poppy syndrome will find you before you've done anything wrong |
| Treat your boss sitting in the open-plan area as normal, not a gimmick | Expect a private office and formal title deference by default |
| Contribute ideas regardless of seniority — respect is earned through input, not tenure | Assume seniority alone earns automatic deference from the team |
| Accept team lunches and casual social contact with your manager as part of the job | Decline every informal social invitation — visible disengagement reads worse than an awkward lunch |

### Greece: Paternalism as a Trust Contract

Greek management style is, by nearly every account, directive and hierarchical — decisions form at the top and move down for implementation — but framing it as simple top-down control misses the actual mechanism holding it together. Management guides consistently describe Greek hierarchy as paternalistic in the literal sense: it mirrors family structure, where the person with authority is expected to look after those beneath them in exchange for loyalty, not merely obedience. Greece's Hofstede uncertainty avoidance score sits among the highest globally, which helps explain the appeal of a system built on established, personal, relationship-anchored authority rather than improvised flat structures.

This produces workplace norms that read as formal to an outside eye but function as relationship infrastructure on the inside: titles and surnames until the senior person offers otherwise, harmony prioritized over hitting an arbitrary deadline, and family ties treated as a legitimate, even expected, channel for finding work or building a team. An employee who reads this as nepotism rather than trust-building will miss what the system is actually optimizing for — not efficiency, but durability of the relationship the whole hierarchy depends on.

### Australia: Egalitarian on the Surface, Enforced by the Group

Australian workplace hierarchy inverts the visible signals almost completely: managers go by first name, sit among their teams, and treat disagreement in a meeting as healthy rather than disruptive, backed by a Hofstede power distance score of just 36 — genuinely low, and genuinely reflected in daily office behavior. But the mechanism that keeps this egalitarian surface stable is its own kind of social enforcement: tall poppy syndrome, the well-documented tendency to cut down anyone who grows visibly above the group, whether through overt success, self-promotion, or simply standing out too early in an unsettled hierarchy.

The nuance worth catching, per recent cultural analysis, is that tall poppy syndrome isn't simple envy — it's a correction mechanism applied most aggressively to people whose position in the group is still unresolved, which means a new arrival who hasn't yet earned informal standing is at higher risk of the "cut down" than an established colleague with the same achievement. The flatness is real, but it's maintained by the group actively policing anyone who tries to act like the hierarchy has actually disappeared.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely counterintuitive contrast: Greece's hierarchy is visible and formal but built to protect the individual within a personal relationship, while Australia's hierarchy is invisible and informal but built to protect the group's sense of equality — sometimes at an individual's expense. A high performer in Greece who builds the right relationship with a paternalistic boss can be genuinely looked after, promoted, shielded. A high performer in Australia who broadcasts that same success too loudly, too early, risks being quietly cut down by the very colleagues whose egalitarian culture supposedly rewards merit. Formal hierarchy, in other words, can be more forgiving of visible ambition than informal hierarchy — a reversal most newcomers don't see coming from either direction.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering a question comparing Greek and American workplaces, one respondent described Greek colleagues as prioritizing relationships and work-life balance over the American instinct toward competition and individual achievement, noting that a Greek manager who invests time in knowing you personally is doing something structurally different from small talk — it's the actual foundation the working relationship rests on.*

> *Medium (Lauren Seberos, "Greek Work Culture") — Described flexibility around punctuality and deadlines as a genuine cultural value rather than laxity, and cautioned newcomers against reading a relaxed timeline as a lack of seriousness — the underlying commitment to the relationship and the work, she noted, remained fully intact even when the clock didn't.*

> *TTEC Jobs (careers blog citing InterNations Athens activity) — Noted that InterNations' Athens chapter functions as a genuine professional bridge for newcomers, since so many Greek job opportunities move through personal networks rather than open postings — attending expat and local networking events isn't supplementary job-hunting, it's frequently the actual mechanism.*

> *Substack (Kevin Baker, "The Satirical Australian Workplace: High Performers Will Be Targeted") — Wrote, only half joking, that visible ambition in an Australian office invites a "low-grade correction mechanism" from colleagues before the high performer has done anything wrong beyond standing out — advice he framed less as cynicism and more as an operating manual for anyone new to the culture's real rules.*

### Conclusion

If you want a boss who will personally advocate for you once trust is built, and you're willing to invest real time earning that trust formally, Greece's hierarchy rewards patience with genuine protection. If you want to work alongside your manager rather than beneath one, and you're willing to keep your wins quiet until the group has decided you've earned them, Australia's flatness rewards humility more than it rewards achievement announced too soon. The advice I'd give a friend heading either direction: in Athens, earn the relationship before you need it; in Sydney, earn the group's comfort with your success before you show it off.
