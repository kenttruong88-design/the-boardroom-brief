# The Overtime Ledger and the Trust Economy: A Working Guide to Japan and Peru

**🇯🇵 Japan · 🇵🇪 Peru**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Tokyo, the average employee logs 172 hours of overtime a year against an OECD average of 49, and roughly one in ten workers exceeds the 80-hours-a-month threshold linked to karoshi, or death by overwork, according to the Japan Institute for Labour Policy and Training. In Lima, 72% of the workforce operates entirely outside the formal labor system, per the International Labour Organization, meaning the office culture question for many arrivals is not "how much overtime" but "is there a contract at all." Two very different economies, two very different definitions of what it means to have a job.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wait for your boss to leave before you do, especially in your first year | Assume remote or flexible hours apply to you just because your contract mentions them |
| Attend the nomikai (work drinking party) at least occasionally — skipping is noted | Refuse a drink outright without an excuse; "I don't drink" works better than "I'd rather not" |
| Keep suggestions to yourself for the first six months and observe how decisions actually get made | Interrupt or contradict a senior colleague in a group meeting, even if you're right |
| Use both hands when receiving a business card, and read it before pocketing it | Leave your business cards in a bent or worn holder — it reflects on you |
| Submit paperwork exactly as instructed, even if a faster digital method exists | Expect fax machines and hanko stamps to have disappeared — many haven't |

#### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest time in small talk and relationship-building before getting to business | Walk into a meeting and open with the agenda — it reads as cold |
| Address senior colleagues with formal titles (ingeniero, doctor, licenciado) until invited otherwise | Assume a flat org chart; decisions run up and down a visible hierarchy |
| Expect your jefe to ask about your family or health — it's a form of care, not overreach | Mistake warmth from a manager for informality about deadlines or authority |
| Build "confianza" (trust) through consistent in-person presence | Rely purely on email or Slack to build a working relationship |
| Treat multinational subsidiaries as culturally distinct from traditional Peruvian firms | Generalize Lima corporate culture from one company's practices |

### Japan: The Discipline of Presence

Japan's corporate culture still runs on a logic where visible effort matters as much as output. The average worker put in 1,654 hours over the last fiscal year, per government data reported by the South China Morning Post — actually below the historical OECD comparison figure of 1,735 — evidence that reform is happening, slowly. The government capped overtime at 45 hours a month and 360 a year back in 2019, and companies like Panasonic and Fast Retailing have piloted four-day weeks. But the Japan Institute for Labour Policy and Training's 2026 winter report notes that enforcement remains inconsistent, and cultural pressure to stay until the boss leaves persists independent of what the clock says.

The deeper structure is consensus-based decision-making, or nemawashi: informal groundwork laid before a meeting so that the meeting itself simply ratifies what's already been agreed. For a newcomer used to workplaces where meetings are where decisions get made, this is disorienting — the real negotiation happened over coffee last week, and you weren't in the room. Hofstede Insights scores Japan at 92 on uncertainty avoidance, among the highest in the world, which tracks with the elaborate procedural correctness around everything from business cards to seating order.

### Peru: The Architecture of Relationship

Peru's formal corporate sector is a minority experience economically but a majority experience for anyone reading a guide like this one, since expat hires land almost exclusively in multinationals, banks, mining firms, and larger domestic companies. Within that formal tier, the operating principle is hierarchy softened by paternalism. Commisceo Global's management guide describes Peruvian bosses as expected to lead with authority "balanced by warmth," sometimes extending concern to an employee's family, housing, or health — a blending of professional and personal that a manager from a more boundaried culture might read as intrusive rather than caring.

Hofstede Insights places Peru at 64 on power distance, meaningfully higher than Japan's 54, and low on individualism at 16, indicating a culture where group and family loyalty outrank individual initiative as a professional virtue. Communication tends to be indirect, and "confianza" — a relationship of earned trust — functions almost as unofficial currency: contracts and org charts matter less than who has personally vouched for whom.

### The Reckoning

Both countries are hierarchical, but the hierarchy expresses itself in opposite emotional registers. Japan's chain of command is procedural and impersonal — you follow protocol because it is protocol, not because you love your boss. Peru's chain of command is personal and almost familial — you follow your jefe because a relationship, not a rulebook, obligates you. A manager who thrives on Tokyo's structured deference can find Lima's paternalism smothering, and vice versa: someone who's built their career on Peruvian confianza can find Japanese nemawashi's invisible pre-negotiation maddeningly opaque.

The irony is that both systems produce the same practical outcome for a foreign hire: don't expect to influence decisions quickly. In Japan, you're locked out by procedure until you've earned procedural standing. In Peru, you're locked out by relationship until you've earned personal trust. The timeline is similar even though the mechanism is completely different, which means the advice "be patient and observe" applies to both, for entirely different reasons.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A foreign hire at a Japanese firm advised that the real onboarding task isn't learning the job, it's learning to keep suggestions to yourself for at least six months, since credibility has to be earned before anyone will act on your ideas, no matter how good they are.*

> *r/japanlife — One expat described nomikai as an unofficial part of the job description: technically optional, practically mandatory, and the place where actual team dynamics — who trusts whom, who's really in charge of what — get worked out over drinks that the org chart never mentions.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — An engineer who transferred into a Japanese company's Tokyo office described the shock of a "brutal" pace during their first six months, noting that senior staff staying late wasn't about workload so much as a visible signal of commitment that new hires were expected to match.*

> *Internations Lima — A European finance transfer wrote that adjusting to Peru meant re-learning what a meeting was for: not decision-making, but relationship confirmation, with the real negotiating happening in one-on-one conversations well before anyone sat down at the table.*

> *r/expats — Someone who moved from a flat-hierarchy Scandinavian company to a Lima-based multinational said the single hardest habit to unlearn was giving direct feedback in front of a group; in Peru, that same feedback delivered privately landed completely differently, and publicly it read as a loss of face for the manager.*

---

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two moves, the question to ask yourself isn't "which culture is more hierarchical" — they both are — but "which currency am I better at earning: procedural trust or personal trust." Japan rewards those who can demonstrate discipline, consistency, and deference to process; Peru rewards those who can build warmth, presence, and personal rapport. Neither rewards someone who tries to shortcut the system with their home country's playbook.

My honest advice, the kind I'd give over a drink: don't take either job expecting to change how things get decided in your first year. Take it to learn a completely different theory of how trust gets built at work — and keep your passport current, because you'll want to compare notes with whoever you were before you left.
