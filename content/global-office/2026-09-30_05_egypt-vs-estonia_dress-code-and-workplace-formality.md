# A Suit in 40-Degree Heat, or Jeans at a Company That's Ranked First in the World

**🇪🇬 Egypt · 🇪🇪 Estonia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Cairo's professionals put on full suits in year-round heat that one expat flatly described as leaving you "uncomfortable regardless" of what you wear, because looking sharp outranks feeling comfortable. Tallinn's professionals, in a country whose expats just ranked it first in the world for work culture, increasingly show up in "dark jeans, knit tops, and clean sneakers" and call it fully professional. Both places take how you look at work seriously — they've just landed on opposite conclusions about what "serious" means.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇪🇬 Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a full suit to client-facing or embassy-adjacent roles, regardless of the heat | Assume heat is a valid excuse to dress down for a first meeting |
| Cover legs to the knee and shoulders/upper arms, especially for women | Wear tank tops, sleeveless tops, or short skirts outside expat enclaves like Maadi |
| Keep a blazer or tunic on hand even in informal-seeming offices | Assume Western casual is acceptable outside embassy social events or expat-heavy settings |
| Expect more relaxed norms specifically inside expat/embassy social circles | Extrapolate embassy-party norms to the general public workplace |
| Prioritize modest, polished clothing over trend-following | Treat revealing clothing as a neutral fashion choice — it changes how you're treated in public |

#### 🇪🇪 Estonia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to "neat and put-together," not corporate, at most tech and startup employers | Show up in a full suit to a startup expecting it to read as competence rather than overdressing |
| Keep one properly tailored outfit for client meetings or traditional-sector roles | Assume every Estonian office is as casual as the tech scene — traditional firms still expect more |
| Invest in quality basics — dark jeans, knit tops, clean shoes — since the same outfit works work-to-social | Over-accessorize or dress in a way that signals "trying too hard" |
| Dress for the low-hierarchy culture: understated, not deferential | Assume formality signals seniority the way it might elsewhere — it often reads the opposite here |
| Expect genuinely cold-weather practicality (proper coats, boots) to matter as much as style | Prioritize fashion over function in Baltic winter — locals clearly don't |

### Egypt: Formality as a Climate-Defying Standard

Egyptian office dress holds firm against the one factor that would reasonably soften it elsewhere: the weather. First-hand accounts consistently describe full business suits as the norm for men "despite the heat," with women moving between business dresses, tunics, and suits depending on seniority and role. Outside the office, the modesty standard tightens rather than loosens — legs covered to the knee, shoulders covered, tank tops and short skirts avoided — a norm multiple expats frame not as an abstract cultural preference but as a practical response to how revealing clothing correlates with unwanted public attention, while being clear that this is never a justification for harassment itself.

Hofstede's numbers for Egypt make the logic legible: a Power Distance of 70 and Uncertainty Avoidance of 80, among the higher combinations in the dataset, point to a culture where hierarchy is visibly respected and rules exist specifically to minimize ambiguity — dress code included. The one genuine relief valve is geographic and social rather than climatic: expat-heavy neighborhoods like Maadi and embassy social events carry meaningfully looser norms, to the point that Western casual wear becomes broadly acceptable there in a way it isn't ten minutes away.

### Estonia: The World's Best-Rated Work Culture Wears Sneakers

Estonia's relationship to formality is best understood through a genuinely startling data point: in the most recent Expat Insider survey, Estonia ranked 1st in the world for work culture and job contentment, even as its overall ranking slipped to 29th of 53 destinations on salary and healthcare concerns. Expats cited "encouraging work atmosphere, low hierarchy, and focus on personal freedom" as the defining feature, with one Filipino respondent specifically crediting "the vibrant start-up scene and local innovation." That low-hierarchy culture shows up directly in the wardrobe: Estonia's tech-dominated economy has normalized dark jeans, knit tops, and sneakers as fully professional attire, with the guiding principle being to look "neat and put-together, not corporate" — clothes that work equally well at a client meeting and a Friday drink with the same colleagues.

It's worth noting the accounts genuinely diverge depending on which Estonia you're describing: older first-hand reports from Tallinn describe "suit and tie at work" as the standard, particularly in more traditional or government-adjacent roles, while startup and tech employees increasingly treat that as an artifact of a different era. Hofstede's Estonia estimates (Power Distance 40, Individualism 60, Uncertainty Avoidance 60) sit in a genuinely different register from Egypt's — lower hierarchy, more individualist, meaningfully more tolerant of ambiguity — which tracks with a workplace where what you wear signals less about your rank and more about your personal taste.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't formal-versus-casual — it's what formality is *for* in each place. In Egypt, dressing sharply is a hierarchy-and-respect signal layered onto a modesty norm with real social stakes attached, and it holds constant regardless of physical discomfort. In Estonia, dressing down deliberately is itself the status signal in a flat, startup-driven culture where looking too polished can read as trying too hard, or worse, as corporate rather than competent.

The irony worth sitting with: Estonia, a country whose own workers rank its workplace culture first in the world, has arrived at that ranking partly by discarding the formal dress markers that Egypt still treats as inseparable from professional respect — and neither country's employees would necessarily recognize the other's approach as "serious" at all.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Tales Mag Real Post Report, Cairo — A contributor summed up the heat-versus-formality tension bluntly: "It's extremely hot year round so expect to be uncomfortable regardless," while another noted their own practical compromise — "I wear skirts but try to have them cover my knees" — as the daily negotiation between comfort, modesty norms, and professional expectations.*

> *Expat.com Egypt forum — Responding to a newcomer's question about how to be taken seriously professionally in Cairo, one commenter's most-repeated piece of advice wasn't about suits or hemlines at all, but about accessories: "wear sunglasses as much as possible even in hotels" — a small, specific detail about projecting polish that no formal business-etiquette guide mentioned.*

> *Tales Mag Real Post Report, Tallinn — One respondent's account captured the genuine split in how Estonians dress: "everyone in Estonia is very fashion forward and likes to dress up" in public generally, while the office standard itself runs "business casual" with an emphasis on looking "professional and classy" rather than following a strict written code.*

> *Panaprium (Estonia lifestyle blog) — The clearest single piece of advice for a newcomer joining an Estonian tech company: skip the blazer. The going standard is "dark jeans, knit tops, and clean sneakers or loafers," with the explicit goal of looking "neat and put-together, not corporate" — overdressing, per the piece, reads as trying too hard rather than as respect.*

> *Expat Insider 2026 survey (reported via 3 Seas Europe) — Beyond the aggregate #1 work-culture ranking, one Filipino expat's specific praise for "the vibrant start-up scene and local innovation" was the most concrete, individually-attributed explanation offered for why Estonia's low-hierarchy, low-formality office culture scores as well as it does with the people actually working inside it.*

### Conclusion

If you're deciding between these two, the honest framing isn't "which is stricter" — it's "which kind of formality will you actually be able to sustain." Egypt asks you to prioritize modesty and polish over physical comfort, indefinitely, regardless of the thermometer; budget for good tailoring and accept the heat as a cost of doing business, literally. Estonia asks you to unlearn the instinct that professionalism requires a blazer at all, and to trust that a flat, startup-native culture means showing up overdressed can cost you more credibility than showing up in sneakers. Pack accordingly — and in Cairo's case, pack sunglasses.
