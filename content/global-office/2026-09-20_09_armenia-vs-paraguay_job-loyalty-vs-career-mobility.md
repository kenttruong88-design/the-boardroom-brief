# Stay and Get Poached Remotely, or Stay and Just... Stay: Loyalty in Yerevan and Asunción

**🇦🇲 Armenia · 🇵🇾 Paraguay**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Armenia's most talked-about career move isn't switching companies — it's switching countries, or at least switching who pays you while staying physically in Yerevan, as ICT salaries running at 2.7 times the national average pull tech talent toward remote contracts with Europe and North America. Paraguay's most talked-about career move is barely a move at all: the International Labour Organization found only 5% of Paraguay's salaried workers are even employed by a company with more than 50 people, a labor market so structurally small-scale that "career mobility" often just means staying exactly where you are and getting better at it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇲 Armenia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand that top local talent is actively courted by remote-first foreign employers | Assume local salary benchmarks reflect what skilled competitors are offering |
| Offer genuine professional development and global mobility options if you want to retain staff | Rely on loyalty alone to keep skilled employees from taking remote foreign offers |
| Expect a two-week to one-month notice period depending on tenure | Assume Western-style notice norms without checking local labor law |
| Recognize the ICT sector as an outlier — 2.7x the national average wage | Generalize IT-sector career mobility patterns to the wider Armenian labor market |
| Budget for real competition from remote-hiring firms abroad | Treat physical relocation as the only form of "brain drain" worth worrying about |

#### 🇵🇾 Paraguay

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect long tenures once trust is established, especially outside tech | Assume frequent job-switching signals ambition the way it might elsewhere |
| Build genuine personal relationships at work — they underpin retention here | Treat the workplace as a purely transactional arrangement |
| Recognize the small-employer structure (most jobs at firms under 50 people) as the market norm | Expect corporate-ladder mobility patterns typical of larger economies |
| Offer competitive pay and real development paths if hiring locally | Assume stability alone will retain talent without growth opportunities |
| Note tech and software roles as the more mobile, competitive exception | Apply the "stable, loyal" generalization uniformly across every sector |

---

Armenia's labor story right now is really two labor markets running in parallel. Multiplier and Vardanyan & Partners both describe an ICT sector accounting for 5.5% of GDP with salaries dramatically outpacing the rest of the economy, and repatriation-focused resources like RepatArmenia note that professionals are increasingly able to "remain in Armenia while working for international companies remotely" — meaning the traditional loyalty-building lever (raise their local salary, retain the employee) is competing directly against an entirely different currency: a foreign paycheck with no relocation required at all. Outside tech, the picture is starker — Quora respondents describe high unemployment and remuneration that hasn't kept pace with the cost of housing and food, driving what the ILO documents as decades of sustained economic emigration to Russia, the US, and the EU. The country isn't choosing between loyalty and mobility as an abstract cultural value; it's living through a real structural fork where the two paths (stay local, or work for elsewhere while staying put) are increasingly distinct career strategies rather than a spectrum.

Paraguay's employment culture, by contrast, is genuinely built around staying put — but the mechanism is structural as much as cultural. With only 5% of salaried workers at companies larger than 50 people, per ILO data cited by Biz Latin Hub, there simply isn't a dense internal job market to hop around in the way there is in a large economy with hundreds of similarly-sized competing employers in the same city. Hiring guides consistently describe a culture that "values loyalty, personal relationships, and stability," translating into long tenures once trust is established — but this reads less like a national personality trait and more like a rational response to a market where changing employers means changing industries or cities more often than it means a lateral move to a direct competitor down the street. The exception, again, is tech: software and full-stack development roles are explicitly flagged as the most sought-after and mobile positions in the country's urban labor market, mirroring Armenia's IT-sector exception in miniature.

### The Reckoning

The ironic parallel is that both countries' "loyalty vs. mobility" story is really a story about which sector you're in, dressed up as a national cultural trait. Armenia looks intensely mobile because its highest-visibility sector (ICT) is genuinely globalized and hypercompetitive, while its broader labor market actually suffers from too little internal mobility, pushing people toward emigration instead. Paraguay looks intensely loyal because its dominant market structure (small employers, thin competition) genuinely rewards staying, while its own tech sector shows the identical mobile, competitive pattern Armenia's does. A recruiter or new hire generalizing from "Armenians job-hop" or "Paraguayans are loyal" is really just describing whichever sector happened to be visible to them first.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A response on Armenia's unemployment situation noted that hundreds of thousands of Armenians travel abroad annually for seasonal work, describing this as a normalized, expected part of the country's economic rhythm rather than an emergency measure — a pattern locals plan their year around rather than treat as exceptional.*

> *Quora — A thread on the Caucasus tech scene described Armenia's IT sector as genuinely globally competitive, with local engineers regularly fielding offers from European and American firms without needing to relocate at all, thanks to strong remote-work infrastructure in Yerevan specifically.*

> *workwealthandtravel.com — A first-person account of living in Paraguay described the slower, more relationship-driven career pace as a genuine adjustment from a faster-moving home labor market, but ultimately a welcome one, noting that professional relationships there felt more durable specifically because switching jobs constantly wasn't the local default.*

> *hackinglives.substack.com — A newsletter piece framing Paraguay as an under-the-radar option for relocating professionals specifically flagged the country's tax treatment of foreign-sourced income as a reason remote workers were increasingly choosing to base themselves there while keeping international clients — a Paraguay-specific twist on the same "work locally, get paid globally" pattern reshaping Armenia's labor market.*

> *Quora — A response comparing brain drain across countries listed Armenia among nations experiencing significant population decline tied to emigration over the past three decades, framing it not as a recent trend but as a multi-generational structural feature of the country's labor story that any newcomer should understand as background context, not breaking news.*

### Conclusion

If you're hiring or working in Armenia, understand that retention now competes directly against remote foreign employers, not just other local companies — competitive pay and real development paths matter more than loyalty appeals. If you're hiring or working in Paraguay, understand that the market's apparent loyalty is substantially a function of its small-employer structure, not a guarantee that people won't leave if a genuinely better option appears — tech workers already prove this. The honest line for a friend: in both countries, look past the national reputation and ask which sector you're actually in, because in Armenia and Paraguay alike, tech plays by different rules than everything else.
