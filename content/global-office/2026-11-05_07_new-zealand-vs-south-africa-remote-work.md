# New Zealand Will Let You Work From Home. South Africa Will Let the Power Grid Decide.

🇳🇿 New Zealand · 🇿🇦 South Africa

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

New Zealand's biggest obstacle to remote work is a cooling job market that keeps shrinking the number of listings that say "remote" at all. South Africa's biggest obstacle to remote work is that the power sometimes simply isn't on. Neither country has a legal right to work from home. Only one of them has to build a generator budget into that decision.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇿 New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect flexible hours as a near-default — the large majority of employers now offer them | Assume every job will be fully remote just because flexibility is widespread |
| Watch job-ad volume, not just worker sentiment — remote listings have been shrinking even as demand for them stays high | Assume a strong worker preference for remote work means employers are expanding remote roles to match |
| Expect overtime to be culturally rare and genuinely frowned upon in most offices | Bring an always-on, hustle-culture expectation into a New Zealand workplace uninvited |
| Ask directly about your specific employer's hybrid policy — practice varies by region and industry | Assume national averages (around half hybrid, a chunk fully remote) describe your specific employer |
| Enjoy the comparatively flat hierarchy — flagging a problem or declining extra hours is culturally normal | Assume deference to a manager, rather than directness, is the safer default approach |

#### 🇿🇦 South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask specifically about load-shedding contingency — generator access, backup data, battery systems — before accepting a remote role | Assume reliable power and internet access the way you might in a country without scheduled blackouts |
| Get any flexible-work arrangement in writing — there's no statutory right to remote work or to disconnect | Assume a verbal or informal flexibility arrangement will hold once leadership or policy changes |
| Expect remote roles to be concentrated almost entirely in IT, finance, and a handful of other sectors | Expect a remote-friendly employer to be the norm outside of those specific industries |
| Treat a hybrid or flexible-work policy as potentially reversible — many firms are now pushing full return-to-office | Assume a policy written during the pandemic is still the policy in force today |
| Expect a more hierarchical workplace culture, especially as a newcomer — some accounts describe real friction for foreign hires | Assume flattened, Kiwi-style directness will be read the same way in a South African office |

New Zealand's shift toward flexible and remote work shows up clearly in its own census data: Stats NZ figures reported by RNZ found 17.7 percent of employed adults mostly working from home in 2023, up from 11.9 percent in 2018 and 8.5 percent in 2013 — a genuine structural change, not a pandemic blip that reversed. Employer practice has kept pace on paper: separate Stats NZ data reported by HCAmag found 81 percent of New Zealand employers offered flexible working hours in 2023, rising to 90 percent among large employers, with the highest uptake in professional, scientific, and technical services. But a cooling job market is quietly narrowing what "remote" actually means in practice: Trade Me's own remote job listings, consistently under 2 percent of all postings, fell a further 30 percent year-on-year by one measure cited in the same reporting — worker appetite for flexibility is rising even as employer-posted remote opportunity is shrinking, a genuine mismatch rather than a simple success story. Separate survey data cited in the same coverage found more than 80 percent of New Zealand employees say flexible hours matter when considering a job change, and over three-quarters say remote work specifically matters — demand that the market, for now, isn't fully meeting.

South Africa's starting conditions are structurally different in a way that has nothing to do with cultural attitude and everything to do with infrastructure. There is no statutory right to work remotely or to disconnect under the Basic Conditions of Employment Act or Labour Relations Act — flexible work exists entirely at an employer's discretion, not as a legal entitlement, a point BusinessTech's own legal reporting makes explicitly. Worker appetite for remote work is, if anything, higher than in many comparable markets: a BCG global survey reported by CHRO South Africa found 44 percent of South Africans want fully remote work against a 24 percent global average, with only 4 percent wanting a full return to on-site work. The supply side hasn't kept up at all — CareerJunction data cited by BusinessTech put remote job listings at a flat 3 percent of the market for roughly a year and a half, with 59 percent of what little remote work exists concentrated specifically in IT. And then there's the structural handicap with no New Zealand equivalent whatsoever: scheduled power cuts, known locally as load-shedding, that can run six to twelve hours a day at their worst, directly undercutting any home-based work arrangement that assumes continuous electricity and internet — a real operational risk that larger firms address with generators and backup connectivity their home-based employees usually can't match.

The employer side of the story in South Africa is more cautious than the worker-demand numbers alone suggest. A 2020 Willis Towers Watson survey of 66 South African employers, covering roughly 207,000 employees, found businesses predicting that 33 percent of staff would still be working from home three years out — down from a pandemic peak of 38 percent — with only 15 percent of respondents saying their job structures fully supported a flexible workforce, and 24 percent reporting no formal flexible-work policy at all as of that survey. Separate BCG survey data reported by CHRO South Africa found only 4 percent of South African respondents wanted a full return to on-site work, a gap between what employers are planning to scale back and what employees say they actually want that helps explain why so many of the forum accounts above describe flexibility as something negotiated person by person rather than guaranteed by policy. New Zealand's employer side looks almost the mirror opposite: with 81 percent of employers already offering flexible hours as of 2023, the open question there isn't whether flexibility exists in principle but whether the specific, fully remote roles workers increasingly want are being created fast enough to match that appetite.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't that one country embraces remote work and the other resists it — South African workers, by survey data, want remote work at nearly double the global rate, considerably more enthusiastically than their legal or infrastructural environment currently supports. It's that New Zealand's limiting factor is a soft one, a job market not yet generating enough remote-coded listings to match worker demand, while South Africa's limiting factor is a hard one: a national grid that can simply remove the option regardless of what any employer policy says. Hofstede's data adds a useful wrinkle here too — South Africa's power distance score (49) sits meaningfully above New Zealand's notably flat 22, meaning a South African employee flagging a problem, or pushing back on an inflexible policy, carries more social risk than the same conversation would in Wellington or Auckland.

For someone relocating, the planning implication is concrete rather than cultural. In New Zealand, the real question is whether your specific role and industry are among the shrinking pool still advertised as remote, not whether flexible hours exist at all — they almost certainly do. In South Africa, the real question is infrastructural before it's contractual: does your prospective employer actually have a working plan for load-shedding, and is any flexibility arrangement written down anywhere that would survive a change in management.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit, via The New Zealand Herald — a Londoner who relocated to New Zealand said working conditions there were far better than in the UK, noting that "no one expects overtime" and that workers get more public holidays and time off around Christmas — though they flagged New Zealand's housing situation as a genuine downside in the same breath.*

> *Expat Arrivals — a South African expat named Pierre, now living in New Zealand, described real early friction as a foreign hire: colleagues who wouldn't greet him, stretches where nobody at work spoke to him at all, and practical advice to expect undesirable tasks and a career step backward until local experience is built up.*

> *Briefly.co.za — a South African woman who relocated to New Zealand described her culture shock in distinctly physical terms: unwalled gardens, no security guards, children walking to school alone — a reminder that the adjustment between these two countries runs well beyond office policy into how safe daily life itself feels.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing a remote-friendly move, the honest difference is this: New Zealand will mostly deliver on its flexible reputation, with the caveat that the specific remote role you want may be harder to find than the national mood suggests. South Africa's workforce wants exactly the same flexibility, often more intensely, but the country's own grid may unilaterally override whatever policy your employer puts in writing.

I'd tell a friend taking either job the same thing: ask about the job market trend line before you move to New Zealand, and ask about the backup generator before you sign anything in South Africa — in both cases, for very different reasons, the official policy is not actually the whole story.
