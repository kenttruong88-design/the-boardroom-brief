# Salary 'To Be Discussed' in Lima, Salary in the Advert in Madrid: The Price Tag Problem

🇵🇪 Peru · 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Peru, a job advertisement that says the salary is to be discussed is not forbidden, merely customary. In Spain, as of this month, it is supposed to be rather more difficult. Baker McKenzie reports that Royal Decree-Law 39/2026 entered into force on 19 November, requiring employers to give candidates the starting pay or pay range. Meanwhile, about 69 percent of employed Peruvians work informally, according to a report using INEI's 2024 employment survey, which makes the question of what a job pays more elastic than the advertisement suggests. Two countries, two theories of what a candidate is entitled to know before committing. Only one has put it in the Official State Gazette.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask the recruiter for the pay range before advancing; refusal is a signal | Treat "sueldo a tratar" as a reason to stop the conversation |
| Offer a range of three figures: a floor, a realistic figure and an ambitious one | Name a single number early |
| Negotiate the whole package, including bonuses, hybrid work, health cover and training | Fixate on base pay alone |
| Check the net offer after AFP or ONP pension deductions and income tax | Sign on the gross figure |
| Ask how the July and December gratificaciones are treated | Assume the monthly figure is the whole year |
| Research three to five similar listings on Bumeran, Computrabajo or LinkedIn | Justify your ask by your personal expenses |

### Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate the gross annual figure | Negotiate a monthly net number |
| Ask whether pay is split over 12 or 14 payments | Compare offers without dividing by the same number |
| Read the relevant convenio colectivo before you ask for anything | Mistake a mandatory collective-agreement raise for a negotiated one |
| Ask for the range; employers are now supposed to state one | Volunteer your previous salary; employers may no longer ask for it |
| Request pay information in writing under the new rules; employers have two months to answer | Treat colleagues' pay as an unmentionable subject |
| Raise flexible compensation such as meal vouchers or transport | Overlook non-salary benefits such as schedule and training budget |

## Peru: The Number Is a Work in Progress

Peru's pay system starts with a large fact: most work is informal. The UNIR report, using INEI's 2024 employment survey, puts informality at 69.33 percent of the employed, with 93.3 percent in rural areas and 60.85 percent in Lima and Callao, and more than half of informal workers earning less than the minimum wage. For a relocating professional, the formal Lima office is the exception, and its pay norms are set by a smaller and more selective world.

Within that world, secrecy is ordinary. Gestion's article on missing salaries lists the reasons employers give, as relayed by recruitment executives: a published figure can prompt existing staff to demand raises, let rivals outbid you, attract unqualified applicants, and reveal that the pay has not yet been decided. It also notes that some employers ask for salary expectations to find candidates who will accept less than budgeted, and that candidates who learn the figure late may be invested enough to take less. One recruiter quoted suggests asking for 20 to 25 percent more than your current pay if you already have a job. Sueldo Justo, a Peruvian careers site, adds that it is not prohibited to advertise pay as negotiable, and that candidates have no obligation to disclose their current salary.

The numbers show why the conversation is delicate. Sueldo Justo cites an average Bumeran salary expectation of S/ 3,595 a month, against INEI's average effective labour income of S/ 1,805.7 for July 2024 to June 2025, and a 2026 minimum wage of S/ 1,230. Deel notes that two gratificaciones, due by 15 July and 15 December, each equal a month of pay plus a 9 percent bonus, bringing annual pay to about 14 salaries. A Peruvian offer, in other words, is a monthly figure that is only part of the story, and nobody will mention the rest unless you ask. The same site gives average expectations by level of S/ 2,212 for juniors, S/ 3,348 for seniors and S/ 5,563 for managers or supervisors, and reports that the most commonly offered benefits are objective-based bonuses (57 percent of employers), training (56 percent), flexible hours or hybrid work (55 percent) and health cover (51 percent). It adds that 53 percent of Peruvian companies plan to expand headcount in 2026, with most expecting to raise pay by 3 to 10 percent. The negotiating room, then, is real but modest, and largely located in the extras.

## Spain: A Number, Eventually, by Decree

Spain is moving in the other direction, though not without hesitation. The Local reported in June that pay is traditionally kept private during recruitment and that discussing salaries is widely seen as taboo. Spain's Women's Institute puts the gender pay gap at 15.74 percent, or about €4,781 a year, and the statistics institute's figures show average annual earnings of €30,372 for men and €25,591 for women. Lewis Silkin's August analysis of the draft decree noted that it omitted any pre-employment right to pay information and any ban on asking about salary history. The November rules reported by Baker McKenzie went further: candidates must be told the starting pay or range and the applicable collective agreement, employers may not ask about salary history, and clauses barring staff from disclosing their own pay are void.

Employees can also ask in writing for their own pay and average pay by sex for comparable work, with a two-month deadline for the reply. Companies with 50 or more employees must justify pay gaps of 25 percent or more, and those with 100 or more face a 5 percent threshold, per Baker McKenzie. Public appetite appears to exist. The InfoJobs-ESADE report found 83 percent of the active population backing publication of all salaries as a way to close the gender gap, though only 31.5 percent of companies with over 50 employees agreed, against 60.5 percent of firms with under ten.

None of this removes the old negotiation habits. Spain Handbook advises negotiating the gross annual figure, asking whether pay is split into 12 or 14 payments, and reading the convenio colectivo, which sets minimum pay by sector and region. It also estimates that Madrid and Barcelona pay 20 to 30 percent more than cities such as Valencia or Seville, though housing often cancels the difference. The same guide estimates the room for negotiation at 15 to 25 percent in technology, against 5 to 10 percent in hospitality, where the collective agreement does most of the talking. It also points to retribucion flexible, which lets employees pay for meal vouchers, transport passes, nursery costs or health insurance from gross pay and so reduce taxable income. Spanish pay conversations, once they begin, are technical rather than theatrical.

## The Reckoning

Peru and Spain now ask different things of the candidate. In Peru, the burden is on you to extract the information, to research listings, build a range and press the recruiter, and your leverage depends on how scarce your skills are. In Spain, the state is attempting to move the burden onto the employer, at least on paper, with a published range, no salary history and a written right to compare. A Peruvian candidate negotiates with imperfect information. A Spanish one is meant to negotiate with a number already on the table.

The caution runs in both directions. Peru's secrecy is partly a product of informality and employer discretion, and advice there leans toward preparation. Spain's reforms are new, were still a draft in August, and will reach small employers slowly, so the gap between the Gazette and your actual recruiter may last a while. Treat the law as leverage, not as a guarantee, and verify the final text, since the reporting on thresholds and deadlines has shifted several times this year.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — a poster weighing a Barcelona offer of €48,000 gross over 14 payments asked how much would reach their account, and one reply estimated roughly €34,000 a year after tax, which they called ample for a single person.*

> *Blind — commenters in a Spanish software salary thread disagreed on whether six figures are realistic, with one noting that 14 monthly payments make annual totals look lower than headline numbers suggest and another saying pushing hard on the initial offer works in Spain as elsewhere.*

> *BritishExpats — a prospective Valencia employee found an €800 monthly offer too low after researching rent, and replies split between calling it survivable with shared housing and calling it unworkable.*

> *Expat.com — in a long-running Lima thread, one poster advised finding work from your home country for better pay, saying most Lima jobs come through personal contacts.*

> *Expat.com — another Lima poster described a full-time role with a US-based company paying $1,300 a month plus bonuses, and said local manual jobs paid a small fraction of that.*

## Conclusion

If you are moving to Spain, ask for the range, negotiate the gross annual figure, confirm 12 or 14 payments and read the collective agreement. Expect the law to help, and do not rely on it before you have the offer in writing. If you are moving to Peru, assume the first number is not the last, ask for the range before you invest time, work out the net after pension and tax deductions, and ask how the two annual gratificaciones fit into the package.

The honest comparison is that Spain is trying to make the number visible and Peru still treats it as something you earn the right to hear. One system hopes you will be informed. The other assumes you will be persistent. Both reward the same candidate, the one who asked early and wrote it down.
