# The Beer Fridge and the Lunch Clique: How Canada and Singapore Each Weaponize Friendliness at Work

🇨🇦 Canada · 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Toronto, skipping the office's rolling Friday beer cart marks you as slightly odd, not disloyal. In Singapore, skipping the daily noon lunch invitation marks you as a small, ongoing threat to team cohesion — decline enough times and colleagues simply stop asking, a social fate known locally as being "bo jio'd" out of existence. Neither country will admit, on paper, that any of this is mandatory. Gallup's 2026 workplace data suggests the gap between paper and practice matters: only 14 percent of Singaporean employees report feeling engaged at work, against 21 percent of Canadians — despite, or perhaps partly because of, Singapore's far denser culture of enforced togetherness.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the Friday drink cart or team lunch occasionally, even for one round | Skip the "how was your weekend" small talk and dive straight into business |
| RSVP to team lunches and potlucks, even if you leave early | Assume "we should grab coffee sometime" is a real invitation — it's often just politeness |
| Order a mocktail if you don't drink — nobody will ask twice | Overdo the drinking at the holiday party — loosened tongues sink careers fast |
| Expect five minutes of chit-chat before meetings get to business | Take "that's an interesting idea" at face value — indirectness often masks disagreement |
| Show up to the holiday potluck or team event; attendance is quietly noted | Bring up salary, religion, or politics at office social events |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say yes to the lunch invite in your first weeks, even if you'd rather eat alone | Eat lunch alone at your desk repeatedly and expect no social cost |
| "Chope" your hawker seat with a tissue pack or lanyard like everyone else | Turn down an invitation without a decent, stated excuse |
| Attend the annual D&D (dinner and dance) — the closest thing to a mandatory event | Ignore hierarchy at social events; seniority still shapes who speaks first |
| Accept festive treats and greetings around CNY, Hari Raya, and Deepavali | Discuss race, religion, or politics casually — it's culturally and legally sensitive |
| Arrive on time; punctuality reads as a form of respect | Expect small talk to stay small — questions about salary or marital status are normal |

## 🇨🇦 Canada

Canadian office socializing runs on plausible deniability. According to the OECD, Canadians logged roughly 1,700 hours of work in 2023, close to the organization's 1,736-hour average across member countries — a schedule that supports a culture built less around scheduled bonding than ambient friendliness. Statistics Canada's Labour Force Survey found 82.6 percent of employed Canadians were commuting into an office by May 2025, up from the year before, which means the small talk that greases Canadian meetings — the few minutes on weekend plans before anyone opens a laptop — has migrated back from Zoom to the actual hallway. Hofstede Insights scores Canada low on power distance and high on individualism, and the office reflects it: invitations to team lunches or Friday drinks are framed as optional, RSVPs are casual, and a manager is more likely to describe the relationship as "you don't work for me, you work with me" than to summon anyone anywhere.

The catch is that "optional" in Canada is a social technology, not a legal one. Gallup puts Canadian workplace thriving at 50 percent, comfortably above the global average of 34 percent, which tracks with a culture where nobody is dragged to the office party. But absence is quietly logged all the same, and the indirectness Canadians prize as politeness can read as opacity to newcomers, since a polite non-answer in a meeting usually needs to be read as a full one. The reward for playing along is a workplace culture that, by international standards, causes comparatively little daily friction — engagement sits modestly below the wider North American average, but the stress numbers are gentler than Singapore's.

## 🇸🇬 Singapore

Singapore's Ministry of Manpower caps the standard work week at 44 hours before overtime applies, and permits up to 72 hours of overtime a month — a regulatory ceiling that still leaves plenty of room for the workday to bleed into the evening. Hofstede's data helps explain why socializing feels less optional here: Singapore scores 74 on power distance against Canada's markedly lower reading, and a stark 20 on individualism, marking it a collectivist, hierarchical culture where group harmony functions as a genuine professional asset rather than a personality trait. The predictable result, chronicled in detail by the Singaporean culture outlet Rice Media, is a lunch culture with its own vocabulary: the "Office Lunch Clique," the daily "Lunch?" that isn't really a question, and the social penalty of being "bo jio'd" — left out — for declining too often.

The numbers suggest the toll is real. Gallup's 2026 Singapore Workplace Report found just 14 percent of employees engaged, well below the Southeast Asian regional average of 25 percent, with 43 percent reporting daily stress, a figure that climbs to 53 percent among workers under 35. And yet 40 percent of Singaporean workers still register as "thriving" in their overall lives, ahead of the regional average, suggesting the exhaustion is compartmentalized rather than total. The one clearly load-bearing ritual is the annual D&D — dinner and dance — Singapore's answer to the office holiday party, where attendance is nominally voluntary and functionally universal.

## The Reckoning

Put the two head to head and an uncomfortable pattern appears: the country that treats socializing as elective, Canada, scores better on engagement than the one that treats it as an unspoken civic duty, Singapore. That is not proof that forced bonding backfires as a universal law — Singapore's foreign workforce has historically rated the country favourably in MOM's own surveys, and its productivity numbers hold up fine — but it does suggest that mandatory-feeling fun and genuine engagement are not the same variable, and conflating them is where well-meaning HR departments go wrong on both sides of the Pacific.

The more useful contrast for a newcomer is what the obligation actually buys. In Canada, opting out costs you almost nothing visible and almost everything invisible: you simply become a colleague people stop looping in. In Singapore, opting out costs you visibly and immediately, but the culture is at least honest about the price, and paying it — showing up, choping a seat, sitting through the D&D — buys a form of trust that formal meetings rarely produce on their own. Neither system is more "authentic" than the other. They are simply charging in different currencies.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A contributor comparing Canadian and American office norms described a Canadian employer that kept beer fridges stocked throughout the building, with a manager summarizing the culture as "you don't work for me, you work with me" — friendliness treated as a management philosophy, not just manners.*

> *Quora — Asked how a non-drinker survives an office culture that treats alcohol as mandatory team-building, one respondent suggested ordering a drink that merely looks alcoholic rather than explaining a personal choice to a room that has already decided abstaining is a small betrayal.*

> *Quora — A contributor describing Singaporean work culture called the office a "second home" for many local employees, noting that long hours were common and that family time and stress management were rarely built into the expectation.*

> *Rice Media — A Singaporean writer detailed the "Office Lunch Clique" ritual in unsparing terms: the daily "Lunch?" that functions as a summons rather than an invitation, the practice of choping hawker seats with tissue packets before the group has even arrived, and the quiet social penalty of being "bo jio'd" for declining too often.*

> *Quora — Someone reflecting on cultural shocks after moving to Canada warned that colleagues frequently "don't mean what they literally say" in meetings, describing the adjustment less as culture shock and more as learning to read a long string of polite non-answers correctly.*

### Conclusion

Neither country will put "mandatory fun" in an offer letter, and neither needs to. Canada enforces its office rituals through vagueness — nobody requires the Friday drink, but nobody forgets who never shows up. Singapore enforces its through visibility, where the daily lunch invitation and the annual D&D sit close enough to compulsory that treating them as optional is itself a statement. A newcomer serious about building a working life in either place should budget real time for both: the weekly hallway small talk in Toronto, the hawker-stall lunch clique in the CBD. Skipping either is possible. Being fully trusted while skipping either is not.

If a friend asked me over a drink, I'd tell them to go to the first few lunches and the first D&D no matter how tired they are — because in both countries, you're never really being evaluated on the food.
