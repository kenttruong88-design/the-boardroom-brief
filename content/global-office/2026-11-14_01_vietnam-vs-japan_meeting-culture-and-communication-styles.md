# In Vietnam the Silence Means Politeness; in Japan the Meeting Is a Formality

🇻🇳 Vietnam · 🇯🇵 Japan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two of Asia's most hierarchical offices have arrived at the same unusual conclusion: the person most likely to disagree with you in a meeting is the one saying nothing at all. In Japan, only 8 percent of employees are engaged at work, according to Gallup. In Vietnam, the figure is 9 percent. Both countries, in other words, are producing large quantities of quiet, courteous and largely unreadable staff. The difference lies in what happens to the silence, and in whether the real meeting occurred last Tuesday.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask a colleague to repeat an instruction back to you in their own words, because a yes may only mean the speaker does not wish to embarrass anyone | Treat a nod or an agreeable yes as confirmation that the task was understood |
| Raise a concern with the person's line manager, or through whoever brought them into the company | Skip a layer and take a problem straight to the director |
| Give corrective feedback privately, and begin with what is working | Criticise a colleague in front of the team |
| Join the team-building trip and the group lunch, which carry real relationship weight | Assume an office that is quiet is an office that is unfriendly |
| Expect after-hours messages and plan your boundaries openly | Assume that the official 7 a.m. to 5 p.m. schedule describes the actual day |
| Invite questions explicitly, since staff may wait to be asked | Read the absence of questions as the absence of confusion |

### In Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Speak to key stakeholders one-on-one before you raise a proposal in the room | Present a new idea cold in a full meeting and expect a decision |
| Treat a phrase such as "that is a little difficult" as a polite no | Press for a direct answer in front of the group |
| Follow up by email after the meeting, where people can answer without losing face | Interpret silence or nodding as agreement |
| Read the circulated ringi document carefully before it reaches you for approval | Assume the slow approval cycle means the decision is in doubt |
| Attend the meetings you are invited to, including long ones in Japanese, if the team expects your presence | Skip the first or second after-work drinks invitation without a good reason |
| Write down context, glossary and background rather than assuming it is understood | Argue with Western-style bluntness, which may read as a serious breach rather than candour |

## Vietnam

Vietnamese offices operate on a formal chain of command with the manners of a family dinner. Hofstede Insights scores Vietnam at 70 for power distance and 20 for individualism, which is to say hierarchical and collectivist, and that matches what foreigners report. An American teacher in Ho Chi Minh City told VnExpress International that he wanted to raise a matter directly with the director and was advised to go through his line manager instead. An English video editor, struck by how quiet his office was, learned that silence is often read as politeness. He posted a plea in the company chat asking colleagues to talk to him more. About 15 people read it. Nobody replied, and he resigned at the end of April.

The meeting itself tends to be where agreement is displayed, not where it is reached. Saadi Salama, writing in VnExpress International, described subordinates at state agencies who would not back an idea openly and asked him to put proposals on paper so their bosses could judge them. Consultants who have employed Vietnamese staff make the same point more bluntly. On Expat.com, an employer of 35 Vietnamese workers explained that saving face leads staff to say yes when they do not understand, and that managers should ask people to repeat instructions back. The thread is from about 2012, so the details are dated, but the mechanism recurs in current reporting.

The statistical backdrop is not flattering. Gallup's 2026 report, as reported by Tuoi Tre News, puts Vietnamese engagement at 9 percent, the lowest in Southeast Asia against a regional average of 25 percent, and estimates the cost at roughly 12.6 percent of GDP a year. A Navigos survey cited by VnExpress found that 60 percent of foreign respondents reported culture shock, with language barriers the leading cause. Meetings are thus a poor instrument for measuring how a Vietnamese team actually feels. The hallway and the group lunch are better. Time is also less tidy than the schedule suggests. The same teacher reported that official hours ran from 7 a.m. to 5 p.m. but colleagues often stayed until 9 p.m., and that he felt obliged to answer official emails and messages after hours, unlike in the United States. A Vietnamese-American intern at an export company, by contrast, called the team-building trip for about 100 employees a good way of bonding. The relationships that a meeting fails to reveal are, it appears, formed elsewhere, usually over food and usually after the meeting has ended.

## Japan

Japan has industrialised the same instinct. According to Gallup, 8 percent of Japanese employees are engaged, against a global average of 20 percent, while 71 percent are not engaged and 21 percent are actively disengaged. Yet daily stress is reported by 39 percent, slightly below the global figure of 40 percent. The country appears to be disengaged without being visibly upset, which is perhaps the most Japanese possible outcome. Gallup also finds that 57 percent of Japanese workers say it is a good time to find a job, so the quiet is not a sign of people with nowhere else to go. It is a sign of people who have decided that the meeting is not where one registers objections.

The mechanism is nemawashi, the practice of building consensus through quiet one-on-one conversations before the formal gathering. Rework's guide notes that the meeting itself can look like a formality, and one Hacker News commenter put it more plainly: debate happens behind closed doors and the meeting is largely ceremonial. Another observed that disagreement is softened into a difference of opinion, and that visible frustration signals a serious problem. A formal approval document, the ringi, then circulates for stamps. Asana's survey of 1,106 Japanese knowledge workers found that they estimate they lose 2.1 hours a week to meetings that were not needed, which suggests that even the people running the ceremony have noticed its cost.

The foreign experience is mixed. In an informal poll of seven non-Japanese workers by SoraNews24, three mentioned meetings. One described meetings held to discuss other meetings, another said mandatory weekly sessions often led nowhere, and a third said non-Japanese speakers were expected to sit through long Japanese-language meetings simply to show their face. The same commenters noted that heavy written documentation, a byproduct of the system, can be a quiet virtue. Hacker News readers confirmed it: precise, context-rich writing is what makes cross-border teams work. A former member of an internal tech team who worked with a Japanese counterpart said that, because of the time difference and language barrier, both sides wrote everything down, and that the habit made the documentation clearer than it had been before. Commenters added that visible effort, such as staying late, can matter more than outcomes, which is why the meeting runs long even when the decision is already made.

## The Reckoning

Both countries sit in what the Rework guide calls the high-context group, where a flat refusal is uncommon and a long pause can mean no. They differ in where the real decision lives. In Japan it is made before the meeting, through a documented and sequential process, and the room merely ratifies it. In Vietnam it is made by whoever is senior, and the room is a place to be seen agreeing. Hofstede Insights gives Japan a power distance of 54 and Vietnam 70, a gap that explains why the Japanese manager seeks consensus while the Vietnamese manager is more likely simply to be obeyed.

For the newcomer, the practical risk is also different. In Japan, one mistakes a finished decision for an open discussion and arrives with a proposal that was settled a week ago. In Vietnam, one mistakes courteous compliance for comprehension and discovers the misunderstanding three weeks later. The Japanese system is slow but legible, provided you know where to look. The Vietnamese system is faster but opaque, provided you do not ask twice. Neither system rewards the instinct, common among newcomers from flatter offices, to think aloud in front of the group. In Tokyo that instinct reads as an attempt to reopen a settled question. In Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City it reads as an implied criticism of whoever last spoke.

## Conclusion

Choose Japan if you prefer your opacity documented, sequential and eventually stamped. Choose Vietnam if you prefer it warm, personal and delivered over lunch. In either case, the useful skill is the same: ask the question privately, in writing or one on one, and never in front of the group.

Plan for six to twelve months of adjustment, which is the range Move to Asia's chief executive gave VnExpress, and consider a trial stay first. Meetings in both countries will confirm what you already knew. The difference is whether anyone will tell you what they actually think, and in neither case will it be in the meeting.
