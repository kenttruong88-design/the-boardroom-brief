# Zimbabwe Teaches You to Hustle. Canada Teaches You to Wait for Sign-Off.

🇿🇼 Zimbabwe · 🇨🇦 Canada

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Zimbabwe, an estimated 58.5 percent of workers operate in the informal economy, which means more of the country is, functionally, running its own startup than any pitch competition could ever produce (African Development Bank). In Canada, a Gallup poll measuring whether people feel optimistic about finding a new job found Sub-Saharan Africa scoring higher than the United States and Canada combined — 50 percent versus 47 percent — despite reporting far lower rates of "thriving" overall. One continent is hustling without a safety net. The other has the safety net and, increasingly, is not sure the job underneath it is worth keeping. Both facts are true at once, and both complicate the easy story that "startup energy" lives only where the funding does.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇿🇼 Zimbabwe

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Carry both USD cash and local currency, and confirm which one a quoted price actually means | Assume a bank transfer will clear on the day you need it to |
| Build at least one direct relationship with a supplier or client before relying on a website or app alone | Expect online payment or booking systems to replace phone calls and in-person follow-up any time soon |
| Treat multiple income streams as normal, not as a sign your main job is failing | Treat a single employer as your only source of financial security |
| Budget real time and money for power and connectivity gaps | Schedule anything mission-critical around a single internet session |
| Ask local hires directly what training they want — formal education often skips practical digital skills | Assume a university degree means someone has used the tools your business runs on |
| Expect negotiation on almost everything, including salary and payment terms | Quote a first price as if it's final |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn your company's org chart and sign-off chain before proposing anything that costs money | Expect a good idea to move faster than the approval process around it |
| Say "that's a great question, let me follow up" when you don't have an answer | Say what you actually think in a meeting before reading the room |
| Expect a formal, documented interview and compensation-banding process, even at small firms | Expect a handshake deal, even from a founder who seems casual |
| Budget for a higher cost of living in Toronto and Vancouver than the salary number alone suggests | Assume national averages apply in the cities with the actual job openings |
| Network specifically toward the smaller hubs — Waterloo, Montreal, Vancouver — where tech roles outside banking concentrate | Assume Canada lacks a startup scene just because it isn't Silicon Valley |
| Expect real statutory protections: parental leave, minimum vacation, notice periods | Expect the same protections, or pace, if you move into an early-stage startup |

### 🇿🇼 Zimbabwe: the economy that built itself without permission

Zimbabwe's headline numbers read like a country mid-repair: GDP growth jumped from 1.7 percent in 2024 to 8.3 percent in 2025 on the back of agriculture and mining, inflation fell to 2.9 percent by August 2026 — its lowest in more than three decades — and the parallel exchange-rate premium that once defined daily commerce has narrowed to under 20 percent (World Bank). None of that, however, describes how most Zimbabweans actually make a living. The African Development Bank puts informal employment at 58.5 percent of the workforce, unemployment at 20.4 percent overall and 27.9 percent among young people, and the national poverty rate at 40 percent. Formalizing that economy is listed as a government reform priority, which is a polite way of saying the state is still catching up to what its own citizens built without it.

What that produces, on the ground, is not a "startup culture" in the Silicon Valley sense of pitch decks and seed rounds — Zimbabwe barely registers in international venture-capital datasets — but something closer to permanent, low-margin entrepreneurship as the default employment condition. A salaried job is frequently a base, not a ceiling; side income, trading, and informal services fill the gaps that formal payrolls and patchy banking leave open. Hofstede Insights has never published an individual cultural-dimensions score for Zimbabwe, and no credible regional cluster score exists either — a genuine data gap worth naming rather than papering over with an invented number.

Harare and Bulawayo do have a small, visible tech and incubator scene — accelerators, Seedstars-style competitions, telecoms-backed innovation funds — but it runs on a fraction of the capital and infrastructure reliability that founders elsewhere take for granted. The constraint is not ambition; it is everything ambition needs to compound. A founder here routinely plays supplier, developer, accountant, and debt collector in the same week, because the specialized middle layer of an economy — reliable contractors, functioning courts for contract disputes, predictable credit — is still thin. That is also precisely why the people who do succeed here tend to be unusually resourceful hires anywhere else in the world.

### 🇨🇦 Canada: the economy that built permission into everything

Canada's labor market is the mirror image: stable, documented, and increasingly uneasy about its own momentum. Gallup's 2026 State of the Global Workplace puts employee engagement for the United States-and-Canada region at 31 percent and "thriving" at 51 percent, both respectable by global standards, but the same report notes regional job optimism sliding for a second straight year, with the bloc now ranking second-to-last among the regions Gallup tracks. Hofstede's widely cited individualism score for Canada sits at 80, among the highest in the world — a culture built for people who expect autonomy and personal accountability, layered onto workplaces that, in practice, run on committees, compensation bands, and documented process.

Canada's actual startup geography is smaller and more specific than outsiders assume: a real scene concentrated in Toronto, Vancouver, Montreal, and Kitchener-Waterloo, producing genuine global companies — Shopify, Hootsuite, FreshBooks — but still a fraction of the scale, funding, and risk tolerance of its southern neighbor. Veteran founders on Hacker News describe the pattern consistently: strong engineering talent, a real but modest venture ecosystem, and a steady outflow of people south for salaries two to three times higher. The corporate default, meanwhile, is unusually well-cushioned — statutory parental leave, guaranteed vacation, mandated notice periods — which is exactly the safety net that makes leaving for a five-person startup feel like a real decision rather than a survival necessity.

This is the part that surprises newcomers most: Canadian corporate life is not slow because people are unambitious, it is slow because the default assumption is that the institution, not the individual, carries the risk. Decisions get documented because someone, eventually, might audit them. Meetings exist to distribute accountability as much as to make choices. None of this is unique to Canada, but it is unusually consistent across sectors — banking, government, and even many startups past their first ten employees — in a way that catches arrivals from faster-moving or less formalized economies off guard.

### The Reckoning

Put plainly: Zimbabwe does not lack entrepreneurial instinct, it lacks capital, infrastructure, and the formal institutions that let a good idea become a registered, scalable company rather than a permanent side hustle. Canada has the opposite problem — the institutions, the capital pools, and the legal scaffolding all exist, but the appetite for genuine startup risk sits downstream of a culture that, by its own workers' admission, often rewards process over initiative.

Neither country's "startup mindset" and "corporate mindset" are opposites so much as two different responses to scarcity: scarcity of safety nets in one case, scarcity of urgency in the other. A founder moving from Harare to Toronto will likely find the paperwork maddening but the capital finally real. A corporate employee moving from Toronto to Harare will find the capital maddeningly absent but the hustle, finally, unavoidable. What neither will find is a clean swap of one problem for the other — more likely, each will trade a familiar frustration for an unfamiliar one, and spend the first year mistaking that unfamiliarity for dysfunction before realizing it is simply a different, equally coherent, system of incentives.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Techzim.co.zw — Joseph Bunga, founder of restaurant-booking startup eatout.co.zw, describes hiring developer teams who vanished near deadlines: "People can sell very well but when it comes to the deadline, not only do they not deliver, they disappear." He eventually taught himself PHP rather than keep losing months to unreliable contractors.*

> *Hacker News (commenter witoldc) — recalling time spent across several developing economies, writes that "the biggest sense of entrepreneurial spirit I saw was actually in Zimbabwe," despite arriving with low expectations shaped by the country's economic headlines.*

> *Hacker News (commenter hsckr) — on Canada's tech market, argues the core problem isn't a shortage of talent but retention: junior job postings routinely demand experience levels that push new graduates to apply south of the border instead.*

> *BetaKit — David Wilkin, co-founder of 10KC, says he wishes he'd insisted on a dated workback schedule from every acquisition prospect early on: serious buyers provided one without hesitation, while the eventual fake acquirer "got really weird about it" and stalled for months before the deal collapsed.*

> *Hacker News (commenter zmitri, referencing a blog post by Jaclyn Konzelmann) — on leaving a stable role at Microsoft for a five-person Toronto startup, notes that the city's tech culture is modest by design, "which is good or bad depending on what you want," and that nearby Kitchener-Waterloo felt closer to a Silicon Valley engineering ethos than Toronto itself.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between them on vibes alone, choose this instead: Zimbabwe will teach you to build something from nothing, fast, with people who have been doing exactly that their entire working lives — but you will need your own capital, your own backup plan for power and payments, and patience for talent markets still catching up to demand. Canada will hand you real institutions, real protections, and real (if modest) venture capital — but it will also hand you a sign-off chain, a compensation band, and a strong cultural pull toward not rocking the boat. Neither failure mode is fatal; both are foreseeable, if you read past the brochure. Pack accordingly, and bring a lawyer in Toronto, a generator in Harare, and realistic expectations in both.
