# Twelve Weeks and a Grandmother: Having Children in Sri Lanka and Australia

🇱🇰 Sri Lanka · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Sri Lanka's private-sector mothers are entitled to twelve weeks of maternity leave, paid for entirely by their employer, with no state scheme to share the bill. Australia's government will pay a family for 26 weeks, at the minimum wage, from a federal budget. Both arrangements are described by their respective governments as supportive. Both leave a conspicuous gap where the childcare is meant to go. The difference lies in who fills it: in Sri Lanka, usually a grandmother; in Australia, usually a waiting list and a fee schedule.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Sri Lanka

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask HR in writing which law covers your contract (Maternity Benefits Ordinance or the Shop and Office Employees Act) before you announce anything | Assume the leave is longer because you have complications or twins; the law makes no provision for extension |
| Give notice on time: the employer should hear a month or fortnight before the expected delivery, and within a week after it | Count on statutory paternity leave in the private sector; there is none, and generosity is left to the company |
| Check you have worked at least 150 days in the year before notice, which the Ordinance requires | Expect a workplace crèche to exist merely because the law mentions one |
| Plan childcare around family before you plan it around the market, and have a second option for when a grandparent falls ill | Rely on a single relative as your whole childcare system |
| Ask about nursing breaks before returning, since awareness of them is uneven even in HR departments | Assume your manager will treat the leave as unremarkable on your return |

### Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your employer's own paid leave policy first; the government pay is a floor, and some employers add weeks on top | Assume the government payment replaces your salary; it is paid at the National Minimum Wage rate |
| Confirm whether the waiting period for new residents or temporary visa holders applies to you before you plan your finances | Arrive and assume you will qualify on day one |
| Join childcare waitlists as early as you can, and visit centres before enrolling | Believe a place will appear when you need it |
| Understand the 3 Day Guarantee, which gives eligible families at least 72 subsidised hours per child per fortnight | Mistake a subsidy guarantee for a guaranteed place |
| Talk to both parents' employers about leave taken in sequence; it is becoming available, but it is not universal | Take for granted that a father's employer will offer primary carer leave |

## Sri Lanka: Twelve Weeks, Paid by Your Employer

The law is clear, if narrow. According to the Daily Mirror, women in the private sector get 12 weeks (84 days) of paid, compulsory maternity leave under either the Maternity Benefits Ordinance of 1939 or the Shop and Office Employees Act of 1954. WageIndicator, which tracks the rules for workers, puts the shop and office figure at 70 days and says the Ordinance pays 6/7 of wages, or about 86 per cent, with a requirement of 150 days worked in the previous year. There is no extension for illness or multiple births. The 2018 amendment removed the old limit that confined full benefits to the first two children, which is progress of a kind.

The employer bears the full cost, and the Daily Mirror notes that no state scheme offsets it. Fathers in the private sector have no statutory paternity leave; some large companies grant a few days voluntarily. The public sector is more generous: 84 days on full pay, 84 on half pay and 84 unpaid, plus three paid days for fathers. The pattern is familiar to anyone who has compared state employment with the rest of the economy.

Childcare is where the national strategy tends to be a relative. The World Bank noted in 2021 that female labour force participation stood at 36 per cent against 75 per cent for men, and that a child under five lowered a woman's participation by 7.4 per cent in a 2018 study. The Daily Mirror cites the 2024 Labour Force Survey at 29.8 per cent for women. Echelon's account of a December 2025 report on 45 working mothers in Colombo found that only one was using a daycare centre, that private centres cost around Rs30,000 a month, and that thirteen relied on their own mothers. A small interview study of eight first-time manager-mothers described family-based care as a makeshift arrangement in a country without affordable, reliable alternatives. The grandmother, in other words, is infrastructure. She is unfunded, unregulated, and subject to her own health.

The statute also covers fewer people than it appears to. The Daily Mirror notes that the Ordinance excludes casual workers, and that the informal sector is essentially outside the system. Echelon reports that most of the Colombo mothers worked informally, that only 22 per cent had retirement benefits and that roughly a third had paid leave. Employers must provide nursing breaks, according to the Daily Mirror, but the crèche provision is not yet fully implemented. In the interview study, one mother said an HR executive was unsure whether she was entitled to breastfeeding breaks at all. A right that the department administering it cannot confirm is best described as aspirational.

## Australia: Generous on Paper, Invoiced in Practice

Australia's scheme is genuinely national and genuinely shared. The Fair Work Ombudsman states that for children born or adopted between 1 July 2025 and 30 June 2026, a family can share up to 24 weeks of Parental Leave Pay; from 1 July 2026 the family maximum is 130 days, or about 26 weeks. Services Australia gives the rate as $948.10 a week before tax at the 2025 level, equal to the minimum wage, and the same for everyone regardless of earnings. From July 2026 the Tax Office also pays a 12 per cent superannuation contribution on the payment. Services Australia warns that new residents and some temporary visa holders may have to wait before they can claim, which is worth discovering before the baby, not after.

Childcare is a separate chapter. The Sector, reporting the Australian Bureau of Statistics in October 2026, says 20 per cent of mothers of children under 15 who wanted a job but could not start within four weeks cited childcare being unavailable, up from 9 per cent in 2024-25. Out-of-pocket childcare costs rose 4.4 per cent in the year to September 2026, slightly above headline inflation of 4.3 per cent. The 3 Day Guarantee began on 5 January 2026, giving eligible families at least 72 subsidised hours per child each fortnight, though the same report notes it does not guarantee a place. The Grattan Institute has argued that Australia ranks equal first in the world for women's education but 70th for women's economic participation, and that childcare fees rose more than 3 per cent a year above inflation across a decade. Australia has built the subsidy. It has not entirely built the seats.

The employer layer matters more than the headline suggests. The government scheme is the floor, and the Fair Work Ombudsman is explicit that employer-funded entitlements depend on your contract, enterprise agreement or workplace policy. This is where the difference between a large institution and a small firm quietly reappears. Public service agreements and big employers tend to add paid weeks and, increasingly, fathers' leave; a small business may add nothing beyond the statutory minimum. For a newcomer, the practical advice is to read the enterprise agreement the way one reads a lease, with suspicion and a highlighter.

## The Reckoning

On paper, the contest is lopsided. Australia offers about double the paid duration, funded by taxpayers instead of the individual employer, with a rate that does not depend on how sympathetic your HR director is. Sri Lanka offers a shorter leave whose cost is concentrated on a single payroll, which gives employers a quiet reason to hesitate over hiring women of a certain age. The Daily Mirror observes that maternity must not harm increments, pensions, seniority or promotions. Whether that holds on the ground is a separate question, and one the eight manager-mothers answered with sarcasm from colleagues and a vague sense of lost standing.

On the ground, the contest narrows. Both countries have a childcare problem that the leave policy was never going to solve, and both rely on informal labour to bridge it: relatives in Colombo, money and patience in Sydney. An Australian household pays more directly and gets more rights. A Sri Lankan household pays less in cash and more in dependence, which is a currency without a refund policy. For a migrant, the decisive variable is usually not the weeks of leave but whether your visa, your residency date and your family's proximity let you use them.

There is also the question of what leave does to a career once it ends. Australia treats time away as a lawful, common and administratively tracked event, and its main complaint is the cost of returning, not the permission to leave. Sri Lanka treats the leave as a legal minimum and the return as a private negotiation, conducted with a supervisor whose attitude is not regulated. Neither country has made a career penalty impossible. Australia has simply made it more visible, which is the first step towards arguing about it.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/AusFinance — a commenter notes that two parents taking primary carer leave back to back used to be uncommon, is becoming available in some public service agreements, and is worth asking about even when it is not written into the contract.*

> *Reddit r/AusFinance — another commenter describes a workplace offering 12 weeks of paid primary carer leave, usable before the child turns 18 months, on top of four weeks of paternity leave, and calls it the best thing ever.*

> *Mumsnet — posters in a 2015 thread agree that childcare in Australia is hard to secure for newcomers: waitlists are long, you need an address and a workplace first, and a nanny is very expensive while an au pair is much cheaper.*

> *Expat.com — a Colombo poster says daycare is fairly affordable by international standards, with hours typically around 8 a.m. to 5:30 p.m. and some centres following international syllabi, though the thread offers no prices and the claims are unverified.*

> *Echelon — a mother interviewed in Colombo says she left several daycare centres because children were treated like patients, and she was not willing to leave hers in them.*

## Conclusion

If you are choosing a country for the first years of a child's life, Australia wins on entitlement and Sri Lanka wins on the cash price of a daycare place that most families do not use. Check the employer's own policy before the government's, check your visa status before either, and join the waitlist before you have a due date. In Sri Lanka, secure written confirmation of your leave and a backup for the grandmother.

Neither country has solved the problem of raising children while holding a job, which is reassuring in a way: you will be unprepared on either continent.
