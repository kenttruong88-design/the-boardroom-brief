# Czechia Hands You a Laptop and a Pleasant Silence; Switzerland Hands You a Process and Declines to Smile

🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Czechia, according to a survey of employers reported this year, 76 percent of companies that hire foreign workers offer them no structured integration support at all. In Switzerland, about two-thirds of pupils choose a vocational route in which training is a legal and cultural fixture of working life, and a workplace so shaped can be expected to believe that someone, somewhere, should explain how things are done. The two countries share an alphabet of reliability and a distaste for fuss. They differ in whether the new arrival is taught, or merely observed until competent.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for a written outline of your first 90 days; do not wait for one to arrive. | Assume that silence means someone has a plan for you. |
| Request a named colleague who can answer process questions in English. | Rely on a single orientation session to carry you through the first quarter. |
| Learn enough Czech to follow team conversation; language is the barrier most often cited. | Expect every office conversation to be held in English because the job posting was. |
| Join the team events and the lunches; colleagues often socialise outside work. | Treat attendance as optional in a country where it is mildly expected. |
| Ask for feedback explicitly and on a date. | Wait for a performance review that may not be scheduled. |

### In Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive on time, which means slightly early, on every day including the first. | Treat punctuality as a suggestion. |
| Read the contract for the probation period; one month is the default and three months is common. | Assume a probation is a formality when notice is only seven days. |
| Greet people on arrival and say goodbye on leaving; accept the coffee invitations. | Decline the social rituals on grounds of productivity. |
| Use the formal Sie and surnames until invited to use first names. | Switch to first names to build rapport quickly. |
| Observe existing processes for the first weeks before proposing changes. | Announce that the old process is inefficient in week two. |

## Czechia

Czech onboarding is best described as informal, and in many companies as an aspiration. The Expats.cz report on a Gi Group and Grafton Recruitment survey, published on 1 May 2026, found that 84 percent of Czech companies employ foreign workers, up from roughly two-thirds a year earlier, with some 935,000 foreign workers in the country, an 11 percent rise on the year. Yet 76 percent of those companies offer no structured integration support, 18 percent offer structured support for cross-cultural collaboration, and a further 6 percent provide it to managers only. Only about a quarter of employees have access to training. Language was the barrier most cited, flagged by roughly half of employees and more than two-thirds of employers. A recruiter quoted in the report argued that an initial onboarding session is not enough, which is a polite way of observing that most places do not manage even that.

The pattern is not confined to foreigners. A Grafton and Gi Group survey of 1,500 employees and 324 companies, reported by Expats.cz in April 2026, found that 48 percent of graduates struggled to understand company processes in their first role, while employers believed the figure was 65 percent. White-collar graduates had the most difficulty, at 55 percent, and 35 percent were surprised by the pace and workload. Employers, for their part, said only 1 percent of graduates were fully job-ready, and the report names internships as the strongest single factor in easing the transition. The Expats.cz report on foreign workers links part of the problem to top-down management traditions, which may leave companies less inclined toward mentoring and open communication. That is the publication's inference, not a measured result, but it is a plausible one.

None of this means that Czech workplaces are cold. The Expat.com guide to work culture in Prague describes hierarchies that are usually less rigid than visitors expect, communication that is direct and practical, and colleagues who often socialise outside work and expect participation in team-building and company events. Probation periods typically run up to three months, and notice of two months is the most common arrangement. The mentoring on offer tends to be the incidental sort: a helpful colleague, a lunch, a favour. It is warm and unscheduled, which is excellent when it appears and unavailable when it does not. Feedback is similarly informal. An Up Benefity survey of more than 1,000 employees, reported by Expats.cz in January 2026, found that only about a quarter say their employer regularly collects workplace feedback, and 36 percent say it makes no effort to ask.

## Switzerland

Swiss onboarding begins long before the first day, in school. According to swissinfo, about two-thirds of pupils around age 15 choose the vocational route, with more than 90 percent of young people finishing upper secondary education with a school or apprenticeship certificate. Apprentices divide their time between a host company, where they gain practical experience, and a vocational school, and the country offers some 230 vocational professions. Youth unemployment averaged 2.5 percent in 2021. The inference worth drawing, and it is an inference, is that a large share of Swiss colleagues have spent their formative years being trained at work by other people and now expect to do the same. A Hacker News commenter in 2019 added a less admiring note: apprentices are sometimes overworked, and the laws against it are poorly enforced, in that commenter's view.

The structured part continues at the employment contract. According to a Zurich law firm's summary published by IamExpat.ch, the default probation period is one month, though employers often set three, and either side may end the contract on seven days' notice during that time. After probation, three months' notice is standard, longer for senior roles. This produces a particular pressure: the formal onboarding is thorough, and the evaluation is quiet, continuous, and not announced. Ibani's guide to Swiss workplace etiquette observes that the cultural codes shaping a newcomer's first weeks are written into neither the contract nor the handbook; newcomers learn them through friction. Upreer adds that much internal communication, including corridor talk and informal decision-making, takes place in German or Swiss German, so an English-only newcomer misses a large part of the flow.

The mentor you receive will probably be direct but not warm. Upreer describes Swiss colleagues as specific and plain about work that falls short, usually in person and calmly, with conflicts settled privately. IamExpat.ch's comparison of Swiss and American work cultures notes that Swiss managers rarely compliment outright, so growing responsibility may be the only praise you receive, and that colleagues can seem distant at first. InterNations Expat Insider 2023, as reported by The Local, ranked Switzerland 47th of 53 destinations for ease of settling in, though it placed salary and job security second. The 2026 edition, reported by IamExpat.ch, still has Switzerland in the bottom six of 31 countries on settling in.

## The Reckoning

Compare them and the difference is one of design versus temperament. Switzerland builds competence on purpose, through apprenticeships, probation clauses and clear processes, and distributes encouragement sparingly. Czechia builds competence by proximity: you absorb the work from whoever is nearby, with a decent lunch included. Hofstede Insights scores Czechia at 57 for power distance and 74 for uncertainty avoidance, against 34 and 58 for Switzerland, which fits a Czech workplace that is somewhat more hierarchical and anxious about rules, and a Swiss one in which authority rests more on expertise and consultation.

For a newcomer the choice is between a system that explains itself and rarely reassures, and a culture that reassures informally and rarely explains. If you prefer to learn the rules from a document, Switzerland suits you, provided you accept a long probation of social distance. If you prefer to learn from people, Czechia suits you, provided you ask for the people. Neither country will hold your hand for you, but one will at least give you a form on which to request it.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/zurich — paraphrased experience of a new hire asking how to impress on the first day: commenters advised arriving punctually, accepting coffee and lunch invitations, greeting and saying goodbye to colleagues, and bringing something to share, while warning that Swiss offices can be more reserved than expected and that one's mentor may appreciate the gesture more than anyone else.*

> *Reddit r/askswitzerland — paraphrased experience (adjacent theme, integration more than mentoring): commenters said arriving early is read as a strong signal, that leaving visibly earlier than everyone else can draw disapproval, and that expats who do not learn the local language often end up feeling excluded.*

> *Hacker News — paraphrased experience of someone who knows the Swiss apprenticeship system: it begins after the ninth school year and runs three to four years, with school and tests alongside the job, and employers sometimes overwork apprentices without much enforcement.*

> *Expat.com Switzerland forum — paraphrased experience of a worker who moved from a London company to a Geneva role: working hard and helping colleagues was read as not being a team player, the expected pace included regular coffee breaks and long lunches, and the respondent came to value the team once adjusted.*

> *IamExpat.ch citing InterNations Expat Insider — paraphrased experience of a Geneva expat (adjacent theme, social integration): making local friends is very hard, even through associations and a child's school, and relationships tend to remain at the acquaintance level.*

## Conclusion

If you are moving to Czechia, treat your first month as self-directed. Ask for a plan, a named colleague and a feedback date, and learn enough Czech to follow the room. The goodwill is real and the structure is thin, so supply the structure yourself and you will be fine. If you are moving to Switzerland, read the probation clause, keep to the schedule, use surnames and wait for the invitation to use first names. Expect honest and quiet assessment, and treat an extra responsibility as the compliment it is.

The honest summary is that both countries will train you eventually, in different ways and at different costs. Czechia asks you to find the mentor, and Switzerland asks you to deserve the mentor, and in both cases the paperwork has been completed before anyone says hello.
