# Egypt Pays in Pounds and Hope, the UAE Pays in Allowances and Footnotes

🇪🇬 Egypt · 🇦🇪 UAE

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Egypt, the state announces the private-sector minimum wage at EGP 7,000 a month, about USD 150 according to Egyptian Streets, and then the nation spends the year discussing whether anyone can live on it. In the UAE, nobody announces anything. The salary arrives as a package with an anatomy, and the part that determines your retirement lump sum is a modest organ called basic pay. One country argues about the number. The other hides it in a breakdown. A prospective migrant should know which argument they are signing up for.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for a written contract and confirm social insurance is registered, since about 60% of the workforce is informal | Accept a verbal promise as a salary structure |
| Check any offer against the EGP 7,000 private-sector minimum before celebrating | Assume the minimum applies to every employer in practice |
| Quote and negotiate with inflation in mind; ask when the next review falls | Treat a figure agreed today as stable for the year |
| Expect colleagues to discuss pay, cost of living and fuel openly | Be surprised when a candidate pool rejects offers over workload |
| Ask what the full package contains, including transport and meals | Count on overtime being separately rewarded |

### UAE

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for the split between basic salary and allowances in writing | Judge an offer by the headline total alone |
| Calculate your gratuity from basic pay: 21 days per year for the first five years | Assume allowances count toward end-of-service pay |
| Ask for a written final-settlement breakdown before signing any clearance | Sign an all-dues-received form before the money arrives |
| Know that wages and gratuity are due within 14 days of your contract ending | Accept that gratuity can be withheld over a dispute |
| Compare your offer with peers in the same role, not only your passport peers | Mention nationality-based pay as a negotiating position |

## Egypt

The Egyptian approach to salary is less a culture than a continuing negotiation with arithmetic. According to Ahram Online, the private-sector minimum wage was set at EGP 6,000 in May 2024, covering about 18 million workers, and business owners immediately warned of layoffs. The head of the Printing and Packaging Chamber said factories would cut staff if demand failed to support the higher cost. Bloomberg Law reports that the National Wages Council later raised the floor to EGP 7,000 a month in 2025. Each increase has been announced as relief and received as a rounding error.

The reason is visible in the price data. Egyptian Streets reports that inflation reached 31.9% in March 2023, almost double wage growth, and was still 26.2% in August 2024. The pound slid from about 19 to the dollar in 2022 to about 47.5 by late 2025. The same report, citing a World Bank source, says roughly 60% of the workforce is informal, with no contract, no social insurance and no guaranteed benefits. A salary in this setting is not a fixed quantity. It is a rate of decay.

Young professionals have drawn the logical conclusions. Egyptian Streets interviewed a former school HR executive who said she could save only EGP 1,000 to 2,000 a month and leaned on her mother for necessities. She also said that 60 to 70% of the candidates she presented to employers turned the offers down because the workload did not match the pay. Her job, in effect, was persuading people to accept less than they were worth.

The Gulf, meanwhile, is the pressure valve. Egyptian Streets, reporting on Egyptian men working there, finds that many plan to stay two to five years, earn enough to buy a house and start a business, then return. Some of them find delayed salaries and Gulf prices waiting. The plan is sound in principle and sensitive to execution, which is true of most plans that depend on an employer paying on time. Hofstede Insights, for what it is worth, scores Egypt at 70 on power distance versus the UAE at 90, which suggests the hierarchy you leave behind is merely a milder version of the one waiting for you.

## UAE

The UAE has a more orderly relationship with money, provided you read the small print, which is where it lives. Gulf News, summarising the 2021 Labour Law, explains that gratuity is calculated on the last basic wage, not gross salary. It requires at least a year of continuous service, pays 21 days of basic pay for each of the first five years and 30 days thereafter, and is capped at two years of wages. Wages and gratuity are due within 14 days of the contract ending, and an employer cannot hold gratuity back because of a dispute or because you resigned. This is considerably more protection than most Egyptian employees can claim, and it is delivered with a straight face.

The catch is the word basic. On an Expat.com forum, a Dubai job seeker found his contract split his negotiated total into a basic salary of roughly 57% and a housing allowance. Respondents explained that this is normal, that it is hard to find an offer which does not do it, and that the basic share drives end-of-service benefits. The total is unchanged. The benefit that depends on the lower number is not. Gulf News also advises that you ask for a written final-settlement breakdown before signing any clearance, which tells you how often the paperwork is a negotiation. If the money is late, the route is a complaint to the Ministry of Human Resources and Emiratisation, which according to the same guide usually tries an amicable settlement before the matter reaches court. Employers may deduct sums legally owed, such as loan recovery or court-ordered amounts, but only through proper procedure. The system works best for the employee who kept copies.

There is also the passport question. A Reddit post covered by Business Today in August 2025 alleged that identical jobs pay Europeans double what Asian or African colleagues earn. The report offers no verified data, and commenters offered the market explanation that firms hire whoever accepts the lowest wage. Treat it as an unproven but widely repeated suspicion. It is not an official statistic, only a thing people in the UAE say to each other quietly.

## The Reckoning

Head to head, Egypt offers a salary that is argued about openly and eroded steadily, and the UAE offers one that is protected by rules and obscured by structure. In Egypt the risk is the number: inflation of 26% to 32% in recent years and a pound that lost more than half its value against the dollar make any figure provisional. In the UAE the risk is the composition: a total that looks generous and a basic wage that decides what you take home when you leave. Egyptians complain loudly about pay and know exactly what it is. Expats in the UAE complain rarely and sometimes learn at the exit interview.

For someone choosing between them, the practical difference is who does the arithmetic. In Egypt the economy does it to you, publicly. In the UAE you must do it yourself, in advance, on the offer letter. The second is the better deal, but only for people who read offer letters.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit — paraphrased experience: a poster argued that in Dubai, pay for the same job, hours and responsibilities depends more on nationality than on skill, with Europeans earning twice as much as Asian or African staff. A hospitality worker replied that firms swap in whichever nationality will accept less, and another said many people accept low pay simply because it still beats what they would earn at home.*

> *Expat.com — paraphrased experience: a Dubai job seeker was puzzled that his contract split the agreed salary into a basic wage and a housing allowance. A veteran poster said the split affects end-of-service benefits and disputes, and another said nearly every offer is built this way.*

> *British Expats — paraphrased experience: in a 2016 thread, a candidate offered AED 12,000 basic plus an AED 8,000 living allowance was told it was too low, and that he should ask about sick pay, bonus and redundancy terms. One poster reminded him that gratuity is small and is no substitute for a pension.*

> *Egyptian Streets — paraphrased experience: an Egyptian engineer in Saudi Arabia, facing delayed salaries and rising costs, sent his wife and newborn home. Another Egyptian, who had worked in Qatar, concluded that life in the Gulf was too expensive to bring his family. Many such men plan to stay two to five years, then return to buy a house.*

> *Egyptian Streets — paraphrased experience: a young marketing associate said most of her income goes on fuel and meals, and that she works largely to keep the job that pays for the commute.*

## Conclusion

If you take a job in Egypt, you are buying into a salary that needs defending against inflation, preferably with a contract, registered insurance and a review date. If you take one in the UAE, you are buying a more predictable structure that rewards readers: get the basic and allowance split in writing, do the gratuity sum before you accept, and keep your final-settlement paperwork until the money has landed. In both countries, the headline number is the least informative thing on the page.

The Gulf will pay you in dirhams, and the Nile will pay you in stories about dirhams.
