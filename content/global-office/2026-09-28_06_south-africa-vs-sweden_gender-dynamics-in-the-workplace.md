**Equal on Paper: Gender at Work in South Africa and Sweden**

**🇿🇦 South Africa · 🇸🇪 Sweden**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Sweden's parliament is 45% women. Its corporate boardrooms are not — women hold roughly 13% of senior leadership positions, and one widely cited estimate put full management parity at 52 years away at the current pace. South Africa, meanwhile, has a constitution and an Employment Equity Act that are unambiguous about gender equality, and a labour market where women's employment rate has actually fallen over the past decade, from 36.9% to 35.8%. Two countries with famously progressive laws on paper; two boardrooms that look nothing like the legislation that governs them.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇿🇦 South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly about parental leave, flexibility, and salary benchmarking before accepting an offer — protections exist, but enforcement varies by employer | Assume the Employment Equity Act guarantees equal pay in practice; document your role, responsibilities, and comparable salaries yourself |
| Report harassment through formal HR channels and in writing, even if the informal response is dismissive | Accept "he's just like that with everyone" as a resolution to a harassment complaint — escalate in writing if the first response minimizes it |
| Build a support network of peers who can vouch for your contributions in meetings | Assume your ideas will be credited automatically; repeat and re-attribute your own proposals if colleagues restate them as new |
| Negotiate pay explicitly and ask for the comparator data if you suspect a gap | Assume "breadwinner" framing from management is a legitimate reason for pay differences — it isn't, legally or otherwise |
| Take pregnancy and family leave you're entitled to and document everything around it | Announce a pregnancy without first understanding your employer's actual (not just legal) track record on maternity protections |

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take your full share of parental leave if you're a father — of 480 shared days, a meaningful portion is reserved specifically for each parent and lost if unused | Assume taking extended parental leave will hurt your career; it's a mainstream, expected choice for Swedish fathers |
| Build a genuine Swedish professional network early — access to opportunities runs heavily through personal connections | Assume gender-equal laws mean equal outcomes for everyone; access to networks and leadership tracks still varies by who you know |
| Use Sweden's strong statutory protections (parental leave, anti-discrimination law) as a floor, not a ceiling, for what to expect | Mistake Sweden's progressive reputation for a guarantee of inclusion, particularly if you're a woman of color or a foreign hire without local networks |
| Expect open discussion of work-life boundaries as completely normal, not a special accommodation | Treat visible caregiving (leaving for daycare pickup, openly discussing parental leave) as something to downplay professionally |
| Ask pointed questions in interviews about actual board and leadership composition, not just stated policy | Take a company's stated equality commitments at face value without checking its actual management gender ratio |

### Sweden: The Paradox at the Top

Sweden's parental leave system is the policy the rest of the world tries to copy: 480 days of shared leave per child, 390 of them paid at 80% of salary, with a portion reserved specifically for each parent on a use-it-or-lose-it basis. Richard Orange, a British journalist who took two full six-month leaves with his children in Malmö, described the experience as genuinely identity-altering — his "ego and ambitions were pulled without warning to the absolute edge" of his attention, a shift he found "strangely liberating" even as his career ambitions "awkwardly reemerged" years later. The system doesn't just permit fathers to step back; it structurally expects it, and the social infrastructure — open preschools, daytime parent networks — is built around fathers being present on weekday mornings, not just mothers.

And yet Sweden's corporate hierarchy tells a very different story than its parental leave statistics. Women hold 45% of parliamentary seats but, per one widely cited analysis, only around 13% of corporate leadership roles, with private-sector management parity projected decades out at the current rate of change. Writer Lovette Jallow, writing from the intersection of race and gender in Swedish professional life, offers a sharper diagnosis of why: "Nepotism runs deep in Sweden," she writes. "It's not what you know — it's who you know. Career growth is based on having the right Swedish networks." Her broader argument is that Sweden's gender-equality reputation disproportionately benefits Swedish-born white women, while foreign women and women of color encounter the same network-gatekeeping that limits everyone's path into leadership, compounded by the added barrier of being an outsider to the network in the first place.

### South Africa: Progressive Law, Uneven Floor

South Africa's constitutional and legal framework for gender equality is, on paper, as strong as any in the world, backed by an Employment Equity Act designed explicitly to correct historical imbalances. In practice, the numbers move slowly and sometimes backward: Statistics South Africa's own data shows the national female employment rate declining from 36.9% to 35.8% over the past decade, even as female labour force participation ticked up — meaning more women are looking for work without a matching rise in who actually gets hired. Parliamentary representation has genuinely improved, reaching 42.8% in 2024, but local government tells a less flattering story: women hold only 32% of mayoral positions and 37.7% of municipal council seats.

The everyday texture of that gap shows up in specific, recent, named accounts rather than abstractions. A Durban logistics employee profiled by IOL discovered she was earning "R8,000 less per month than a male colleague doing the same job. Same qualifications. Same responsibilities. Same performance rating" — and was told, when she raised it, that her colleague was "the breadwinner." A call centre worker in Durban was let go two weeks after announcing her pregnancy, under the cover of "operational requirements"; she described feeling "punished for becoming a mother." And a senior manufacturing manager summed up a familiar boardroom dynamic in five words: "They listen, but they don't hear" — her proposals landing only once a male colleague repeated them back to the room.

### The Reckoning

The uncomfortable symmetry here is that both countries have built strong legal scaffolding around gender equality and both watch that scaffolding fail to reach the boardroom in strikingly similar ways — just at different altitudes. Sweden's failure point is near the top: parental leave is used, harassment law is real, and the system still produces a corporate leadership gap that would take generations to close at current speed, disproportionately affecting anyone without deep-rooted Swedish networks. South Africa's failure point sits earlier, at the level of basic enforcement: pay gaps, pregnancy discrimination, and dismissed harassment complaints that a strong legal framework technically prohibits but does not, in the specific case, prevent.

The practical difference for a newcomer is what kind of vigilance is required. In Sweden, the risk is complacency — assuming a famously equal country means an equal outcome for you personally, when the real gatekeeper is a professional network you don't yet have access to. In South Africa, the risk is closer to the ground: knowing your legal rights precisely, because the gap between what the law promises and what an individual manager will actually do can be wide enough to require documentation, escalation, and, sometimes, a fight.

### Conclusion

If you're a woman weighing these two countries, the honest advice is this: in Sweden, use every legal protection available to you immediately and aggressively, because the system is genuinely built to support it — but don't assume the network doors open automatically just because the parental leave policy is generous. In South Africa, know your rights in writing before you need them, because the law is strong but enforcement depends heavily on the specific manager you happen to get. Neither country has actually solved this. They've just each solved a different half of the problem, and left you to notice which half is missing before it costs you something.
