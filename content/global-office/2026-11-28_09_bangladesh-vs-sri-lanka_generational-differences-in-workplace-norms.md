# Dhaka's Juniors Want Forty Hours, Colombo's Want a Departure Gate

🇧🇩 Bangladesh · 🇱🇰 Sri Lanka

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Every office has a generational dispute, but few have measured it as precisely as Bangladesh. In September 2024 the Centre for Policy Dialogue, working with FES Bangladesh, surveyed 500 young people. Most of the jobseekers expected a forty-hour week. Most of the young people already employed reported working more than that. Sri Lanka, a country with a very different economy and an equally unhappy conversation about its youngest workers, has produced fewer numbers and more opinions. If you are weighing a posting in either, the question is not whether the generations disagree. It is who is going to win, and what you will be doing on the weekend while they decide.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask at the offer stage what the real working day is, since the standard day moved from eight hours to nine | Assume the written contract describes the hours people actually keep |
| Get overtime and benefits terms in writing | Expect overtime pay by default, because most employed respondents in the 2024 survey reported none |
| Show deference to seniors in meetings, then raise disagreements privately | Open with "flat hierarchy" talk in front of a senior manager |
| Attend the farewells and cultural programmes, even when they are announced late | Treat them as optional extras in your first year |
| Ask what the promotion criteria are, and ask more than once | Wait for feedback to arrive unprompted |
| Keep religion, politics and personal health out of office conversation | Bring your full online self to the workplace |

### Sri Lanka

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Establish whether the employer is private or public sector, because the working rhythms differ sharply | Assume one national office culture |
| Ask about flexible and hybrid arrangements explicitly | Assume that a hybrid policy on paper operates in practice |
| Offer ideas to senior colleagues as questions, with evidence | Present a new method as a correction of an older one |
| Find out how promotions actually happen in the organisation | Assume merit is the only input |
| Accept in-person contact with older colleagues as part of the job | Rely only on chat messages for people who prefer a conversation |
| Plan for the possibility that mid-career colleagues are interviewing abroad | Build a team that depends on one irreplaceable person |

## Bangladesh: The Gap Has a Commute

The most useful document on Bangladesh's generational friction is the 2024 CPD and FES study, because it compares expectation with experience. Among 250 jobseekers, 144 expected to be paid overtime. Among 250 young employees, 57 reported receiving it. Flexible hours were expected by 166 jobseekers and reported by 99 employees. Health insurance was expected by 145 and reported by 75. Occupational safety, a dull phrase for not being injured, was expected by 141 and reported by 42. The study measures what the young expect, not what their managers expect, so it describes one side of the argument. It is nonetheless a precise description of that side.

The surrounding labour market explains why the young accept the gap. The Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics Labour Force Survey 2023, as reported by The Business Standard in December 2024, found youth unemployment of 7.2 percent and a NEET rate, meaning young people neither working nor studying, of 18.9 percent, rising to 22.1 percent for young women. About 31.5 percent of unemployed youth held tertiary degrees. The Daily Star reported in April 2024, citing the International Labour Organization, that about half of Bangladeshi workers' hours exceed the legal limit, and that the standard workday had moved from eight hours to nine with little public debate. Employers who can fill a vacancy by lunchtime have limited reason to ask what Gen Z wants.

The older generation's expectations are inherited rather than surveyed. Hofstede's scores for Bangladesh, in a 2018 Prothom Alo reproduction of the 2010 data, put power distance at 80 and individualism at 20. Those figures are old, and the economy has changed, but they fit the pattern that younger staff describe. The Financial Express reported in May 2026 that the workplace culture young Bangladeshis complain about is instruction-heavy, short on context for junior staff, slow with feedback and vague on promotion criteria. Fahmida Khatun of the CPD told the same piece that people leave for higher pay, mobility, institutional trust and personal security, which is a polite way of saying the brochure was optimistic.

The friction is also social rather than purely contractual. The Business Standard reported in July 2025 that many firms now run etiquette workshops for entry-level hires, covering email writing, eye contact and asking for clarification, on the theory that pandemic-era graduates missed the in-person apprenticeship. The same piece noted that younger staff are pressing for less rigid hierarchy and fewer late-night messages, and suggested older generations might also reconsider some inherited traditions. A Daily Star diary column describes the other side of the culture: mandatory cultural programmes rehearsed over weekends, and farewell events that arrive every other week and are not to be complained about. The older generation, in other words, has not merely hired the young. It has scheduled them.

## Sri Lanka: The Gap Is Real, the Data Is Not

Sri Lanka's version of the argument is conducted largely in columns, panels and reader comments. This is a limitation, and it should be stated plainly: there is little published quantitative work on generational workplace differences in the country. The closest hard figures come from ACCA's Global Talent Trends Survey 2025, discussed at an Echelon roundtable in September 2025. It found that 69 percent of Sri Lankan respondents work on site full-time while 85 percent would prefer a hybrid arrangement, a gap the organisers called wider than the global one. The same roundtable cautioned against designing policy around Gen Z alone, since life stage and job function often matter more than birth year. This is a sensible warning that almost nobody follows.

What the opinion pieces describe is recognisable. The Daily Mirror reported in June 2024 that younger employees are told to be brave and seek opportunities, then dismissed as lacking experience. A December 2024 Daily Mirror piece on local work culture said the private sector rewards staying late and taking work home, while the public sector is seen as slower, with pay and promotion tied to tenure. Daily FT, covering a Great Place To Work CXO forum in October 2025, reported a theme of shared values across generations and named 15 organisations as Best Workplaces for Young Talent, meaning employees under 35. A forum about multigenerational leadership that produces an award for the young is a fair summary of the situation.

Academic commentary in the Sunday Observer in June 2025 described the direction of travel: management shifting from hierarchical models towards flexible, feedback-driven ones, with younger staff favouring digital channels and older employees preferring direct conversation. It cited no local survey, which tells you how settled the conclusion is not.

The more consequential generational effect is departure. A Daily Mirror column in February 2026 argued that Sri Lanka is losing mid-career doctors, engineers, accountants and IT professionals because of low pay, limited progression and promotions influenced by political loyalty. It gave no headcount. The Central Bank, as reported by Daily FT in October 2025, recorded 143,037 departures for foreign employment in the first half of 2025, down 5 percent on the year. That figure covers all occupations, so it cannot be read as a measure of professional emigration. It is, however, the generational disagreement expressed in its most final form.

## The Reckoning

Bangladesh has a clearer dataset and a clearer villain. The gap there is between promised and delivered conditions, and the young are measuring it. The older generation is less a rival ideology than a management style: instruction-heavy, hierarchical and unaccustomed to explaining itself. Sri Lanka has a more diffuse contest. The public and private sectors run on different clocks, hybrid work is wanted more than it is offered, and the most capable people have a credible option of leaving. A Bangladeshi graduate who is unhappy has a nine-hour day to complain about. A Sri Lankan professional who is unhappy has a visa application.

For a newcomer, the practical difference is who adjusts. In Bangladesh, expect the organisation to be the fixed point and your expectations to be the variable, at least until the labour market tightens. In Sri Lanka, expect more negotiation, particularly in private firms that have already seen colleagues leave and would rather not see more. In both, the safest assumption is that the stated policy and the lived norm differ, and that the younger staff know precisely by how much.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Local press, paraphrased experience: A young employee at a Bangladeshi software-as-a-service start-up described unlimited leave and generous remote days being withdrawn with the explanation that staff had abused them, and being told to accept it, while farewell events consumed a couple of working hours every other week.*

> *Local press, paraphrased experience: A software engineer in Dhaka said his company had a toxic work culture, that he regularly worked past six and sometimes on weekends, and that remote days were the only thing sparing him a two-hour commute.*

> *Local press, paraphrased experience: A recent graduate at a Dhaka technology firm said she never intended to be unprofessional, but that nobody had told her which of her habits were not acceptable.*

> *Local press, paraphrased experience: A former Sri Lankan public-sector worker told Daily Mirror readers the sector was slow and that staff resisted ideas from younger colleagues, though she had no wish to adopt private-sector overwork either.*

> *Local press, paraphrased experience: A Sri Lankan reader said a previous employer promoted work-life balance on paper while sending after-hours emails and expecting late nights, and that the experience ended in a breakdown.*

## Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two, ask the question the surveys keep asking: what do the hours, overtime and promotion rules look like for someone under thirty-five in this particular company? In Bangladesh the answer is probably documented somewhere and probably not in your favour. In Sri Lanka it will depend on the sector, and the honest answer may be that nobody has measured it.

Neither country has a generation gap problem so much as a communication one: the young are asking for specifics and the old are answering with tradition. Whichever you choose, get the specifics in writing. It will not end the argument, but it will at least establish which side is quoting the contract.
