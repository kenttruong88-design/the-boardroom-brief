# The Family Business and the Friendless 42%: Pakistan vs. UK

**🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Pakistan, your boss may call you "brother" within the first month and genuinely mean a version of it, right up until the moment you ask for a raise. In the UK, your boss will almost certainly never call you anything of the kind, and two in five of your eventual colleagues won't consider you a friend even after years at the same desk. Neither country is lying about its warmth. They're just measuring it on completely different instruments.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept an invitation to a colleague's home for a meal — declining repeatedly reads as rejection | Take "we are like family" literally when it's time to negotiate pay |
| Ask after a colleague's parents and extended family by name over time | Treat a soft "we'll see" or "we'll try" as a genuine maybe — it usually means no |
| Let senior colleagues extend hospitality to you without rushing to repay it immediately | Push a workplace friendship into formal, written terms — it can read as cold |
| Build relationships slowly through repeated small gestures rather than one big one | Assume closeness with a Pakistani colleague translates automatically to closeness with an Indian one, or vice versa — it depends entirely on the specific people |
| Keep a private, careful read on which "family" language is sincere and which is managing you | Publicly call out a boss's family framing as manipulative, however justified it feels |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Suggest a specific, low-stakes activity — a pub drink, not a home visit — to build a friendship | Invite a new colleague to your home early; many will feel it's premature |
| Expect real friendship to form slowly, often through repeated small-team proximity | Assume a friendly, chatty colleague is automatically a close friend — most aren't |
| Decline optional socialising occasionally without over-explaining | Feel obligated to attend every after-work drink — 1 in 5 workers already feel pressured and resent it |
| Treat "we should get a drink sometime" as a genuine future possibility, not an immediate booking | Push for a firm plan on the spot; let it happen at its own pace |
| Accept that most colleagues will remain warm acquaintances rather than close friends, and that this is normal | Read the lack of a deep friendship as personal rejection — the data says it's the norm, not you |

### Pakistan: Family Language, Load-Bearing and Otherwise

Hofstede Insights scores Pakistan at just 14 on individualism, among the most collectivist readings in the world, and a vanishingly rare 0 on indulgence — a profile that predicts exactly the kind of workplace where personal and professional relationships blur completely, often without anyone treating that as unusual. The guidance given to foreign businesspeople entering Pakistan consistently flags indirect communication as the real skill to learn: a soft "we'll see" or "we'll try" is a no dressed as a maybe, and reading it correctly matters more than any contract clause.

Where this collectivism gets complicated is in how deliberately it's sometimes deployed. A widely circulated account — a South Asian tech worker's thread about a boss insisting "we are like brothers" right after signing a contract, and a cousin's employer claiming to value him "more than a brother" while underpaying him for nearly a decade — captures a pattern familiar across Pakistani and Indian offices alike: family language used sincerely by some employers and strategically by others, specifically to discourage the kind of hard boundary-setting that a purely transactional relationship would invite. The uncomfortable skill for anyone working in this environment is learning to enjoy the genuine warmth on offer while staying alert to the moments it's being used as leverage instead.

The payoff for getting this right is real, not merely theoretical. Workplace guidance aimed at expats repeatedly stresses that relationship-building in Pakistan happens through repetition rather than a single grand gesture — accepting a meal invitation, asking after a colleague's parents by name over successive conversations, letting hospitality flow one direction for a while before quietly reciprocating. None of this is written down anywhere a new hire would find it, which is exactly why foreign managers who skip it tend to find their Pakistani teams correct but never quite warm, while the ones who invest in it describe a loyalty that looks, from the outside, genuinely familial rather than performed.

### UK: Warm, Reserved, and Statistically Lonelier Than It Looks

Hofstede puts the UK at 89 on individualism, near the global ceiling, and the workplace-friendship data bears that out starkly. A Relate study of more than 5,000 people found 42% of UK workers don't count a single workmate as a close friend, even though 58% have at least one — meaning the office is, for a large minority, a place of pleasant, functional acquaintance rather than anything deeper. A separate Wildgoose survey of over a thousand UK companies found the stakes of this aren't trivial: workplace loneliness is estimated to cost UK employers £2.5 billion a year, and severely lonely individuals an estimated £9,900 each in lost wellbeing and productivity, while 1 in 20 people who started a job remotely found it specifically hard to build any colleague friendships at all.

What's genuinely counterintuitive is that the socialising which does happen is experienced as obligation almost as often as pleasure. A Nigel Frank survey found 22% of UK workers feel actively pressured to socialise with colleagues outside work, rising to 29% in London specifically, and over half of those who feel that pressure are women. The preferred format, when people do want to connect, skews deliberately low-commitment: a monthly after-work drink was the top choice in the Wildgoose data, well ahead of anything resembling the home-hosted hospitality that's assumed by default in Pakistan.

The gender split in that pressure data is worth sitting with. Women reported feeling pressured to socialise at nearly double the rate of men in some regional breakdowns, and the same survey found parents felt no less pressure than people without children — undercutting the easy assumption that it's simply early-career workers with fewer outside commitments who feel obligated to show up. The UK's famous workplace reserve, in other words, coexists with a surprisingly unevenly distributed social tax on the people least likely to say so out loud.

### The Reckoning

The real gap isn't warmth versus coldness — it's where the relationship is allowed to start. In Pakistan, the default assumption is that a work relationship is already, at least partly, a personal one, and the labor is in figuring out which parts of that assumption are genuine and which are being managed. In the UK, the default assumption is the reverse: a work relationship starts as strictly professional, and anything deeper has to be built deliberately, slowly, and usually over a pint rather than a home-cooked meal. A Pakistani employee transferred to London can read the UK's reserve as coldness before they understand it's simply a different, slower on-ramp. A British employee transferred to Lahore can find the instant intimacy of "family" language either moving or faintly suspicious, often both at once, and reasonably so.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *teamblind.com — In a thread about South Asian coworker dynamics, one Pakistani contributor at a major consultancy wrote plainly, "I feel that a lot of Indians don't want to talk to me," worried it might affect how they were perceived for promotion, while adding that they had no complaints at all about their current, more welcoming team.*

> *teamblind.com — Another contributor, Indian, described keeping work friendships with Pakistani colleagues carefully apolitical — avoiding "politics, religion, country, and cricket" on the clock — while those same friendships opened up entirely outside the office, where the same topics became jokes between friends rather than risks.*

> *brandsynario.com — A widely shared account described a boss insisting "we are like brothers" immediately after a contract was signed, which the employee recognized instantly as a cue to set firm boundaries rather than a genuine offer — noting that if the claim were sincere, the obvious next step would be a partnership stake, not just warmer language.*

> *boards.straightdope.com — One forum member described deliberately keeping work and social life separate, attending just enough optional events to avoid seeming uncooperative, while another, in the same thread, called their coworkers "the best part of my job" — illustrating how wide the range of normal actually is within a single culture, UK included.*

> *Advice worth having before you land: in Pakistan, let hospitality happen on the host's terms and repay it slowly rather than immediately — rushing to settle the social debt can read as transactional. In the UK, don't mistake the pub invitation for an immediate, deep friendship; treat it as the first of many small, low-stakes steps.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two for the relationships you'll actually build, the honest framing is this: Pakistan will hand you something that feels like family faster than almost anywhere else, and you'll need real discernment to tell which parts of it are genuine. The UK will hand you almost nothing for months, and that absence isn't a verdict on you — it's just the default setting for 42% of everyone else's coworkers too. The thing I'd tell a friend over a drink, which in London is also the entire plan: go slow in both places, just for opposite reasons.
