# Uruguay Spends the First 20 Minutes of Every Meeting Talking About Football. Chile Spends Zero.

**🇺🇾 Uruguay · 🇨🇱 Chile**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

One tech worker who relocated from Russia to Uruguay describes his first team meetings with something close to disbelief: "we spent the first 20 minutes talking about football and the weather, and only discussed work in the last 10 minutes" — and liked it enough to start exporting the habit to his US-based colleagues, telling them to "calm down and take things slowly." A few thousand kilometers north, a Blind poster praising Chilean work-life balance still describes an office culture where, per multiple business guides, contradicting a superior is considered outright discourteous. Two neighboring Southern Cone economies, two genuinely different answers to who gets to speak first in a meeting — and how long before anyone gets around to the agenda.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇾 Uruguay

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect meetings to open with genuine, unhurried small talk — football and weather aren't filler, they're the warm-up | Don't rush straight to the agenda; it can read as brusque rather than efficient |
| Accept that your manager may weigh in on non-work matters — Uruguayan management leans paternalistic | Don't treat that involvement as overreach; it's a normal extension of a relationship-based hierarchy |
| Let decisions flow upward and be patient — approvals often reflect a leader's personal judgment, not a pure logic chain | Don't offer unsolicited input in a group setting unless you've been explicitly invited to weigh in |
| Build trust incrementally through repeated, low-key interactions | Don't apply high-pressure persuasion tactics; tactful repetition works better than forceful argument |
| Watch how your Uruguayan colleagues adapt pace with international teams — many actively push back against being rushed | Don't assume the relaxed tempo signals low ambition or low output; it's a cultural style, not a performance indicator |

#### 🇨🇱 Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use formal titles and surnames until explicitly invited to use first names | Don't skip the "Estimado/Estimada" formality in written communication, especially early in a relationship |
| Let senior staff lead meetings and speak first | Don't contradict a superior in front of the group; it's considered genuinely discourteous, not just blunt |
| Expect instructions to be detailed and directive — managers specify both the task and how they expect it performed | Don't expect consensus-building meetings; Chilean management style is notably autocratic by regional and global comparison |
| Invest in relationships — who you know shapes outcomes as much as formal competence | Don't assume junior colleagues in a meeting are there to contribute; many attend purely to observe |
| Show small, visible deference — a nod, a verbal acknowledgment — toward leadership in the room | Don't expect Chile's famous work-life balance reputation to extend to how decisions get made; the two are separate axes |

### Uruguay: Paternalistic, But Unhurried

Uruguayan management runs on a paradox worth understanding precisely: genuinely paternalistic hierarchy, delivered at a genuinely relaxed pace. Commisceo Global's management guide describes "clearly defined roles" with supervisors selected for superior experience and competence, and relationships with employees that "usually overlap into personal areas" — a boss who asks about your family isn't overstepping, that's simply what Uruguayan management looks like. Consulting subordinates on major decisions can even undermine a manager's credibility, per the same guide, which sounds rigid on paper.

What the paper doesn't capture is tempo. One tech worker's own account of relocating from a fast, hierarchical Russian work culture to a Uruguayan one describes meetings built almost entirely around relationship-maintenance before business: "we spent the first 20 minutes talking about football and the weather, and only discussed work in the last 10 minutes." He didn't just adapt to it — he started actively defending it to his American colleagues, telling them the team needed to "calm down and take things slowly." Decision-making does flow upward, and patience with that process matters, per Commisceo's guide, precisely because choices "may reflect personal preferences of leaders rather than pure logic" — trust-building, not argument-winning, is what actually moves things forward.

### Chile: Directive, Efficient, and Notably Autocratic

Chile's hierarchy is less ambiguous about itself. Commisceo Global's management guide doesn't hedge: Chilean managers are "notably autocratic," with "managers tell subordinates what they want to be done and how they expect them to perform the task," and employees "generally follow instructions without challenge, as contradicting someone of higher status is considered discourteous." BizLatin Hub's etiquette guide adds the formal texture: proper titles and surnames, "Estimado"/"Estimada" in written correspondence, first names only once explicitly invited — a slower social thaw than Uruguay's meeting-opener chitchat, layered onto a faster, more directive decision process once business actually starts.

Junior staff have a defined, limited role in this system: they "frequently attend meetings for observation purposes only, rarely participating in decision-making," per BizLatin Hub, which can read as exclusion to a newcomer used to flatter structures but functions, within the system, as an apprenticeship model rather than a snub. And crucially, Chile's international reputation for strong work-life balance — echoed directly in a Blind thread praising Santiago's "excellent WLB" and "working to live, not living to work" ethos — coexists with, rather than contradicts, this directive hierarchy. Chileans can clock out on time and still, within the office, defer sharply to whoever outranks them.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely useful distinction here isn't "which country has more hierarchy" — both have real, respected hierarchy by international standards. It's where each culture spends its social capital. Uruguay spends it on relational warmth before the agenda: the football chat isn't wasted time, it's the mechanism that makes the eventual paternalistic decision-making feel earned rather than imposed. Chile spends it on directive clarity once the meeting starts, then recoups the relational investment somewhere else entirely — in the country's famous after-hours boundary protection. Hofstede's power-distance scores put both countries meaningfully above Northern European norms, consistent with both cultures' comfort with top-down authority — but the qualitative texture, warmth-first versus efficiency-first, is a distinction no single number captures.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Guru'Guay, Montevideo job-market blog (first-person account from Zuhair, a tech worker who relocated from Russia)* — Describing his first team meetings in Uruguay: "we spent the first 20 minutes talking about football and the weather, and only discussed work in the last 10 minutes." He later found himself exporting the habit to his US-based colleagues, telling them "we need to calm down and take things slowly" — a rare case of an expat actively converting his new employer's team to the local pace rather than the other way around.

> *Blind (teamblind.com), "Chile is a best place to work" thread* — One commenter's blunt endorsement of Chilean work culture: "excellent WLB," describing the overall vibe as "super chill" and summarizing the philosophy as "working to live, not living to work" — a reminder that Chile's formal, directive office hierarchy and its reputation for protected personal time are two entirely separate systems, not a contradiction.

> *Commisceo Global, Chile management guide (practitioner cross-cultural training material)* — The guide's single most useful line for a newcomer walking into a Chilean office: "contradicting someone of higher status is considered discourteous" — a plain statement that disagreement, even well-intentioned, needs a different delivery mechanism than a direct "I don't think that's right" in the room.

> *Commisceo Global, Uruguay management guide, same source family* — The guide notes that Uruguayan managers who want buy-in on a point should "tactfully repeat important points" rather than push hard for immediate agreement — persuasion through patient repetition, not pressure, which tracks closely with the unhurried meeting-opener culture described above.

### Conclusion

If you're heading to Uruguay, budget real time for the small talk — it isn't a detour from the meeting, it's a prerequisite for the paternalistic trust that actually gets decisions made, and rushing past it will cost you more time than it saves. If you're heading to Chile, hold your disagreement until you understand the room: directive instructions from above aren't an invitation to debate them openly, and the country's genuinely excellent work-life balance is a separate, later reward for accepting that during business hours, the hierarchy runs one direction. The honest version, over a drink: in Montevideo, talk about football first; in Santiago, save your opinion for after the meeting — and either way, don't mistake a relaxed office for a flat one.
