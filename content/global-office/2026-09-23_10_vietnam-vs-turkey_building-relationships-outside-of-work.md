# Coffee, Phở, and Tulip Glasses: Building Friendships Outside Work in Vietnam and Turkey

**🇻🇳 Vietnam · 🇹🇷 Turkey**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

![Vietnam vs Turkey — Building relationships outside of work](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/global-office/2026-09-23-10-vietnam-vs-turkey-building-relationships-outside-of-w-hero)
*Photo: Kampus Production via Pexels*

In Hanoi, a colleague inviting you for phở after work isn't a small gesture — it's a specific, legible signal that the relationship is deepening, understood by both parties the same way. In Istanbul, being offered a small tulip-shaped glass of black tea during a meeting isn't optional politeness — declining it, per multiple business etiquette guides, can genuinely register as a minor social misstep. Both cultures treat outside-work relationship-building as functionally inseparable from doing business well. The specific rituals — a coffee shop table in Vietnam, a tea glass in Turkey — differ, but the underlying rule is identical: skip this, and you'll be transacting with people who never quite trust you.

| Use Vietnam's genuine café culture actively — informal coffee-shop meetups are a real venue for introductions, information exchange, and eventual deal-making, not just socializing | Treat business as separable from personal relationship — in Vietnam's business culture, business is frequently understood as an extension of personal relationships, not a parallel track |

### Vietnam

Vietnamese friendship and relationship-building operate on a logic of accumulation rather than declaration. According to multiple culture and business guides, friendship here "grows through repeated shared time — classes, workplace cohorts, café meetups, hobby groups, and long chat threads — more than through one dramatic confession," a framing that stands in useful contrast to more instant-rapport cultures. Vietnam's genuinely distinctive café culture functions as real social infrastructure: coffee outlets are documented as a cornerstone of both social life and business culture, spaces where "information is exchanged, introductions are made, friendships are deepened and ultimately deals are made" in informal settings that carry real functional weight, not just ambiance.

Hofstede's data characterizes Vietnam as strongly collectivist, with relatively high power distance reflecting real deference to hierarchy and authority — yet the friendship-building process itself is notably peer-driven and reciprocal: an invitation to coffee, phở, or a cheap dinner is described across guides as a specific, recognized signal of growing trust, one that both parties are expected to understand and eventually reciprocate. Vietnamese business culture treats this outside-work relationship-building as inseparable from commercial trust — multiple guides describe business there as, functionally, "an extension of personal relationships," meaning the coffee invitation isn't adjacent to the deal, it's part of how the deal becomes possible at all.

### Turkey

Turkish relationship-building runs through a different but equally load-bearing ritual: hospitality, and specifically tea. Served in small tulip-shaped glasses at meetings, during negotiations, and in casual office interactions alike, tea functions, per multiple business culture guides, as "the lubricant of Turkish professional life" — a constant, low-key social ritual that punctuates nearly every kind of interaction. Turkish business culture guides consistently describe an initial period of real formality — first meetings are frequently expected to focus mostly on getting acquainted rather than substance — that gradually relaxes as personal rapport genuinely develops, a sequencing where the relationship precedes, rather than follows, real business comfort.

Hospitality carries real social weight beyond politeness: declining an invitation to dine with a business associate is described in etiquette guides as genuinely impolite, a stronger social expectation than the more optional "maybe next time" register common in many Western business cultures. Hofstede scores Turkey moderately collectivist (37 on individualism, indicating a collectivist orientation) with in-group loyalty, particularly to family, carrying real cultural weight — a value system that extends outward to colleagues and business associates through the deliberate, unhurried relationship-building the culture expects before real trust and business comfort take hold.

### The Reckoning

The genuine parallel between Vietnam and Turkey is how completely both reject the idea that business relationships and personal relationships can run on separate tracks — in both cultures, per the guides consulted, they are functionally the same track, and treating them as separable is itself a foreigner's mistake that gets noticed. The divergence is in ritual specificity and pacing: Vietnamese relationship-building is peer-paced and accumulative, built through repeated small gestures (the coffee, the phở, the café meetup) that compound gradually and can be initiated by either party. Turkish relationship-building is more front-loaded and hospitality-driven — the tea, the meal invitation, the dedicated first-meeting "getting to know you" time — with a clearer initial threshold (accepting the tea, accepting the meal) that then unlocks a more comfortable, formality-reduced ongoing relationship.

The practical risk for a newcomer differs accordingly. In Vietnam, the risk is under-investing in repetition — showing up once for coffee and assuming that's sufficient, when the relationship actually needs sustained, ongoing small gestures over real time. In Turkey, the risk is under-investing in the front door — rushing past the tea, the small talk, the getting-to-know-you first meeting, and trying to get to business too fast, which can read as a genuine misstep rather than efficient.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone asking generally how to befriend colleagues received an answer specifically shaped by Vietnamese experience: consistency in small shared activities — the same coffee spot, the same lunch group — mattered far more than any single big social gesture in actually being accepted as a genuine friend rather than just a workplace acquaintance.*

> *LinkedIn (a contributor writing "What makes a friendship in Vietnam?") described friendship there as something that reveals itself through accumulated small actions over time rather than an explicit declaration, noting that foreign colleagues sometimes mistakenly wait for a clear verbal signal that Vietnamese friendship simply doesn't work by.*

> *Expat.com forum (Istanbul, Türkiye forum, "How to make friends in Turkey" thread) — A newcomer was advised by longer-term expats that accepting hospitality — a tea, a meal — early and often was the single most effective way to move a professional relationship toward a genuine friendship, more so than any particular topic of conversation.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a question about the origin of Turkish tea culture and its social role described how declining a poured tea, even absentmindedly, was noticed by colleagues in a way that surprised them initially, since in their home culture a beverage offer carried little social weight by comparison.*

> *Yabangee (an Istanbul-focused expat lifestyle blog, "How to Make Friends as a Foreigner in Istanbul") described the shift in a workplace relationship after a colleague was first invited to a family member's home for a meal, noting that professional interactions afterward became noticeably warmer and more flexible — a threshold the writer hadn't anticipated as a discrete, identifiable moment.*

### Conclusion

If you're relocating into either culture, the practical takeaway is to stop treating "networking" and "making friends" as different activities — in both Vietnam and Turkey, they're functionally the same task, pursued through different specific rituals. In Vietnam, show up consistently for the small, repeated gestures — the coffee, the phở, the café meetup — and let trust compound. In Turkey, accept the tea, accept the meal, and give the first meeting the getting-to-know-you time it's expected to take before pushing toward business. Priya's line for a friend moving to either place: don't wait for someone to tell you the relationship has started — in both countries, it starts the moment you say yes to the invitation, so say yes.
