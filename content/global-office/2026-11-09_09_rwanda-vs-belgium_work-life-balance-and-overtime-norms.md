# Rwanda Keeps Changing the Clock-In Time. Belgium Keeps Shortening the Week.

🇷🇼 Rwanda · 🇧🇪 Belgium

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Rwanda's government has spent the past few years adjusting when the workday officially starts, provoking enough public grumbling that The New Times ran a piece asking, not entirely rhetorically, "do we have a work-life balance?" Belgium's government, facing a comparable productivity conversation, passed a Labour Deal in 2022 granting workers the right to request compressing their hours into a four-day week and, separately, the right to simply not answer the boss's emails after hours — a right that made international headlines and that Belgian civil servants still cite with evident satisfaction. One country is renegotiating the start time. The other renegotiated the entire premise.

## Rwanda: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect the standard workweek to run close to the legal 45-hour maximum in many sectors | Assume "end of day" has a hard, universally respected stop time |
| Treat punctuality and visible early arrival as a genuine marker of seriousness | Be surprised when government office hours policy changes again with little notice |
| Expect overtime to be governed by statute, but inconsistently enforced in smaller firms | Assume private-sector norms match the more structured public-sector rules |
| Respect that long hours are often read as a sign of national economic ambition, not dysfunction | Dismiss local complaints about long hours as simply cultural — they're an active public debate |
| Build in buffer time for meetings that start punctually but run long | Expect a four-day week, compressed hours, or similar flexibility arrangements |

## Belgium: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Know that the right to disconnect outside working hours is now a genuine legal protection | Expect it to be honored identically across every employer and sector without exception |
| Consider requesting a compressed four-day week if your role and employer qualify | Assume the four-day option means a shorter total workweek — the hours are usually just rearranged |
| Expect regional variation — Flanders, Wallonia, and Brussels each carry distinct workplace norms | Treat Belgian work culture as a single, uniform national standard |
| Respect strict boundaries around lunch breaks and after-hours contact | Call or email a Belgian colleague about work matters late in the evening without good reason |
| Expect strong union representation to shape how overtime disputes get resolved | Assume individual negotiation, rather than collective agreement, is how hours get settled |

## Rwanda: Ambition, Measured in Hours

Rwanda's work-life balance conversation is unusually public and unusually recent. The New Times has reported on repeated adjustments to official government working hours in recent years, changes defended by officials as productivity measures and criticized by others — including, memorably, Kenyan professionals working in Rwanda who welcomed a subsequent reduction in hours, according to Kenyans.co.ke's regional reporting — as simply exhausting. Rivermate's and Playroll's summaries of Rwandan labour law put the standard workweek near 45 hours, with overtime technically regulated but, by most accounts, loosely enforced outside large formal employers and the public sector.

What underlies this is a national narrative that treats long hours as evidence of seriousness about development, not as a problem to be solved. The New Times' own opinion pages have run pieces explicitly questioning whether this bargain is sustainable, which is itself notable — a public debate about overtime culture happening in a country whose growth story is frequently told, internationally, as an uncomplicated success. Hofstede's framework doesn't carry an official Rwanda score, but comparative work on East African labour markets consistently describes a high-ambition, achievement-oriented culture where visible effort is itself a credential, a pattern that tracks with what both local commentary and expat accounts describe on the ground.

The policy churn itself tells its own story. When The New Times reported "mixed reactions" to adjusted working hours, the complaints weren't really about the specific start time changing — they were about the sense that the boundary itself is still provisional, subject to revision from above with little advance warning. Kenyans.co.ke's reporting on regional professionals welcoming a subsequent reduction captures the flip side: when the government does loosen the policy, workers from neighboring countries notice immediately, because the hours had genuinely been a competitive disadvantage of working in Kigali versus comparable regional hubs.

## Belgium: Legislating the Boundary

Belgium took the opposite approach: rather than debating hours informally, it wrote the boundary into law. The 2022 Labour Deal, detailed in Lockton's, Crowell's, and DLA Piper's client coverage, gave workers the right to request a four-day compressed week — working the same total hours over fewer days — and gave many employees, federal civil servants first, a formal right to disconnect from work communications outside scheduled hours. The right-to-disconnect provision in particular drew global attention precisely because it treated something most cultures leave implicit — "don't email people at 9pm" — as something worth codifying.

The caveat Belgian commentary consistently raises is that compression isn't reduction. A four-day week under the Labour Deal typically means the same weekly hours squeezed into fewer, longer days, not a genuine cut in total workload — a distinction some early online reactions, including threads on the policy when it passed, were quick to point out with a mix of enthusiasm and skepticism. Hofstede's individualism score for Belgium sits solidly in Western Europe's higher range, consistent with a culture where personal time is treated as a right to be formally protected rather than something negotiated case-by-case with a sympathetic manager.

There's also a federal structure wrinkle worth knowing before you sign anything. Belgium's employment rules are shaped at both federal and sometimes regional level, and union density and collective-bargaining coverage vary by sector and by whether you're in Flanders, Wallonia, or Brussels — which means the right to disconnect that made international headlines in 2022 landed first and most firmly in the public sector, with private employers adopting it at uneven speed depending on their own sectoral agreement. The law exists nationally; how quickly you personally feel its effects depends heavily on which collective agreement actually covers your contract.

## The Reckoning

The contrast here isn't really about hours worked — it's about whose job it is to protect the boundary. In Rwanda, that boundary is informal, contested, and currently being argued over in op-eds and government policy memos; the worker's main lever is cultural goodwill and, increasingly, public commentary pressuring institutions to reconsider. In Belgium, the boundary is statutory, enforceable, and already won; the worker's main lever is simply knowing the law and invoking it.

Neither system has fully solved the underlying tension between ambition and rest. Rwanda's long-hours culture is explicitly tied to a national development narrative that makes "slow down" a genuinely harder sell politically than it would be elsewhere. Belgium's legal protections are real, but regional and sectoral variation means the on-the-ground experience of "the law" differs considerably depending on where, and for whom, you work.

It's worth noting, too, that both countries are visibly mid-negotiation rather than settled. Rwanda is still litigating the question in public op-eds and policy adjustments; Belgium is still watching how unevenly its 2022 reforms actually land sector by sector. Anyone relocating into either system is arriving partway through an argument, not after it's been resolved.

> *ExpatExchange — paraphrased experience: responses on what expats find most challenging in Rwanda frequently mention adjusting to long, sometimes unpredictable office hours that don't map cleanly onto the stated legal workweek.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Kigali — paraphrased experience: a posted account describes a demanding but genuinely motivated local office culture, where staying late is read less as dysfunction and more as visible commitment to shared goals.*

> *The New Times, opinion section — paraphrased experience: a staff opinion piece questioning whether Rwanda "has" a work-life balance argues that the national emphasis on productivity has outpaced any serious public conversation about rest, not because nobody wants one, but because raising it can read as insufficiently ambitious.*

> *Expat.com Rwanda forum — paraphrased experience: a thread on local usages and practices describes punctuality and visible early arrival as genuinely important markers of professionalism, more so than in several other African markets discussed on the same forum.*

## Conclusion

If you're weighing a move, the practical calculus differs by direction. Moving into Rwanda means accepting that the boundary around your time is still being negotiated nationally, not just at your own desk, and that visible effort carries real social currency you'll be expected to display. Moving into Belgium means doing your homework on which specific protections — the four-day option, the right to disconnect — actually apply to your sector and employer, because the law is real but not uniformly applied. One country is asking whether it has a work-life balance. The other has one, mostly, written down — and is still finding out, sector by sector, how firmly the ink has dried.
