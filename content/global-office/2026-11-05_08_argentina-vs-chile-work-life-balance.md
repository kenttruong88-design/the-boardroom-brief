# Chile Just Legislated Its Way to a Shorter Week. Argentina Is Still Negotiating What Time the Day Starts.

🇦🇷 Argentina · 🇨🇱 Chile

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Chile passed a law in 2023 cutting its legal workweek from 45 hours to 40, becoming only the second country in Latin America, after Ecuador, to hit the standard the International Labour Organization recommends. Argentina's legal cap, unmoved, sits at 48 hours — among the highest in the world. And yet neighbors who share a border, a language, and a continent's worth of asado culture have ended up with starkly different relationships to their own time off, in ways the law alone doesn't explain.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇷 Argentina

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect office hours to genuinely start later than you're used to, especially outside factory or production roles | Schedule an early-morning meeting in Buenos Aires assuming colleagues will be sharp and present by 9am |
| Ask explicitly about overtime surcharge rates (legally 50 percent extra on a normal day, 100 percent on a rest day or holiday) | Assume overtime pay works the same way it does in your home country without checking local labor law |
| Know that a telework law formally recognizes a right to digital disconnection, even though enforcement varies | Assume that right is automatically enforced just because it exists in law |
| Treat relationship-building — handshakes, small talk about family or sport — as a genuine prerequisite to business, not a delay before it | Push straight into an agenda item in a first meeting without any social warm-up |
| Expect brutal rush-hour commutes to be a real, structural cause of lateness, not a personal failing | Read a colleague's consistent lateness as laziness rather than a predictable commuting reality |

#### 🇨🇱 Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect the legal workweek to keep shrinking on a set legislative schedule (44, then 42, then 40 hours by law) | Assume the current legal cap is permanent or final — it's a phased reduction already written into law |
| Protect Sunday as a near-sacred family day — it's a strong, widely shared cultural boundary | Schedule work obligations on a Sunday assuming it's treated like any other day off |
| Verify any job contract is actually honored before signing or paying anything, especially in lower-wage sectors | Assume a written contract will automatically be respected without independent verification |
| Expect a comparatively more hierarchical, harder-to-read workplace structure as a newcomer | Assume Chilean workplace culture is as flat or quickly legible as some neighboring countries |
| Expect flexible and remote-work arrangements to be increasingly common and often open-ended | Assume remote work in Chile is a strictly temporary, pandemic-era arrangement everywhere |

---

Argentina's legal workweek cap of 48 hours is, per fact-checking outlet Chequeado's analysis of ILO data, among the highest legal ceilings in the world, grouped with Bolivia, Paraguay, Peru, and Uruguay rather than with more restrictive European norms. Actual hours worked average closer to 38 a week, per ILO figures — but that average masks real inequality: more than half of Argentine employees work over 40 hours regardless, and the gender gap is sharper than the global norm, with women averaging 32 hours against men's 42. A regional Jobint/Bumeran-Zonjobs survey of over five thousand respondents across five Latin American countries, reported by iProfesional, puts a number on the lived frustration: 67 percent of Argentine respondents say their company lacks a genuine work-life balance culture — the worst figure of the five countries surveyed — and only half say they personally achieve balance at all, again the lowest share in the group. Fifty-seven percent describe corporate talk about balance as pure rhetoric with no real measures behind it, and the top cited obstacles are unsurprising: no clear company policy, colleagues doing work outside normal hours, difficulty setting personal limits.

Chile's recent legislative history tells a genuinely different story. The 2023 law cutting the workweek from 45 to 40 hours phases in gradually — 44 hours after the first year, 42 after three, 40 after five — and also, for the first time, legally permits compressed four-day workweeks that were previously disallowed outright. The same regional Bumeran-Zonjobs survey shows the on-the-ground difference this has started to make: only 59 percent of Chilean respondents say their company lacks a good balance culture (still high, but notably better than Argentina's 67), and 65 percent say they personally achieve balance against Argentina's 50. Remote-work adoption backs up the broader shift toward flexibility: a 2020 survey of Chilean companies by the Asociación Chilena de Seguridad found over 95 percent had at least some employees working remotely, with nearly half of surveyed firms moving their entire workforce remote at some point, and two-thirds setting no end date for the arrangement at all — treating it as a durable change rather than an emergency measure.

What the statistics don't fully capture is the texture BAexpats.org forum members have debated at length in a long-running thread on Argentine work ethic: posters describe office start times varying sharply by rank and industry, brutal rush-hour commutes as a genuine structural cause of lateness rather than a personal failing, and a split verdict on individual competence versus organizational dysfunction — individual Argentine professionals are repeatedly praised as highly capable, while distrust, procrastination, and weak planning at the organizational level are blamed for most of the frustration, not laziness. A separate Expat.com guide to Buenos Aires work culture confirms the practical texture: a genuine, unhurried lunch hour eaten later than North American norms, decision-making concentrated at the top in traditional firms, and meetings that routinely start late because relationship-building outranks the clock. Chile's cultural boundary looks different in kind: an ExpatsBlog.com interview with a US expat teaching English in Santiago describes a fragmented, hourly-pay work culture in his specific sector, but one hard, nationwide rule cutting across everything else — "the entire day Sunday is usually spent with the family" — alongside a pointed warning not to sign or pay for anything before verifying a contract will actually be honored.

Both countries also share a regional trait that neither law nor survey fully explains: a deep-seated reliance on the clock running later than visitors expect. Hofstede's shared high score on uncertainty avoidance across the two countries (86 for both) sits oddly next to the loose, relationship-driven scheduling both expat accounts describe, until you read it as a preference for predictable social ritual rather than predictable clock time — the late dinner, the long Sunday, the extended small talk before a meeting, are all, in their own way, highly ritualized and rule-bound, just not bound by the hour hand. For a newcomer used to reading punctuality as the main signal of professionalism, that distinction is the single hardest habit to unlearn in either country, and misreading it as simple disorganization is the single most common mistake expat accounts in both Argentina and Chile describe.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely useful irony is that Chile, often stereotyped within the region as the more buttoned-down, business-first neighbor, has just legally out-flexibilized Argentina, while Argentina, often associated with a more relaxed, late-dinner, late-night sensibility, scores worse on nearly every measured work-life balance metric in the region. Hofstede's own data gives a structural explanation for the difference: Chile's considerably higher power distance (63 versus Argentina's 49) and far lower individualism (23 versus 46) point toward a culture where a near-sacred, nationwide Sunday family boundary makes more sense as a collective norm, while Argentina's higher individualism and masculinity scores track with a culture more prone to leaving work-life balance to informal negotiation, company by company, rather than collective expectation.

For a newcomer, the practical upshot is almost a reversal of assumption. Don't schedule anything early in Buenos Aires — it's a well-known foreign-worker failure point tied to the city's late-dinner, late-nightlife rhythm. Do treat Sunday in Chile as genuinely off-limits, and do independently verify your Chilean contract will be honored before relying on it, a specific, repeated warning that doesn't come up nearly as often in accounts of working in Argentina.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *BAexpats.org — a long-running forum thread debating whether Argentines are less hardworking than Western professionals reached a split verdict: individual competence was repeatedly praised, while weak organizational planning, distrust between colleagues, and "information hoarding" by established staff were named as the real source of frustration, not laziness.*

> *Reddit, r/digitalnomad, via a reachable mirror — a commenter who had studied in Buenos Aires described how "no one is awake at 9am" there, explaining that the city's after-9pm dinners and nightlife peaking around 3 or 4am made early meetings genuinely unworkable until they adjusted their own schedule to match.*

> *ExpatsBlog.com — a US expat teaching English in Chile described juggling two to four language institutes simultaneously just to assemble a full income, while naming one unbreakable rule that cut across his entire work life there: Sunday belongs to family, full stop.*

> *Expat.com, Buenos Aires work-culture guide — the single most-repeated practical note was that meetings there routinely start late not from disorganization but because a few minutes of real small talk, about family or weekend plans, is treated as a prerequisite to doing business at all.*

> *iProfesional, citing Jobint/Bumeran-Zonjobs survey data — 88 percent of Argentine respondents said they would actively look for another job if their current employer lacked a favorable work-life balance culture, a number high enough to suggest the frustration isn't quietly tolerated so much as actively driving turnover.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between the two, the honest framing is this: Chile has recently done the hard legislative work of shrinking the workweek and is visibly reaping the benefit in worker sentiment, while Argentina's comparatively looser, more relationship-driven culture hasn't translated into the balance its reputation implies — by its own workers' own account, in its own regional survey.

I'd tell a friend taking either job the same thing: don't plan your first week around the law alone. Plan it around Sunday in Chile, and around 9pm in Buenos Aires — those are the two facts about each country's relationship to time that the legal cap on the books will never quite tell you.
