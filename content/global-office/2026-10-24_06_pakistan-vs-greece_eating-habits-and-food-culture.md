# The Working Lunch, Abolished: Pakistan Skips It, Greece Won't Let You Leave the Table

🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇬🇷 Greece

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Pakistan, food consumes 37 percent of the average household budget, more than any other category, according to the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics' Household Integrated Economic Survey 2024-25. In Greece, a sit-down lunch can consume two full hours — a problem if your office issues you only one. Neither country will let you eat quickly and alone at your desk, and both consider this a moral failing, not a scheduling inconvenience.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept seconds when offered — refusing reads as an insult to the host | Don't eat or drink with your left hand around colleagues or elders |
| Learn your office's Ramadan hours early — federal offices routinely shorten them | Don't eat, drink, or chew gum visibly in public during Ramadan daylight hours |
| Say yes to street-side mangoes and lychees in season — cheap and genuinely excellent | Don't eat raw street food your first weeks; build up gradually at busy, visible stalls |
| Bring an appetite to any home invitation — a "small meal" never is | Don't expect wine or beer at a business dinner; alcohol is tightly restricted |
| Budget extra for groceries — imported goods vanish from shelves unpredictably | Don't assume good cheese is easy to find; it's scarce and pricey |
| Ask before assuming a shared lunch order includes you | Don't be surprised lunch is rushed while dinner at home is the real event |

#### 🇬🇷 Greece

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan dinner for 9 p.m. or later — arriving at 7 marks you a tourist | Don't expect the waiter to rush your bill; faster tables earn untipped staff nothing extra |
| Treat the free plate of fruit or yoghurt at meal's end as a gift, not an upsell | Don't expect shops or offices open roughly 2–5 p.m. in many towns |
| Linger — a two-hour lunch with colleagues is a normal Friday | Don't expect a stemmed wine glass; tavernas often pour into small tumblers, by design |
| Learn the real numbers on the Mediterranean diet before lecturing anyone on it | Don't assume everyone drinks ouzo; younger urbanites favor beer and cocktails |
| Bring bread appetite — it arrives automatically with nearly every meal | Don't book a one-hour working lunch at a sit-down restaurant; bring a sandwich instead |
| Ask locals for the family-run taverna, not the one with the English menu out front | Don't be startled when the bill arrives rolled in a small metal cylinder — it's custom |

Pakistan's relationship with food is organized around the household and the calendar, not the clock. The PBS survey confirms what any newcomer notices within days: food isn't a discretionary line item here, it's the dominant one. Office lunches tend to be brisk — a shared order of biryani or karahi between meetings — while dinner, at home with extended family, is the meal that matters. During Ramadan, the rhythm inverts: Pakistan's federal government has repeatedly shortened office hours for the fasting month, and even non-fasting employees avoid eating or drinking visibly in front of colleagues who are. The payoff is iftar, less a meal than a nightly civic event.

What unsettles new arrivals is logistics more than spice. Imported goods — decent cheese, wine, specific brands — appear in shops and vanish for months. Alcohol is heavily restricted, reshaping what a "business dinner" even means; there's no closing a deal over a bottle of wine. What compensates is produce: Pakistani mangoes and rice are genuinely world-class and cheap in season, and street food, approached cautiously, rewards the curious more than the brochure admits.

Greece runs on an almost opposite logic: food is less a household expense than a social institution with its own timetable. Dinner before 9 p.m. is for tourists; a proper evening meal stretches past 10. Yet the country's own nutrition research tells an unglamorous story about what Greeks actually eat. The HYDRIA National Health and Nutrition Survey found only 28.3 percent of Greek adults show high adherence to the traditional Mediterranean diet their country is famous for — nearly 40 percent among the over-65s, barely a quarter among everyone younger, who eat more meat, sugar, and alcohol. Hofstede Insights' cultural data offer some structural logic here: Greece scores among the highest in the world on uncertainty avoidance, a preference for ritual that shows up at the dinner table as plainly as in the civil service.

The practical friction, for anyone used to a one-hour lunch, is scheduling. A sit-down meal takes two hours because service is deliberately unhurried — tipping isn't the wage-subsidizing norm it is in the US, so no server benefits from turning your table fast. Pair that with Eurostat and OECD figures that have long placed Greek employees among the EU's longest-hours workforce, and you get a culture that works long days yet still refuses to eat lunch quickly. The arithmetic doesn't quite close, and nobody in Athens seems bothered by it.

**The Reckoning:** Put the two side by side and the irony sharpens. Pakistan spends the larger share of its money on food but the smaller share of its time eating it; Greece does the reverse, pouring modest household budgets into meals it refuses to rush. Pakistan's food culture is domestic — the real meal happens at home, after work, among relatives — while Greece's is public, built around the taverna table as a place to be seen lingering. A Pakistani office shrinks its hours for a religious calendar; a Greek office won't shrink its lunch for any calendar at all.

The cruelest twist falls on the deadline-driven newcomer trying to import "efficient" eating into either country. In Pakistan, you'll be fed quickly at work, then expected to show up unhurried for a multi-course family dinner you cannot skip. In Greece, you'll struggle to seat a weekday lunch meeting anywhere but a bakery, only to find colleagues vanish for two hours on a Tuesday and return having settled more business over dessert than you managed all morning. Efficiency, it turns out, was never the metric either country was optimizing for.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/PakistaniFood — a poster trying Western recipes noted they all assume eggs are cheap and plentiful, while at home people often ration egg intake for cost and health reasons, and asked whether that gap was as wide as it felt.*

> *Tales from a Small Planet (talesmag.com), Islamabad post report — a diplomatic household reported that fresh poultry can be bought live and dressed on the spot, that Pakistani mangoes and rice are "probably the best in the world," but warned newcomers to avoid street food entirely and accept gastroenteritis as nearly inevitable over a long posting.*

> *An expat.com forum thread on adjusting to Greece — a contributor described learning to love the free plate of fruit or yoghurt tavernas bring unasked at meal's end, wine served in tumblers instead of stemmed glasses, and the bill arriving rolled into a small metal cylinder so it won't blow off the table.*

> *A first-person account republished by MoneyTalksNews — an American who moved to Athens for work learned that a sit-down restaurant meal effectively requires two hours, since waitstaff aren't tipped enough to rush, making it structurally impossible to fit into a standard one-hour office break.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these postings on food alone, the real question isn't whether you prefer spice or olive oil — it's whether you'd rather protect your evenings or your lunch hours. Pakistan asks you to budget seriously for food, shorten your workday during Ramadan, and save your real appetite for a home-cooked dinner you can't skip. Greece asks you to accept that lunch is a two-hour commitment no meeting should be scheduled against, even as its own nutrition data show Greeks drifting from the diet that made the country famous.

Neither system is more "efficient"; each has simply decided which meal gets to be sacred. Told over a drink: go to Pakistan if you want your calendar built around family dinner, go to Greece if you want it held hostage by lunch — and either way, stop trying to eat at your desk, because nobody there is going to let you.
