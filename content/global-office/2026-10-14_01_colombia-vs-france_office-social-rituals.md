**Lunch as Civic Duty: How Colombia and France Turned the Midday Meal Into Two Very Different Religions**

**🇨🇴 Colombia · 🇫🇷 France**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Bogotá, skipping the office lunch invitation is a minor social injury; in Paris, eating a sandwich at your desk is practically a public confession. Both countries treat the midday meal as sacred infrastructure, which is precisely why foreigners keep getting it wrong in opposite directions — one by showing up too formally, the other by showing up too casually, and both without quite grasping that the real test isn't the food at all.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇴 Colombia
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the lunch invitation even if you've already eaten — declining reads as rejection, not dietary preference | Schedule meetings through the 12–2pm window; nobody will show, and it won't be treated as your problem |
| Learn the names of coworkers' kids and partners — small talk is relationship infrastructure here | Expect punctuality at "after-office" drinks; arriving at 6pm sharp usually means arriving alone |
| Join the office WhatsApp group immediately — real decisions move through it days before any formal invite | Cite workload as an excuse to skip the year-end fiesta; attendance is treated as close to mandatory |
| Greet people individually with warmth before discussing business | Rush the greeting ritual to "get to the point" — it reads as cold, not efficient |
| Say yes to the Friday "onces" (afternoon snack break) at least occasionally | Assume a declined weekend invite ends the friendship — just reschedule, graciously |

#### 🇫🇷 France
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Protect your lunch hour as fiercely as your payslip | Eat a sandwich at your desk — it marks you as either a tourist or in crisis |
| Say "bonjour" individually to each person before anything else, every single morning | Launch straight into a request without the greeting ritual first |
| Master disagreeing loudly about a project at 11am, then lunching cheerfully with the same colleague at noon | Take workplace criticism personally — it's aimed at the argument, not at you |
| Show up to coffee-and-cigarette breaks with an orange juice if you must | Skip them altogether — they're where alliances actually get built |
| Let colleagues set the pace on becoming friends outside the office | Expect the Christmas party to turn into genuine bonding — it mostly won't |

---

Colombia's social calendar is engineered for maximum collectivism, and the data backs up the vibe. Hofstede Insights scores Colombia at 13 on individualism against France's 71 — one of the starker gaps in the entire dataset — meaning the group, not the individual, is the default unit of office life. That shows up concretely: Gallup's State of the Global Workplace puts Colombian employee engagement at 25%, roughly on par with the Latin America/Caribbean regional average of 30%, but life-evaluation "thriving" scores come in at 52%, suggesting people cope with imperfect jobs by investing heavily in the humans sitting next to them. The catch, according to the OECD's work-life balance comparison (as reported by Colombia Reports), is that Colombia ranked dead last among OECD economies, with 26.6% of employees working more than 50 hours a week — so all that warmth is squeezed into a schedule with very little slack to spare.

The legal reforms tell the same story from a different angle. Colombia's workweek has been dropping by law from 48 hours toward 42 hours under a phased 2021 reform, yet a 2025 Universidad Externado study found formal salaried workers still averaging 47.7 hours — about two hours above that year's legal ceiling. The lesson for a new arrival: the social rituals aren't decoration bolted onto the job, they're the coping mechanism for a demanding one, and treating the Friday "onces" or the year-end fiesta as optional extras rather than load-bearing social glue is the single most common early misread.

France runs the opposite experiment: fewer hours, more ceremony, less warmth. The OECD puts French annual working hours around 1,511 — roughly 29 hours a week on average once part-time work is folded in — among the lower figures in the OECD. France's Code du travail mandates a 20-minute break once the workday hits six hours, and under a rule dating to 1894, eating at your desk is still technically against the rules at most workplaces, which is why firms with 50+ staff must provide a canteen. The French, per OECD data, spend an average of 2 hours 13 minutes a day eating and drinking — the highest figure of any OECD country. None of this, notably, is about team bonding; it's about the individual's right to a proper meal, protected by labor law rather than left to company culture.

That individualism cuts the other way after hours. The Local France notes that French colleagues generally keep work and social life in separate, well-ventilated rooms — a boss getting visibly drunk at the staff Christmas party is, per the same account, hard to imagine. Meetings eat roughly 40% of an executive's working life, disagreement is loud, frequent, and considered entirely professional rather than personal, and *le piston* — knowing the right people — is rated important by about 90% of surveyed professionals. The social contract is precise: colleagues who argue fiercely over a deliverable at 11am can share an unbothered lunch at noon, because the argument was never really about them.

The head-to-head reveals something counterintuitive: the country with dramatically more free-floating hours (Colombia) spends it on obligatory togetherness, while the country with a shorter legal workweek (France) uses its reclaimed time to protect personal boundaries, not collective ones. A Colombian colleague who skips the office party is making a statement; a French colleague who skips the after-work drinks is just going home, and nobody reads anything into it. Where Colombia's social rituals function as a substitute for institutional support, France's function as a substitute for small talk — which, perversely, means the famously bureaucratic country is actually the easier one to have a quiet, private life in.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum (France) — One longtime resident described a strict daily rhythm where "you cannot have lunch before 12 or after 14h because nothing is open," while another pushed back that this depended entirely on the employer — at one demanding job, finding "three minutes to go to the toilet" was already a stretch.*
> *The Local France — Readers reported that a boss visibly drunk at the office Christmas party would be almost unthinkable, underscoring how rarely French colleagues let their guard down socially even at the one sanctioned party of the year.*
> *Expat.com forum (Colombia) — One poster warned newcomers that scuffed or non-leather shoes get noticed in business meetings before anything else is said, and that a disheveled appearance reads as disrespect to both yourself and your employer.*
> *Tales from a Small Planet (Bogotá Post Report) — Multiple diplomatic-community respondents noted that Colombians devote weekends almost entirely to family and home-hosted gatherings, so expats who wait for a bar invitation may wait a long time; the real social door opens through a trusted personal introduction, not an open-office vibe.*
> *Tales from a Small Planet (Bogotá Post Report) — Another respondent advised that learning Spanish quickly is less about politeness and more about necessity, since the English-speaking expat social circle is small enough to exhaust within a few months.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between them on social rituals alone, the honest advice is this: in Colombia, budget emotional energy for constant, warm, slightly exhausting group participation, because the rituals are doing real structural work that labor law isn't; in France, budget patience for formality and protect your lunch hour like it's a legal right, because it is one, and don't mistake a loud 11am disagreement for a grudge. The deeper difference isn't collectivism versus individualism in the abstract — it's that Colombia asks you to join the family, while France simply asks you to show up, say bonjour to everyone individually, and eat your sandwich somewhere respectable.

Over a drink, I'd put it more bluntly: move to Bogotá if you want to be adopted, move to Paris if you want to be left alone — just don't expect either one to warn you which.
