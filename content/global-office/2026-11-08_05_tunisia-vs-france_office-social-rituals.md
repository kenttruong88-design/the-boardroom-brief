# In France It's Illegal to Eat Lunch at Your Desk. In Tunisia, Nobody Would Dream of It Anyway.

🇹🇳 Tunisia · 🇫🇷 France

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

France has, since a public-health provision tucked into its labor code, formally banned employees from eating lunch at their own desks — a rule routinely cited by outlets from NPR to Sporkful, and one that most French offices enforce not through fear of inspectors but through simple social pressure: a sandwich hunched over a keyboard reads as a small act of self-neglect. Tunisia has no such law, and needs none, because the idea of skipping a proper midday meal — ideally one involving coffee, conversation, and the slow re-litigating of everyone's weekend — would strike most Tunisian offices as not merely sad but faintly uncivilized. Both countries treat the workday's social rituals as load-bearing. They have simply built entirely different structures to hold the weight.

## Do's and Don'ts: Tunisia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the offered coffee or tea before discussing business — refusing it outright can read as abrupt | Rush straight into the agenda; greetings and small talk about family and health are the actual opening act |
| Join colleagues for the midday break rather than eating alone at your desk | Schedule anything important during the adjusted, shortened Ramadan work hours without checking first |
| Expect Friday to carry a slightly different, more relaxed rhythm, often tied to couscous lunches | Assume a quiet office means nothing is happening — much real business is settled over coffee, not in meetings |
| Greet each colleague individually when entering a room, even a crowded one | Decline a second round of tea too quickly — it can read as wanting to leave the conversation |
| Build the relationship first; deals follow trust, not the reverse | Treat small coffee breaks as optional downtime rather than the actual social infrastructure of the office |

## Do's and Don'ts: France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take a real lunch break away from your desk — eating at your workstation is both culturally frowned upon and, officially, against the labor code | Skip the office "pot" (drinks for a promotion, departure, or birthday) — attendance is a quiet but real obligation |
| Treat lunch as a genuine hour-plus social reset, not a quick refuel | Bring up salary, religion, or personal finances at lunch — French office small talk has firm boundaries |
| Join the apéro when invited; declining repeatedly reads as standoffish | Assume informality at the pot means the hierarchy has dissolved — it hasn't, it's just relaxed its posture |
| Use "bonjour" individually when entering a shared office, not a single group greeting | Eat a sad desk sandwich in front of colleagues who are visibly, collectively, going to lunch |
| Let conversation range widely — food, culture, politics — lunch is meant to be substantive | Rush lunch to "get back to being productive" — that instinct itself reads as slightly suspicious |

## Tunisia: Coffee Is the Meeting

Tunisian office culture, shaped by a layered history of Ottoman, French, and Maghrebi Arab influence, treats the coffee or tea break not as a pause from work but as the venue where the actual social contract of the workplace gets renewed daily. Rivermate's guide to Tunisian workplace norms describes greetings as a genuine ritual rather than a formality — colleagues are expected to ask after each other's families and health before any substantive conversation begins, and skipping that step to get to business faster is read as rude rather than efficient. Expat.com's Tunis-focused work culture guide echoes this: newcomers who try to compress small talk are often quietly recalibrated by colleagues who simply keep talking until the appropriate amount of rapport has been established.

Ramadan reshapes the entire rhythm rather than merely pausing it: Tunisian administrative and private-sector working hours are formally adjusted during the month, often compressed into a single shorter block, and the adjusted hours are reported each year by local outlets like Kapitalis and Webdo. Fridays carry their own gravity too, frequently anchored by couscous as the week's communal meal — a detail international business guides repeatedly flag as more than culinary trivia, since skipping it without a very good reason is read socially, not just gastronomically.

The coffee ritual itself has its own internal etiquette that outsiders routinely miss. Declining a first offer is acceptable; declining a second can register as a quiet rebuff, since the host has by then signaled genuine hospitality rather than mere politeness. Business guides covering Tunisia note that seniority is visible in who initiates the coffee round and who is served first, a small but consistent hierarchy marker inside what otherwise looks like a purely social moment. None of this is written down anywhere an incoming employee is likely to read; it is transmitted entirely through repetition and gentle correction, which is precisely why it takes most newcomers several weeks to stop accidentally offending people who are, by every other measure, being extremely welcoming to them.

## France: The Lunch Break Has Teeth

France's relationship to the office lunch is, improbably, backed by actual statute: multiple outlets, including NPR's coverage of the rule, note that French labor law prohibits eating at one's workstation, a provision rooted in a public-health measure that has quietly calcified into cultural identity. The Connexion France's reporting on "eating alone at work" finds that even as younger French workers increasingly default to a quick solo lunch, doing so still carries a faint whiff of social failure — something to apologize for, not something to be efficient about.

The apéro and the "pot" — informal office drinks marking a departure, a promotion, or simply the end of a hard week — function as France's answer to Tunisia's coffee ritual, and The Local's coverage of apéro culture makes clear that attendance, while nominally optional, is read as a genuine signal of belonging. Hofstede Insights' comparative data places France meaningfully higher on power distance than most of Northern Europe, and the lunch-and-apéro system is one of the only places that hierarchy visibly softens: a director lingering at the pot with junior staff is a small, deliberate act of accessibility, not an accident.

The ritual has also proven durable against the pressures that have eroded it elsewhere. Connexion France's reporting notes that even as remote work and busier schedules push younger employees toward a faster, solo lunch, the social expectation hasn't moved nearly as quickly as the behavior — meaning a growing number of French workers now eat alone while still feeling slightly guilty about it, a specifically French kind of cognitive dissonance. Managers in more traditional firms will sometimes still visibly notice, and occasionally comment on, an employee's absence from the shared lunch table, treating it as a minor social data point worth filing away rather than a neutral scheduling choice.

## The Reckoning

Both countries have decided that the office cannot run on meetings and email alone, and both have built a daily ritual to compensate — but Tunisia distributes that social labor continuously, in small coffee-sized doses throughout the day, while France concentrates it into two scheduled set-pieces, lunch and the occasional pot, and polices the boundaries of both fairly strictly. An employee who tries to treat Tunisian coffee breaks as optional will find themselves socially stalled no matter how good their actual work is. An employee who tries to eat a sandwich at their French desk will find a colleague gently, almost ritually, horrified.

The deeper difference is what each ritual is protecting. Tunisia's version protects relationship-building as a prerequisite for trust, which is itself a prerequisite for anything getting done. France's version protects a harder line between work time and the self — the lunch break is less about bonding with colleagues specifically than about reasserting that an employee is a person first, which is its own kind of social contract, just a more individually guarded one.

## Conclusion

If you're moving into a Tunisian office, block out real time for coffee and treat it as work, because it functionally is. If you're moving into a French one, block out real time for lunch and leave your desk, because the alternative is not just culturally awkward but, on paper, not strictly legal. Either way, the actual lesson is the same: in both places, the parts of the day that look like a break from work are, quietly, where the job gets done.
