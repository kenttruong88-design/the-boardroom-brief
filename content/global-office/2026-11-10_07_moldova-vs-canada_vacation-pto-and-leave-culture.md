# Moldova Gives You Four Weeks and a Funeral Holiday; Canada Gives You Two and a Pep Talk

🇲🇩 Moldova · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Moldova, one of the poorest economies in Europe, guarantees its workers at least 28 days of annual leave before a single public holiday is counted. Canada, one of the richest economies in the world, guarantees most of its workers ten working days and a warm sense of national modesty. According to a 2025 comparison by Resume.io, covering statutory leave and paid public holidays in 185 countries, Moldovans get 39 days a year against a global average of 30. The contest between these two systems is less about generosity than about what each country has decided is the natural price of a job.

## Moldova: Do's and Don'ts
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect 28 calendar days of paid leave, but only after six months of service | Plan a long August break in your first spring on the payroll |
| Note that public holidays sit on top of the 28 days, so the Christmas, Easter and May clusters are effectively free | Assume the 28 days are working days; weekends are counted inside them |
| Read the 2027 change closely: leave is due to be counted in working days instead | Trust a contract that still says "calendar days" without asking when it will be updated |
| Get overtime agreed in writing, since the law requires the employee's written consent | Accept open-ended evening work as a gesture of loyalty |
| Plan around Julian-calendar Christmas on 7 and 8 January, a separate event from 25 December | Expect a holiday that lands on a weekend to be moved to the Monday |
| Ask HR about the extra four days available to parents of two or more children under 14 | Assume local pay will match what a posted expatriate earns |

## Canada: Do's and Don'ts
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your province first; the statutory minimum is set provincially, not nationally | Assume the federal rules apply; they cover banks, airlines and railways, not most employers |
| Negotiate extra weeks before signing, since offers routinely exceed the legal floor | Take the minimum as final; it is a starting position |
| Learn whether vacation pay is 4 percent or 6 percent of earnings in your province | Forget that the money is a percentage of pay, not a fixed number of days |
| Ask about blackout periods in tourism, logistics and retail | Book a July trip before the manager has confirmed the roster |
| Ask whether a statutory holiday that falls in your leave costs you a vacation day | Assume that a job change carries your seniority-based weeks with you |
| Use the days, since the unused kind tend to evaporate | Treat a second week as a sign of ambition |

## Moldova: The Arithmetic of Abundance

Moldovan law is straightforward on paper. Under Article 113 of the Labour Code, annual leave is "at least 28 calendar days, excluding non-working public holidays," as Logos Press reported in October. The European Training Foundation and Eurofound, in their 2024 country report, confirm the same floor and add a 40-hour standard week, with overtime capped at 240 hours a year and permitted only with the employee's written consent. Sick leave is shared out sensibly: the employer pays the first five days and social insurance takes over after that.

The holiday calendar is generous, and mildly theological. The Moldovan news agency IPN lists non-working days including both Julian and Gregorian Christmas, International Women's Day, two days each for Easter and the Sunday of the Dead, Victory Day, Children's Day, Independence Day and a day devoted to the national language. It also records the grievance of trade unions that holidays falling on weekends are not moved to weekdays, a proposal that was turned down. In other words, the state hands out days with one hand and calendar luck with the other.

Moldovan law also layers entitlements on top of the base. According to Rivermate's country guide, parents of two or more children under 14, workers under 18 and those in harmful conditions receive four extra days, and women are granted at least 126 days of maternity leave. Employees only qualify for annual leave after six months of service, which is the one clause a new arrival should read twice.

Reform is coming. From 1 January 2027, the government plans to count leave in working days, reportedly 22 in place of 28 calendar days, with Labour Minister Natalia Plugaru insisting the change will not reduce time off or holiday pay. The lived reality is subtler. Talesmag's anonymous diplomatic-spouse report from Chișinău describes weekend trips to monasteries, wine country and Romania, a nanny at about five dollars an hour and a pace that invites such outings. Time off is plentiful; the money to enjoy it, as the same report notes, is not. Local salaries are, in its words, much lower than expatriate pay.

## Canada: The Modest Minimum

Canada's rule is two weeks. According to Employment Hero's 2026 provincial guide, most provinces start at two weeks and move to three after five years, with Quebec reaching three after three years and Saskatchewan beginning at three weeks and rising to four after ten. Vacation pay runs at 4 percent of earnings, rising to 6 percent in line with the extra week. Federally regulated employees such as bank staff and airline crews also start at two weeks. A statutory holiday that falls during leave does not consume a vacation day, a small mercy that Canadian employers have not been in any hurry to advertise.

The law is a floor, and the Canadian labour market treats it as a ceiling more often than it admits. On the OECD's numbers, as compiled on Wikipedia, the average Canadian worker put in 1,687 hours in 2025, a touch below the OECD average of 1,736. That is moderate rather than punishing, and it fits a country that does not so much deny leave as discourage its enthusiastic use. An Expedia survey of 1,001 Canadians in 2018, reported by HR Reporter, found that the average worker received 17 vacation days and used 15, and that 54 percent of respondents described themselves as vacation-deprived, against 58 percent globally. Canadians, it seems, are the rare people who are both short of leave and slightly reluctant to finish the little they have.

Culture matters here. Hofstede Insights scores Canada at 39 on power distance, a low figure that implies employees expect to be consulted, and in theory to bargain. In practice the bargaining happens at the point of hire, because non-union staff who change jobs can find themselves back at the statutory minimum, a complaint that recurs among Canadians themselves. Unionised workers, by long-standing pattern, do better: economist Andrew Jackson, writing for the Progressive Economics Forum, put the typical union deal at three weeks rising to four after ten years, and sometimes six weeks for the long-serving. His figures date from 2006, but Reddit commenters describe the same pattern today. For everyone else, leave is a negotiated luxury that the law politely refrains from guaranteeing.

## The Reckoning

On the statutory numbers, Moldova wins easily: 28 calendar days plus holidays against Canada's ten working days plus holidays. Even after the 2027 conversion to working days, a Moldovan worker is promised roughly four and a half weeks. The Moldovan system also comes with a rare administrative virtue, which is that it is a single national rule rather than thirteen provincial and territorial ones. The problem is that a statute cannot create the wealth that makes a holiday worth taking. A Canadian on two weeks can buy a flight; a Moldovan on four weeks can usually reach Orhei Vechi.

The Moldovan culture of power is also a long way from Canada's. A Moldovan academic study applying the Hofstede framework put the country at 90 on power distance, against Canada's 39. That helps explain why Moldovans may be reluctant to argue with a manager over a leave date, and why Canadians may be more willing to try. The honest conclusion is that Canada offers more room to negotiate and Moldova offers more to negotiate from. For a foreign worker, the question is whether you prefer your leave as a legal right or as a deal you must close yourself.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/AskACanadian — paraphrased experience: Commenters in a thread on why Canada offers just two weeks say the comparison to the United States keeps people content, and that unionised and public-sector workers do far better. Several warn that changing employers can reset you to the two-week minimum, so extra time has to be negotiated again each time.*

> *Reddit r/askTO — paraphrased experience: When a European asked whether to move to Toronto, commenters mostly told them to stay put. One Toronto-born poster said they regretted moving back from Europe, and another said the work-life balance on the two continents was not comparable.*

> *British Expats forum — paraphrased experience: A poster working as a supervisor in Vancouver said salaried staff there get only the provincial minimum, and that summer and the weeks around the holidays are blacked out for leave. Another noted that Canadian vacation pay is a percentage of earnings, and that nothing obliges employers to make staff actually take it.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report — paraphrased experience: A diplomatic spouse who lived in Chișinău from 2021 to 2023 describes a walkable, safe city with cheap childcare and easy weekend trips. Work for partners tends to mean the embassy or a remote job, because local salaries are far below expatriate ones.*

> *ExpatExchange — paraphrased experience: An expat in Chișinău describes life as slower and more relaxed than in the United States, with family and summer forest picnics at the centre of the calendar. A digital nomad in the same thread calls the country affordable, with decent internet and coworking spaces.*

## Conclusion

If your priority is time, Moldova's law is the better deal, and the 2027 change to working days is worth reading before you sign, because the headline may shrink even as the minister promises it will not. Moldova also opened a two-year digital nomad visa in September 2025 for remote workers earning roughly 1,500 to 2,000 US dollars a month, which suits those who bring a foreign salary with them. If your priority is earning power, Canada is the stronger choice, provided you treat the two-week minimum as an opening bid and negotiate leave in writing before accepting.

The practical advice is the same in both places: read the contract, ask what counts as a day, and use what you are given. Moldova gives more of it and Canada gives you more to do with it, which is, in its own way, a fair trade.
