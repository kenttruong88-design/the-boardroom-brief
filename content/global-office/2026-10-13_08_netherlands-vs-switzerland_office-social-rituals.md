**Bring Your Own Croissant, Mind Your Own Sandwich**

🇳🇱 Netherlands · 🇨🇭 Switzerland

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In a Dutch office, Friday afternoon means the *vrijmibo* — a round of beer and deep-fried snacks right at your desk cluster, no invitation required, everyone welcome. In a Swiss office, the social ritual is the *apéro*, and it runs on quieter rules: arrive on time, bring a small gift if you're hosting, and never forget the croissants on your birthday. Both cultures build real rituals around food and drink at work. Only one of them, by an international survey's own ranking, makes it genuinely hard for an outsider to turn those rituals into actual friendship.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to the *vrijmibo* (Friday afternoon drinks) — it's informal and genuinely open to everyone on the floor | Treat the invitation as optional small talk; skipping it repeatedly reads as standoffish |
| Expect a short, often sandwich-based lunch, typically 30 minutes to an hour | Expect a long, multi-course midday meal the way you might in Southern Europe |
| Bring real snacks (*bitterballen*, cheese sticks) if you're contributing to a borrel | Show up with just raw vegetables — it won't cut it against the beer |
| Say "Gezellig!" and mean it when accepting a last-minute invite | Overthink the invite as a bigger social commitment than it is |
| Expect flat, informal interaction regardless of who's in the room | Assume hierarchy dictates who initiates the social moment |

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Eat lunch with colleagues rather than at your desk — it's a genuine ritual, not just a break | Treat lunch as a solo task to get through quickly |
| Bring croissants on your own birthday — the custom runs the opposite direction from many cultures | Wait for colleagues to celebrate you; the office expects you to do the celebrating |
| Arrive on time for a work *apéro*, and bring a small host gift if it's at someone's home | Show up late the way you might to a casual home gathering |
| Invest real effort in learning the local language and greeting rituals | Assume English alone will get you into the social fabric, even at an international firm |
| Expect integration to take real, sustained effort over time | Assume a few team events will be enough to build genuine friendships quickly |

---

The Dutch build office sociability around deliberate informality. The *vrijmibo* — literally "Friday afternoon drink" — anchors the week: colleagues gather for beer or wine and distinctly Dutch snacks like *bitterballen* and *kaasstengels*, almost all deep-fried, with the unwritten rule that raw vegetables don't count as a real contribution. The invitation itself tells you something about the culture — it's informal, low-stakes, and the expected response, per Dutch culture writers, is an enthusiastic "Gezellig! Let's go!" rather than a weighed RSVP. Lunch runs the opposite direction: short, often just 30 minutes, and built around the famous sandwich (*broodje* or *boterham*) rather than a hot meal — what one Dutch lunch-culture piece dryly calls "the cold Protestant sandwich." Hofstede Insights scores the Netherlands at just 14 on Masculinity, one of the lowest scores recorded anywhere and a long way from a culture built on competitive status display, which tracks with an office culture where the after-work drink is flat, egalitarian, and genuinely optional rather than a status performance for whoever's watching.

Switzerland's rituals run on more structure, even when the occasion is purely social. The *apéro* — drinks and small bites, sometimes substantial enough to replace dinner as an *apéro riche* — follows real etiquette: arrive on time, greet people individually in small groups, take small portions and nibble rather than pile a plate, and bring a host gift like wine or chocolate if you're invited to someone's home. At work, eating lunch at your desk is actively discouraged; colleagues treat the shared midday meal as one of the office's real rituals, alongside an oddly specific custom where you, not your coworkers, are expected to bring croissants on your own birthday. Hofstede scores Switzerland at 70 on Masculinity — more than five times the Dutch score — which helps explain why Swiss social rituals carry more formal weight and more implicit evaluation than their Dutch equivalents, even when the stated purpose is identical: getting colleagues to unwind together.

**The Reckoning**

Here's the uncomfortable number for Switzerland: HSBC's Expat Explorer survey, covering roughly 7,000 expats across 37 countries, ranked Switzerland dead last — 37th out of 37 — for making friends, and near the bottom for social life, local friendships, and local work culture, despite ranking first for income and disposable income. The Netherlands doesn't appear in the same conversation as a friendship failure; its rituals are built to be low-friction and inclusive by design. Switzerland's rituals are just as real and arguably richer in etiquette, but etiquette and inclusion aren't the same thing — a culture can have abundant, well-defined social rituals and still be one where an outsider struggles to turn attendance into belonging. The croissant custom captures it precisely: it's a lovely, specific ritual, but it's also a test you have to already know about to pass.

### Conclusion

If you're choosing based on which culture's office rituals are easier to walk into cold, the Netherlands wins by design — the *vrijmibo* is deliberately low-stakes, and Dutch directness means you'll generally be told plainly if you're getting something wrong. Switzerland's rituals are no less genuine, but they come with more encoded etiquette and, per the actual survey data, a much steeper climb from attendance to real friendship. Priya's actual advice: in Amsterdam, just show up to the Friday drinks and say yes enthusiastically — the ritual does the work for you. In Zurich or Geneva, invest in the language and the etiquette early, because the croissant custom is the rule, not the exception — nobody's going to tell you about the next one.
