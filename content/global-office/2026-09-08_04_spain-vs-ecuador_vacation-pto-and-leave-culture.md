# Thirty Days vs. Fifteen: What "Time Off" Actually Means in Spain and Ecuador

**🇪🇸 Spain · 🇪🇨 Ecuador**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Spanish law guarantees workers 30 calendar days of vacation a year — roughly 22 working days — on top of 14 public holidays, a floor so protected that Article 38 of the Workers' Statute makes it non-waivable even if an employee would rather take the cash instead. Ecuadorian law guarantees 15. That's not a rounding difference; it's a doubling, between two Spanish-speaking economies that share more cultural DNA than almost any other pairing in this series. The gap says less about how much either country values rest and more about who each economy has decided should absorb the cost of it — and for anyone weighing a move, that distinction matters far more than the headline number.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect your employer to propose vacation dates at least 2 months in advance — this is a legal requirement, not a courtesy | Try to negotiate away vacation days for extra pay — Spanish law makes this non-waivable |
| Plan around August: many businesses close entirely or shift to intensive mornings-only hours (jornada intensiva) | Schedule major client work in August assuming normal availability — up to 80% of big-city populations leave |
| Check whether your role pays "14 salaries" (extra payments in July and December) — common practice, not universal | Assume a 12-salary contract is broken; ask HR to clarify the payment structure directly |
| Take your full legal entitlement — unused Spanish vacation typically cannot be cashed out except at termination | Bank vacation days indefinitely; Spanish leave doesn't work like a rolling PTO balance in the US sense |
| Respect the lunch-hour business closures common outside major cities | Show up expecting Anglo-style continuous 9-to-5 availability everywhere |

#### 🇪🇨 Ecuador

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm your 15-day entitlement starts only after a full year of service | Expect prorated vacation to be generous before your first anniversary — it's strictly proportional |
| Track the seniority bonus: one extra day per year after 5 years, capped at 30 total | Assume your leave balance is fixed forever — it grows meaningfully with tenure |
| Negotiate limited carryover (up to 3 years by agreement) if you genuinely can't use all 15 days one year | Try to skip the mandatory annual minimum — a portion must legally be taken every year regardless of agreement |
| Confirm vacation pay is calculated and paid in advance, based on average salary | Assume unused leave is forfeited on exit — Ecuadorian law requires payout of accrued vacation on termination |
| Budget your own travel and rest time deliberately, since the legal floor is roughly half Spain's | Assume the US dollar economy and lower legal PTO floor mean a lower overall quality of life — cost of living offsets much of the gap |

---

Spain's 30-day standard sits near the top of the OECD's leave rankings and is inseparable from a broader cultural instinct that treats sustained annual rest as a right the state actively protects, not a benefit employers dispense at will. That shows up seasonally and structurally: Grant Thornton estimates roughly 18% of Spanish businesses close entirely in August, turnover at listed companies falls by up to half that month per Spain's own stock exchange data, and the practice is old enough that most sectors have built entire operating rhythms — intensive summer hours, staggered regional holiday calendars — around a workforce that simply isn't fully present for a third of the year. Hofstede's data puts Spain at a comparatively moderate 57 on power distance, and the country's leave law reflects that: rest is negotiated collectively through statute and collective bargaining rather than granted individually as a mark of managerial goodwill.

Ecuador's 15-day floor, by contrast, sits closer to the Latin American median and reflects a labor code built around different priorities — proportional accrual, seniority-based increases, and mandatory payout on termination all protect the worker's economic claim to leave, but the baseline volume of rest is roughly half Spain's. Hofstede's data shows Ecuador scoring notably higher on power distance (78 vs. Spain's 57) and lower on individualism, consistent with a more hierarchical, group-oriented society where entitlements tend to accrue with tenure and loyalty rather than being distributed flat and universal from day one. The country's use of the US dollar as its national currency — a detail expats consistently flag as making Ecuador unusually approachable financially for anyone earning in dollars — softens some of the practical sting of fewer vacation days, since the lower cost of living means each of those 15 days can often stretch further than a Spanish employee's 30 would in a comparably priced European city.

The reckoning: Spain has decided rest is a systemic entitlement large enough to visibly reshape the national economy every August, while Ecuador has decided rest is an individually accruing right that grows as you prove your commitment through tenure. A newcomer used to unlimited-PTO American norms will find both systems bracingly rigid in the same direction — you cannot simply negotiate your way to more or less time off in either country — but the actual lived experience diverges sharply: Spain hands you the whole 30 days on day one and structurally clears space in the calendar for you to use it, while Ecuador makes you wait a year, then build toward a bigger allowance the longer you stay.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering whether offices in Spain close in August the way they do in Italy, one long-time resident said the honest answer is "it depends," but noted that in residential neighborhoods of Barcelona, "businesses close during August, schools are closed," and the people who do keep working typically shift to reduced morning-only hours rather than a normal day.*

> *The Local (Spain) — A piece explaining what's open and closed each August cited Grant Thornton's estimate that about 18% of Spanish businesses shut entirely for the month, while larger chains stay open — a split several expat commenters said caught them off guard in their first summer, when a favorite neighborhood café simply vanished for three weeks with a handwritten sign and no online notice.*

> *Quora — Someone who had lived in Ecuador for 18 months described loving the slower pace of life and the warmth of the people, but flagged that navigating a bureaucratic, sometimes unpredictable government apparatus around anything official — including standard employment paperwork — required more patience than the laid-back lifestyle reputation suggested.*

> *Hacker News — In a widely discussed thread titled "I'm a Digital Nomad. It's Not as Fun as It Looks," several commenters converged on a specific warning applicable well beyond Ecuador: workers who relocate to lower-cost, slower-paced economies often end up working just as many hours as they did at home out of habit, effectively forfeiting the very leave and lifestyle benefits that motivated the move in the first place.*

### Conclusion

If what you actually want is a country that structurally forces you to stop working, Spain's non-negotiable 30 days and August shutdown will do that for you whether you like it or not. If you're moving to Ecuador for the lifestyle and the dollar-denominated affordability, the legal floor of 15 days means the discipline to actually rest has to come from you, not from the calendar — and per the digital nomads who've tried and failed at exactly this, that's a harder habit to build than it sounds. Pick the country whose rest culture you can't out-hustle, because left to your own devices, you probably will.
