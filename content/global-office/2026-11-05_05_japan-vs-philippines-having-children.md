# Japan Will Pay Your Partner to Stay Home. The Philippines Will Just Assume Your Mother Already Is.

🇯🇵 Japan · 🇵🇭 Philippines

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Japan offers, on paper, the single most generous paid paternity leave of any country measured by UNICEF — more than thirty weeks, fully paid. Roughly one in twenty new fathers actually takes any of it. The Philippines, by contrast, has no comparable paper entitlement for fathers worth bragging about, and yet somehow the childcare still gets done, mostly by grandmothers, aunts, and a woman the family calls a yaya, none of whom appear in any UNICEF ranking at all.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for hoikuen (public daycare) the moment you know you're expecting — the points-based system is brutally slow and competitive | Assume a daycare spot is guaranteed just because you want one and can pay for it |
| Expect your employment type (full-time, standard hours) to directly affect your daycare application score | Apply for daycare assuming part-time, freelance, or remote work scores the same as standard full-time employment |
| Know that paternity leave uptake varies enormously by employer and department — ask directly what's actually normal, not just what's legally available | Assume generous legal paternity leave means your specific workplace will support you taking it |
| Budget emotionally for a genuinely long odds-based wait on daycare placement, with backup/private waitlists running deep | Assume overflow or private daycare centers will have open spots on short notice |
| Understand that attitudes toward having children are shifting fast, especially among younger women — don't assume older cultural narratives still apply uniformly | Assume every Japanese colleague shares the same view on parenthood and career tradeoffs |

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect extended family — grandparents, aunts, a live-in yaya — to be the real childcare backbone, not formal daycare | Assume institutional daycare will function the way it might in a country with stronger public infrastructure |
| Know the maternity leave law (105 days, extendable) is genuinely generous by regional standards | Assume generous maternity leave on paper means equal career protection in practice |
| Expect working mothers to carry a documented "double burden" of long paid hours plus unpaid domestic work | Assume the formal workweek alone reflects a working parent's actual total workload |
| Ask directly about your specific employer's paternity-leave practice — a major reform bill is pending but not yet law | Assume current paternity leave matches what's being proposed in pending legislation |
| Build in real slack for long commutes and long hours when planning childcare logistics | Assume a 40-hour nominal workweek reflects actual time away from home, given reported averages near 59 hours in official low-balance rankings |

Japan's paternity-leave system is, by the numbers, almost absurdly generous. UNICEF's 2019 "Family-Friendly Policies" report, using OECD and EU data, ranked Japan first in the world for paid paternity leave at over thirty weeks fully paid, well ahead of South Korea's seventeen. The catch, documented in the same analysis, is uptake: as of 2017, only about five percent of Japanese fathers took any paternity leave at all. More recent government data, reported by The Japan Times, shows real movement — 34 percent of male central-government employees took leave in the most recent fiscal year studied, beating the government's own 30-percent target ahead of schedule — but the variance by department is almost comic: the Finance Ministry hit 83.3 percent, Health and Labor 77.5 percent, Defense a mere 10.9 percent. Outside the civil service, the nationwide rate among all employed new fathers sat closer to 7.5 percent. Underneath all of it sits a demographic shift that should worry anyone staffing a Japanese office for the next decade: a Rohto Pharmaceuticals youth survey found 62.6 percent of young respondents say they don't want children, with women's "no" response now exceeding men's for the first time, and 61.4 percent of young women specifically citing career risk as a reason — a ten-point gap over young men's 51.2 percent.

The practical obstacle sitting underneath those statistics, for anyone who does have a child, is Japan's hoikuen daycare allocation system — a points-based bureaucracy that a first-person Metropolis Japan essay describes with real exasperation. The writer's own under-one-year-old son was rejected from public daycare because the ward's scoring system ranks full-time employees above part-time workers, contract staff, and parent-students; his own wife, working part-time while in graduate school, scored too low to qualify despite the family's genuine need. The family ended up on a private-center waitlist roughly fifty families deep, lobbying local officials for reconsideration — a system the writer argues structurally punishes anyone outside a traditional full-time employment pattern, including most freelance, remote, or flexibly employed parents, foreign or Japanese.

The Philippines runs on almost the opposite model: formal institutions matter less because the extended family is doing most of the real work. Republic Act 11210 extended maternity leave to 105 days regardless of marital status, one of the more generous entitlements in Asia, and a pending bill — House Bill 9891, the "PAPA Leave Act" — would extend fathers from the current baseline of seven days to 105, with a darkly specific enforcement mechanism: a father who takes the leave but doesn't actually perform caregiving duties could, under existing law, be held liable under the Anti-Violence Against Women and Their Children Act. But generous leave laws sit on top of a genuinely difficult underlying reality. The ILO's "Role Reversal" study documents a global outlier pattern: Filipino women are two to three times more likely than men to work what the ILO classifies as excessively long hours, carrying what researchers call a "double burden" of paid work plus unpaid domestic labor. The study's own case examples are vivid — a marketing executive working sixty-hour weeks with a long commute who relies on a nanny to make it function, and a former bank manager who left her career specifically after her husband pushed her to prioritize their daughters' schooling over her own job. Those individual stories sit against a national backdrop in which Remote's Global Life-Work Balance Index ranked Manila 59th of 60 cities studied, with an average 40.6-hour nominal workweek that official statistics suggest understates actual time commitments once commuting and domestic labor are included.

### The Reckoning

The real irony is that Japan's system is generous on paper and conservative in practice, while the Philippines' system is comparatively modest on paper and radically improvised in practice. Japan built an elaborate, well-funded entitlement that most fathers quietly decline to use, in a culture where Hofstede's own data flags extreme scores on both masculinity and uncertainty avoidance — a combination that keeps rigid gender roles and a rigid, risk-averse daycare bureaucracy locked in place even as younger attitudes visibly shift underneath it. The Philippines never built the same formal daycare infrastructure, but its deeply collectivist extended-family default quietly absorbs a load that Japan's nuclear-family model has to solve through a government point system instead — at the direct cost, per the ILO's own data, of Filipino women's working hours.

For a newcomer with children, the practical planning differs sharply. In Japan, start the daycare paperwork before you've told your employer you're expecting, and treat your contract type as a direct input into whether your child gets a spot at all. In the Philippines, the planning question isn't "which daycare" so much as "which relative," and whether your own household can realistically replicate the extended-family backup most locally raised children rely on by default.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Metropolis Japan, first-person essay — a Tokyo father described receiving "a thin envelope" from the ward office rejecting his son from public daycare, admitting the family had been "in serious denial" and never doubted he'd get a spot, only to discover the scoring system penalized his wife's part-time, graduate-student status.*

> *International Labour Organization, 'Role Reversal' case study — a Filipino marketing executive named Liza was documented working over sixty hours a week with a one-hour one-way commute, relying on a nanny simply to keep the arrangement functional, a routine the ILO frames as a structural "double burden" rather than an individual failure to cope.*

> *International Labour Organization, 'Role Reversal' case study — a former Filipino bank manager named Celine left her career after her husband pushed her to prioritize their daughters' school performance over her own job, a decision the ILO's researchers present as a common, not unusual, outcome.*

> *The Japan Times, reporting on government leave-uptake data — paternity-leave rates swung from 83.3 percent in the Finance Ministry to 10.9 percent in the Defense Ministry within the same national government, showing how dramatically a legally identical entitlement can function depending entirely on departmental culture.*

> *Philstar.com, reporting on the pending PAPA Leave bill — the bill's drafters included a provision that a father who takes paid leave but doesn't actually perform caregiving duties could be held liable under the country's domestic-violence statute, an enforcement mechanism with no real precedent in how paternity leave is typically legislated elsewhere.*

### Conclusion

If you're planning a family inside either system, the single most useful thing to know going in is which structure is actually going to do the work: Japan's state apparatus, slow and point-based as it is, or the Philippines' extended family, informal and overburdened as it often is. Neither should be mistaken for the other, and neither, per the data above, is as reliable as its own country's official rhetoric suggests.

I'd tell a friend relocating with young kids the same thing either way: find out who's actually going to be holding the baby at two in the afternoon on a Tuesday — a ward-office spreadsheet or your mother-in-law — because the answer tells you more about your coming years than any leave policy on paper ever will.
