# Philippines or Hong Kong: Where Time Off Is a Right on Paper and a Negotiation in Practice

🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Hong Kong guarantees its workers a minimum of seven days of paid annual leave and the Philippines guarantees five, which sounds like a rounding error until one notices that Hong Kongers actually use theirs. The median employee there took 16.5 days off last year, second in Asia-Pacific, while the Philippine statute remains a modest five-day suggestion that most good employers quietly ignore. For anyone weighing a posting in Manila or Hong Kong, the question is less what the law grants than what the manager will sign.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the leave clause in your contract before accepting, since the legal floor is only five days | Assume the statutory five days is what your employer offers, because many offer 12 to 15 |
| Submit leave requests in writing and keep the email trail, especially before Holy Week and Christmas | Rely on a verbal nod from a busy supervisor and then simply go on leave |
| Ask whether sick days draw from the same pool as vacation days | Expect a separate sick leave allowance, since the law does not mandate one |
| Ask about holiday pay or compensatory time off if you are asked to work a holiday | Assume holiday work converts automatically into a day in lieu |
| Check whether unused service incentive leave converts to cash when you resign | Forget to claim it in your final pay |

### Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Count your leave in years of service, as entitlement climbs from 7 to 14 days over nine years | Expect 20 days in your first year, as a European might |
| Stitch leave around public holidays for four or five day breaks | Plan a single three-week trip and expect applause |
| Confirm the holiday calendar, which now includes Easter Monday and will grow to 17 days by 2030 | Treat the 15 statutory holidays as a perk when they are a legal minimum |
| Negotiate the leave clause and the notice period in the same conversation | Accept the statutory minimum from a finance employer without asking |
| Take the rest day and use it, as the law gives at least one in every seven days | Mistake an answered 11pm email for a mandatory reply |

## Philippines

The Philippine Labor Code is admirably brief on the subject. Article 95 grants a service incentive leave of five paid days a year to employees with at least a year of service, and, as law firm NDV Law summarises, unused days are generally commutable to cash. Establishments with fewer than ten employees are exempt, as are employers who already provide five days of paid vacation. The statute is, in effect, a floor beneath a floor.

What employers actually offer is another matter. According to Shield GEO Services, an employer-services consultancy, many companies give 12 to 15 days a year, which leads staff to assume the legal minimum is higher than it is. The same source notes that the Philippines has no mandated paid sick leave, so the careful employee spends vacation days on the flu. The Philippine Star noted back in 2012 that the country has 12 regular holidays under the Labor Code, plus assorted special and local ones, and that employers regularly argue that this generosity costs productivity. The labor sector counters that the benefits were hard won. Neither side has ever been in a hurry to settle the matter.

The Star's columnist also tallied the specialised leaves then on the books, among them seven days of paternity leave, seven for solo parents and ten for victims of violence, and concluded that a determined employee could be absent for 185 of 303 working days. The exercise was theoretical, the author conceded, and the country, he added, ranked among the top three in ASEAN for holidays and leaves and among the bottom three for productivity. Those figures are fourteen years old and the law has moved since, but the argument they illustrate has not. It is the most Philippine of economic debates, conducted at length and resolved by a long weekend.

The culture around leave reflects a hierarchy more than a statute. Hofstede Insights scores the Philippines at 94 on power distance, among the highest in the world, which helps explain why a request for leave can feel like a petition to a minor sovereign. In practice, expats report that approval depends on the supervisor, the client schedule and the season. This is, one concedes, a perfectly human arrangement, though it is difficult to put on a spreadsheet.

Shift-based employers add a wrinkle that no leave policy addresses. One poster on a Philippine workplace forum described working nine days in a row because rest days rotate weekly to suit the client, and a team leader's answer was that scheduling follows client needs. The days off exist, in the sense that they exist somewhere on the roster.

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong's Employment Ordinance is more specific and, arguably, more rigid. According to the Labour Department, annual leave starts at seven days after the first year and climbs one day a year to a maximum of 14 days at nine years of service. Statutory holidays currently stand at 15, with Easter Monday added from 2026, Good Friday from 2028 and a seventeenth day from 2030. Employees on continuous contracts also receive at least one rest day in every seven. Hofstede Insights rates Hong Kong at 68 on power distance and 17 on indulgence, a combination consistent with a place that respects the org chart and distrusts leisure.

The remarkable part is that people take the leave. According to a Deel analysis of more than 4,500 full-time workers across Asia-Pacific, reported by Human Resources Online in June 2026, Hong Kong employees took a median of 16.5 days of annual leave in 2025, and 68.6 percent used at least 80 percent of their entitlement, second only to Singapore. They favour brevity, with 37.5 percent of multi-day requests covering just two days, and they tend to pair leave with public holidays. It is the vacation equivalent of eating lunch at the desk.

The statutory minimum is not much of a guide to the experience. The Instant Group found that Hongkongers averaged about 41 working hours a week with roughly seven days of annual leave, as reported by the South China Morning Post, and that just over one in four said they were happy at work. The same Deel data found that employees on flexible leave arrangements across the region took more time off than those on fixed contracts, which suggests that the cheapest way to improve a holiday culture is to stop auditing it.

The hours behind that restraint are hard to ignore. Census and Statistics Department figures cited by Hong Kong Labour Rights put median weekly hours at 43.4 last year, with more than 220,000 employees working at least 60 hours a week. The same analysis notes that Remote.com ranked Hong Kong 44th of 60 economies for work-life balance. The leave is real, in other words, but it is taken from a long working week.

Parental leave is where Hong Kong's rules look most orderly. The same analysis puts statutory maternity leave at 14 weeks, paid at up to 80 percent of wages. A newcomer should still read the fine print, since an employer can top this up or leave it exactly where the ordinance does, and the ordinance is not known for its imagination.

## The Reckoning

Head to head, Hong Kong wins on enforceability and the Philippines wins on nothing the statute can claim. Hong Kong gives a higher floor, a rising ladder, a larger holiday calendar and a labour department that publishes its guidance in plain English. The Philippines offers five days and a strong tradition of employers doing better than the law, which is excellent news for employees at good companies and no news at all for the rest. The distinction matters most for someone arriving without local bargaining power.

On actual use, the comparison favours neither country cleanly. Hong Kongers take their days but work long hours around them, and the same Hofstede restraint that keeps the calendar full keeps the inbox open. Filipino workers have more holidays on the calendar and more discretion, or discretion held by someone else, over the days between them. The sensible expat in either place reads the contract, not the statute, and treats the manager as the real legislator.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit — an employee in the Philippine outsourcing sector said their supervisor told them to email leave before Holy Week, then never answered. They planned to take the leave anyway and brace for a notice to explain, which rather defines the gap between approved and tolerated.*

> *Reddit — a customer success manager in the Philippines said their contract offered only five vacation days a year and asked whether working a holiday earned time off or extra pay, since the paperwork was silent. Nobody in the thread seemed shocked by the question.*

> *Expat.com — expats in a thread about Philippine working conditions disagreed on whether labour rules are applied at all. One described local staff at an international firm as well treated and paid roughly triple the going rate, while another called the rules mostly useless for household and shop workers.*

> *South China Morning Post — a veteran Hong Kong journalist wrote that extreme overtime was routine in her newsroom. She described working from 10am to 2am for more than six months before illness forced more than a year away, which is one way to use up leave.*

> *MoneySmart — twenty expats in Hong Kong mostly treated the long hours in finance, law and consulting as a worthwhile trade, and said hiking trails and islands made the weekends feel earned.*

## Conclusion

If paid time off is a deciding factor, Hong Kong offers the firmer legal footing and a culture where taking leave, in two-day instalments, is normal. The Philippines offers a lower statutory floor and, at the better employers, more generous practice, with a good deal more rides on the individual supervisor. Either way, read the leave clause and the notice period before the visa, and put every request in writing.

The honest summary is that both places give you the days you can prove you were promised, and a paper trail is the only reliable travel insurance.
