# Romania Will Give You Vacation Days. Sweden Will Make You Take Them.

**🇷🇴 Romania · 🇸🇪 Sweden**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Consider the bureaucratic math first. Romania's Labour Code guarantees 20 working days of paid annual leave; Sweden's Annual Leave Act guarantees 25. On paper, that is a five-day gap, a rounding error, barely worth changing continents over. In practice, it is closer to a chasm, because a striking share of Romanian employees treat their entitlement as a theoretical right rather than a cashable one — a 2018 eJobs Romania survey, still the most cited data point on the subject, found that one in five took no vacation at all that year, while Swedes treat theirs as closer to a civic obligation, with entire offices and whole sectors of the economy functionally closing for July. Hofstede Insights' cultural-dimensions index has a tidy word for the gap: indulgence, its measure of how comfortable a society is with simply enjoying itself. This is a guide to what that one word looks like on a Tuesday in August, for anyone about to sign the paperwork.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇷🇴 Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Request leave in writing and keep a copy — the 20-day entitlement is real, but enforcing it is a paperwork sport | Assume unused days just vanish; by law they must be carried over or paid out, but only employers who remember that |
| Plan around the "punte" (bridge-day) calendar early; 2026's roughly 16 public holidays fill the popular minivacanțe fast | Expect a holiday on a weekend to shift to a weekday — Romania, unlike some neighbors, doesn't move it |
| Negotiate extra contractual leave where you can; many Bucharest private-sector offers add 3–5 days above the legal floor | Be surprised when colleagues check email on vacation — per the eJobs survey, four in ten say they routinely work through it |
| Set a travel budget before you set dates; national surveys regularly find most Romanians say they can't afford a week-long trip away from home | Mistake the legal minimum for the national habit — only 17% of surveyed employees said they actually use their full allotment |
| Keep the Labour Inspectorate (ITM) in mind as a real escalation path, not a bluff, if leave is withheld | Let carried-over leave sit unresolved for years; it has a documented habit of quietly disappearing into HR folklore |

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Submit summer leave requests by spring; Swedish teams often lock in July plans as early as April | Schedule anything client-facing in July — much of the country, and its inbox, is functionally offline |
| Expect, and budget around, your semestertillägg — the legally mandated holiday-pay top-up on top of normal salary | Treat the four-consecutive-week summer block as optional; the law permits it and most people simply take it |
| Learn fika as a scheduling unit, not a snack break — it structures the workday around deliberate pauses | Feel virtuous for skipping lunch to work through it; colleagues will read that as a management problem, not dedication |
| If you become a parent here, plan to actually use your share of the 480 days of shared paid parental leave | Assume the 25-day statutory floor is generous by local standards; many kollektivavtal agreements push it closer to 30 |
| Price in Sweden's cost of living, especially in Stockholm, before comparing vacation days to your old salary | Expect to simply replicate a US or UK paycheck; relocation threads describe real, sometimes steep, pay cuts for the extra time off |

### Section: Romania

Romania's statutory floor is unremarkable by European standards: 20 working days, consistent with the EU's general expectations and a touch below the regional norm. What distinguishes Romania is not the entitlement but the follow-through. According to Eurostat's labour-cost data, Romanian employees average among the lowest hourly compensation in the EU, around €13.6 an hour including social contributions, a fact that correlates uncomfortably well with the reluctance to spend money on travel while unpaid bills wait at home. Romania-Insider's reporting on national surveys has repeatedly found that more than half of Romanians say they cannot afford a seven-day holiday away from home in a given year — which makes "use it or lose it" leave policy read less like a perk and more like an unfunded mandate.

The compensating pleasure is the calendar itself. Romania's roughly 16 to 17 public holidays in 2026 cluster conveniently enough that savvy employees build entire minivacanțe around a single well-placed day of annual leave — the national sport of hunting for a "punte," or bridge day, between a holiday and the weekend. It is a culture that has made an art form of stretching five days into nine, even as the eJobs Romania survey found that 57% of employees took ten days off or fewer across the entire previous year, and a full one-fifth took none. Four in ten employees admitted to working regularly during whatever vacation they did take, which rather undercuts the point of having a vacation at all — though it does explain why a Romanian inbox rarely runs an honest auto-reply.

### Section: Sweden

Sweden's Annual Leave Act sets the floor at 25 days, and the Swedish state does something Romania's framework does not: it pays you extra to take them. The semestertillägg, or holiday-pay supplement, guarantees a top-up — a legal minimum of 0.43% of monthly salary per vacation day for salaried employees, often higher under collective agreement — specifically so that taking leave never costs you money relative to working. Employees are further entitled to take four of those weeks consecutively between June and August, and, per reporting in The Local Sweden, at least 20 of the 25 days must be used within the year rather than banked indefinitely. The result, confirmed by employees who have relocated for Swedish jobs, is not merely permission to disconnect but active discouragement from doing otherwise.

This spills into national infrastructure. Swedish media, including Sveriges Radio, have documented the practice of sommarstängt — shops, clinics, and entire companies posting "closed for the summer" signs for weeks at a stretch, an arrangement that would strike a Romanian employer as an act of commercial self-harm. Leave culture also extends into parenthood: Sweden's 480 days of shared parental leave per child, financed through Försäkringskassan, normalizes extended absence for fathers as much as mothers, reinforcing the broader cultural premise that a life well lived includes substantial, unapologetic time away from the office. The cost of that premise shows up on the payslip — Stockholm's living costs are high, and more than one relocating professional has reported a meaningful pay cut in exchange for the extra weeks off.

### Section: The Reckoning

Strip away the atmosphere and the comparison is less about days granted than days actually spent. Sweden's 25-day floor, taken almost in full by social expectation and reinforced by cash incentives, functions as a real benefit. Romania's 20-day floor, undermined by economic anxiety and a culture of quiet presenteeism, functions for a meaningful share of the workforce as a number on a contract rather than a number on a calendar. The legal gap is five days; the lived gap, per the available survey data, is considerably larger.

The honest trade is this: Sweden sells you certainty of rest at the price of a flatter paycheck and a country that is genuinely, structurally quiet for a month; Romania sells you a cheaper cost of living and a denser, more improvisational holiday calendar, at the risk that "paid leave" quietly becomes "leave you're too anxious to take." Neither country is lying about its vacation policy. They are simply describing two different relationships between a worker and their own time off, and only one of them currently backs that relationship with a legally mandated bonus for using it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — a tech worker weighing a Stockholm relocation from the Bay Area found the better work-life balance real, but was warned by commenters that Nordic software salaries can run 50% or more below US pay for the same role, making the vacation gain a direct trade against take-home pay.*

> *NBC News (named interview) — an American who moved to Malmö for a tech job said she was still adjusting, a year in, to her 30 contractual days off; she submits summer-leave requests in April, and by July more than half her incoming emails are vacation auto-replies.*

> *Avocatnet.ro Romanian legal forum — a restaurant cook who had gone three years without taking any annual leave asked how to recover it; a labor lawyer confirmed the leave was legally owed and could only be cashed out at termination, and another poster suggested filing with the Labour Inspectorate if the employer kept stalling.*

> *Yahoo News/CNN Style (TikTok interview) — a Canadian who relocated to Sweden said she felt guilty taking vacation her first year, until her employer required at least three consecutive summer weeks off and she realized four or five weeks away was simply what everyone around her did.*

### Conclusion

If the deciding question is "will I actually get time off," the honest answer favors Sweden: the law, the money, and the social pressure all point the same direction, toward the exit. If the deciding question is "can I afford to live well on what's left," the honest answer is more complicated, and Romania's lower cost of living does real work to close a gap that its vacation culture does not. Neither government is hiding anything; Romania's labor code and Sweden's are both public documents, and both countries' survey data on who actually uses their leave is freely available to anyone willing to read past the brochure.

Pack accordingly: in Sweden, pack for five consecutive weeks, because the office will be closed anyway; in Romania, pack for a long weekend, and bring your laptop, because statistically, you will probably open it.
