**In Lagos the Meeting Starts When Everyone Agrees It Has. In Vienna It Started Three Minutes Ago, Without You.**

**🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇦🇹 Austria**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

A 9 a.m. meeting in Lagos is a negotiation before it is a meeting: over whether 9 a.m. meant 9, whether the traffic excuses everyone equally, and whether anyone actually intends to discuss the agenda or simply establish, once more, that you are a person worth doing business with. A 9 a.m. meeting in Vienna is a meeting at 9 a.m., with a circulated agenda, a seating order nobody wrote down but everyone obeys, and a hard stop at 10. Both systems produce deals, on roughly the same timeline, by the time the ink is down. Neither side believes the other's method could possibly work, and both are quietly right about why their own does.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up on time yourself, even knowing others may trickle in over the next half hour | Assume "we will see" is agreement — in context it is often a polite no |
| Spend the first ten minutes on family, football, or food before the agenda | Skip small talk to "get to the point" — it reads as cold, not efficient |
| Address seniors by title — Chief, Doctor, Engineer, Alhaji — until told otherwise | Challenge a senior colleague's position openly in the room; raise it privately after |
| Accept offered food or drink; declining can read as declining the relationship itself | Treat a verbal agreement as the agreement — get it in writing before you leave |
| Build in slack before and after every meeting for Lagos or Abuja traffic | Schedule back-to-back meetings assuming any one of them starts on time |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive five minutes early — "on time" already reads as cutting it close | Improvise an agenda item on the spot; it reads as unprepared, not nimble |
| Circulate a precise, written agenda in advance and follow it to the minute | Drop formal titles or move to first names before you're invited to |
| Use "Sie" and full professional titles (Herr Doktor, Frau Magister) until told otherwise | Oversell with enthusiasm or hard-sell tactics — Austrians read hype as insincerity |
| Bring data, sourcing, and a rehearsed answer for every line on the agenda | Cancel or reschedule at the last minute — it is read as a serious breach of trust |
| Wait to be given the floor, and let the most senior person speak first | Mistake a slow, methodical decision process for indecision or disinterest |

Nigeria's meeting culture runs on relationship capital rather than the clock. Cyborlink's business-etiquette profile scores Nigeria at a Hofstede Power Distance of 80, meaning the most senior person in the room leads it and decisions flow downward afterward, not during discussion; the room defers first and debates, if at all, later and privately. Punctuality is flexible enough that local shorthand calls it "Nigerian time," and the Lagos commute does the rest of the damage: Gallup's 2026 State of the Global Workplace found 55% of Nigerian employees reported significant stress the previous day, well above the global average of 40%, with commuting cited across multiple local business accounts as a primary driver of meetings starting late and running defensive from the first agenda item onward. None of this means the meetings are unserious — it means seriousness is demonstrated differently, through patience, hospitality, and a willingness to let the agenda wait for the relationship to catch up. FM Magazine's 2017 interview with Zenith Bank marketing manager Odia Babby underscores the fix professionals actually use once the small talk is done: insist on documented plans by email, because the warmth of the room does not reliably survive the drive home.

Austria runs the opposite experiment. businessculture.org's meeting-etiquette guide describes Austrian managers working from detailed, pre-circulated agendas "followed rigorously," with meetings starting and ending on schedule, documentation expected in German, and every participant arriving having done the reading. Cyborlink's profile adds the procedural choreography: the highest-ranking person enters first, subordinates stand, and the floor is given rather than taken — speak when you are asked to, not when you have something to say. Oddly, Hofstede Insights' dimension scores — cited once, because one citation is the house limit — put Austria's Power Distance at just 11, among the lowest ever measured, implying a flat, consultative culture. In the meeting room this reads less as equality than as *procedural* equality: everyone, regardless of rank, follows the same rigid script, which is its own kind of hierarchy, just one enforced by the agenda rather than the org chart. Austrians are also, per Cyborlink, allergic to exaggeration and hard-sell enthusiasm, preferring arguments that lean on data and precedent over charisma.

The Reckoning is this: Nigeria's meetings are slow to start and fast to bend; Austria's are fast to start and nearly impossible to bend. A Nigerian negotiator expects the stated terms to move, repeatedly, through a long back-and-forth that Cyborlink calls standard practice, with opening offers understood by both sides as opening offers. An Austrian negotiator, per the same etiquette literature, regards an opening position as close to a final one, and reads a counterpart who keeps revising the ask as either poorly prepared or not serious about the deal. Each side, handed the other's playbook, would conclude the opposite number is wasting their time — the Nigerian side finding Austrian rigidity cold and unnegotiable, the Austrian side finding Nigerian flexibility evasive and possibly dishonest. Both read "see you at nine" and plan, without realizing it, for a different nine entirely.

Put the two in the same room and the comedy writes itself: the Austrian arrives at 8:55 with a printed agenda and three contingency slides; the Nigerian arrives closer to 9:15, having already decided, over the drive in, that the deal depends more on who picks up the phone next Tuesday than on anything the agenda says. Both walk away convinced they handled the meeting correctly, because by their own country's rules, they did.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *BusinessDay NG — a Lagos commuter, quoted in a 2020 opinion piece on the city's meeting culture, summed up the toll of in-person-only business as: "This is no longer work, it is suffer-head. Dear Boss, can I work from home?" The same piece noted fewer than one in five of the author's business partners were comfortable conducting meetings over Zoom or Google Docs as late as 2020, keeping everyone in traffic to show up in person.*

> *Expat Exchange — an expat profiled in the site's Vienna guide described the adjustment to Austrian bluntness directly: Austrians are very direct, which can come off as rude at first, "but it's just their way of being honest." The same account flagged lateness to a meeting or social event as read as outright disrespect, not a personality quirk.*

> *FM Magazine — Odia Babby, a marketing manager at Zenith Bank interviewed on Lagos business culture, advised newcomers to hold regular meetings and "insist on documented plans through emails or other means," because verbal commitments in the room rarely survive the commute home.*

> *FM Magazine — Abiodun Olorunnisola, head of risk management at Stanbic IBTC Pension Managers, put Lagos deal-making plainly: trust, not the agenda, is what closes business, while "traffic in Lagos is a nightmare, and it is the number-one killer of productivity," the thing that quietly wrecks the schedule everyone agreed to that morning.*

### Conclusion

Neither system is the malfunctioning one. Nigeria's meetings optimize for the relationship that will outlast any single contract; Austria's optimize for the document that will survive any single disagreement. Move for work and you will need both skills, in reverse order of what feels natural: in Lagos, slow down and let trust do the work the agenda can't; in Vienna, speed up, write it down, and stop treating the agenda as a suggestion.

The honest advice, stripped of brochure gloss: in Nigeria, block out ninety minutes for a sixty-minute meeting and spend the first third on nothing you'll bill for; in Vienna, show up five minutes early with a printed agenda and don't expect anyone to admire your flexibility, because they won't.
