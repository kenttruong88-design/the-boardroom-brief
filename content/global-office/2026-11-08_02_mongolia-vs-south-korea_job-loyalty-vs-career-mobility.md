# Mongolia Will Let You Leave. Korea Would Prefer You Didn't.

🇲🇳 Mongolia · 🇰🇷 South Korea

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In one country, quitting after eighteen months barely registers as news. In the other, it can still follow you into a reference check a decade later. Mongolia's labour market is young, informal, and allergic to the idea that staying put is a virtue; South Korea's is aging, formalized, and only reluctantly admitting that its younger workers have started treating tenure as a trap rather than a trophy. South Korean business press has reported that young "chwijunsaeng" (job-prep) workers now leave their first job after an average of 1.7 years — practically a rounding error by the standards of the system that raised them. Both countries are having the same argument about loyalty. They are just having it at wildly different volumes.

## Do's & Don'ts: Mongolia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect your new hire to have had two or three jobs in the last three years, and not read it as a red flag | Assume a signing bonus buys you long-term retention; it rarely does, and everyone knows it |
| Prioritize describing culture, flexibility, and growth potential in job postings — young Mongolian workers consistently rank these above salary in surveys | Lean only on compensation to compete for talent against companies offering better working conditions |
| Build informal mentorship paths, since formal internal promotion ladders are often thin or nonexistent | Expect university credentials alone to translate into job-readiness; employers widely report skills gaps |
| Accept that your best junior hire may be scouted by a competitor within the year | Take it personally — rapid movement is a structural feature of a small, youth-heavy labour market, not a personal betrayal |
| Invest in on-the-job training even if you expect to lose some trainees | Assume Ulaanbaatar's private-sector dynamics apply to public-sector or mining-sector jobs, which run far more rigid |

## Do's & Don'ts: South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand the seniority-based pay (hobong) system before negotiating anything | Expect your salary to track your output in year one; it will track your tenure first |
| Recognize that job-hopping, while rising fast among younger workers, is still viewed warily by older management in traditional chaebols | Announce an outside offer casually in conversation as leverage — it can read as a loyalty test failed, not won |
| Watch for cracks in the system: resistance to seniority pay is now openly reported in business press and inside firms like Samsung | Assume "lifetime employment" culture is dead just because headlines say Gen Z is job-jumping; it is eroding, not gone |
| Build relationships with your direct manager early — hierarchy still shapes internal mobility more than performance reviews | Push hard for a promotion out of turn; it tends to backfire reputationally even when the performance case is strong |
| If you're an outside hire, expect some skepticism about commitment until you've put in visible time | Expect fast, Mongolia-style lateral movement between firms to be read as ambition rather than instability |

## Mongolia: Loyalty Is Negotiable, Culture Isn't

Mongolia has one of the youngest labour forces in Asia, and the ADB's "From School to Work" research on youth employment there describes a market defined less by rigid career ladders than by scarcity of good formal jobs relative to the number of young, often well-educated people chasing them. The ILO's policy review on youth employment echoes this: skills mismatches are common, informal work is widespread, and the traditional expectation of staying at one employer for a career is simply not the operating assumption for most workers under thirty-five.

Mongolia's formal private sector is also simply thinner than its population of educated young graduates can absorb, a mismatch the ADB's research describes in some detail: universities keep producing graduates faster than the formal economy produces the kind of structured, ladder-style roles those graduates expect. The predictable result is a labour market where lateral movement between the limited number of decent employers isn't a sign of restlessness so much as the only available career strategy.

What's notable is what Mongolian workers report actually wanting. According to a survey from the Ministry of Labour and Social Protection, young job-seekers consistently rank workplace culture, organisational values, and flexible hours above salary as reasons to stay — or leave. That's a meaningfully different calculus from the stereotype of "job-hoppers chasing the next paycheck." If anything, Mongolia's labour market rewards employers who can articulate why staying makes sense culturally, because purely financial retention offers tend not to work when a competitor two blocks away in Ulaanbaatar is offering a better manager and the same money.

Underneath the mobility is a structural fact worth naming plainly: Mongolia's economy still leans heavily on mining, and commentary on its "Dutch disease" dynamics — a resource sector so dominant it distorts the rest of the economy — has been a recurring theme in coverage of Ulaanbaatar's labour market for over a decade. That concentration means the private-sector jobs outside mining and government are disproportionately smaller, newer, and more precarious, which only reinforces the job-hopping pattern: workers move because the alternative, in a thin market, is betting everything on one employer's survival.

## South Korea: The Seniority System Is Cracking, Slowly

South Korea built one of the most durable loyalty-based labour systems in the industrialized world, and it is now visibly straining. The Korea Times reported in 2025 on growing resistance to the seniority-based pay structure (hobong) from younger employees who see tenure-based raises as disconnected from actual contribution — and crucially, this resistance is now coming from inside major firms, not just activist unions. Coverage of labour tension at Samsung Electronics, reported by East Asia Forum, has framed generational friction over pay and promotion as a genuine strategic headache for Korean corporate HR, not a fringe complaint.

Korea JoongAng Daily has separately tracked a rise in "job-jumping" among younger Korean professionals, a trend that would have been borderline scandalous a generation ago and is now openly discussed in business coverage as a labour-market shift companies have to plan around. None of this means Korean corporate culture has become Mongolia-casual about tenure — far from it. Hierarchy, seniority, and the social weight of "loyalty" remain deeply embedded, reinforced by everything from seating charts to drinking-culture norms at after-work dinners. But the gap between the system's stated values and its younger employees' actual behaviour is now wide enough that Korean HR departments are writing policy memos about it, which is itself the story.

There's a darker mirror image to this churn, too. Korean business press has separately reported that employment instability among middle-aged and older workers in South Korea is among the highest in the OECD — meaning the system that promises loyalty a payoff in your fifties is, for a growing share of workers, failing to deliver on that promise at exactly the point they most need it. Seen from that angle, younger workers abandoning the seniority bargain early isn't recklessness; it's a rational read of what happens to the people who kept their end of it.

## The Reckoning

Mongolia's labour market never really pretended loyalty was the point; it's small, young, and informal enough that movement is simply how talent finds its price. South Korea built an entire social contract around the opposite premise — stay, and the system will eventually reward you — and is now watching a generation quietly, then not so quietly, opt out. Hofstede's long-term orientation scores put South Korea among the highest in the world, a culture built to defer gratification for structural stability; Mongolia's labour behaviour looks almost deliberately short-term by comparison, though that's survival pragmatism as much as cultural preference.

The practical upshot for anyone relocating: in Mongolia, your resume's job-hopping will barely raise an eyebrow, but your employer will expect you to reciprocate the flexibility you're asking for. In Korea, that same resume may need a better story ready for the interview panel, even as the country visibly argues with itself about whether that skepticism is still earned. Give it another decade, and don't be surprised if the two countries' labour markets end up looking less like opposites and more like Korea slowly, grudgingly, catching up to where Mongolia already is.

## Conclusion

If you're hiring in Mongolia, build your retention pitch around culture and flexibility, not just salary, and don't take the inevitable departures personally. If you're navigating South Korea's labour market, understand that the seniority system is real, still functioning, and actively contested all at once — which means your own career strategy there should hedge: respect the hierarchy you're standing in while quietly tracking how fast its floor is shifting underneath you.
