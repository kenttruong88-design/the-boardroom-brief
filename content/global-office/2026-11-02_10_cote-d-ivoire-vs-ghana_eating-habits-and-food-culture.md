**Chocolate's Biggest Exporters Still Can't Agree on What's for Dinner**

🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire · 🇬🇭 Ghana

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana supply roughly half the world's cocoa between them, a statistic that embarrasses both nations slightly, since neither has much of a domestic habit for eating the finished product. According to the FAO's food balance sheets, the two neighbours also spent the past three decades eating in opposite directions. Côte d'Ivoire's daily calorie supply fell by more than seven percent between the early 1980s and the late 2000s, while Ghana's climbed by nearly two-thirds over the same stretch, overtaking its neighbour by the end of it. One country has been eating leaner. The other has been eating considerably more, largely on credit extended by a cocoa harvest that pays neither of them particularly well. For anyone weighing a move to Abidjan or Accra, the food question turns out to be less about taste than about logistics, inflation, and who exactly controls the ladle. The short version: one country eats on a fixed timetable with unpredictable shelves, and the other eats on an unpredictable budget with remarkably reliable shelves. Choose your inconvenience accordingly.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Eat with your right hand from a shared bowl at a maquis | Reach in with your left hand, even to pass a condiment |
| Order attiéké with grilled fish and expect one generous plate | Expect a starter, dessert, or a tidy three-course structure |
| Stock up on butter, cream, or yogurt the moment you see it | Assume the supermarket will restock dairy on any fixed schedule |
| Learn basic market French for haggling over produce | Expect English to get you a fair price at a roadside stall |
| Budget time for slow, informal meal service | Expect punctual seating or a set mealtime in most households |
| Try kedjenou or garba from a known street vendor | Skip the maquis scene assuming "real" dining means a hotel restaurant |

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a waakye breakfast from a street vendor before 7am | Expect to "grab something" mid-morning once the rush has sold out |
| Buy tomatoes and peppers in bulk at harvest and freeze them | Shop only off-season and assume West African prices stay low |
| Eat fufu or banku by scooping with your right hand | Chew banku or fufu — it is swallowed, not bitten |
| Carry cash or mobile money for informal food vendors | Expect a card reader at a chop bar or roadside stall |
| Budget well above guidebook estimates for groceries | Trust the "cheap living" reputation once you've seen a receipt |
| Ask a vendor where dairy or wheat products are sourced | Assume imported packaged staples are priced like they are at home |

### Côte d'Ivoire

Côte d'Ivoire's plate has not historically needed much embellishment. Yam remains the dominant staple, at close to 187 kilograms consumed per person annually according to FAO data, trailed by cassava, with rice and maize playing distinctly supporting roles. The country's red meat consumption runs roughly double that of most of its coastal neighbours, and its signature dishes — attiéké, the fermented cassava couscous now recognised by UNESCO, and kedjenou, chicken stewed slowly with vegetables in a sealed pot — reward patience over speed. What the FAO's longer trend line reveals, though, is less comforting: total daily calorie availability actually fell by more than seven percent between the early 1980s and the late 2000s, and animal protein availability dropped by nearly a quarter. The country that ships out roughly a third of the world's cocoa beans was, by the numbers, eating a measurably thinner diet than it had a generation earlier.

Much of daily eating happens outside the home kitchen, through an informal economy that the World Bank estimates employs close to seven million urban workers, more than agriculture or the formal private sector combined. Two-thirds of informal firm owners are women, many of them running the maquis — open-air grill restaurants — that structure Ivorian social life more reliably than any restaurant guide could. As of 2018, roughly a third of urban households were already cutting food consumption or pulling children from school to cope with costs, a reminder that the maquis economy is resilience dressed up as hospitality. Newcomers accustomed to tidy three-course dinners should recalibrate: a single generous plate, loose timekeeping, and no dessert course is simply how the meal is built, not a restaurant having an off night. The CFA franc's peg to the euro has, at least, kept that plate's price relatively stable compared with much of the region, which is a modest comfort when the supply chain itself is the variable you actually have to manage.

### Ghana

Ghana's dietary trajectory has moved in almost the opposite direction. FAO figures show daily calorie availability rising by roughly two-thirds between the early 1980s and the late 2000s, with protein availability up by about half and poultry consumption increasing more than fivefold, largely through imports. Cassava, at over 200 kilograms consumed per person a year, edged out yam as the leading staple, while fruit and vegetable availability nearly doubled over the same period. Much of this diet is assembled before sunrise by an informal workforce that the Ghana Statistical Service estimates accounts for roughly 27 percent of GDP and employs at least eight in ten working Ghanaians. Street vendors selling waakye, the rice-and-beans breakfast staple, routinely start cooking around three in the morning so that nurses, drivers, and market traders can eat on the way to work rather than before it — the country's answer to a commuter coffee chain, minus the loyalty app.

The reputation of West Africa as a cheap place to eat has not survived contact with recent Ghanaian inflation data. The World Bank's Africa's Pulse report recorded food inflation reaching 37.2 percent in September 2022, among the highest in Sub-Saharan Africa, alongside a cedi that lost roughly 60 percent of its value against the dollar that year, the worst currency performance in the region. Cocoa farmers, meanwhile, have had limited reason to celebrate the recent global price boom: Ghana sets its cocoa producer price administratively, and IFPRI research notes that despite a 58 percent increase announced for the 2023/24 season, many farmers found illegal small-scale mining a more reliably profitable use of their land than the crop the country is famous for growing. The arithmetic is not subtle: a nation that exports roughly a fifth of the world's cocoa is simultaneously watching its own grocery bill outrun almost every other country in the region it sits in.

### The Reckoning

Here is the shared irony neither country particularly advertises: together, Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana account for roughly half of the world's cocoa bean production, and neither has built much of a domestic chocolate-eating culture to go with it. The beans leave as raw commodity; the finished bar comes back, if it comes back at all, as an imported luxury stocked in the supermarkets aimed at expatriates. The two countries' broader dietary paths, meanwhile, have diverged in a way that flatly contradicts their shared cash crop and shared coastline: Côte d'Ivoire's measured calorie supply fell across three decades while Ghana's climbed by nearly two-thirds over the identical period, a gap wide enough to suggest two different development stories dressed in the same cocoa-farmer uniform.

Culturally, the overlap is real but easy to overstate. Hofstede Insights groups both countries within a broadly similar West African profile — strongly collectivist, high power distance, meals understood as a shared and hierarchical event rather than an individual transaction — and the communal eating customs in both Abidjan and Accra bear that out. Where the two genuinely split is currency and nerve: Côte d'Ivoire's CFA franc peg kept its food-price swings comparatively boring through the 2022 global shock, while Ghana's freely floating cedi turned a grocery run into a genuine economic indicator. One nation's food culture runs on patience and a fixed exchange rate; the other runs on hustle, street vendors who open before dawn, and a currency that keeps everyone paying close attention.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Talesmag (Accra post report) — A diplomatic family found they needed to ship in peanut oil, olive oil, masa harina, and their preferred hot sauce, since fresh whole wheat was hard to source reliably — but they grew to genuinely love Ghanaian food, calling it very inexpensive for anyone willing to eat it.*

> *Talesmag (Abidjan post report) — One poster described a simple rule for produce: if you see something you want, such as asparagus, buy it immediately, because it might not reappear on a shelf for weeks or months afterward.*

> *Expat.com forum (Côte d'Ivoire) — A French expat who cooks most of his own meals said his biggest challenge was sourcing butter, cream, and yogurt, so he started making his own yogurt; he was also struck by enormous single-dish portions, no dessert course, and loose respect for mealtimes.*

> *Expat.com forum (Ghana) — One poster called the country's cheap-living reputation a myth after pricing bread, milk, and tomatoes at what felt like European levels; another pushed back, noting that buying organic bread, non-dairy milk, and bulk seasonal tomatoes to freeze keeps the real cost far lower than a bad week at the supermarket suggests.*

> *Ghana News Agency (reader-sourced reporting) — A waakye vendor in Tema said she starts cooking around three in the morning, since getting the pot ready early is what keeps her stall competitive against the dozens of others along the same road.*

### Conclusion

The practical difference for a relocating professional is less about cuisine than about which kind of uncertainty you would rather manage. In Côte d'Ivoire, prices move slowly and the inconvenience is supply — dairy, imported staples, and anything resembling specialty cooking arrive sporadically, so residents learn to buy on sight and cook in batches. In Ghana, supply is generally fine; it is the price tag that moves, sometimes by double digits within a single year, which rewards anyone willing to shop seasonally, freeze produce, and treat the local chop bar as more dependable than the imported-goods aisle.

Either way, nobody eating well in Abidjan or Accra is doing it by accident, and nobody is doing it with the chocolate their own country grows. If you take one piece of advice to a dinner party in either capital, make it this: compliment the cook, not the currency — in Ghana, at least, you may be asked to explain yourself, and in Côte d'Ivoire you will simply be handed a second plate before you have finished the first, dessert or no dessert.
