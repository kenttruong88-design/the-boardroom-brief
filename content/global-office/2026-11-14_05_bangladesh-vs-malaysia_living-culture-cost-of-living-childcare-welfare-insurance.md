# Bangladesh Will Rent You a Flat for Pocket Change and Bill You Separately for the State; Malaysia Sends the Invoice to a Pension Fund

🇧🇩 Bangladesh · 🇲🇾 Malaysia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A one-bedroom flat in Dhaka can be had for roughly $200 to $400 a month, according to ExpatExchange, a figure that induces a brief and unwise optimism in the prospective emigrant. In Kuala Lumpur, Numbeo puts the equivalent at about RM1,826, but Malaysia has, since October 2025, also begun enrolling foreign employees in its pension fund. Both countries are cheap by Western standards. They differ chiefly in who is expected to pay when the cheapness ends: in Bangladesh, the household; in Malaysia, the household, though with paperwork and a statutory contribution first.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate rent with a local colleague present, since forum posters report foreigners being quoted prices almost double | Accept the first figure from a broker because it sounds cheap in dollars |
| Budget for electricity as a volatile line item, not a fixed one | Assume an air-conditioner is a one-time purchase |
| Arrange private health cover or an employer plan before arrival | Rely on the public system or on any form of state insurance |
| Price private tutoring into any school budget for children | Treat tuition fees as the whole bill for schooling |
| Choose a neighbourhood such as Gulshan, Banani or Bashundhara with your commute in mind | Pick an address on rent alone and discover the traffic afterwards |
| Confirm maternity terms in writing, including the six-month service threshold | Assume leave entitlements apply from your first week |

### In Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your employer how the October 2025 EPF rule applies to your work pass | Assume you are exempt because your contract says you are a short-term hire |
| Buy private health insurance with generous annual and lifetime limits | Treat SOCSO as medical insurance, since it covers employment injury and related benefits |
| Book childcare before relocating, and ask about holiday closures | Expect a nursery place in central Kuala Lumpur at short notice |
| Keep receipts for childcare, since tax relief is claimed after the fact | Count on the relief to ease monthly cash flow |
| Live where you can commute without a car, or budget for one | Choose an isolated campus-adjacent suburb without transport |
| Request EPF withdrawal documentation when you leave | Leave the country without proof of departure and termination |

## Bangladesh

On price alone, Bangladesh wins, which is precisely what makes it dangerous to quote. ExpatExchange lists a nice one-bedroom at $200 to $400 a month, a two-bedroom at $400 to $800, and a restaurant meal at $5 to $15, though the page dates from April 2023. Forum posters on Expat.com, writing mostly in 2010 to 2012, add that foreigners are often quoted prices nearly double the local rate and that costs vary sharply between Gulshan and everywhere else. The same thread prices a doctor's visit at about 500 taka and a month of household groceries at 3,000 to 4,000 taka, which tells you mostly how old it is. Treat all of these as the lower bound of a negotiation, not a quotation.

The more serious problem is that prices are moving. According to Bangladesh Bank figures reported by IANS, citing The Daily Star, headline inflation averaged 9.21 percent in the final quarter of the 2025-26 fiscal year, and food inflation reached 8.7 percent in June. Middle-class households, the report says, are cutting discretionary spending and delaying healthcare and education. A household survey by the Power and Participation Research Centre, covering more than 8,000 homes, found 69.59 percent naming rising prices their single biggest concern and 64.55 percent worried about their children's education. One Mohammadpur household described in the IANS report saw its two-bedroom electricity bill pass 3,000 taka in a month it had expected to stay under 2,000, despite rationing the air-conditioner.

Welfare is where the brochure runs out. According to the ILO's social protection platform, effective coverage of at least one social protection benefit in Bangladesh is 28.4 percent, only 0.4 percent of the population is affiliated to health insurance, and public spending on health is 0.7 percent of GDP. UNESCO's education monitoring report, cited by Prothom Alo, finds that families bear 71 percent of education costs, and that 67 percent of urban families were paying for private tuition as of 2010, up from 48 percent in 2000. A government fee policy from 2024 caps non-tuition charges at 2,465 taka a year for state-supported schools in city corporation areas, which is helpful, and says nothing about the tutoring. Maternity leave runs to 16 weeks, according to Rivermate's summary of the Labour Act, but requires six months of service. The state, in short, is a rumour; the family is the welfare system.

## Malaysia

Malaysia is more expensive and considerably more legible. Numbeo, on a page updated this month, reports a city-centre one-bedroom at RM1,825.69, an inexpensive meal at RM15, basic utilities at RM221.67 and an average net monthly salary of RM3,822.03. Private full-day preschool averages RM852.52 a month per child, while international primary school averages RM50,919 a year, with a range from RM25,000 to RM122,110. The range is the part to read twice.

The welfare architecture is real, and since 1 October 2025 it reaches expatriates. According to ASEAN Briefing, non-permanent-resident foreigners on valid work passes now contribute 2 percent to the Employees Provident Fund, matched by 2 percent from the employer, unless the EPF Board grants a written exemption; the savings can be withdrawn on departure with proof. Employers also contribute 1.25 percent of wages, up to a RM6,000 ceiling, to SOCSO's employment injury scheme. Female employees receive at least 98 consecutive days of maternity leave, fully paid after 90 days of service in the preceding nine months, and employers may not dismiss a pregnant employee for pregnancy, according to the Department of Labour.

Childcare is where the statute meets the market. Malay Mail, citing the Department of Statistics, reports that childcare centres in Kuala Lumpur fell from 218 to 193, an 11 percent decline, while demand rose. Mothers quoted in the same report pay RM1,200 to RM1,500 per child per month, and the tax relief extended in Budget 2026 is paid after the fact and only to those who owe tax. Health is a separate matter: an Expat.com thread of some vintage cites RM30,000 for a complicated appendicitis case and more than RM80,000 for a heart attack, which is the argument for private cover, expressed in ringgit. The same thread, now about eleven years old, put a live-in or live-out maid at RM1,250 to RM1,800 a month, which is the figure that tends to survive in expatriate memory. ExpatExchange contributors add that foreigners may use public hospitals without enrolling, while private hospitals cost more and respond faster, a trade-off familiar to anyone who has queued anywhere.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, the two countries offer a clean trade. Bangladesh sells low rent and charges the difference in risk: inflation of 9.21 percent, health insurance affiliation of 0.4 percent, and a family expected to finance most of its children's schooling. Malaysia sells higher rent, a mandatory pension contribution, paid maternity leave and a functioning if thin childcare market, and charges the difference in monthly outgoings and in tax paperwork. Neither is a bargain in the way the spreadsheet suggests. Each simply sends the bill to a different address, and the prudent expatriate learns the address before the first invoice arrives.

Hofstede Insights, for what a 1980s survey framework is worth, scores power distance at 80 for Bangladesh and 100 for Malaysia, with individualism at 20 and 26 respectively. Both societies, in other words, expect the household and the hierarchy to absorb shocks before the individual does. The practical consequence is that an expatriate's employer is likely to matter more than any ministry: ask what the company covers, in writing, before asking what the country does.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com (Dhaka forum) — Posters from roughly 2010 to 2012 said foreigners are often quoted nearly double the local price, put a furnished two-bedroom in Gulshan at 50,000 to 70,000 taka, and priced a three-bedroom with driver and maid at about $1,500 a month. One felt Dhaka was expensive for the amenities on offer, especially after dark.*

> *ExpatExchange — A Dhaka resident described food, transport and housing as relatively inexpensive compared with other cities in the region, while noting that affluent neighbourhoods cost more than rural ones.*

> *Prothom Alo — A Bashundhara parent said a Class 5 daughter costs nearly 10,000 taka a month once tutoring and transport are counted, and more than 16,000 taka with a younger child in nursery, and that quality schooling leaves the family no real choice.*

> *Expat.com (Kuala Lumpur forum) — A family of three, expecting a fourth, asked whether RM14,000 a month would do. Most replies said yes, one local said RM7,000 would manage, and several warned that medical costs, insurance limits and maternity cover were the items most likely to go wrong.*

> *Malay Mail — Working mothers in Kuala Lumpur said they use neighbourhood babysitters because licensed centres are unaffordable, and that a year-end tax rebate does little for families who must pay fees upfront each month.*

## Conclusion

If the aim is to stretch a salary, Dhaka does it with the least friction and the least protection, provided you can absorb inflation near 9 percent; if the aim is to raise children with a pension, a maternity clause and a hospital that bills in a recognisable currency, Kuala Lumpur costs more and explains itself better. Either way, negotiate the package before the visa: private health cover, school allowances and a written account of the EPF position are worth more than any discount on rent.

The honest summary is that both countries are affordable until someone gets ill, has a child or turns sixty. Plan for those three events and the rest is merely a question of which currency the disappointment arrives in.
