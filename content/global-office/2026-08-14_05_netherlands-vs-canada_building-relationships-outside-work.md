# Everyone Will Be Nice to You. Almost No One Will Become Your Friend.

**🇳🇱 Netherlands · 🇨🇦 Canada**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The Dutch, according to research from the Netherlands Organisation for Scientific Research, replace nearly half their personal connections — 48% — every seven years, and yet breaking into an established Dutch friend group as an outsider is notoriously slow going. In Canada, 85% of employers believe workplace friendships improve retention and actively encourage them, while roughly half of Canadian job seekers say they deliberately avoid socializing with colleagues outside work at all. Two countries, two entirely different flavors of the same experience: everyone is pleasant, almost no one is easy to actually get close to.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Propose specific plans weeks in advance — "coffee sometime" gets nothing, "coffee on the 14th at 4pm" gets a yes | Expect a spontaneous invite or drop-by visit — it's genuinely not how Dutch social life works |
| Join something recurring — a sports club, a course, a weekly borrel — repetition builds trust faster than one-off events | Take direct feedback from Dutch colleagues personally — bluntness is a communication style, not hostility |
| Show up consistently to the same group over months — this is how outsiders actually get "in" | Assume friendliness in English conversation means you've been invited into someone's inner circle |
| Attend borrelen (after-work drinks) when invited — it's a genuine, low-stakes entry point | Expect to be invited to someone's home quickly — that's a much later-stage signal of closeness |
| Be patient — expat and local social clocks run on different timelines here | Give up after a few unanswered invitations — Dutch calendars are just genuinely booked out weeks ahead |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take "we should get coffee sometime" as sincere but non-binding — following up yourself is expected | Assume workplace friendliness will naturally extend into a real friendship without deliberate effort |
| Use organized channels — company social groups, volunteer days, sports leagues — Canadians engage more readily there | Take it personally if colleagues keep work and personal life strictly separate — it's a common norm, not a snub |
| Expect real regional variation — smaller cities can be harder to break into if everyone already has an established circle | Assume all Canadians are equally reserved — this varies significantly by city and by whether you're in an immigrant-dense area |
| Text or message first if you want to move a work relationship toward friendship — initiative is generally well received | Expect topics like religion or politics to come up early — reserve them for once a relationship is established |
| Give it real time — many Canadians report friendships taking years to solidify, not months | Assume Canadian politeness equals emotional openness — the two are frequently reported as quite separate things |

### Netherlands: Warm, Direct, and Extremely Hard to Enter

Dutch social life runs on planning and repetition rather than spontaneity. Multiple expat guides, including [Undutchables](https://undutchables.nl/about-us/blog/the-dutch-and-their-agendas), describe agenda culture as a defining, near-universal feature: proposing a coffee two weeks out is completely normal, while vague "let's hang out soon" invitations tend to go nowhere. [IamExpat](https://www.iamexpat.nl/expat-info/dutch-news/friendship-or-networks-netherlands-part-1) reporting notes a related cultural distinction — Dutch social connection is frequently activity-based rather than emotionally disclosive, with people doing something specific together (a drink, an event) rather than the open-ended emotional check-ins more common in Southern European friendship norms.

The NWO's 48%-turnover-every-seven-years statistic initially reads as evidence of a fluid, easy-to-enter social culture. In practice, per multiple expat accounts, it more likely reflects the fact that Dutch people are actively reshuffling within an already-established framework of activity-based connections — clubs, courses, work — rather than casually admitting newcomers into existing tight circles. Getting in as an outsider means joining one of those repeating structures and showing up consistently, not waiting for an organic invitation.

### Canada: Structurally Friendly, Structurally Separate

Canadian workplace culture, per a Harris Poll survey conducted for Express Employment Professionals, shows a real split: 85% of employers believe workplace friendships boost retention and actively try to cultivate them, offering lunch events, retreats, and volunteer days as vehicles. Yet on the employee side, roughly half of job seekers — 49% — say they deliberately try not to engage with colleagues outside work, and 54% say they don't feel the need to be friends with the people they work with, according to the same [Benefits Canada](https://www.benefitscanada.com/news/bencan/survey-finds-85-of-employers-believe-workplace-friendships-support-increased-employee-retention/) reporting. Canadian workplace norms lean toward keeping personal and professional life genuinely separate — many employees hesitate to invite coworkers for drinks precisely because it might read as unprofessional, the opposite instinct from Dutch borrelen culture, where after-work drinks with colleagues are the norm rather than the exception.

The result, widely reported by newcomers, is a specific kind of friction: Canadians are reliably pleasant on first contact, but converting that pleasantness into a real friendship takes deliberate, sustained effort — and some longtime residents report it never fully happening at all.

### The Reckoning

Both cultures produce the same complaint from outsiders — "everyone is nice, but I can't seem to make real friends" — for structurally opposite reasons. The Dutch are direct and organized about it: friendship has a visible process (recurring activities, advance planning) that, once understood, is learnable and repeatable. Canadian friendliness is softer and less structured, which paradoxically makes it harder to reverse-engineer — there's no clear on-ramp to follow, just accumulated time and initiative with no guaranteed payoff.

Hofstede Insights data shows the two cultures scoring closely on individualism, both landing among the more individualist nations measured, which explains why neither society organizes friendship around collective obligation the way more collectivist cultures do. The meaningful difference shows up elsewhere — the Netherlands scores notably low on masculinity, consistent with its more consensus- and activity-oriented social style, while Canada's culture is often described as comparatively indulgent, favoring individual enjoyment and looser structure, which tracks with its less scripted, less predictable path to real friendship.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A longtime resident who moved to Canada as an adult wrote that after twelve years they still hadn't made what they'd call a real friend, describing most Canadians they'd met as maintaining a permanent "arms-length" pleasantness that never quite closed the distance, no matter how many casual interactions accumulated.*

> *r/Netherlands — An expat in Amsterdam observed that Dutch colleagues would happily chat in fluent English at work, but the same people never extended an invitation home or into their existing friend group — the language was open, the social circle was not.*

> *Internations — A member of the Amsterdam community described InterNations welcome events as functionally necessary rather than optional for newcomers, since they'd found no other reliable route into the city's social life beyond joining structured expat-oriented gatherings first, before slowly branching into more local ones.*

> *r/expats — Someone comparing their experience across postings said the single best predictor of finally making Dutch friends was choosing one recurring activity, in their case a rowing club, and simply not missing a session for several months — six weeks of the same group, they said, did more than six unrelated events combined.*

### Conclusion

If you're relocating for the social life as much as the job, know what you're optimizing for. The Netherlands rewards structure and patience — pick a recurring activity, plan two weeks out, and the door does eventually open. Canada rewards persistence without a clear roadmap — friendliness is abundant and easy, but converting it into something deeper is a longer, less predictable project with no guaranteed finish line.

What I'd tell a friend moving to either: in Amsterdam, put something recurring in your calendar before you've even unpacked; in Toronto, say yes to the coffee and then actually schedule the next one yourself — nobody else is going to chase you down to do it.
