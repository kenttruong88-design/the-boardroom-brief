# Kenya Waits for the Boss, Britain Waits for You to Guess What 'Interesting' Meant

🇰🇪 Kenya · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries share English as a business language, a fondness for tea and a deep suspicion of the meeting that could have been an email. They then part company completely on what a meeting is for. According to Hofstede's cultural data, Kenya scores 70 on power distance and 25 on individualism, while the United Kingdom scores 35 and 89. Put simply, one country expects the senior person to speak last and be obeyed, and the other expects everyone to speak at once, mostly to apologise. For anyone weighing a move between them, the vocabulary will be familiar and the grammar of the room will not. A Briton in Nairobi may be baffled that a meeting is allowed to be pleasant. A Kenyan in London may be baffled that it is allowed to be over in twenty-five minutes, with nobody having said what they meant.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet everyone present with a handshake before any business begins | Open a meeting with the agenda and skip the preamble |
| Use titles and surnames until a Kenyan colleague signals otherwise | Switch to first names on day one because it feels friendly |
| Book meetings well ahead and confirm them two or three days before | Assume a calendar invite is a commitment |
| Arrive on time yourself and take lateness from others calmly | Make a point of someone else's arrival at 9:00 for an 8:00 meeting |
| Let each attendee speak and take every contribution seriously | Cut a long contribution short to protect the schedule |
| Frame disagreement with a senior person as a respectful question | Challenge the boss in front of the room |

### United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read tone, not just words, and ask for clarification when unsure | Take "that's an interesting idea" as approval |
| Soften requests into questions ("would you mind...") | Issue plain imperatives in emails |
| Arrive on time and finish on time | Overrun, then apologise at length |
| Use first names from the first handshake | Wait to be invited to drop the title |
| Offer modest self-assessment and let results speak | Narrate your own achievements in a team meeting |
| Join the post-meeting drink or tea round | Treat the informal chat as optional |

## Kenya: The Meeting Is the Relationship

The Cultural Atlas notes that Kenyan meetings open and close with handshakes, business cards and small talk, that seating generally follows rank, and that discussions run long because everyone is expected to contribute. It also records that a meeting set for 8:00 may not properly begin until about 9:00, a flexibility known as "Swahili Time." This is less chaos than a different theory of what a meeting is for. The agenda is the pretext; the relationship is the product. The same source advises booking appointments at least two weeks ahead and warns that rushing tends to lengthen discussions, not shorten them, which is the sort of finding that ought to be framed and hung in every project management office.

Hierarchy does the rest. A Kenyan press piece on local work culture describes deference to bosses discouraging honest upward feedback, so that speaking up requires careful calculation, and observes that leaving on time can seem like a statement. The practical result is that the person who says least in the room may have the most to say afterwards, to one trusted colleague, in private. The Cultural Atlas adds that in the public sector superiors make decisions and pass them down, while in private firms staff generate more of the ideas but the boss still makes most of the calls. Visitors who read silence as agreement tend to learn otherwise in the following quarter.

The context matters too. According to Kenya's Economic Survey 2025, as reported by The Star, the country added 848,200 jobs in 2024, bringing total employment to 20.8 million, and roughly 90 percent of the new jobs outside small-scale agriculture were informal. A formal office desk is therefore a prized possession, and people protect it with courtesy, caution and a well-maintained WhatsApp group. Expat Arrivals notes that English is widely used in business and that the working day runs roughly eight or nine to five, so the language will not trouble you. The subtext will.

## United Kingdom: The Meeting Is the Preamble to the Email

Britain's relationship with meetings is one of mutual resentment. A 2018 study by eShare, reported by Workplace Insight, found office workers spending around 10 hours and 42 minutes a week preparing for and attending meetings, with 2.6 of the 4.4 weekly meetings judged unnecessary. A more recent Raconteur and Attest survey of 1,000 UK professionals, published in June 2025, found that 18 percent spend more than ten hours a week in meetings. The same eShare study put the average meeting at 6.8 attendees, and costed the unnecessary ones at more than £35,000 per business per year, or over £191bn across the economy. Raconteur's reporting adds that almost one in ten employees spends more than 15 hours a week in meetings, and that some 90 percent of UK and US workers suffer a "meeting hangover" at least occasionally. Nobody has been formally rescued.

What sets the British apart is how they say things. Experts for Expats observes that disagreement is routinely expressed through understatement, that requests are phrased as suggestions, and that praise is restrained. "Not bad" is applause; "I'm not sure that's quite right" is a demolition order. Hierarchy exists but is camouflaged. The chief executive will cheerfully be called Dave, then will not be contradicted by anyone under the rank of director. Experts for Expats also notes that emails soften instructions into polite questions, and that confidence is expected to come wrapped in modesty, with performance left to speak for itself. A Hacker News commenter with manufacturing experience offers the best field guide: a British supplier begins with understatement and grows blunter only as the problem worsens, which is useful if you can survive until the blunt part.

The reward for decoding all this is a flat, efficient, punctual meeting that ends on time and produces an action list. The price is that nobody will tell you, in plain language, that your presentation went badly. You will learn it from the silence that follows, the invitation list for the next one, and a colleague who says that it was brave.

## The Reckoning

Kenya's meetings are slower, warmer and more hierarchical, and they reward patience and relationship-building. The UK's are shorter, cooler and nominally egalitarian, and they reward the ability to hear the opposite of what was said. In Nairobi the signal is who speaks and who stays silent. In London the signal is which adjective was chosen.

For a newcomer, Kenya is easier to read but slower to move, and the UK is quicker but treacherous in translation. Neither culture is dishonest. Both simply bury the truth in a different place: in Kenya under rank, in Britain under politeness. Pick the burial ground you are better at excavating. The mechanics are simple: where authority is steep, authority speaks and juniors read the room, and where the individual is king, each person is expected to hold a view but express it with deniability. Punctuality offers a final contrast. A Kenyan colleague may forgive a late arrival with a shrug, while a British one will forgive it with a smile and remember it until retirement.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — paraphrased experience: An American who spent three years in English workplaces found communication weaker than back home, with plain instructions often unclear and training patchy. They also noted that after-work drinking and smoke breaks were the real route into the team, and that on birthdays you bring your own cake. Another member of the thread disputed the grimmer parts of the account, so treat it as one person's view.*

> *Expat.com — paraphrased experience: A Swedish intern who spent six months in Kenya found business practices familiar from the UK but looser on timekeeping. Their advice was to arrive on time anyway, since local colleagues forgive lateness while foreigners are expected to be punctual, and to expect frequent personal questions about family that deserve real answers.*

> *Hacker News — paraphrased experience: A commenter raised in Africa and now living in London described taking polite British requests phrased as questions at face value, answering them literally, and then wondering why nothing happened. Another commenter in the same thread said British understatement routinely trips up Americans, who hear "I would prefer not to" as a flat refusal.*

> *K24 Digital (Kenyan press) — paraphrased experience: A Kenyan commentator argued that the communal warmth of the office is a genuine strength, but that bonds can quietly turn into pressure. Saying no too often reads as cold, and the advice offered is to set quiet personal limits rather than make public stands.*

## Conclusion

If you are moving to Kenya, budget extra time, learn the handshake sequence and cultivate one senior ally who will tell you what the room actually thought. If you are moving to the UK, translate every adjective downward by two grades and treat the pub as a mandatory second meeting room. In both countries, the agenda on the invitation is merely a suggestion.

Whichever you choose, you will spend a good deal of time in rooms where the real decision is made somewhere else. The only difference is whether they have the decency to serve you a biscuit first.
