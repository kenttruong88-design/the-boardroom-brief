# The Long Lunch and the Early Cold Plate: Greece, Austria, and the Hours You Will Lose to Eating

**🇬🇷 Greece · 🇦🇹 Austria**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Athens, a restaurant may expect you to occupy a table for two hours and consider this a modest ask; in Vienna, dinner can arrive at six in the evening, consist of bread and cold cuts, and be over before the Greek table has ordered dessert. Both countries will tell you, with total sincerity, that they eat well. Both are correct. The difference is that one culture treats a meal as a destination and the other treats it as a brief, efficient stop on the way to the rest of the evening, and a relocating employee who assumes the two run on the same clock will spend their first month either starving at 7 p.m. or waiting, increasingly impatient, for a bill that nobody is in a hurry to bring.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇷 Greece

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clear two hours for a sit-down dinner, not one | Expect the bill to arrive unprompted |
| Say yes to the free fruit or yoghurt plate at the end of the meal | Say "just a little" when offered more food — it will be ignored |
| Shop the weekly *laiki* (farmers' market) for cheaper, better produce | Assume supermarket prices reflect what locals actually pay for staples |
| Expect dinner reservations from 9 p.m. onward in summer | Show up at a taverna at 6 p.m. expecting a full menu and a crowd |
| Tip in cash, modestly — service is rarely added automatically | Rush a table of elderly diners mid-conversation over coffee |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Eat your main meal at midday — lunch menus are cheaper and more substantial | Expect a hot, hearty dinner; it may just be bread, cheese, and cold cuts |
| Carry cash for small cafés and markets | Assume every restaurant takes cards |
| Shop on Saturday — nearly everything closes Sunday and public holidays | Leave grocery shopping for Sunday morning |
| Learn the word "Tischwein" and order wine by the glass without embarrassment | Confuse an invitation to dinner with a free pass — "I'll invite you" just means they're paying |
| Linger over a single coffee at a Kaffeehaus for as long as you like | Rush the waiter, or expect the bill before you ask for it |

Greece spends more of its household budget feeding itself than almost anyone else in the European Union. According to Eurostat, Greek households devoted roughly 16.9 percent of total spending to food and non-alcoholic drink, against an EU average closer to 12 percent that year — a gap that has narrowed but not closed since. This is not, mostly, a story about indulgence; it is a story about a food culture that still runs through small, independent shops and neighborhood markets rather than big-box retail. The weekly *laiki*, the open-air farmers' market that appears in nearly every Athens neighborhood on a fixed morning, remains cheaper and, locals insist, simply better than the supermarket equivalent, and a full week of produce, olives, and nuts can be had for under 25 euros — a detail several transplants independently discover and then can't stop mentioning to friends back home.

The paradox, and the OECD has the numbers on this, is that Greece's famous Mediterranean diet coexists with some distinctly un-Mediterranean health outcomes. The OECD places Greek adult obesity at roughly 34 percent and smoking at around 29 percent, both among the higher rates in the bloc, and attributes 77 percent of the country's cardiovascular disease burden to modifiable risk factors including poor diet, smoking, and inactivity. The olive oil and the long, convivial meals are real; so, increasingly, is the fried food and the fast-casual lunch squeezed between the dwindling number of hours anyone can afford to spend on an actual midday rest. A relocating employee inherits both halves of this inheritance, not just the postcard one — and tends to be presented, repeatedly and without irony, with more food than they ordered, because asking for "just a little" is treated as a conversational opening gambit rather than an instruction.

Alcohol follows the same quietly moderate-but-social pattern. Per the Union of Greek Brewers, Greeks drink roughly 35 liters of beer and 27 liters of wine per person annually — respectable, unremarkable figures by European standards — and a 2024 YouGov survey found people drink noticeably more when out than at home, which tracks with a culture organized around the table rather than the bottle. Overt drunkenness, several residents note, is genuinely rare; the two-hour dinner simply isn't built for speed-drinking.

Austria inverts nearly every one of these defaults. Eurostat's most recent household-budget data puts Austrian food spending at just 10.9 percent of total expenditure — one of the lowest shares anywhere in the EU, against a bloc average of 14.3 percent — which sounds like austerity until you notice that Austrians simply spend their money, and their calories, earlier in the day. Surveys from Austrian broadcaster ORF/Vol.at consistently find breakfast ranked as the meal Austrians are least willing to skip, and the Austrian default dinner — the "Kalte Platte" of bread, cheese, cold cuts, or a simple soup, eaten around six in the evening — is treated not as a deprivation but as sensible, time-efficient, and faintly virtuous. The large, hot meal of the day happens at lunch, when business lunch menus are genuinely cheap, and the kitchen closes early enough that showing up at a Gasthaus at nine expecting a full dinner service is a reliably bad idea.

Where Austria does not economize is beer. The Austrian Brewers' Association's own 2026 annual report puts national per-capita beer consumption at 91.2 liters a year — more than double the Greek figure, and among the highest rates in Europe — a statistic that sits oddly next to a food culture otherwise associated with restraint, punctuality, and closing the kitchen at a sensible hour. Wine gets more reverence than volume: order it as "Tischwein," house wine by the glass from an open carafe, and nobody blinks; treat the Kaffeehaus the way Vienna intends it, which is as a venue for owning one coffee for several hours while reading a newspaper, and the waiter will not hurry you, because hurrying you was never the business model. One geographic and economic detail is worth noting bluntly: Numbeo's grocery-price comparisons put Vienna consistently above Athens, even though Austrians spend a smaller share of household budgets on food overall — a reminder that "cheap to eat in" and "cheap to buy groceries" are not the same claim, and a relocating employee doing the arithmetic on a new salary should check both.

Hofstede Insights' country-comparison data gives some structural shorthand for why these habits diverge the way they do: Greece scores 100 on Uncertainty Avoidance — the highest recorded score of any country Hofstede has measured — against Austria's 70, and Austria's Power Distance score of 11 sits far below Greece's 60. None of that predicts a menu, but a culture that scores near the ceiling on discomfort with ambiguity produces rituals (the free dessert plate, the fixed market day, the extended goodbye) that remove uncertainty from a meal, while a flatter, lower-ceremony culture produces a dinner you can finish in twenty minutes and go back to your evening.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the real divergence isn't Mediterranean versus Central European cuisine — it's what each country considers an acceptable use of an evening. Greece treats dinner as the evening's main event, starting late and ending whenever the conversation does; Austria treats dinner as a formality to be dispatched before the actual leisure time begins. An employee relocating from Vienna to Athens should expect to eat later, stay longer, and be fed more than they asked for; one moving the other way should expect a smaller, colder, earlier plate and should not read it as the host being unwelcoming — it is, by local standards, simply Tuesday.

The irony sharpens on the drink side. The culture built around unhurried, three-hour tables (Greece) drinks measurably less beer per person than the culture built around brisk, efficient, lunch-is-the-main-meal dining (Austria) — proof, if proof were needed, that how long people sit at a table and how much they drink while sitting there are two entirely separate variables, and a planner who assumes otherwise will misjudge both the grocery bill and the Friday-night calendar.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum, Greece — A longtime resident described slowly absorbing local habits without noticing: iced frappé coffee in summer, the small complimentary plate of fruit or yoghurt that arrives unasked at the end of a taverna meal, and wine served in small tumblers rather than stemmed glasses. The adjustment, they said, happened by accretion rather than decision.*

> *My Big Fat Funny Life (personal blog), Greece — A visitor let a Kalamata restaurant owner choose their "light lunch" and received three plates of meze, grilled fish, greens, bread, olives, and a mountain of feta — followed, thirty minutes later, by a straight-faced offer of dessert. Their advice to future arrivals: never say "just a little," and pack stretchy clothing.*

> *The Local, Austria — A writer who grew up with Latin American dinners starting after 8 p.m. described resisting Austria's early, cold evening meal for years, convinced she was losing something. She eventually compromised at a 6:30 dinner of soup, bread, and cheese, and reported sleeping better, feeling less sluggish, and — unexpectedly — gaining less weight than on her old schedule.*

> *IES Abroad student blog, Vienna — An American student new to Vienna found grocery shopping intimidating with no German and a vegetarian diet to navigate. She settled into a routine built around Hofer for basics, Billa for variety, strict avoidance of Sunday shopping since almost everything closes, and the Too Good To Go app for discounted bakery surplus — getting a week of groceries down to just over fifty euros.*

> *Internationalliving.com (named feature essay), Greece — An American who relocated to Greece found inexpensive house wine at tavernas removed any temptation to overindulge, noting it's rare to see anyone visibly drunk, and that the neighborhood farmers' market consistently beat the supermarket on both price and produce quality.*

### Conclusion

The practical calculus for anyone actually making this move comes down to time, not taste. Greece will cost you hours — long lunches, longer dinners, a market day you have to plan around — in exchange for food that is cheap, fresh, and social by default. Austria will give those hours back by six in the evening, in exchange for a dinner that is smaller, colder, and considerably less negotiable about portion size. Neither is the "better" food culture; they are optimized for different lives, and Hofstede's numbers on uncertainty avoidance and power distance offer a decent clue as to why one culture ritualizes the table while the other streamlines it.

If you're the kind of person who finds a two-hour dinner restorative, go to Athens and clear your evening calendar without guilt. If you'd rather eat, talk for twenty minutes, and get back to your own life, Vienna will suit you — just do your grocery shopping on Saturday. Either way: pack the stretchy pants for Greece, and don't expect the Viennese waiter to rush your coffee just because you've finished drinking it.
