# The Annual Reckoning: How the United States and Singapore Each Pretend Feedback Is a Gift

🇺🇸 United States · 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In the United States, a manager will tell you that you are "crushing it" in a Tuesday stand-up and then, six months later, hand you a performance-improvement plan you did not see coming, because American feedback is optimized for morale in the moment and candor on paper. In Singapore, a manager may say almost nothing at all between reviews, on the theory that if your number were about to change, someone senior would have mentioned it by now, which is itself a kind of information if you know how to read silence. Both systems produce the same output — an annual document that decides your bonus, your visa sponsorship renewal, and your general will to keep showing up — but they arrive there by entirely different roads, and a new arrival who reads one culture's feedback in the other's grammar will misjudge, at some point, whether they are about to be fired.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 United States
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your manager directly, in your first month, how often they give feedback and whether reviews are tied to a forced distribution — most firms won't volunteer the quota. | Don't assume a glowing verbal review means a glowing written one; the two documents are often written for different audiences. |
| Keep a written log of praise, deliverables, and dates — in an at-will state, your own paper trail is your only insurance. | Don't skip the self-assessment form, even when it feels like busywork; unfilled boxes are read as disengagement, not modesty. |
| Treat a Performance Improvement Plan as a legal document, not a coaching conversation, and start job-hunting the same week. | Don't quietly accept a vague PIP metric like "communication issues" — ask for a specific, measurable example in writing. |
| Learn your manager's actual rating scale (stack-ranked, forced curve, or open) before you need to care about it. | Don't vent about your review on a company Slack or in a group chat — screenshots travel further than you expect. |
| Negotiate your next six months' goals explicitly at the end of every review — ambiguity now becomes your fault later. | Don't assume HR is there to help you; in most U.S. firms, HR exists to protect the company, including from you. |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pay attention to what is *not* said in a review — an absence of correction is often the only positive signal you'll get. | Don't interpret a quiet manager as an indifferent one; silence between appraisals is frequently deliberate, not neglectful. |
| Ask a trusted senior colleague, privately, how blunt feedback is "supposed" to be read at this specific company — it varies more than the stereotype suggests. | Don't publicly question a rating or ranking in front of peers, even diplomatically; raise disagreements one-on-one, afterward. |
| Clarify your bonus structure (AWS, variable component, grading against a curve) before your first review cycle, since it is rarely explained unprompted. | Don't assume you can decline or appeal a Performance Improvement Plan outright — employees generally cannot simply refuse one. |
| Build a habit of asking your manager for concrete examples when a rating feels generic, such as "poor communicator." | Don't expect your manager to proactively coach you the way a U.S. manager might; many consider that the employee's own responsibility. |
| Note that resignation carries real friction here — with job-hopping rarer, think two cycles ahead before reacting to one bad review. | Don't bring workplace grievances to government bodies like MOM as a first step; most disputes are expected to be resolved internally first. |

The American performance review is, on paper, a triumph of process. According to Gallup's long-running workplace tracking, only 31% of U.S. employees were engaged in their jobs in 2025, dropping to 29% engaged against 18% actively disengaged more recently — a share barely above the 26% recorded when Gallup started tracking engagement in 2000. The company line is that managers are supposed to fix this through regular, structured feedback; the inconvenient footnote is that only around 29% of American workers say anyone actually discussed their progress with them in the past six months, and just 27% received recognition for good work in the past week. The theatre of the sandwiched compliment — praise, criticism, praise — survives mostly as something managers are trained to perform rather than something employees report receiving.

What the American system is honest about is consequence. The Bureau of Labor Statistics put median U.S. job tenure at 3.9 years in January 2024, the lowest figure since 2002, a number that tracks neatly with a labour market where a bad review cycle can become a Performance Improvement Plan and a PIP can become an exit package within a single fiscal quarter. On forums like Blind, the debate among tech workers is rarely whether PIPs exist — everyone assumes they do — but which firms have quietly turned them into a quota, with Amazon repeatedly singled out as the company that, in the words of one often-cited thread, "bakes it in by mandate even when it is not necessary." The American review, in other words, is candid about the stakes and vague about the warning signs, which is precisely the combination that makes a PIP landing the week before a holiday feel less like paperwork and more like ambush.

Singapore's system inverts the problem. According to Randstad's Workmonitor survey of Singapore employees, 47% go through only one formal review a year, 88% say that review exists mainly for the manager to hand down feedback rather than receive it, and a full 15% report never being asked for their own input at all — even as 80% say they can, in theory, have an open conversation if they push for one. The gap between the theoretical openness and the practiced reticence is the whole story: Hofstede's cultural-dimension comparison scores Singapore well above the United States on power distance and well below it on individualism, which is academic shorthand for what every expatriate learns within a month — that junior staff here are generally expected to absorb instructions rather than interrogate them, and that correction travels down the hierarchy more easily than feedback travels up it.

### The Reckoning

Here is the twist that catches people moving between the two systems in either direction: the country that talks about feedback constantly is the one more willing to let you go over it, and the country that barely discusses it is the one where you are now least likely to leave. Singapore's Ministry of Manpower recorded a resignation rate of just 1.2% in the first half of 2025, the lowest since the metric was introduced roughly two decades ago, with average job tenure climbing to eight years — a trend recruiters have taken to calling "job hugging," in which workers hold on to roles out of caution rather than loyalty, wary of being last in and first out if they jump. Americans, true to form, quit and get cut at a pace that would read as organisational chaos in Singapore; Singaporeans, true to form, will absorb a vague "poor communicator" rating for years rather than risk the market, even while privately asking online whether they can refuse the improvement plan attached to it.

The honest comparison, then, is not which culture is kinder but which risk each one asks you to carry. In America, you are told clearly and often that you might be cut, and the system assumes you can simply find another job, which the 3.9-year median tenure suggests most people eventually do, willingly or otherwise. In Singapore, you are told rarely and gently that anything is wrong, and the system assumes you will stay anyway, which the 1.2% resignation rate confirms most people now do, by choice or by caution. Neither arrangement is dishonest, exactly — they are just optimized for different fears.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (r/jobs) — an employee finishing a Performance Improvement Plan was summoned to an unscheduled meeting with HR on the Friday before Christmas, with no agenda given beforehand; the anxious wait ended with news that they had narrowly passed, alongside a warning there would be no second chance.*

> *Reddit, via a People Matters SEA report — a foreign graduate hire in Singapore says the mentor assigned to guide him answered most questions with "figure it out yourself," and grew noticeably warmer only when senior managers were in the room.*

> *Blind — tech employees comparing notes describe Amazon's performance-improvement process as tied to an internal quota in a way that Google's and Microsoft's are not, with one commenter calling Amazon's version comparatively "generous" only next to firms that skip the warning step entirely and fire outright.*

> *TikTok, via Must Share News — an Australian expat three years into a Singapore job says she had to unlearn the instinct to challenge decisions in meetings, since juniors here are generally expected to hold their views until invited to share them, something she stresses is a difference in norms rather than a flaw in either one.*

> *An online forum post relayed by The Independent, Singapore — a employee placed on a Performance Improvement Plan after four years with no prior complaints asks whether she can refuse it or escalate to the Ministry of Manpower, after being told only that she was a "poor communicator"; most replies warn that a PIP is rarely optional and often precedes an exit already decided.*

### Conclusion

If there is one adjustment worth making before you land in either country, it is this: recalibrate what silence and praise actually mean before you trust either one. In the United States, warmth in the room is not a reliable predictor of what lands in the file, so build your own record and ask blunt questions about quotas and rating curves early, while you still have the standing to ask them casually. In Singapore, the absence of a correction is frequently the correction — the all-clear you're looking for — so learn to read a quiet manager the way you'd read a quiet smoke detector: no news is, for once, actually the news. The deeper difference is not directness versus indirectness, which is the brochure version everyone already half-believes; it is that one country has built an economy where you are expected to absorb shocks by changing jobs, and the other has built one where you are expected to absorb them by staying in the job you have. Pack accordingly, and if a friend asks me over drinks which system I'd rather be judged by, I tell them honestly: I'd rather be fired with a clear reason than kept on forever with none.
