**America Will Tell You to Just Ask. Singapore Will Tell You the Odds, Then Let You Decide.**

🇺🇸 United States · 🇸🇬 Singapore

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Only 7% of Singaporean professionals say they feel "extremely comfortable" asking for a raise, according to a Jobstreet by SEEK report — and yet 73% of the people who actually ask, succeed. In the United States, a B2BReviews survey finds over 80% of employees feel entitled to a raise, but only 60% intend to ask. Both countries have, independently, arrived at the same uncomfortable truth: the asking is the hard part, not the getting.

The American survey breaks the hesitation down with unusual precision: of those who stay silent, 32% say they simply don't know how to raise the subject, 28% fear outright rejection, 22% worry about job security, and 20% worry specifically about damaging the relationship with their manager. None of these fears, notably, is "I don't think I deserve more" — the B2BReviews data shows the median American worker wants roughly a 10% raise and believes, on some level, they've earned it. The gap is procedural anxiety, not a lack of conviction.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your state's pay-transparency law before applying — Illinois, Minnesota, New Jersey, Vermont, Massachusetts, and others now require posted salary ranges | Assume federal law requires this — there is no federal pay-transparency mandate, only a growing state patchwork |
| Research your "level" via Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, or Payscale before negotiating | Accept a title without confirming its level — being quietly down-leveled against comparable peers is common and hard to detect after the fact |
| Use a competing offer as leverage if you have one | Assume loyalty alone will trigger a raise — the data shows asking, not tenure, is what moves the number |
| Keep a running log of concrete accomplishments ahead of any comp conversation | Rely on your manager to remember your wins for you |
| Ask explicitly whether off-cycle raise budgets exist separately from year-end reviews | Assume there's only one annual opportunity to raise the topic |
| Verify which state's law applies if you work remotely for an out-of-state employer | Assume your home state's transparency law automatically covers a job based elsewhere |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Know that discussing pay with colleagues is legal and cannot be lawfully banned by an employer, even though social norms discourage it | Assume salary secrecy is a legal requirement — it's a cultural norm, not a rule, outside rare contract clauses |
| Clarify whether a quoted figure is base salary or total compensation, including CPF-equivalent elements and bonus | Negotiate against a number without confirming what it actually includes |
| Negotiate explicitly against Singapore's cost of living, not your home-country salary | Accept an offer calibrated to your previous market, especially if relocating from a lower-cost country |
| Factor in Singapore's comparatively low tax rates when weighing take-home pay against other offers | Compare only base salary figures across countries without adjusting for tax |
| Ask directly about relocation or joining bonuses | Assume they'll be volunteered without being asked |
| Check your contract for a salary-confidentiality clause before assuming full freedom to discuss pay | Assume every employer treats this identically — rare clauses exist |

The American pay-transparency patchwork is a genuinely new phenomenon rather than a longstanding norm: per the Littler law firm's 2025 tracker, Illinois, Minnesota, New Jersey, Vermont, and Massachusetts all introduced or activated disclosure requirements within the past two years alone, joining earlier movers like Colorado, California, New York, and Washington — a state-by-state rollout, not a national policy, which means the negotiating advantage of a posted range depends heavily on your zip code. The B2BReviews data adds useful texture: 67% of American workers say they don't discuss salary with colleagues, but of the minority who do, nearly a quarter (23%) actively use that information to negotiate, and a full 75% say they'd ask for a raise on learning a coworker earns more. Hofstede's figures put the US at 91 on Individualism, the highest of any country in this comparison series, which tracks with an ask-culture built on the assumption that advocating loudly for yourself is simply what competent professionals do — even though the same survey shows most Americans quietly don't.

The negotiation-industrial complex built around this anxiety is itself worth a line. Levels.fyi, the crowdsourced compensation-leveling platform founded in 2017 by Zuhayeer Musa and Zaheer Mohiuddin, grew a dedicated negotiation-coaching arm that reportedly helped clients secure a combined $31 million in salary increases, with some individual clients gaining $80,000 to $130,000 in first-year compensation, at a per-session price in the low hundreds of dollars. It's a small, telling data point: Americans will pay a stranger, by the hour, to say the thing their own culture insists they should already be comfortable saying for free.

Singapore inverts the confidence-to-outcome ratio almost perfectly. The Jobstreet figures show only 37% of Singaporean employees satisfied with their salary — among the lowest rates in the region — despite 71% believing they're paid fairly, a genuine gap between "fair" and "satisfying" that the report's own analyst, Samantha Tan, described as people feeling they're paid correctly but not meaningfully rewarded. NodeFlair's research adds the social layer: nearly half of Singaporeans consider discussing pay with others socially "inappropriate," a taboo that persists despite carrying no legal weight whatsoever. Hofstede scores Singapore at just 20 on Individualism and a strikingly low 8 on Uncertainty Avoidance — the lowest uncertainty-avoidance figure recorded for any country across this entire series, an oddly risk-tolerant number for a culture with a reputation for order and planning, and worth noting here without over-explaining it away.

### The Reckoning

The real asymmetry isn't confidence, it's information. American salary anxiety persists despite an increasingly transparent legal environment — the posted ranges exist, but the B2BReviews data shows most workers still won't act on what they imply. Singaporean salary anxiety persists despite a genuinely favorable success rate for anyone who does ask (73%), suggesting the barrier there is almost entirely social rather than structural or legal. Confidence and outcome, in other words, are tracking in almost opposite directions on either side of the Pacific — Americans feel entitled and don't ask; Singaporeans feel awkward, ask rarely, and succeed when they do. In both cases, the actual employer pushback appears to be smaller than the fear of it — which should be the single most useful sentence in this entire article for anyone about to have the conversation.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind, Singapore compensation thread — A commenter advising someone moving from an India-based role into a Singapore PM position recommended pushing for SGD 15,000–20,000 more on base than initially offered, warning explicitly that candidates relocating from lower-cost markets tend to get systematically lowballed relative to peers moving from the US or UK.*

> *Blind, Singapore offer-negotiation thread — Separate commenters recommended targeting SGD 10,000–15,000 or more above an initial tech-role offer, checking specifically for joining or relocation bonuses, and noted that visa sponsorship has become noticeably harder to secure amid recent layoff cycles.*

> *Blind, Singapore offer-negotiation thread — Another commenter flagged that Singapore's steep one-bedroom rents (SGD 3,000–5,000 a month) should be factored directly into the negotiated base figure, since initial offers rarely account for it on their own.*

> *r/MechanicalEngineering (via mirror) — Several engineers reported that internal raise requests essentially never produce meaningful movement in their field, and that switching employers entirely has become the real mechanism for a raise — a blunt rebuttal to the idea that American "ask culture" reliably delivers results.*

> *NodeFlair blog — The platform's own write-up on Singaporean workplace norms describes a widespread pattern of avoiding salary conversations "from embarrassment or fear of appearing over- or underpaid," even in the complete absence of any legal restriction — a useful corrective for arriving Americans who assume the country's efficiency implies US-style openness about pay.*

### Conclusion

If you're relocating for either market, the actual work is the same: find out what the number should be before you're in the room, and then say it out loud, because both countries' own data suggests the silence costs you more than the ask ever will. The American advantage is a legal one — use the posted range as your opening anchor where the law gives you one. The Singaporean advantage is a statistical one — remember that 73% success rate is real, even if it doesn't feel believable at 7% comfort. Either way: the brochure wants you to think the culture decides your salary. Mostly, it's just whether you ask.

One last comparison worth sitting with: Hofstede's Uncertainty Avoidance score for Singapore, at 8, is the lowest recorded for any country in this entire series — a strange number for a place whose salary culture reads, on the surface, as cautious and avoidant. Don't let the reputation substitute for the actual behavior of the room you're standing in. The data, repeatedly, says the room is more open to the conversation than it looks. Walk in with your market research done, your number ready, and the genuine expectation — backed by both countries' own statistics — that the conversation will go better than your nerves are currently predicting. The legal protections differ by location, but the underlying psychology, on both sides of the Pacific, is suspiciously identical: everyone is quietly waiting for someone else to say the number first, and the data above is as close as either country's workforce gets to simply admitting it out loud, so let your own well-researched number, stated plainly and well ahead of time, be the one that finally, mercifully breaks the silence in the room.
