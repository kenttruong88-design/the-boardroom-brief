# Lunchbox Diplomacy: Surviving the Table in Asunción and Taipei

**🇵🇾 Paraguay · 🇹🇼 Taiwan**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Paraguay, refusing a shared cup of tereré can read as refusing a handshake. In Taiwan, the real workplace test isn't what's on your tray — it's whether you can eat a chicken leg with chopsticks while your new colleagues watch and pretend not to notice. Both countries organize an enormous amount of social and professional life around food. Neither organizes it the way a relocation brochure suggests.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇾 Paraguay

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept tereré when it's passed to you, even if you don't finish it | Decline the shared cup outright — it's a trust ritual, not a beverage order |
| Learn the circulation order: one person holds the thermos, one pours, the cup moves in a circle | Pass the cup out of turn or top it up yourself; that's the server's job |
| Keep Sunday free for asado — it is treated as close to mandatory family time | Schedule a Sunday obligation without checking; you may be quietly skipping the week's main social event |
| Pace meals as a social occasion, not a pit stop | Eat and run; colleagues will notice if lunch is treated as a 10-minute refuel |
| Expect a carbohydrate-heavy, home-style daily diet — rice, pasta, cassava, potatoes | Expect much workplace catering; home-cooked or home-brought food is the norm outside asado |

#### 🇹🇼 Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get comfortable with chopsticks before you arrive — competence is assumed, not optional | Make a visible show of struggling; it draws attention in a culture that values quiet competence |
| Treat the night market as a legitimate, frequent dinner option, not a tourist detour | Assume cheap means low-quality; a NT$1.50 scallion pancake can outclass a sit-down meal |
| Accept the boxed lunch (biandang) at meetings graciously, even when it's mediocre | Complain openly about biandang quality in the room; grumbling about it afterward is the accepted outlet |
| Explore night-market vegetarian stalls — they are extensive and taken seriously | Assume vegetarian options are an afterthought; Taiwan's Buddhist-influenced vegetarian food culture is mainstream |
| Expect lunch to be efficient — a short, functional break between tasks | Expect a long, leisurely lunch hour on a normal workday; that's reserved for client meals, not desk days |

### Paraguay: Hospitality With Rules You Don't Get Told

Paraguay's food culture runs on two institutions: the Sunday asado and the daily tereré circle, and both carry social weight far beyond nutrition. Britannica's overview of Paraguayan daily life describes the asado — grilled beef and chorizo, with cassava, potato salad, and cornbread — as something "every family across the country, without fail" gathers around, which functions less like a cookout and more like the week's load-bearing social event. Missing it without a very good reason registers.

Tereré, the cold infusion of yerba mate drunk through a shared metal straw, is the other pillar, and it is explicitly about equality rather than refreshment: the cup moves through a group in a fixed circulation, and "if you share tereré, you share your heart," as the ritual is described by Paraguayan cultural documentation (terere.org). Colleagues use it the way other cultures use a coffee break — a recurring, low-stakes pause that happens to double as the main mechanism for building trust across a team. Outside these set pieces, the daily diet is plainer than visitors expect: rice, pasta, potatoes, and cassava dominate, with asado-level indulgence reserved for the ritual occasions rather than spread across the week.

### Taiwan: Efficient by Day, Theatrical by Night

Taiwan's workday food culture is built for speed. The boxed lunch, or biandang, is the default at meetings and seminars — not a perk so much as a logistical assumption, priced around NT$49 and, by the admission of the people who eat it constantly, often more "mediocre" than memorable, according to discussion on Forumosa, a long-running English-language forum for foreign residents of Taiwan. What biandang buys the workplace is speed and predictability, not culinary ambition; the actual food culture Taiwan is known for happens after hours.

Night markets are where that reputation is earned, and they are not a tourist layer sitting on top of ordinary life — they are ordinary life. A Taipei-based newsletter writer who eats near Shuangcheng Street market several times a week describes the markets as solving a real logistical problem for working families who need something "quick, delicious, and varied" after long days, at prices — a loaded scallion pancake for around $1.50, xiaolongbao for about $3 — that make the daily biandang look expensive by comparison (deiwei.substack.com). Vegetarian stalls are genuinely abundant rather than an afterthought, a reflection of Taiwan's Buddhist-influenced vegetarian tradition running alongside its reputation for pork and seafood. Hofstede's framework, for what it's worth, places Taiwan among the more long-term-oriented and uncertainty-avoidant cultures in its model — a useful, if abstract, way of explaining why the daily structure (efficient, provided-for, low-variance) and the after-hours culture (exploratory, varied, socially loose) can coexist without contradiction.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't formality versus informality — it's where each culture puts its ritual weight. Paraguay concentrates its food meaning into two recurring, almost sacred touchpoints (Sunday asado, the daily tereré circle) and leaves the rest of the week's eating fairly plain. Taiwan diffuses food meaning across the entire day — breakfast stalls, a quick biandang lunch, snack culture, and a night market dinner that can function as the evening's actual social event — so there's no single "main" ritual to learn, just a constant low hum of food-as-social-infrastructure.

The trap for a newcomer is applying the wrong instinct in the wrong country. Treat Paraguay's tereré circle casually — decline it, or handle the cup wrong — and you've misread a trust ritual as small talk. Treat Taiwan's biandang lunch as the main event and skip the night market because you're "not that hungry," and you've missed where the actual culture, and a fair amount of after-hours bonding with colleagues, is happening.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Forumosa (Taiwan expat forum) — A foreign resident admitted the hardest adjustment to office biandang culture wasn't the food itself but the exposure: "eating a chicken leg with chopsticks while your co-workers are looking on" turned a routine lunch into a small daily performance review of table manners nobody warned them about.*

> *deiwei.substack.com — A Taipei-based writer who treats the neighborhood night market as a weekly fixture rather than a novelty was struck that the food there is reliably healthier and cheaper than what passes for a quick meal back home — a pleasant surprise that inverted his assumption that fast, cheap food meant a nutritional trade-off.*

> *terere.org (Paraguayan cultural documentation) — The explainer on tereré customs makes a cultural misread easy to predict: treating the shared cup as just a drink, rather than understanding that the person who pours, the person who passes, and the fixed order of the circle are all doing social work — missing the sequence reads as missing the point entirely.*

> *reachtheworld.org (Paraguay food-culture field notes) — A visitor documenting Paraguayan home life learned, after the fact, that asaditos sold by street vendors in the evening aren't just dinner-on-the-go for tired workers — they're informal neighborhood meeting points, and skipping them means skipping a layer of community life that never appears on a restaurant recommendation list.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving for work, the practical question isn't "will I like the food" — you will, in both places — it's whether you can read where the social stakes are hidden inside something that looks like simple eating. In Paraguay, learn the tereré circle in your first week; it will do more for your standing on a team than any meeting performance. In Taiwan, don't let the workday's efficient, forgettable biandang convince you that food culture is an afterthought here — it's just been moved to after 6pm, and showing up for it matters. The honest version I'd tell a friend: in Paraguay, share the cup; in Taiwan, skip the pre-dinner snack and go to the night market hungry.
