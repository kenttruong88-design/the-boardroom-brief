# In Cairo the Boss Decides; in Frankfurt the Process Does, and Neither Will Say Thank You

🇪🇬 Egypt · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries have mastered the art of the hierarchy, and they have done it in opposite directions. In Egypt, authority travels through people: the manager is a figure to be known, consulted, flattered and, ideally, not offended. In Germany, authority travels through procedure: the manager is a role, the role has a job description, and the job description has a committee. Hofstede Insights scores Egypt at 80 on power distance and Germany at 35, which is roughly the distance between a family dinner and a tax audit.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest time in friendly relationships with colleagues and managers; personal rapport does real work here | Treat the first conversation as purely transactional and expect efficiency to follow |
| Give feedback privately and specifically | Criticise a senior colleague in front of the team, where it reads as embarrassment rather than information |
| Expect decisions to take consultation and time | Interpret silence in a meeting as agreement |
| Explain how any change affects reporting lines and responsibilities | Pitch change solely on commercial benefit |
| Stay on good terms with your direct manager | Assume that a poor relationship will end in a tidy dismissal; reports suggest pressure to resign is the favoured route |
| Keep conversation conservative, especially around women colleagues | Joke about cannabis, radical politics or religion |

### In Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use Sie and surnames until someone senior offers du | Switch to first names because the office looks informal |
| Agree responsibilities and deadlines explicitly | Expect your manager to read your mind, or your mood |
| Accept blunt feedback as feedback about the work | Wait for praise; silence is the compliment |
| Raise disagreements with data, risk assessments and a clear argument | Argue from enthusiasm alone |
| Keep personal life and work life largely separate | Suggest after-work drinks on day one |
| Stop working when work ends | Answer email at night to impress anyone |

## Egypt

The Egyptian workplace is hierarchical in the official sense and personal in the practical one. According to Commisceo Global, seniority, experience and position shape how managers establish authority, and employees expect managers to guide, solve problems and take responsibility for important decisions. The same guide notes that deference is not the same as having no opinions: employees may hesitate to challenge senior managers publicly, but silence does not necessarily mean agreement. A 2023 CIPD survey of Egyptian HR professionals, cited there, found 33% describing a formal, hierarchical workplace and 25% a family-oriented culture, which suggests the two are not mutually exclusive. Treat the sample as illustrative rather than definitive; the guide itself does.

Relationships are the working currency. A poster on the Expat.com forum described Egyptian offices as personal rather than professional, with close relationships, little personal space and long conversations about politics, appearance and food, provided the work still gets done. Suggestions for improvement, the poster added, can be heard as personal criticism, and a dispute either escalates or evaporates depending on whether colleagues like you. The same thread observed that people with connections to senior management may be handled cautiously, resented, or paid more. It is a system in which knowing the boss is a competence.

The cost shows in engagement. Egyptian Streets, citing a 2017 Gallup study, reported that 88% of Egyptian employees were not engaged or actively disengaged, against 85% globally, with only 12% engaged. Interviewees there said rewards for good work are rare while mistakes draw harsh criticism. Another Egyptian Streets essay, on gender and Cairo office life, adds that women in leadership are judged more harshly than men in the same chair, and that bias can survive even in progressive companies. If you are moving for a management role, expect to be asked to be both authoritative and approachable, and to be graded on both.

Managers, for their part, are expected to show interest in employees' circumstances and keep regular contact, according to Commisceo Global, while keeping clear professional boundaries and delivering criticism, where appropriate, in private. In practice that means the Egyptian manager is part supervisor, part patron and part pastoral service, a job description no German employment lawyer would recognise. The reward is loyalty; the risk is that when the relationship sours, there is no process to fall back on.

## Germany

German hierarchy is quieter and, to the outsider, more confusing. A commenter on Hacker News argued that Germans are more status-conscious than neighbouring countries, with people often expecting to be addressed by title and bosses not always wanting first names. The Wharton School's write-up on du and Sie explains the logic: the formal Sie traditionally separated management from staff, supported mutual respect and made firing people or giving negative reviews easier. Plenty of firms, especially in tech, advertising and publishing, now use du across ranks, while more conservative employers keep Sie. Reading which type you have joined is your first management task.

Behind the form sits process. Expat.com's Berlin guide says managers are expected to give clear direction on goals and deadlines and answer questions, that decisions can take several meetings, and that communication is direct, formal and fact-based. A Local Germany columnist, writing in 2011 about a single workday, found that praise is rare and that a boss simply thanking him for a report was a good sign; the working assumption is that no news is good news. The Feierabend, the formal end of the working day, is celebrated rather than apologised for. The OECD-based hours table compiled on Wikipedia puts Germany at about 1,386 annual hours per worker for 2023, against 2,369 for Egypt, and the boss is not the reason for the difference.

The efficiency has a price. Gallup's 2026 report puts German employee engagement at 11%, down from 16% in 2022, with 74% not engaged and 15% actively disengaged; the Europe average is 12% and the global figure 20%. Clear processes have relieved Germans of ambiguity rather than warmed them. Even firms advertising a flat hierarchy, as one language-school blogger who has worked with expats noted, retain clear lines of authority and set decision procedures. Flat, in German, means the org chart is shorter, not that it is optional.

The boundaries are the genuine perk. Expat.com's Berlin guide notes a statutory minimum of 20 paid leave days a year, with many employers offering up to 30, and says staff are not expected to check email outside working hours or work unpaid overtime. Arriving early does not automatically entitle you to leave early, so agree the arrangement with your manager in advance. A boss in Germany expects dedication during the day and has, in principle, no claim on the evening. Whether this is liberating or merely lonely depends on how much you were hoping your manager would ask about your weekend.

## The Reckoning

Taken head to head, the two systems answer the same question differently: who protects you from the boss? In Egypt, you do, through relationships, goodwill and the careful avoidance of public disagreement. In Germany, the structure does, through defined roles, written agreements and employment rules that make the manager's mood a minor input. Hofstede's power distance scores, 80 for Egypt and 35 for Germany, describe the expectation behind this: Egyptians accept that power is unequal and attach it to a person, while Germans prefer it divided, documented and limited. Neither country has convincingly solved engagement, with Gallup's regional figures at 14% for the Middle East and North Africa and 13% for Europe against 21% globally in the 2025 report.

Practically, the Egyptian boss rewards loyalty and punishes surprise; the German boss rewards reliability and punishes vagueness. Move to Cairo and you must learn to read a room; move to Germany and you must learn to read a contract. The first is harder to explain to HR, and the second harder to renegotiate.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — A poster describing Egyptian offices said the atmosphere is personal rather than professional, with little personal space and a tendency for well-meant suggestions to be taken as criticism. Whether a disagreement blows up or fades, they said, depends on whether people like you.*

> *Expat.com — A call-centre trainee found colleagues friendly, curious and quick with questions, and unexpectedly collaborative: food is shared and a late teammate gets covered. They had also heard that staying on good terms with managers matters, since an unwanted employee may be pressured to leave rather than formally fired, though they had not seen it themselves.*

> *Egyptian Streets — A Cairo office worker recalled a job interview that dwelt on her children, marital status and marriage plans, and later watched female managers be held to a harsher standard than their male peers.*

> *Hacker News — One commenter said Germany is notably more hierarchical than its neighbours, with people expecting titles and some bosses preferring not to be called by their first names.*

> *The Local Germany — A columnist who spent a workday in a German office noted that a colleague's blunt email was simply efficient, that small talk was reserved for friends, and that his boss's plain thank-you for a report was as warm as it got.*

## Conclusion

If you are a relationship person who dislikes paperwork, Egypt will reward you, provided you remember that the relationship with your manager is also your career insurance. If you prefer clarity to warmth and want your evenings back, Germany will oblige, provided you can live without being told you did well. Ask any prospective employer in either country how decisions are made, who can overrule whom, and what happens when you disagree; the answer, or the pause before it, will tell you most of what you need.

In short, one country gives you a boss who knows your family and the other a boss who knows your contract, and both will wonder why you expected anything else.
