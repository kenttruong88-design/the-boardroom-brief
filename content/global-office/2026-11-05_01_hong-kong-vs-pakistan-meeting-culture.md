# Silence Is a Verb: Running a Meeting in Hong Kong and Karachi Without Causing an International Incident

🇭🇰 Hong Kong · 🇵🇰 Pakistan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Hong Kong, a meeting is a ceremony with a predetermined outcome, and the point of attending is to watch the outcome be announced in the correct order of seniority. In Pakistan, a meeting is a negotiation that starts only once everyone has had tea and established who actually knows whom. Both cultures will tell a newly arrived manager that they "value open communication." Neither culture means it the way the newly arrived manager thinks.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive early and let the most senior person enter and sit first | Sit wherever is convenient — seating order signals rank, and getting it wrong is noticed |
| Offer your business card with both hands, text facing the recipient | Pocket a business card immediately — read it first, visibly, as a show of respect |
| Treat hesitation or a long pause as a "no" and move the conversation along gracefully | Push for a direct yes or no answer — you will get neither, and you will look impatient |
| Prepare a clean, low-color, data-first deck | Use red text for anything except numbers you want flagged as a serious problem |
| Expect the real decision to be made after the meeting, quietly, by seniors | Assume consensus in the room means the matter is actually settled |
| Keep your calendar light before 6pm — client and internal meetings regularly run until 7 or 8 | Schedule a hard stop at 5pm and expect colleagues to honor it the way they would elsewhere |

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the tea when it's offered, even if you don't want it | Decline refreshments outright — it reads as a rejection of hospitality, not a dietary choice |
| Use titles — "Sir," "Sahib," or "Begum" plus the surname — until invited to drop them | Address a senior colleague by first name on day one, however friendly the room feels |
| Budget real slack for meetings starting 15–30 minutes late | Schedule back-to-back meetings assuming the first one starts, or ends, on time |
| Listen for "that may be difficult" or "Inshallah" as the actual answer to a request | Take a vague, agreeable-sounding response as a firm commitment |
| Learn who the well-connected people in the room are — influence tracks relationships as much as titles | Assume the most senior title in the room is automatically the most influential voice |
| Raise disagreement privately with a manager after the meeting, not during it | Contradict a senior colleague's point directly in front of the group, even diplomatically |

Hong Kong's meeting culture is, on paper, extremely efficient: agendas circulate in advance, decks are dense with numbers, and everyone arrives on time. What the paperwork doesn't capture is how much of the actual communication happens in what isn't said. The Cultural Atlas's guide to Hong Kong business etiquette notes that seating and introductions follow strict seniority — the most senior person enters first, is introduced first, and is seated at the head of the table — and that a flat "no" is rare in professional settings; hesitation, a change of subject, or a long pause typically signals refusal more reliably than any word does. Visiting executives who push for a direct answer in the room often get one that sounds like agreement and isn't, because the actual decision gets made afterward, privately, among people one rank up from the table they were just sitting at.

That formality coexists with a genuinely brutal schedule. A Hong Kong Federation of Trade Unions survey of 773 workers found hours there running at "a world-leading" pace, with 80 percent of respondents working 45 to 48 hours a week reporting enormous pressure, rising to 88 percent among those working 52-plus hours — and meetings are a named culprit. A Hong Kong PR-industry survey covered by Campaign Asia identified "too many meetings" and "non-urgent work" as the leading drivers of overtime, with most employees quietly resentful rather than vocal about it. InterNations data reported by The Standard put expatriates in Hong Kong at an average 44.4 hours a week against a 40.2-hour global norm, and ranked the city 50th of 52 destinations overall, with 46 percent of expats specifically flagging a business culture that discourages thinking outside the box. The meeting, in other words, is less a forum for ideas than a scheduling obligation everyone privately wishes were shorter.

Pakistan runs on a different logic entirely, one where the meeting's real content is relational before it's informational. Hofstede's cultural-dimension data place Pakistan notably higher than Hong Kong on uncertainty avoidance, which tracks with on-the-ground reporting: The News International describes hierarchical structures that "discourage open communication," with employees rarely included in decisions that affect them, while a Dawn business column argues that many Pakistani firms are organized less around transparent process than around staying in a senior manager's good books — a dynamic that, left unmanaged, breeds a culture focused on not getting caught rather than on getting things right. Reader letters in The Express Tribune describe junior staff explicitly told not to offer creative suggestions, expected to answer "yes, Sir" without elaboration, and operating inside an informal system of sifarish — personal connections and references — that determines who actually has pull in a room regardless of job title. A separate PropPakistani column cites an online poll in which 58 percent of professionals believed their workplace discriminated by gender, and describes a management style unwilling to "relinquish control," leaving little real space for discussion even when a meeting is nominally called to have one.

Indirectness in Hong Kong runs deeper than meeting-room body language. The South China Morning Post's own etiquette reporting describes a communication style built for reading between the lines: a passing mention of a 3pm flight may function as an unstated request for a car to the airport, and business cards are offered with both hands and studied visibly before being put away, never pocketed immediately, as a basic show of respect. Hong Kong's institutional environment rewards this kind of indirect trust-building — the territory ranked 14th of 180 countries on the 2023 Corruption Perceptions Index, a score high enough that visiting executives often assume the local business culture is more transparent than its meeting etiquette actually is. Pakistan's own business press is more openly self-critical on the same point: a Dawn columnist argued that too few Pakistani leaders prioritize building an actual culture of open discussion, and that when transparency becomes a casualty of hierarchy, what grows in its place is a workplace focused less on getting the right answer than on avoiding blame once a decision goes wrong — a dynamic that makes the meeting room, in both countries, a poor venue for actually surfacing bad news early.

### The Reckoning

The useful irony is that both cultures produce meetings where the stated purpose and the actual purpose diverge, just in opposite directions. Hong Kong's meetings look transparent — data-driven, punctual, seniority-ordered — but the real decision happens afterward, off the record, among the people who outrank everyone else in the room. Pakistan's meetings look informal and relational — tea, small talk, flexible timing — but the real decision was often already made before the meeting even started, by whoever has the right connections, which may or may not map onto the official seating chart at all. In neither country does the meeting itself function as the place where minds are changed; it functions as the place where a decision made elsewhere is ratified, performed, or quietly delayed.

For someone managing across both offices, the practical skill isn't learning to run a "better" meeting by Western standards — it's learning to read where the actual decision-making has moved to, and showing up there instead. In Hong Kong, that means building relationships with the people who speak last and least. In Pakistan, it means finding out who is actually well-connected, which is rarely printed on an org chart.

### Conclusion

If there's one adjustment worth making before your first week in either city, it's this: stop treating the meeting as the decision-making event. In Hong Kong, treat it as the formal announcement of a decision already arrived at through quieter, more senior channels, and spend your energy building access to those channels. In Pakistan, treat it as a relationship-maintenance ritual that happens to have an agenda attached, and spend your energy on who you know rather than what you can prove in the room.

Neither approach is irrational once you see what it's actually optimizing for — hierarchy with Hong Kong's precision, or hierarchy with Pakistan's warmth. If a friend asked me which skill to pack, I'd tell them: bring patience for silence in one city, and patience for tea in the other, and don't mistake either for an actual answer to your question.
