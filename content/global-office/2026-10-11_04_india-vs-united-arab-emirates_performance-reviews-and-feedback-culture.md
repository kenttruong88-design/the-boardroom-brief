# The Report Card and the Nationality Spreadsheet: India vs. UAE

**🇮🇳 India · 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In an Indian office, the annual review is a formality everyone has already privately negotiated weeks in advance, through hints, hallway conversations, and the occasional strategically timed favor. In a UAE office, the annual review is a formality everyone privately understands is shaped at least as much by passport as by performance. Both systems produce a document at the end. Neither document is really the point.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep a written record of your own work and wins throughout the year | Wait until review week to remember what you accomplished |
| Read a manager's indirect hints as real feedback, not small talk | Expect a manager to criticize you directly to your face in the moment |
| Raise concerns about a senior colleague privately, carefully, and rarely | Escalate a complaint about your boss through a formal channel as a first move |
| Use peer and 360-degree feedback channels where they exist | Assume the absence of criticism in a review means there's nothing to improve |
| Plan your next raise around a job change roughly every few years if increments stay flat | Expect declining optional weekend work to go unnoticed come appraisal time |

#### 🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask explicitly in interviews how the review and bonus process actually works for your nationality tier | Assume identical job titles mean identical pay or review outcomes across nationalities |
| Stay visibly cordial in any feedback conversation, even a frustrating one | Raise your voice or show visible anger in an appraisal meeting — it can trigger disciplinary action |
| Build quiet relationships with senior compatriots who can vouch for you internally | Rely on a labor court or formal grievance process as your main plan — it's slow, costly, and career-risky |
| Negotiate salary hard at hire, since reviews may move titles more than pay | Expect a strong annual review to automatically convert into a promotion rather than a bonus |
| Track your own output in writing, the same habit that protects you elsewhere | Assume being an outstanding performer guarantees a path to a senior role over a local or Western hire |

### India: Feedback by Inference

Hofstede Insights scores India at 77 on power distance, one of the higher readings globally, and SHRM India has drawn the direct line from that number to workplace behavior: high power distance "makes employees more deferential to authority," which in practice means candid upward feedback — a subordinate telling a manager something isn't working — happens rarely and carefully, if at all. The review itself, several HR commentators note, often functions less as new information and more as a formal record of conclusions both parties quietly reached months earlier through hallway cues, delayed replies, and the subtle redistribution of good projects.

What makes this workable rather than purely opaque is a second, less discussed habit: documentation as self-defense. Advice circulating among Indian developers on forums like Reddit's r/developersIndia consistently recommends keeping a running written record of daily work — summarized in email, so there's evidence to point to if a review turns negative or a raise doesn't materialize. One frequently echoed piece of advice even specifies literally pasting that email thread into the room if a manager later claims not to remember your contributions. The same advice culture treats job-hopping, roughly every three years, as the more reliable lever for a real pay jump than waiting on the internal review cycle to deliver one — a tacit admission that the formal feedback mechanism and the actual compensation mechanism aren't always the same conversation.

There is a genuine effort underway to change this from the inside. SHRM India's own commentary points to 360-degree feedback systems — gathering input from peers and subordinates, not just a single manager — as the recommended fix for a culture where upward criticism rarely surfaces on its own. The honest caveat, made in the same breath, is that most managers simply haven't been trained to deliver constructive feedback well even when a 360-degree system hands them the data, so the structural fix and the behavioral one are advancing at noticeably different speeds.

### UAE: Feedback Filtered Through Nationality

The UAE has no individually published Hofstede score of its own — it's measured only as part of Hofstede's broader "Arab World" cluster, which registers high power distance (80) and strong uncertainty avoidance (68), context this article states plainly rather than inventing a country-specific number that doesn't exist. What the regional data doesn't capture, and what employer-facing research increasingly surfaces instead, is how directly review and promotion outcomes track nationality rather than tenure or output. A widely discussed 2018 feature essay on UAE HR practices described annual appraisals as "deeply contentious," where careers are "made or broken," and argued that outstanding employees of Asian and African nationalities can typically expect a larger bonus rather than the promotion a similarly rated Western or Emirati colleague would receive — because senior roles disproportionately go to locals and Western expats regardless of the appraisal outcome.

The newer survey data softens that picture only slightly. Robert Walters' Middle East Salary Survey for 2026, reported by Khaleej Times, found 26% of UAE employees felt less positive about their role after their most recent performance review, against 21% who came away more optimistic — a near-even split that still skews negative — while 66% said they were considering a new role entirely this year, and 46% doubted their own employer's ability to retain staff at all. Formal routes for dissent remain narrow: unions and collective bargaining aren't permitted, and labor courts are widely described as a last resort given the career risk, cost, and backlog involved — which leaves most feedback, positive or negative, running through informal channels, exactly as in India, just organized around nationality tiers instead of hierarchy alone.

The essay's sharpest observation may be the quietest one: competition between nationality groups over influence — including, allegedly, informal control over whose résumés even reach a hiring manager — becomes its own layer of office politics, one a newly arrived employee has no way of seeing on an org chart. A performance review in this environment isn't purely a measure of the work; it's a measure of the work filtered through a hierarchy of nationality that nobody puts in writing but everyone on the floor can describe in detail if asked privately.

### The Reckoning

Here's the genuinely strange symmetry: both systems route around their own official feedback mechanism, just along different axes. India routes around hierarchy — the review formalizes what deference already settled informally. The UAE routes around nationality — the review formalizes what a worker's passport already mostly determined. An Indian manager who prides themselves on reading the room will find the UAE's nationality-sorted outcomes blunter and less negotiable than anything hierarchy alone produces back home; a UAE-based manager accustomed to openly nationality-tiered bonus structures may be unprepared for how much unofficial, face-saving diplomacy an Indian direct report expects before any criticism lands.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *reddit.com/r/developersIndia — One widely upvoted comment thread advised new hires to keep a running email record of daily tasks specifically so they have something to "paste" if a manager later disputes their output during a review cycle — treating documentation less as professional hygiene and more as evidence banked against a dispute everyone assumes is coming.*

> *teamblind.com — A call center lead at a major UAE-based employer, reviewing their time there, wrote plainly that "everyone is scared from being reported for minor things," describing a culture where voicing an opinion risked a formal warning rather than a conversation.*

> *teamblind.com — A senior scheduler and forecaster at the same employer added that underperforming staff were rarely let go, which left them feeling that effort wasn't connected to outcomes either way — and noted that employees promoted internally into a role earned roughly half what an external hire in the identical position received.*

> *thehrdirector.com — In a widely cited feature essay on UAE HR practices, the author described review season as a period when staff deliberately "try to embody a risk averse persona" to keep their year-end record clean, precisely because ongoing, lower-stakes feedback through the rest of the year was described as uncommon.*

> *Advice worth having before you land: in India, don't wait for direct criticism to tell you something's wrong — by the time it's said aloud, it's usually already been decided. In the UAE, ask about the nationality-based structure of pay and promotion before you accept the offer, not after your first review makes it uncomfortably clear.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two for how honestly you'll be told where you stand, the uncomfortable answer is that neither will tell you outright — India will make you infer it from tone, and the UAE will make you infer it from a bonus number that may have more to do with your passport than your output. The practical move is the same in both places, and it's the one piece of advice both forums independently landed on without coordinating: write everything down, keep the receipts, and treat the formal review as a courtesy copy of a decision made somewhere else entirely.
