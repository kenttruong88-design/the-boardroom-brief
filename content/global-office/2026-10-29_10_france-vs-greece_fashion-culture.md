# In France, Nobody Will Fire You for Wearing Jeans. In Greece, Nobody Will Fire You for Wearing a Three-Piece Suit to a Beach Town. Context Is Everything.

**🇫🇷 France · 🇬🇷 Greece**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Ask a labor lawyer whether a French employer can force you into a suit, and the answer is a flat no — French law doesn't regulate what you wear to work, full stop. Ask a Greek office manager the same question about dress-down Fridays, and the honest answer is that the concept barely exists yet outside tech. One country has almost no legal or cultural machinery for enforcing a look. The other has plenty of informal machinery and, by multiple accounts, not much appetite for loosening it. Somewhere between those two defaults sits everything a newcomer needs to know about getting dressed for work in each place.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect roughly two in three French employees to have no imposed dress code at all, per a national employee survey. | Assume "no dress code" means "no standards" — most people without one still dress to protect their company's image. |
| Loosen up gradually if you see colleagues doing the same — ties coming off and sandals appearing as summer heat rises is a shared, informal signal. | Be the first person in the office to show up in shorts, even on the hottest day — by multiple accounts, almost nobody actually crosses that line. |
| Notice that men are somewhat more likely than women to follow a formal code, while women navigate a wider, less codified range of acceptable looks. | Expect the same formality across company size or sector — a 250-person firm and a 9-person startup, or construction and tech, read very differently. |
| Take a compliment on your outfit at face value — French (and Austrian) coworkers are, by survey data, unusually likely to compliment colleagues' clothing. | Assume younger employees are automatically more casual — survey data shows 18-29-year-olds are actually more likely to follow a dress code than workers over 49. |
| Treat restraint as the real local aesthetic: fewer, better pieces, worn with intention, rather than visible effort. | Overdress for a first day at a younger company — chinos and clean sneakers often read as more correctly calibrated than a full suit. |

#### 🇬🇷 Greece

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to looking "smart and well presented" in traditional offices — multiple business guides describe this as taken seriously, not optional. | Expect "dress-down" culture to have spread the way it has elsewhere in Europe — outside tech, it largely hasn't yet. |
| Pack genuinely lightweight fabrics — summer heat makes breathable clothing close to essential, even inside a formal dress code. | Assume Mediterranean sun means beachwear norms apply to the office — tailored blazers and collared shirts remain the default for men in classic companies. |
| Notice how often black appears as an everyday wardrobe anchor, by multiple expat accounts, across both casual and dressier looks. | Expect women's office wear to be fully standardized — tailored suits with skirts remain common, but trousers are visibly gaining ground. |
| Expect men, more than women by some expat accounts, to pay closer attention to current fashion trends day to day. | Assume everyone has equal access to following trends — at least one long-term resident ties limited disposable income directly to limited fashion variety. |
| Reserve the most formal looks — dark, "expensive"-reading suits with white shirts — for genuinely formal occasions: official events, celebrations, family gatherings. | Treat "everyday style is relaxed" as license for sloppiness — relaxed in Greece still skews more put-together than casual dress in many other countries. |

### France

The headline French statistic cuts against the country's reputation for formality: in a 1,013-person employee survey conducted for Monster, 65 percent said they follow no imposed dress code at work at all, and only 35 percent said they do, split between a strict uniform-level code (14 percent), a formal suit-and-tie-level code (8 percent), and something looser (13 percent). Yet the same survey found that even among the unregulated majority, 58 percent said they still make sure their clothing doesn't damage their employer's image — formality in France, in other words, is less an imposed rule than an internalized one.

That self-policing shows up vividly in how French offices handle heat. French labor law doesn't ban shorts, flip-flops, or tank tops outright, and one employment lawyer confirmed there's no legal barrier to asking your employer about lighter summer clothing. But a former HR association president put the real social rule more plainly than any statute could: "when it is hot, at the office, everyone takes off their tie but no one would consider putting on their shorts." Separate survey data backs that image up in a more flattering direction — French male workers are the most likely in Europe to wear open or sandal-style shoes to work, and strappy, sleeveless tops are described as genuinely "en vogue" even in customer-facing roles, with only around 20 percent of French respondents saying such tops shouldn't be worn at work, against roughly 50 percent elsewhere in Europe. The TalesMag diplomatic reader archive for Paris shows the same drift over time in miniature: a 2008 entry notes "wearing jeans to work is not acceptable," while a 2015 entry from the same forum simply observes, "I see some wear jeans to work. I think its less formal than it used to be."

### Greece

Greece's formality runs on a different logic: less about legal code, more about a standard nobody has seriously challenged yet. Multiple business-culture guides describe looking "smart and well presented" as something Greek professional culture takes seriously, with a classic-company uniform of blazer, slacks, and collared shirt for men (tie optional by occasion), and tailored suits, traditionally with skirts, for women, though trousers are increasingly accepted. The "dress-down" wave that swept much of corporate Europe is described as having made real inroads only in tech; everywhere else, the formal baseline largely holds.

Expat voices on the ground describe the everyday texture underneath that baseline. One longtime poster on an expat forum for Greece, asked directly about local fashion sense, offered a condensed field guide: "main and favourite colour in Greece is black," paired with an observation that "there is no deal between dress or trousers — trousers 97 percent," and that "young men [are] more into the fashion than girls" day to day — while reserving genuinely formal, "classic (expensive)" suits with white shirts for "official situations, celebrations, night out with relatives." A separate poster offered a blunter, more structural explanation for why fashion variety can feel limited outside the capital's wealthier circles: "money is in extremely short supply, so even if we wanted to follow fashion it would be impossible" — a reminder that what reads as conservative taste is sometimes conservative budgeting instead. And the climate itself does real work on the final look: with summer heat intense enough that guides call breathable fabric "absolutely essential," even a formally-coded outfit in Greece tends toward lighter weights than its French equivalent.

### The Reckoning

Line the two up and the real contrast isn't formal-versus-casual at all — France's own data shows most workers actually have no imposed code, and Greece's guides describe its formality as cultural consensus rather than legal mandate. The real difference is where each country's restraint comes from. France polices appearance through an internalized aesthetic: Coco Chanel's old advice to "take one thing off" before leaving the house captures a culture that signals taste through subtraction, which is exactly how a country with no legal dress code still produces offices where almost nobody wears shorts even in a heatwave. Greece polices appearance through a more externally legible social standard — a lingering baseline of smart, put-together dressing that hasn't eroded the way it has in much of Western Europe, reinforced by genuine economic constraints on how much variation is realistic for many households.

Hofstede's framework adds a useful wrinkle here: Greece records the highest uncertainty-avoidance score of any country in Hofstede's global dataset, a documented discomfort with ambiguity that tracks neatly with a culture reluctant to let "smart and well presented" quietly erode into "whatever's comfortable." France scores high on uncertainty avoidance too, but pairs it with one of Europe's more individualist scores, which lines up with a culture where the "rule" is self-imposed taste rather than a collectively enforced standard — ambiguity tolerated, as long as you personally get it right.

### Conclusion

If you're moving from Greece to France, relax: the suit you packed is optional more often than the guides suggest, and a clean, restrained casual outfit will usually read as more correctly calibrated than overdressing. If you're moving from France to Greece, pack the suit anyway, lean lightweight for the heat, and don't expect the "dress-down Friday" you may be used to — outside a handful of tech offices, it hasn't really arrived.

Either way, both countries are telling you the same thing in different accents: nobody is checking a rulebook, but everybody is checking you.
