# Germany Will Pay You to Stay Home. Ireland Will Just Let You Leave the Room.

🇩🇪 Germany · 🇮🇪 Ireland

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Germany will replace up to 65 percent of your salary for having a baby and then cannot guarantee you anywhere to put the baby while you go back to earning it. Ireland guarantees you almost nothing and then hands you a creche bill that, in Dublin, can exceed the mortgage. Both countries call this "family policy."

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for a Kita spot the moment you know you're pregnant — some nurseries open waitlists before the birth. | Don't assume the legal "right to a childcare place" (in force since 2013) means one will actually appear on time. |
| Register with the Elterngeldstelle early and expect weeks of paperwork regardless of how organized you are. | Don't expect the full €1,800 monthly cap — Elterngeld pays 65 percent of your prior net income, not a flat rate, so low earners get less in absolute terms. |
| Split the months with your partner to unlock the two bonus "Partnermonate." | Don't both claim Basiselterngeld at the same time beyond the one overlap month the rules allow, or you lose paid months outright. |
| Build Kita closures — illness waves, staff shortages, holidays — into your actual work calendar, not your optimistic one. | Don't treat Elternzeit as a favor you're asking your employer; it is a statutory entitlement they must grant. |
| File through the Jugendamt as well as applying directly — the municipal matching form can surface spots you never knew existed. | Don't rely on a Tagesmutter as your backup plan; they are typically just as oversubscribed as Kitas. |

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for the National Childcare Scheme subsidy before your child starts creche; the provider deducts it automatically. | Don't expect Maternity or Parent's Benefit to resemble your salary — both pay a flat weekly rate, not a percentage of earnings. |
| Ask your employer directly whether they top up statutory leave pay; many do, quietly, and it is rarely advertised in the contract. | Don't assume the nine weeks of Parent's Leave replace maternity or paternity leave — it's an additional entitlement, still paid at benefit rate. |
| Shop around by postcode — creche fees vary enormously even within Dublin. | Don't delay applying to a creche until after the birth; sought-after ones in cities fill a year or more in advance. |
| Factor childcare costs into any salary negotiation before accepting a role; fees now rival a second rent. | Don't read "subsidised childcare" as "affordable childcare" — the universal rate covers a small fraction of a typical weekly bill. |
| Check whether your household income sits near a subsidy threshold; small payroll changes can shift your subsidy band. | Don't wait for Irish paid leave to catch up to German or Nordic levels; structurally, it hasn't yet. |

### 🇩🇪 Germany

Germany's Elterngeld is, on paper, one of Europe's more generous income-replacement schemes: it pays 65 percent of a parent's average net income over the twelve months before birth, capped at €1,800 a month, for up to twelve months of Basiselterngeld — fourteen if both parents take at least two "Partnermonate" each. Couples can stretch the same money thinner over up to 32 months via ElterngeldPlus, and an additional Partnerschaftsbonus rewards both parents for working 24 to 32 hours a week simultaneously. Since April 2025, households earning above €175,000 (or single parents above €150,000) are excluded entirely. The mechanics are precise, bureaucratic, and genuinely redistributive — exactly what a country scoring 65 on Hofstede's uncertainty-avoidance index would design: a system built to minimize ambiguity, with a form for every contingency.

What the system cannot engineer around is supply. The Bertelsmann Foundation projected a shortfall of 384,000 Kita places by 2023, concentrated in the west, and a survey of 5,000 kindergarten directors found 64 percent had operated below legally required staffing more than a fifth of the time. A Hans Böckler Foundation poll found nearly six in ten working parents hit by closures or shortened hours that year, with 30 percent calling the disruption "very stressful." Berlin's government has since committed roughly €4 billion to the problem, mostly for staff recruitment.

The knock-on effect shows up in who actually uses the leave and who returns to reduced hours. Destatis figures have long shown roughly two-thirds of working mothers in Germany in part-time roles, against a small fraction of fathers — a pattern labor economists call the Teilzeitfalle, the part-time trap, where reduced hours taken for childcare rarely reverse once the youngest child starts school. The generosity of Elterngeld, in other words, buys time off; it does not by itself buy a symmetrical return to full-time work, or a Kita place waiting at the end of it.

### 🇮🇪 Ireland

Ireland's statutory offer is smaller and simpler. Maternity leave runs 26 weeks, paternity leave two, and since August 2024 Parent's Leave has extended to nine weeks per parent, usable any time in a child's first two years, per the Department of Enterprise and reporting from IBEC. All three are supported not by salary replacement but by flat-rate weekly benefits from the Department of Social Protection — a rate that bears little relation to what the parent was actually earning. Whether a parent's income holds up during leave depends almost entirely on whether their employer chooses, voluntarily, to top it up; there is no legal obligation to do so, which fits a country that scores just 35 on uncertainty avoidance — comparatively comfortable leaving such things to individual arrangement rather than statute.

Where Ireland's system bites is afterward, at the creche door. An OECD comparison reported by The Journal found Irish net childcare costs for a dual-earner couple with two children take almost twice the OECD average share of income, placing Ireland joint-fourth highest in Europe, behind only the Netherlands, Switzerland and the UK. Pobal data cited in the same reporting puts average early-years fees at roughly €190 a week, rising past €200 in Dublin and toward €260 in Dún Laoghaire-Rathdown — in practice, €800 to over €1,000 a month, which is before any discussion of after-school or summer-holiday care, both of which are entirely separate line items. The National Childcare Scheme offsets this only partially: a universal subsidy of €2.14 an hour for all families, and an income-assessed subsidy up to €5.10 an hour for households under a €68,000 reckonable-income threshold since August 2026, with a further rise planned for 2027. Useful, but rarely enough to change the basic calculation that a second income in Ireland can be substantially eaten by the cost of making that income possible, which is precisely the arithmetic that keeps the question of whether a parent "goes back to work" from being purely a career decision.

### The Reckoning

Put bluntly: Germany bets on the state, Ireland bets on the market, and parents absorb whichever bet doesn't pay off. In Germany, the income replacement is real and the paperwork is survivable, but there may be nowhere to send the child once the money runs out, and the search itself — Kita waitlists numbering in the hundreds, families applying to dozens of nurseries before securing one — becomes a second unpaid job. In Ireland, a childcare place is comparatively easy to find if you can pay for it, but the state's financial support during leave is thin, and the ongoing cost of care afterward can quietly cancel out a parent's salary, especially a second, typically maternal, one.

Neither system has solved the actual problem, which is that someone has to be available for a small number of unscheduled hours a week for several years, and neither country has fully decided whether that someone should be subsidized, mandated, or simply left to sort it out at the kitchen table. Germany has chosen generous subsidy with unreliable supply; Ireland has chosen reliable supply with thin subsidy. A household moving between the two should expect to trade one headache for the other, not to escape headaches altogether — and should budget, in either case, for the possibility that the second income in the family becomes, for a year or two, a part-time or theoretical one.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Local, Germany — Alexandra Müller, a parent in Stuttgart, recalls standing on three separate Kita waiting lists simultaneously, holding places 120, 80 and 41.*

> *The Local, Germany — Elise Hanrahan, an American researcher in Berlin, went onto unpaid leave when no Kita place appeared in time for her return to work; she says the cost wasn't only financial but professional — "the consequences for my career. It's significant."*

> *The Local, Germany — Natalie Kent, a British expat in Berlin, applied to 38 Kitas before accepting a temporary place that added a long daily commute to her return to work.*

> *The Local, Germany — Rachel Stern, who navigated the Berlin Kita search herself, found her pleasant surprise came from an unlikely source: a Jugendamt matching form, filed as a backup, produced an offer from a nursery she'd never applied to — now her daughter's "hidden gem." Her advice to other parents: apply while still pregnant, and track every application in a spreadsheet, because no one else is tracking it for you.*

> *Hacker News — in a thread debating whether generous parental leave actually raises birth rates, one commenter cited Germany's fertility rate of 1.57 as evidence that paid leave alone doesn't move the number, while another noted that Ireland, with far less generous leave, was "doing better" on the same measure — a reminder that neither country's policy is quite the lever its brochure implies.*

### Conclusion

If the choice comes down to money versus certainty, Germany offers the former and Ireland offers something closer to the latter, though "certainty" in the Irish case mostly means certainty of the bill. A dual-income household with reasonable savings and flexible employers can make either system work; a single earner, or a family without slack in the budget, will feel Germany's supply gap and Ireland's price tag as two versions of the same squeeze. The honest advice for anyone relocating for either country is to read the leave entitlement as a floor, not a plan, and to start the childcare search before the pregnancy test is a month old. Parenthood, it turns out, is the one relocation variable neither government has fully priced in — they've simply priced it differently.
