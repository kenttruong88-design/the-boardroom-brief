# Vietnam Will Hire You to Break Things; Taiwan Will Ask Who Approved That

🇻🇳 Vietnam · 🇹🇼 Taiwan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In 2025, Vietnamese startups raised roughly US$215 million across 41 deals, a total that a single mid-sized American seed fund would regard as a quiet quarter, and which local investors describe, with admirable composure, as "selective repositioning". In Taiwan, meanwhile, the world's most valuable chipmaker tells critics that average weekly hours have not exceeded 50, a reassurance whose force depends entirely on one's baseline. Both economies offer a choice between the young company and the old one. Neither makes the choice as simple as the recruiter's slide suggests, and in both the identity of your manager will matter more than the size of the logo above the lobby.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask a startup how many months of runway remain before you sign | Treat a founder's verbal promise of a title or raise as binding |
| Get any promised pay rise, title or reporting line written into the contract | Assume a foreign passport buys a premium; many employers use local salary scales |
| Learn the basics of the local language, since hierarchy runs through who addresses whom | Challenge a senior colleague in front of the team |
| Cultivate the in-group, because loyalty is the working currency | Expect a two-year plan to survive contact with a funding round |
| Volunteer for tasks outside your job description at a young firm | Confuse a large conglomerate's prestige with a clear career path |
| Keep a remote or regional fallback while you gauge local demand | Rely on being the only person who can do your job |

### In Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for the Employment Gold Card if your profile qualifies | Assume government startup programmes are open to non-citizens |
| Target international firms that support Asia-wide products, where English is working language | Expect English to carry you through a local firm's internal meetings |
| Ask in writing what the standard overtime expectation is | Take "average weekly hours" claims at face value |
| Show respect for seniority and let decisions travel upward | Present a finished decision as a draft for discussion |
| Build relationships with colleagues over meals and small favours | Take silence in a meeting as agreement or disinterest |
| Check the manager's reputation before the company's | Count on a local firm's promotion ladder reaching a foreigner |

## Vietnam

The Vietnamese startup market is, for the moment, in a sober mood. According to VinVentures, cited by Viet Nam News and Vietstock, total startup funding in 2025 was about US$215 million over 41 deals, roughly 30 per cent lower than the year before. The top ten deals accounted for about 72 per cent of the value, and around 60 per cent of fund resources went to follow-on and bridge rounds rather than new companies. Investors, in other words, are mostly keeping existing patients alive, and the money that does arrive is tilting towards proven companies: deals of US$1 million to US$5 million rose from about 21 per cent of the total in 2023 to about 41 per cent in 2025, while sub-million cheques kept shrinking. The practical consequence for a prospective employee is that joining a Vietnamese startup is less a career move than a bet on survival, and the only sensible due diligence is to ask how long the cash lasts. Aniday, a Vietnamese careers platform, notes that startups blur role boundaries and let staff voice opinions more freely, while larger corporations offer, in its words, "well-established management structures" and a stronger brand for future job searches.

That divide maps neatly onto the national character, at least as Hofstede Insights measures it. Vietnam scores 70 on power distance and just 20 on individualism, so hierarchy is accepted and loyalty to the group is the working currency; yet it scores only 30 on uncertainty avoidance, which means rules are kept to a minimum and improvisation is tolerated. The result is an organisation with few written rules and a very clear idea of who is in charge. At a startup that suits those who like to be handed a problem without a manual. At a conglomerate it can mean a flat, founder-centred court in which the reporting line is the only reliable process.

Foreigners should also read the market as it is. Posters on Expat.com report that at one telecom firm the foreign share of the IT department fell from about 20 per cent to about 1 per cent over a decade, as capable local graduates filled the seats, and a manager observed that many young local staff leave within two years in search of newer technology. Aniday adds that Vietnam ranked fifth in Southeast Asia for its startup landscape and had four tech unicorns as of 2023, which is respectable and also means the supply of well-funded employers is short. The expatriate in Vietnam is therefore hired for something specific, such as regional experience, a client relationship or a language, and is rarely hired for being merely competent.

## Taiwan

Taiwan's startup scene has a different shape: smaller, harder and attached to the physical economy. StartupBlink ranks it 22nd among global ecosystems, with its Hardware and IoT sector placed third in the world, and credits the presence of Foxconn, Asus and TSMC for the supply of engineers and suppliers. It also records that funding fell 61.79 per cent in 2023 to US$419.15 million, with the number of deals down 38.61 per cent, so Taiwan's startups are no strangers to a downturn either. The state does what it can: the Employment Gold Card and the Entrepreneur Visa exist to attract foreign talent, though a long-time resident on Forumosa claims that the subsidy programmes themselves remain open to citizens only.

The corporate side is dominated by firms that built the global electronics supply chain and are not inclined to apologise for how. According to the Fortune reporting summarised by the Taipei Times, TSMC says average weekly hours have not exceeded 50 for two years, with a 60-hour ceiling during launches. Its US operations nonetheless carried a Glassdoor approval rating of 27 per cent from 91 reviews, against 85 per cent for Intel from far more. The Ministry of Labor's 2024 survey, reported by Taiwan News, found national average overtime of 14.7 hours a month and 20.4 hours in manufacturing. TSMC, which employs more than 70,000 people worldwide, adds that it runs labour-management meetings and an anonymous online feedback channel, which suggests the company knows the complaint exists. A Forumosa discussion of life as a foreign developer in Taipei paints the ordinary version of the same picture: a nominal nine-to-six day, with engineers at one office routinely staying until seven or eight in the evening because everyone else does. None of this is scandalous by regional standards; it is simply the price of a country that makes things other countries cannot.

The same index puts Taiwan at 58 on power distance and 17 on individualism, close to Vietnam on collectivism, but at 69 on uncertainty avoidance, against Vietnam's 30. That last number explains the experience better than any statistic. Taiwanese companies prefer clear procedure, documented approval and decisions that arrive by committee. A startup founder in Taipei may wear a hoodie, but the mindset behind it often retains the habits of the factory floor, because the founder probably learned the trade at a large manufacturer.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, the two countries disagree about where risk belongs. In Vietnam the rules are few and the risk is personal: you may be asked to do anything, by anyone, with the funding situation unclear. In Taiwan the rules are many and the risk is procedural: you will know exactly what is expected, and the hours will be as long as the process requires. A Vietnamese startup rewards the person who can operate without a map, and a Taiwanese corporation rewards the person who can read a very detailed one.

The size of the company is therefore a weaker guide than it appears. A Vietnamese conglomerate behaves rather like a large startup, with an all-powerful founder and a loose relationship to its own job descriptions. A Taiwanese startup often behaves like a small corporation, with structure inherited from its founders' former employers. Both systems also share a quiet reliance on relationships over paperwork. In Vietnam, trust is extended to the in-group and withheld, politely, from everyone else; in Taiwan, it is built through years of reliable attendance and the avoidance of surprises. A newcomer in either country starts outside the circle, and the speed at which one is admitted depends less on competence than on patience. The sensible expatriate asks not whether the employer is young but who decides, how quickly, and whether the answer can be put in writing.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/VietNam — a commenter familiar with a founder-led conglomerate described it as suited to people who thrive in volatile, startup-like conditions under strict discipline, with elite contacts as the reward, but warned that life differs sharply for subordinates and executives, and that anything promised should be in the contract.*

> *Blind — replying to a Bay Area engineer weighing a move to Vietnamese startups, one respondent advised expecting a pay cut and a different work culture, and cautioned that returning to a large American employer after years at a small firm can prove difficult.*

> *Expat.com — a manager in Vietnam remarked that many young local IT staff leave before two years, and that the foreign share at his firm's IT department shrank because local colleagues learned the job quickly and well.*

> *Forumosa — one longtime resident said that seniority carries heavy weight at Taiwanese firms, that foreign staff can feel treated as tools, and that she had stopped working for local employers; another poster replied that hierarchy and difficult bosses exist everywhere.*

> *Forumosa — a veteran of several Taiwanese startups said government startup assistance had been confirmed as citizens-only, and concluded that the so-called bamboo ceiling for foreigners in local companies is close to impenetrable.*

## Conclusion

If you want speed and are willing to underwrite your own risk, Vietnam will give you a larger remit than your age or experience deserves, and a salary scale that may not match it. If you want structure, a supply chain that explains itself, and a visa regime that has actually been designed, Taiwan is the more legible market, provided you can tolerate its habits of overtime and consensus. In either country, read the contract, ask about the cash, and meet your manager before accepting.

The honest summary is that both countries will let you be a startup person or a corporate person, and both will occasionally disagree about which one you are.
