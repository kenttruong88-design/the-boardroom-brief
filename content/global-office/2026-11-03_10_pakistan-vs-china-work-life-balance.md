# Overtime Without Borders: What Pakistan and China Call a Normal Tuesday

## 🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇨🇳 China

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Pakistan's workforce clocks an average of 47.49 hours a week, according to the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics. China's clocks 48.30, according to its National Bureau of Statistics. On paper, these are two of the most hardworking populations on earth, separated by less than one hour of weekly toil — a rounding error, a long lunch, nothing to write home about. In practice, one of those averages is dragged down by rural laborers working part seasons, and the other is dragged down by nobody, because China's official number undercounts precisely the people this article is about: the salaried urban professional whose actual week, once meetings, meals, and the unpaid hours after "clocking out" are counted, looks nothing like a government spreadsheet. Both countries will tell you, cheerfully, that balance is achievable. Neither means it the way a recruiter in Zurich means it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your prospective employer their actual closing time, before signing, not during orientation week | Assume the 47.49-hour national average describes your office — it's an average that includes rural and part-time labor far outside corporate Karachi or Lahore |
| Build Friday prayer breaks into any meeting scheduled near midday | Book back-to-back calls across the Friday prayer window and act surprised when the office empties |
| Expect to leave after your manager does, especially in your first month | Be the first one out the door, however legitimate your reason |
| Get your pay date in writing, down to the specific calendar day | Assume "end of month" means the actual end of the month — ask any worker who has waited until the 10th |
| Accept the tea, the extra plate, the meeting paused so someone can bring you food | Try to rush past the hospitality ritual to get back to your spreadsheet |
| Ask HR, explicitly, whether your grade is entitled to overtime pay | Rely on the Shops and Establishment Ordinance to enforce itself without you asking |

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask in the interview what time the office genuinely empties on an ordinary Tuesday | Take "flexible hours" in a job posting at face value |
| Find out whether your contract runs on the standard, comprehensive, or non-fixed hour system before you sign | Assume China's statutory 40-hour week and 36-hour monthly overtime cap describe your real schedule |
| Keep your own record of hours, chat logs, and pay slips from day one | Expect a labor dispute to move quickly — arbitration comes before court, and claims have a one-year filing window |
| Notice which major firms (DJI, Midea, Haier) now enforce fixed clock-out times | Assume every employer, foreign-invested ones included, has caught up with them |
| Treat the nap room, the free dinner, and the midnight shuttle as compensation | Mistake perks built for a single 24-year-old for a culture that accommodates a spouse and a toddler |
| Sort your Z-visa and residence permit before anything else | Start work on a tourist visa planning to "fix the paperwork later" |

The Pakistan Bureau of Statistics' most recent Labour Force Survey puts the country's average workweek at 47.49 hours, with men logging 51.43 and women 34.13 — a gap the survey attributes largely to unpaid domestic labor absorbing women's remaining hours rather than any shorter formal workday. Urban workers average 51.34 hours, well above the rural 45.69, which tells you that Pakistan's overtime problem is a city problem, concentrated exactly where the expat reader of this guide is likely to land. The International Labour Organization has long tracked what it calls "excessive hours" — more than 48 a week — as a global marker of weak labor protection, and Pakistan's urban average sits comfortably over that line before anyone has even opened a laptop at home.

What drives this is less a cultural love of overtime than its opposite: a labor market so saturated that refusing extra hours carries real risk. A 2015 ProPakistani piece on the national work culture pointed to high unemployment as the central lever — workers tolerate long days, missing medical insurance, and what one commenter called an authoritarian "danda" management style because the alternative is unemployment, not a different job. Dawn and other local outlets have documented weak enforcement of the Shops and Establishment Ordinance, which theoretically caps hours but rarely survives contact with an actual HR department. Fittingly, Hofstede Insights scores Pakistan at 0 on its Indulgence dimension — the most restrained possible reading — meaning duty and obligation routinely override personal gratification as the default cultural script, overtime included.

None of this is evenly distributed. Pakistan's growing remote-work and freelance tech sector runs on a different clock entirely, closer to deliverables than desk-time, a split visible in the gap between a government ministry's 9-to-5 and a Lahore developer logging in at midnight for a client in Austin.

China's National Bureau of Statistics put the average workweek for urban enterprise employees at 48.30 hours in September 2026, continuing a slow year-over-year climb. China's Labour Law formally caps the standard week at 40 hours with overtime limited to 36 hours a month — stricter, on paper, than Pakistan's framework — which makes the gap between law and lived reality the real story. That gap has a name: "996," nine a.m. to nine p.m., six days a week, a schedule Alibaba founder Jack Ma once defended as "a huge blessing," while JD.com's Richard Liu reportedly dismissed employees wanting better balance as "slackers." The backlash produced its own artifact, the GitHub blacklist "996.icu," and eventually a 2021 ruling from China's top court that the 996 schedule is unlawful — a ruling the tech sector has absorbed unevenly. DJI now enforces a 9 p.m. clock-out, Midea sends staff home by 6:20, and Haier moved to a five-day week; plenty of other employers have made no such adjustment.

The texture of this shows up in individual stories more than in any statistic. A British employee at NetEase's Guangzhou studio went viral on Chinese social media in 2024 after posting an exhausted photo following an 80-hour week — overtime that was never formally required of him but that he felt he owed his team, a distinction that captures how Chinese overtime culture operates less through mandate than through social obligation. That tracks with Hofstede's China score of just 20 on Individualism, among the most collectivist readings on the index: the group's expectations, not the contract's fine print, set the real workday.

**The Reckoning**

Put the two side by side and the numbers lie to you twice. Pakistan's and China's national averages are nearly identical, yet Pakistan's overtime is a story of economic coercion in a labor market with too many applicants and too little enforcement, while China's is a story of social coercion in a labor market with, on paper, some of the stricter overtime law in Asia. Pakistan under-regulates and under-delivers; China over-legislates and under-enforces its own legislation, which is arguably the more impressive trick.

The irony sharpens further up the income ladder. Pakistan's most grueling schedules sit in ordinary urban offices and factories, with elite white-collar and remote-tech workers often enjoying more flexibility than their counterparts in government ministries. China runs the reverse: its worst hours cluster at the top of the prestige economy, in the tech firms graduates compete hardest to join, where burnout functions as a status symbol rather than a hardship to escape. The country with the harsher statutory law produces the more extreme individual anecdotes; the country with the weaker statutory law produces the more extreme population-wide averages.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (r/developersPak) — A thread debating realistic hours for Pakistani tech jobs concluded that a 3-4 hour remote day for strong pay is "very rare but probably possible," not the norm; most posters described 7-9 hour on-site days once commute time, uncounted by any employer, was added back in.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A Cisco engineer who compared their own meeting-heavy week to China's reported 60-72 hour schedules got corrected by commenters who pointed out that China's real total includes unpaid meals and meetings, plus firms running midnight shuttles and free dinners specifically to keep people at their desks past nine.*

> *Pakistaniat.com (standing in for an unreachable Quora thread) — A reader comment described a telecom project manager demanding ten cell sites go live in a single day; the engineer skipped meals and collapsed from heat after completing four, and the manager's only recorded response was "Fire him!"*

> *ProPakistani.pk — A foreign CEO reflecting on five years running a company in Pakistan recalled a Karachi colleague pausing an active meeting specifically to have food served to him at his desk, the kind of hospitality that softens, without shortening, an already long day.*

> *South China Morning Post, reporting on a viral post — A British hire at a Guangzhou game studio worked three consecutive Saturdays and regularly left after 10 p.m., despite overtime never being written into his contract, because he "didn't want to let his team down."*

### Conclusion

If the single number that matters most to a prospective mover is "hours per week," Pakistan and China will disappoint you about equally. If the number that actually matters is "who decides when I go home," the countries diverge sharply: in Pakistan, that decision sits with your specific manager and the specific office culture they've built, which means your actual experience is a coin flip decided in the interview, not the labor code. In China, the decision increasingly sits with your specific employer's compliance posture — a DJI or a Midea is now a genuinely different proposition from a firm still running informal 996 — which means the law, however unevenly enforced, is finally starting to matter.

Neither country will hand you a four-day week, and neither pretends to. Pack accordingly, ask your interviewer the one question that matters — "what time does this office actually empty on a Tuesday" — and when the brochure says "healthy work-life integration," read it the way you'd read a restaurant review that only ever mentions the parking.
