# Kenya vs. Sweden: Haggle in the Market, Whisper in the Office

🇰🇪 Kenya · 🇸🇪 Sweden

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Nairobi, a stranger on a matatu will ask your salary before they ask your name. In Stockholm, two colleagues who have shared an office for a decade could not tell you what the person at the next desk earns, and would find the question faintly obscene. Kenya's informal sector employs roughly 83 percent of the workforce, according to the International Labour Organization, which means most pay is set the old-fashioned way: two people staring at each other until one blinks. Sweden's labour market runs on collective bargaining agreements covering close to 90 percent of workers, which means most pay is set the Swedish way: a union already did the staring for you, months ago, over fika. Neither country believes in posting a number on the wall. They simply disagree, spectacularly, about who is allowed to ask for one out loud.

#### 🇰🇪 Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research market rate via Glassdoor, PayScale, or MySalaryScale before any interview | Accept the first verbal offer as final — treat it as an opening bid |
| Negotiate the full package (housing allowance, transport, medical cover, school fees), not just base pay | Discuss your exact salary loudly with new coworkers — norms vary by office, ask around quietly first |
| Ask directly about the pay band; it is an expected question in professional interviews | Assume a written contract exists — get one signed before your first day |
| Check whether the role falls under a COTU-affiliated union before negotiating solo | Skip verifying statutory minimum wage compliance, which was revised again in 2026 |
| Expect relatives and casual acquaintances to ask your salary outright — it is normal, not rude | Be surprised when a "final offer" moves twice more after you push back once |

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your union's (Unionen, Sveriges Ingenjörer, etc.) published salary statistics before any pay conversation | Say "I'm flexible" when asked for a number — it reads as unprepared, not agreeable |
| State a specific monthly gross figure (bruttolön) with a defensible range | Expect a large first-year raise; annual increases average roughly 2–3 percent regardless of performance |
| Ask separately about pension contributions (tjänstepension), vacation days, and parental-leave top-ups | Discuss salary details openly with colleagues — it remains quietly taboo even as EU transparency rules arrive |
| Join the applicable union early — it is your main source of leverage and benchmark data | Assume "there's no room to negotiate" means there is none — movement exists, it's just quiet |
| Read the kollektivavtal covering your sector before signing anything | Wait for loyalty to produce a raise — changing employers is the fastest route up |

### Kenya

Kenya's formal-sector average gross monthly salary sits near KSh 80,000 (about USD 620), per KNBS Economic Survey earnings data, with the general minimum wage in major cities raised to roughly KSh 18,047 a month after the 2026 Labour Day gazette notice. Those are the only numbers anyone official will hand you. Everything else — what your specific role, your specific employer, and your specific charm are worth — gets settled in a room, verbally, with no published band to anchor the conversation. This is less a bureaucratic failure than a structural fact: the ILO estimates that roughly 83 percent of Kenyan employment sits in the informal economy, where "salary transparency" is not withheld so much as nonexistent, because there was never a payroll system to be transparent about.

What Kenya lacks in published data it compensates for in appetite. Negotiation is not merely tolerated in professional hiring, it is expected, and job-market guidance routinely tells candidates that employers respect people who advocate for themselves and rarely rescind offers over a well-argued counter. The catch is that this individual boldness is not evenly distributed. Research cited by TIFA Research director Margaret Ireri finds that women in Kenya negotiate pay markedly less assertively than men, and often settle for less even when negotiations succeed — one contributor to a gender pay gap that UN Women's 2024 brief on Kenya put at 43 to 46 percent in recent years, with some individual sectors reporting gaps above 50 percent for identical work. A market that rewards nerve, it turns out, rewards it selectively.

The practical upshot for a newcomer: nobody will hand you a number, so you must arrive with one, built from comparison sites, professional networks, and blunt questions asked of people who have done your job before you.

### Sweden

Sweden's average gross monthly salary runs around SEK 38,000 (roughly USD 3,600), according to Statistics Sweden (SCB), with median pay sitting close behind it — a narrow gap that is itself the point. Wage compression is a design feature of a system in which collective agreements, not individual haggling, set the floor for close to 90 percent of the workforce. There is no statutory minimum wage in Sweden; there is instead a small library of sector-specific agreements between unions and employer associations that most people simply defer to.

This is also, formally, becoming Europe's most surveilled pay system. Sweden was among the first EU states to publish draft legislation implementing the bloc's Pay Transparency Directive, which will eventually require employers with 100 or more staff to report gender pay gaps and justify any disparity above 5 percent, with reporting obligations phasing in from 2027. Job applicants will be entitled to salary-range information before an interview, which sounds, on paper, like the end of ambiguity. And yet Sweden's unadjusted gender pay gap still sits near 16 percent, the lowest in Europe but far from zero — proof that a system engineered for equity and a system engineered for silence about individual numbers can be the same system. Swedes will tell you precisely what the union survey says a role pays, and never once what they personally take home.

### The Reckoning

Line the two up and the irony sharpens: Kenya offers almost total individual negotiating freedom against almost total informational darkness, while Sweden offers almost total informational structure against almost total individual silence. A Kenyan candidate can ask for more and frequently get it, but has no institutional data to know what "more" should mean. A Swedish candidate can look up exactly what their union says the job is worth, then must never mention what they personally settled for. Hofstede Insights scores Sweden low on power distance and unusually "feminine" — consensus-driven, discussion over confrontation — while Kenyan workplace culture, shaped by more hierarchical, relationship-first norms, leaves more room for direct one-to-one bargaining once you are in the room.

The other irony is that neither system has actually closed its gender pay gap — Sweden's sits at roughly 16 percent, Kenya's at over 40 percent by some measures — which suggests that transparency and negotiation freedom are each, on their own, necessary but insufficient. Sweden proves you can publish everything and still let a gap persist quietly. Kenya proves you can leave everything to the individual and watch the gap widen for exactly the people least inclined to ask.

> *Blind — an engineer negotiating with a Stockholm tech employer expected the usual noncommittal dance around salary range, but found Swedish recruiters wanted a specific number immediately; what first read as pushy turned out to be standard local practice, not aggression.*

> *The Local Sweden — a piece aimed at newcomers advised researching the relevant union's published salary survey before any offer conversation, since Swedish employers expect a defensible figure, not a vague hope that "the market will decide."*

> *Quora — an expat moving to Nairobi was pleasantly surprised at how much room existed to negotiate the full relocation package — housing, school fees, a vehicle — once the base salary number itself had been agreed, describing it as far more flexible than home-country compensation talks.*

> *British Expats forum — a newcomer to Nairobi noted there were no formal, published salary bands to consult anywhere, and ended up triangulating a fair offer by quietly asking several people already working similar roles what they made.*

### Conclusion

The difference that actually matters for a relocating professional is not the size of the paycheck but the shape of the information you'll be working with. In Kenya, bring a calculator, a thick skin, and a willingness to ask blunt questions of near-strangers, because no institution is going to hand you a benchmark. In Sweden, bring a union membership and patience, because the benchmark already exists in a spreadsheet somewhere and your job is mostly to cite it correctly, then never speak of your own number again.

If a friend asked me which is less stressful, I'd say neither — one just trades the anxiety of not knowing for the anxiety of not being allowed to say.
