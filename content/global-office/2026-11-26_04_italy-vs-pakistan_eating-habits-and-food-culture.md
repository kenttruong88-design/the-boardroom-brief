# Two Hours for Lunch in Italy, One Shared Tiffin in Pakistan

🇮🇹 Italy · 🇵🇰 Pakistan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

According to OECD time-use data reported by Statista, the French spend two hours and 13 minutes a day eating and drinking, with the Italians a short breadstick behind at just over two hours. Americans manage roughly an hour. Pakistan does not appear in that league table, but its offices have their own formal arrangement with food: a delivery rider on a motorbike, a metal tiffin carrier serving three or four colleagues, and a whole office quietly refusing to eat in front of anyone who is fasting. One country treats lunch as a negotiation. The other treats it as hospitality with a deadline. Both consider the desk sandwich a mild form of social failure.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take a proper lunch break, usually 1 to 2 hours, with colleagues | Eat a sandwich alone at your desk; it is described as rare and faintly suspicious |
| Expect a break of around 90 minutes in many offices, and a legal break after six hours of work | Assume the length is written into law; the long midday break is mainly tradition |
| Accept a business lunch invitation; it signals the relationship is progressing | Rush a business lunch that may run up to three hours, or reach for the bill when you are the guest |
| Budget about 8 to 15 euros for a weekday lunch, in line with BVA Doxa estimates | Ignore the meal voucher; around 3.5 million workers use one, averaging 6.75 euros |
| Stay for the espresso at a nearby bar, where working relationships get less formal | Skip the coffee; it is where much of the informal business gets done |
| Ask for healthier options; about 80 percent of surveyed workers want them | Assume every city keeps the two-hour break; it is reported to be shorter in Milan |

### Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect lunch between about 1 and 3 pm in cities, usually brief and simple | Plan a leisurely two-hour lunch on an ordinary working day |
| Share food from a tiffin or join colleagues pooling money for a group order | Order only for yourself in a close-knit office without offering to share |
| Allow longer lunch hours on Fridays, a holy day, when afternoon commerce often winds down | Schedule an afternoon meeting at the last minute on a Friday |
| Eat and drink out of sight during Ramadan if you are not fasting | Eat or drink publicly in front of fasting colleagues; it is considered disrespectful |
| Check the shortened Ramadan hours, such as 9 am to 3 pm in federal five-day offices | Assume the usual eight-hour schedule survives the month |
| Soften a refusal to a lunch invitation; direct refusals are seen as rude | Treat a vague yes as a firm commitment; direct refusals are seen as rude |

## Italy: Lunch Is Not a Break, It Is a Meeting

According to Expat.com, Italian lunch breaks run from one to two hours, with about 90 minutes the usual figure, and the law requires only a pause after six hours of work. The length is cultural, not legal. Expat.com's Rome work-culture guide says lunch is often taken outside the office, is always convivial, and that it is rare for an employee to eat a sandwich alone at the desk. HousingAnywhere adds that lunch can stretch to two hours, and that a business lunch can last up to three, with the host paying. The OECD figure that Italians spend more than two hours a day eating suggests this is not folklore. Wanted in Rome notes that in much of the country, especially outside Milan, many businesses close for the break and towns go quiet in the early afternoon, and that dinner arrives late, with Rome's restaurants busiest around 9:30 pm. The standard office day, Expat.com says, runs roughly 9 am to 6 pm, so the lunch sits in the middle like a small constitutional monarchy.

The institution that makes this affordable is the buono pasto. QuiFinanza, citing 2023 figures, puts the average voucher at 6.75 euros per working day, used by about 3.5 million workers, around 700,000 of them in the public sector. Meanwhile, BVA Doxa estimates a sandwich, drink and coffee at 8.10 euros, and a main course with drink and coffee at 11.60 euros. In an Edenred survey reported by Il Sole 24 Ore, 80 percent of workers called the voucher essential support and 71 percent said losing it would significantly hurt their budget. The arithmetic of the Italian lunch, in other words, is a gap of a few euros that the employer fills and the worker spends on carbohydrates.

The lunch even survives remote work as a grievance. A Camst-Censis report in Il Sole 24 Ore found that 54.7 percent of remote workers usually eat alone, and 26.6 percent think it puts them at a disadvantage at work. Of respondents overall, 85.2 percent said a good lunch break supports relationships with colleagues, and 54.2 percent had received useful work information while eating together. Italy has, in short, measured the commercial value of tortellini.

## Pakistan: Hospitality With a Deadline

Pakistani office lunch is shorter and more improvised. Remitly describes the weekday lunch as quick and simple, usually between 1 and 3 pm in cities, with office workers bringing food from home or buying from a nearby cafe or canteen and aiming to be back on time. Yet the break is not joyless. In Daily Times, an IT engineer at a multinational in Lahore described a 90-minute lunch as the most anticipated part of her day, and a law firm's colleagues pooled money for pizza, which was cheaper and more sociable. Arab News reported in October 2024 that Karachi tiffin carriers, which cost about 520 rupees for three to four people, are delivered to offices and often shared with colleagues, partly because people consider them cleaner and closer to home cooking.

Food in the Pakistani office becomes a matter of religion for a month each year. According to the Cultural Atlas, most Pakistanis abstain from food, water and smoking during daylight hours in Ramadan, and it is considered disrespectful to engage in these activities publicly. Dunya News reported that for Ramadan 2026 the federal government set offices to run 9 am to 3 pm in five-day offices and 9 am to 2 pm in six-day offices, closing at 12:30 pm on Fridays. Qureos reports that the private sector often follows a similar shortened schedule of about six hours a day instead of eight, though it describes this as a trend rather than an official notification. The Cultural Atlas adds that Fridays often bring longer lunch hours and early commercial closure. Remitly says dinner usually starts between 8 and 10 pm, so a short lunch is followed by a late evening, much as in Italy. The Express Tribune notes that the preparation of iftar and sehri meals falls heavily on women in extended families, which is a reminder that the office is only one half of Ramadan's catering.

The result is a workplace where the lunch period is smaller, but the food etiquette is arguably more demanding. Ius Laboris advises employers generally that not all Muslim employees observe Ramadan in the same way, which is a useful rule in any office: do not assume, and do not make a show of your sandwich.

## The Reckoning

The cleanest contrast is time. Italy allots a generous, semi-sacred lunch slot and builds a voucher system to pay for it. Pakistan allots a shorter weekday slot but arranges food around the group, through tiffins, pooled orders and a month of collective restraint that the whole office observes. In Italy, the person eating alone is the oddity. In Pakistan, the person eating in front of fasting colleagues is.

Money and rules differ too. Italy has a state-supported benefit that makes an eight-euro lunch a routine, almost fiscal, ritual. Pakistan has a calendar that rewrites the working day each Ramadan, with the federal timetable moving by hours. What they share is a refusal to treat food as fuel. Both countries expect lunch to build relationships, and both will quietly form opinions about the person who does not join in.

## Conclusion

Moving to Italy, expect lunch to be part of the job, and plan to join it. Find out whether your employer provides buoni pasto, budget for the gap between the voucher and the actual price, and learn that a long lunch with colleagues is a networking event, not a skipped afternoon. Moving to Pakistan, expect shorter weekday lunches, plenty of sharing, and a Ramadan timetable that changes your hours, so confirm the shortened schedule with your employer and be discreet about eating and drinking around fasting colleagues.

If you are Muslim and considering Italy, or Italian-trained and considering Pakistan, ask employers directly about prayer space, dietary provision and Ramadan arrangements before signing, because the public sources reviewed here do not settle the question. One last practical truth is that in either country, eating alone at your desk will be noticed, and not in the way you hope.
