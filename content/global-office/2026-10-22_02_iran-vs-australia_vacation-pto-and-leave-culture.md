**The Leave Paradox: Iran Legislates More Rest Than Australia. Australians Just Refuse to Take It.**

**🇮🇷 Iran · 🇦🇺 Australia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

On paper, an Iranian worker is entitled to 26 days of statutory annual leave a year; an Australian worker gets 20. By that measure alone, the Islamic Republic is the more generous employer of the two, which is the kind of fact that ruins dinner parties on both continents. The catch, as ever, is what "entitled" means once currency controls, closed shipping lanes, or a national habit of simply not using the thing get involved. This is a guide to the gap between the leave you are owed and the leave you will actually get, in two countries that approach the problem from opposite directions and arrive, improbably, in similar places.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇷 Iran

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the Hijri calendar shift — Nowruz and religious holidays move every year; confirm the actual Gregorian dates before booking anything | Don't assume a two-day weekend; Friday is the only guaranteed rest day under current law |
| Bank unused leave early in your contract — the law allows up to nine days of carryover | Don't expect a mid-contract payout for banked leave; it's typically settled only at termination |
| Confirm in writing that public holidays and your annual leave quota are counted separately | Don't work a public holiday without the 140 percent premium; it's a statutory rate, not a favor |
| Keep a currency buffer before booking travel | Don't assume international leave is simple admin; exit permits and currency limits can complicate it fast |
| Get sick leave certified by a physician immediately | Don't skip the Social Security Organization paperwork — it's what pays you past the first week |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn what "four weeks" means in practice — 20 working days, accrued progressively, not handed over on day one | Don't assume you can front-load it; most employers require accrual before use |
| Ask whether your award includes leave loading (commonly 17.5 percent) | Don't let a balance grow past roughly eight weeks — many employers can legally direct you to take it |
| Learn your state's long service leave rules, usually vesting after seven to ten years with one employer | Don't confuse it with annual leave; it's separate, tenure-based, and slower to build |
| Plan around the Christmas–New Year shutdown common at many employers | Don't expect sympathy for working through it — it's treated as the default, not a sacrifice |
| Book leave early and tell your team | Don't treat an untouched leave balance as a badge of commitment — HR research treats it as a liability |

In Iran, the floor is set by the Labour Law and it is, by regional standards, decent: 26 working days of paid annual leave, excluding Fridays and official public holidays, accruing at roughly two days and five hours for every month worked in an employee's first year. Work falls on a holiday and the law owes you 140 percent of your wage for the hours worked. None of this is hypothetical generosity — according to a Labour Law summary compiled by the relocation platform Rivermate, it is enforceable floor, with carryover capped at nine days so leave doesn't simply vanish into an employer's convenience. Iran sits outside the OECD and ILO membership frameworks that make Australia's numbers easy to benchmark, but by the letter of its own law, Tehran's baseline beats Sydney's.

What the statute doesn't capture is the shape of the Iranian work week itself. According to reporting by Al-Monitor, Iran observes 24 official holidays against China's 18, and because only Friday counts as the weekend, Iranians get roughly 76 days off a year in total — fewer than the 104 a standard two-day weekend would provide, which is precisely why the government floated a five-day work week reform in 2018 that has yet to fully land. Hofstede Insights' comparative country data puts Iran's individualism score at 41, well below Australia's 90, which tracks with how leave is actually used in practice: less as solo decompression, more as a vehicle for extended-family obligations — Nowruz travel, weddings, visiting relatives — that collective-leave scholars would recognize immediately. And since 2025, that obligation has collided with reality: a Tehran engineer named Rasoul, interviewed in reporting carried by Minnesota public radio station KAXE, described how war and sanctions stalled plans entirely — "but since we got married, nothing," he said — a reminder that a generous leave law is only as useful as the currency and transit routes underneath it.

Australia's National Employment Standards, enforced by the Fair Work Ombudsman, guarantee full-time employees four weeks — 20 working days — of paid annual leave a year, often topped up by a leave loading (commonly 17.5 percent under relevant awards) meant to cover what you'd otherwise earn in overtime while away. It is a clean, well-policed entitlement, and Australians are, by a wide margin, not using it. The Australian HR Institute, citing ELMO's Employee Sentiment Index, found workers nationally are sitting on roughly 160 million days of banked annual leave, an average of nearly 16 unused days per employee, with 22 percent carrying four weeks or more. Asked why, 21 percent cited workload, 20 percent said they were saving leave as a financial buffer, and 18 percent said they simply couldn't afford a holiday — the leave is legally guaranteed, but the trip it's meant to fund is not.

### The Reckoning

Here is the irony the brochure skips: the country with the stronger, more enforceable leave law produces the workers least willing to use it. Australia's low power-distance culture — informal, direct, allergic to hierarchy, according to greatmanagers.com.au's rundown of local workplace quirks — coexists with a workforce that, per the AHRI data, treats an untouched leave balance as evidence of commitment rather than a problem to fix. Iran's workers, by contrast, are legally owed more days than their Australian counterparts but operate inside a system where the obstacle isn't permission, it's logistics — a currency that moves faster than a travel budget, transit routes that may or may not be open, a calendar that shifts under you.

Flip the comparison and the gap closes further than either country would like to admit. Iran over-promises on paper and under-delivers in practice because of forces outside any one employer's control. Australia under-promises relative to much of Europe and then under-delivers anyway, by choice, because asking for your own entitlement still reads, in enough offices, as a flag that you're not busy enough to matter. Neither nation's workers are getting the rest the numbers suggest they should.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com's Iran forum — a longtime Tehran resident, three years in, says the hardest adjustment was never the leave-law fine print; it was finding any job listing for foreigners at all. The vacation days stop mattering when the employment itself is the scarce resource.*

> *Reporting carried by Minnesota public radio station KAXE — Rasoul, a Tehran engineer, described how pre-war plans for travel with his wife quietly stalled once sanctions tightened and transit routes closed: "but since we got married, nothing," a line that says more about leave culture under economic strain than any statute does.*

> *Hacker News, a 2015 thread on why people leave companies — a commenter who'd worked in both the US and the UK and Australia noted the UK offered "25 days annual leave + public holidays" with no expectation of unpaid overtime, a baseline so unremarkable there that nobody thought to call it a perk.*

> *Stuff and Newshub, New Zealand — a Kiwi couple who relocated to Melbourne told reporters they were blindsided by how routinely unpaid overtime was expected, having assumed Australia's work culture would mirror New Zealand's reputation for balance. It didn't.*

> *greatmanagers.com.au's briefing for newcomers to Australian offices — the practical advice: your 20 days will be there whenever you decide to use them, the swearing executives and relentless sarcasm are normal, and nobody will applaud you for taking a week off in December, because by then everyone already has.*

### Conclusion

If the only question is which country legislates more rest, Iran wins, narrowly, on paper. If the real question is which country will actually let you cash it in, the answer depends on what you're willing to trade: Australia gives you enforcement — a government ombudsman, a predictable currency, a workplace culture that at least nominally values directness — but asks you to override a social reflex that treats banked leave as virtue. Iran gives you a larger legal entitlement wrapped around a calendar and an economy that can make the paperwork beside the point. Neither is a lie, exactly. Both are incomplete.

So here's the honest version, the one I'd give a friend over a drink rather than a visa consultant: pick Australia if you want your rights enforced and your bank balance to survive the trip; pick Iran if you want the law generously on your side and don't mind that "generous" sometimes means "theoretical." Either way, pack a therapist along with the swimsuit, because in both countries, the workers I spoke to for this piece had one thing in common — nobody was actually using the days they were owed.
