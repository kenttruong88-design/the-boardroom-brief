# The Glass Ceiling Wears Different Shoes: Gender at Work in Israel and Spain

**🇮🇱 Israel · 🇪🇸 Spain**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

![Israel vs Spain — Gender dynamics in the workplace](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/global-office/2026-09-23-01-israel-vs-spain-gender-dynamics-in-the-workplace-hero)
*Photo: Christina Morillo via Pexels*

In Israel, a 26-year-old woman can run reconnaissance for an elite intelligence unit at nineteen and be told at twenty-nine that she is "too aggressive" for a product-lead role. In Spain, a woman can outrank her male colleagues on paper and still be the one who fetches the coffee, because someone has to, and it is somehow never him. Neither country would describe itself as behind on gender equality. According to the OECD, Israel's gender pay gap sits at 20.8 percent, among the widest in the developed world; Spain's, per a 2025 EADA/ICSA Grupo report, has actually widened for the first time in seven years to 12.9 percent. The numbers alone won't tell you which country is harder to build a career in as a woman. The texture will.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇱 Israel

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Speak up in meetings early — hierarchy is flat and silence reads as absence, not humility | Assume military service on your résumé matters if you're a foreign hire — it doesn't, but its absence is quietly noted internally |
| Ask directly about reserve-duty (*miluim*) coverage policies before accepting an offer, especially in a two-income household | Expect maternity leave to fully insulate your career trajectory — informal penalties for time away are common in fast-growth startups |
| Negotiate salary hard — "chutzpah" is a compliment, not a complaint | Mistake directness for equality — Unit 8200's 55% female intake doesn't reflect industry-wide VC or leadership numbers (14.8% of VC partners are women) |
| Join or ask about internal women's networks (FemForward and similar groups grew fast post-2023) | Assume a young median company age means young attitudes about promotion pace for mothers |
| Clarify whether "informal" also means "after-hours," since a lot of real decisions happen at 9pm over WhatsApp | Take a Friday deadline literally — Shabbat changes everyone's week, and negotiating around it is normal, not a favor |

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the room before the org chart — informal influence often outweighs formal title | Expect a policy against workplace comments on appearance to be enforced the same way it's written — 40% of women report harassment, most unreported |
| Ask existing female employees, not HR, how promotions to management actually happen | Assume a big company has solved this — women hold under 5% of board seats nationally |
| Use the *reducción de jornada* (reduced hours) parental provisions — they're a legal right, not a favor asked | Bring up salary comparisons casually; Spain's opacity around pay makes the 27% same-job gap hard to even document from inside |
| Build alliances across the long lunch — real trust in Spanish offices is built over food, not Slack | Mistake southern-European warmth and physical familiarity for equal footing — the same charm can mask real asymmetry in who gets heard |
| Push back on "jokes" early and explicitly — cultural tolerance for them is generational and shrinking, but only if someone shrinks it | Wait for a formal HR channel to feel safe before raising discomfort — most women report it doesn't |

### Israel

Israeli work culture prides itself on flatness: junior engineers argue with VPs in stand-ups, and everyone calls the CEO by their first name. That flatness is real, and it does lower some barriers — women entering tech via elite IDF intelligence units like 8200, which runs roughly 55 percent female according to reporting from The Register, arrive with technical credibility that would take years to build elsewhere. But flatness is not the same as parity. A 2022 diversity study covering 424 Israeli startups and 70 VC funds found women hold about a third of positions at mid-size companies and just 14.8 percent of VC partnerships — the gatekeeping layer, not the working layer. Haaretz's tech desk has reported that the army's own statistics on women in tech roles have been accused of "juking the stats," inflating participation numbers that don't hold up at the senior end.

The other variable unique to Israel is reserve duty. Most men serve *miluim* annually until their forties; most women are released from reserve obligations well before that, typically around 26. On paper this should mean women's careers are less disrupted by military call-ups. In practice, according to reporting on Israeli tech hiring, it has helped fuel a different problem: founders openly say they prefer hires with combat or elite-unit backgrounds, a pipeline that structurally advantages men, and startup culture's assumption of total availability penalizes anyone — disproportionately women with young children — who can't match a founder's 80-hour week.

### Spain

Spain's gender dynamics carry a different history. Machismo, as a term, is not a metaphor here so much as a documented cultural residue of the Franco era, when women's legal autonomy was minimal until 1975. Fifty years on, the numbers are sobering rather than shocking: the Center for Economic Policy at Esade has tracked a persistent, multi-decade wage gap, and The Local Spain reported that 40 percent of women say they've experienced sexual harassment, with a striking 72 percent of those who experienced workplace harassment never reporting it, citing fear of stigma or retaliation. Major Spanish companies list women as just 2.8 percent of administrators, a statistic that sits oddly next to a national self-image of modern, EU-integrated informality.

What complicates the picture is generational speed. Younger Spanish managers, particularly in Madrid and Barcelona's growing startup scenes, increasingly treat overt machismo as an embarrassing relic rather than a workplace norm, and Spain's government has floated formal office-conduct guidelines specifically targeting "jokes" and unwanted physical contact. But formal policy in Spain tends to move faster than informal practice — the same generational shift hasn't closed the boardroom gap, where women hold under 5 percent of seats. Hofstede Insights scores Spain at 42 on masculinity, actually lower than Israel's 47, suggesting Spanish culture prizes quality-of-life values over overt competitive assertiveness — a data point that sits uneasily next to the machismo statistics, and is worth remembering before assuming a "softer" national score means a softer workplace reality for women.

### The Reckoning

Here is the irony: Israel is the more meritocratic-feeling workplace day to day, and Spain has the more progressive-sounding policy framework, yet both post gender pay gaps that would embarrass either country's self-image. Israel's flatness helps women get heard in the room; Spain's formal labor protections — reduced-hours rights, harassment guidelines under active government development — offer more paper protection. Neither converts reliably into who actually gets promoted.

The other divergence is where the penalty lands. In Israel, the tax on being a woman in the workforce is mostly paid at the top — in venture capital rooms and founder networks that quietly favor a military-adjacent pedigree. In Spain, the tax is paid earlier and more diffusely, in a thousand small moments of being talked over, joked about, or simply not considered for the stretch assignment — the sort of thing that's individually deniable and collectively devastating to a CV by year ten.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A woman comparing simultaneous offers in Spain, Germany, and Israel wrote that Israel's workplace felt the most instantly candid about ambition — nobody blinked when she said she wanted her manager's job in two years — but that the same bluntness extended to blunt comments about her plans to have children, delivered as a "logistics question" rather than a red flag to her.*

> *Quora — Someone answering "what is life like for ladies working in Israel" described high-tech and finance as genuinely competitive fields for women to enter, crediting military technical units for the pipeline, but noted that the real ceiling showed up later, at the point where informal founder networks decided who got introduced to investors.*

> *Blind — A tech worker's post titled "Being the only woman in team" described the exhausting arithmetic of being the sole woman in every planning meeting: not overt hostility, just a constant low-grade tax of having to prove technical credibility that male colleagues were simply assumed to have.*

> *The Local Spain — In a reader-sourced piece asking whether machismo is still alive in Spanish offices, several women described a familiar pattern: formal policies existed, HR trainings had been run, and yet the person who got interrupted in meetings and the person who got the stretch project rarely required a memo to sort out.*

> *A Substack expat newsletter from Madrid ("Letter from Spain") described the disorienting gap between Spain's loud, informal social culture — where colleagues become genuine friends over three-hour lunches — and a surprisingly rigid, seniority-bound promotion process that the writer said took over a year to fully understand, by which point several male peers had already been quietly fast-tracked.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two moves as a woman building a career, the honest framing is: Israel will likely make you feel more capable faster, and Spain will likely make you feel more supported on paper — and neither feeling should be mistaken for the actual promotion data, which is unflattering in both places. The practical move is the same regardless of country: find the women already three years ahead of you at the company, not the policy document, and ask them what actually happened when they asked for the raise, the title, or the six months off. Priya's honest line for a friend over a drink: pick the country whose *specific company* has women in senior roles who'll return your LinkedIn message — the national statistics are a weather report, not a forecast for your building.
