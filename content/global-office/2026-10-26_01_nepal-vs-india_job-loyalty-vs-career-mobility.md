**Nepal Promotes You in Twelve Years. India Promotes You by Resigning.**

**🇳🇵 Nepal · 🇮🇳 India**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Kathmandu, an employee who changes jobs twice in a decade is considered restless; in Bengaluru, an employee who stays at one company for four years is asked, gently, whether everything is all right. Nepal sent home remittances equal to roughly 27 percent of its GDP last year, according to World Bank data — a national economy substantially built on workers who stayed loyal to employers they could only reach by airplane. India, meanwhile, treats the resignation letter as a performance review: an Indeed survey found six in ten Indian workers have already job-hopped, and nearly four in ten said a better offer elsewhere was simply the fastest route to a raise their own appraisal cycle would not give them. Loyalty, it turns out, is not a national personality trait. It is what people do when mobility is scarce, and what they stop doing the moment it isn't.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇵 Nepal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take the "Loksewa" public-service exam culture seriously in conversation — many young Nepalis treat it as a career finish line | Dismiss a colleague's government-job ambitions as a lack of drive |
| Expect multi-year tenure to be the default and build working relationships at that pace | Expect promotions tied purely to performance reviews — seniority still carries real weight |
| Assume several colleagues have close family working in the Gulf, Malaysia, or South Korea, and factor that into scheduling and goodwill | Treat a stable local job as proof no one in the room is thinking about leaving the country |
| Build in slack for load-shedding and patchy connectivity when setting "always-on" remote expectations | Schedule a mission-critical call assuming uninterrupted power or internet with no backup plan |
| Recognize that a resignation is often a household decision, not just an individual one | Treat someone's reluctance to quit a bad job as simple passivity |

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect multiple short stints on a resume, especially in tech and metro hubs, and ask about them neutrally | Flag every short tenure as a red flag before hearing the reason |
| Benchmark salaries against the market every year — internal raises often lag badly behind switching pay | Assume loyalty will be rewarded with proportional annual increases |
| Build switching and counteroffer costs into retention planning from day one | Assume a strong initial offer alone guarantees a long tenure |
| Note the regional and sector split — metro startups prize mobility, PSUs and smaller towns still prize stability | Generalize "Indian work culture" as one thing without naming the sector |
| Expect some households to push a government or banking job even as the private sector around them churns constantly | Assume job-hopping ambition and stability-seeking can't exist in the same family |

Nepal's labour market is small and thin, and its own statistics office says so plainly. The Central Bureau of Statistics' Nepal Labour Force Survey puts labour force participation at just 38.5 percent, unemployment at 11.4 percent, and underemployment at a striking 39.3 percent. Against that backdrop, the loyalty for which Nepali professionals are sometimes praised looks less like virtue and more like arithmetic: there are not enough comparable jobs to hop between. The ILO has long described overseas labour migration as a defining feature of Nepal's economy, and Nepal Rastra Bank data shows why — remittance inflows hit roughly $16.19 billion in FY2025/26, up 28.1 percent on the year before. The office worker who stays a decade at one Kathmandu firm and the laborer cycling through two-year contracts in Doha or Kuala Lumpur are, statistically, pursuing the same thing: stability bought by not changing employer once the right one is found.

India inverts the premium entirely. Randstad's Gen Z Workplace Blueprint found that 37 percent of Indian Gen Z professionals plan to leave their current job within a year — the third-highest rate of any country surveyed, behind only Poland and Italy — with Gen Z tenure generally compressed to about a year within the first five years of a career. The Indeed survey goes further into the broader workforce: roughly six in ten Indian employees have already job-hopped, 85 percent said they'd list a short stint on their resume regardless, and nearly half called job-hopping a legitimate way to acquire new skills rather than a character flaw. The economics back them up — pay surveys circulated in Indian HR circles routinely show switchers earning raises two to four times larger than those who stay, which makes loyalty an expensive virtue to practice.

### The Reckoning

The two countries are easy to lump together as "South Asian," and the comparison falls apart almost immediately. Nepal's low mobility is substantially involuntary — the domestic market is too shallow to absorb ambition, so the "loyal" employee is often simply the one who could not get a visa, a posting, or a referral abroad yet. India's hypermobility, by contrast, is not restlessness for its own sake; it is a rational response to a labour market that has finally grown deep enough, especially in tech and finance, to reward the switch. Hofstede Insights does not maintain an official country profile for Nepal, but India's own published scores — high power distance, moderate individualism for the region — describe a culture where hierarchy is respected yet increasingly treated as something to be climbed quickly rather than waited out, which is exactly the tension playing out in its job market.

The real irony shows up when the two systems touch. A Nepali professional who lands one international post — often, per several professional-forum accounts, through an India-based multinational first — tends to then out-switch their Indian peers, because one hard-won opening abroad has to compress a decade's worth of career moves into a much shorter runway. Meanwhile, an Indian graduate who lands a coveted public-sector bank job suddenly becomes as tenure-bound as any Nepali civil servant: same incentives, same behavior, opposite passport. Recruiters on both sides of the border have learned to read resumes accordingly — a long single stint from Nepal reads as proof of stability, while the identical tenure from an Indian metro candidate increasingly reads as a quiet admission that nobody else wanted to make an offer.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (via HR Katha reporting) — a nine-month public-sector bank employee described the promise of a "secure, easy-going" government job as a myth, citing staff shortages, impossible rural sales targets, and being made to work until 9 p.m. while unwell, with leaving frowned upon by family and community.*

> *Blind — one thread debated whether hiring managers should penalize candidates with several short stints; one commenter admitted to deliberately asking harder interview questions to screen such candidates out, while another argued this produces false negatives against people with genuine reasons for leaving.*

> *Blind — a Nepali professional described working for Microsoft's India operation before moving on to a monetization role at Meta in London, framing the Indian posting as the stepping stone that made the eventual international move possible.*

> *Expat.com — a long-term poster in Nepal described a local business partnership souring after ethnic loyalties among partners surfaced, alongside practical warnings about dry-season power cuts running up to several hours a day and the roughly $300 inverter considered a basic cost of doing business.*

### Conclusion

If you are weighing Nepal against India for the next stage of a career, the honest question is not "which culture values loyalty more," but "which market can actually reward you for staying, and which can reward you for leaving." Nepal will ask for patience because the domestic ladder is short and social expectation is long; India will ask for nerve because the ladder is tall but nobody is holding the door for you while you climb it slowly. Choose based on which kind of waiting you can actually tolerate — and if anyone tells you either country's job market runs on loyalty alone, ask them who's been paying their rent.
