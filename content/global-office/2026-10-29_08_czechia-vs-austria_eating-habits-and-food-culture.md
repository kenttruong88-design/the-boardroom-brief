# Austria Greets You at Noon With a Single Word for "Meal." Czechia Just Hands You a Beer and Calls It Lunch.

**🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇦🇹 Austria**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Austria, you don't need to ask whether it's lunchtime — colleagues passing in the corridor around midday simply say "Mahlzeit" to each other, a word that literally means "meal-time" and functions as a full greeting on its own, whether or not either person is actually eating. In Czechia, the ritual is less linguistic and more liquid: ordering a beer with an 11:30am lunch is treated as entirely unremarkable, and one expat reader put the resulting physical toll plainly: "if I eat that much heavy food plus beer at lunchtime I have to go lie down afterwards." Both countries have built an entire midday culture around food. Only one of them built a word for it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect the 11:30am lunch break to empty the office at roughly the same time every day. | Eat lunch at your desk by default — stepping out is the norm, not an exception. |
| Accept that ordering a beer with lunch is genuinely unremarkable, even on a workday. | Assume the same applies to every colleague, every day — plenty skip it, it's simply not a scandal if they don't. |
| Join in office birthday and name-day (*svátek*) celebrations — cake and *chlebíčky* sandwiches are standard, and a toast of "všechno nejlepší" is expected if it's your day. | Expect to reach colleagues easily by afternoon during "cucumber season" (July to early September) — extended lunches and autoreplies are the norm. |
| Expect meat-heavy meals, with vegetables often meaning a side salad rather than a main component. | Expect a light, fast lunch purely for efficiency — the midday meal is treated as a real event worth the time. |
| Pace your afternoon around a heavier midday meal than you may be used to. | Schedule an important afternoon call right after lunch without checking the office's actual rhythm first. |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say "Mahlzeit" to colleagues around midday, even in passing, and expect it back. | Treat the greeting as an actual invitation to eat together — it's said whether or not either person is eating. |
| Expect lunch to happen at a fairly fixed midday window, mirrored by the greeting itself. | Assume dinner carries equal cultural weight — breakfast is named the most important meal of the day by nearly half of Austrians surveyed. |
| Expect colleagues, especially older ones, to treat regular, structured meals as personally important. | Assume everyone cooks for themselves — roughly a fifth of Austrians don't cook at all, more among younger men. |
| Use "Mahlzeit" as a light, polite register-setter, similar to "Guten Morgen." | Overuse it ironically unless you're confident of the room — that tends to be a younger, in-on-the-joke move, not a safe default. |
| Expect the custom to extend beyond the office — Austrians say it at home and on weekends too, unlike the more work-bound German usage. | Assume the Austrian and German versions of the word carry identical weight; Austrians lean on it more broadly than their northern neighbors do. |

### Czechia

The Czech workday runs on a fixed midday anchor: 11:30am lunch is described locally as close to "a national institution," with large shares of an office stepping out at roughly the same time rather than eating at their desks. What might raise eyebrows elsewhere barely registers here — ordering a beer alongside lunch is, per local etiquette guides, perfectly acceptable on an ordinary workday, and the culture extends into office ritual too: birthdays and name days get marked with cake or chlebíčky, the open-faced sandwiches that double as party food, complete with toasts of "všechno nejlepší" for whoever's being celebrated.

The reader voices collected by the expat-news platform Expats.cz capture the adjustment from the outside more vividly than any etiquette guide. One newcomer described simply moving their lunch two hours earlier to match local rhythm; another, used to lighter Western meals, reported the practical cost of going along with both the food and the beer at once. A third noted a shift toward meat-forward meals specifically: "more meat, fewer vegetables. Vegetables to a Czech person means salad to other people" — and several readers separately admitted drinking noticeably more beer overall than they had back home, less a vice than a simple absorption into the ambient culture around them. A fourth reader, asked directly why nobody seemed bothered by a lunchtime pint, shrugged it off as simply proportionate: a single beer paired with a large, slow meal reads less as drinking and more as pacing yourself through the rest of the day. That framing, moderation measured against the meal rather than the clock, recurs often enough in the reader archive that it reads as a genuine local attitude rather than a one-off rationalization.

### Austria

Austria's relationship to food shows up first in its language rather than its menu. "Mahlzeit" — literally "meal-time" — functions as a complete, standalone midday greeting, delivered in a cheerful, almost sing-song tone whether or not either person is actually eating, and it expects a reply in kind; colleagues passing each other in a corridor around noon will simply exchange the word and nothing else. It's considered as polite and unremarkable as "Guten Morgen," but distinctly food-coded, and unlike its more office-bound German cousin, Austrians use it at home, in the evening, and on weekends too.

Beneath the linguistic ritual, a 2025 IMAS survey of Austrians found 47 percent naming breakfast as their most important meal of the day, ahead of lunch at 35 percent and an evening snack at 14 percent — a structured, almost ceremonial relationship to regular eating that sits oddly next to the same survey's other finding: 21 percent of Austrians don't cook for themselves at all, a share that climbs to roughly 40 percent among men aged 16 to 34. The culture reveres the ritual of a proper meal more than it insists on personally producing one. That gap between ritual and practice shows up on supermarket shelves too: Austria has one of the more developed markets for ready-made and semi-prepared meals in the German-speaking world, which coexists comfortably with the same population professing near-reverence for a properly timed, properly announced meal. Dinner, notably, doesn't carry the same symbolic weight as breakfast or lunch in the IMAS data, treated by many households as a lighter, often cold affair of bread, cheese, and cured meats rather than a full cooked course, which can surprise newcomers expecting a hot meal to anchor the evening the way it might elsewhere in Europe.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't beer versus no beer, it's where each culture puts its ceremony. Czechia's food culture is communal and situational — a shared 11:30 exodus, a toast at a colleague's desk, a seasonal slowdown everyone quietly participates in during cucumber season — built around actions people do together. Austria's is linguistic and individual — a single word that stands in for the whole social contract around lunchtime, repeated by people who may not even be eating when they say it, next to survey data showing a meaningful share of the population has quietly opted out of cooking altogether.

Put differently: Czechia shows its food culture through what people actually do at lunch, while Austria preserves it mostly through what people say about it. On Hofstede's framework, Austria records one of the lowest power-distance scores in the world, which tracks with "Mahlzeit" functioning as a flat, universal greeting exchanged between a CEO and an intern with equal ease; Czechia's somewhat higher power-distance and uncertainty-avoidance scores sit more comfortably with a culture that formalizes its food rituals through fixed timing and shared celebration rather than a single egalitarian phrase. For someone relocating between the two, the practical takeaway is to adjust what you're listening for: in Prague, watch what the office actually does at 11:30 and follow along; in Vienna, listen for the word itself and treat it as a small daily test of whether you've been paying attention to the culture around you.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expats.cz reader voices — One American reader described the single biggest adjustment plainly: "I used to never eat breakfast back home (U.S.)," but an early shift in Czechia pushed them into a light morning meal they'd never bothered with before — a pleasant surprise about a habit they didn't know they were missing.*

> *Expats.cz reader voices — A British reader offered the opposite kind of surprise, finding the local diet more restrictive than expected despite its reputation for heartiness: "we eat a much more restricted diet here than we did in the UK" — a reminder that "heavier" and "more varied" aren't the same thing.*

> *Expats.cz reader voices — One reader's piece of advice for anyone planning to match the local lunch pace came through less as a tip than a confession: "if I eat that much heavy food plus beer at lunchtime I have to go lie down afterwards" — plan your 1pm meeting accordingly.*

> *The Local Austria — A linguistic note doing double duty as cultural advice: Austrians expect "Mahlzeit" to be returned in kind, even delivered to a mail carrier or someone they've never met, which makes failing to reply read as a minor social stumble rather than simple unfamiliarity with the custom.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving from Austria to Czechia, drop the instinct to treat lunch as a quick linguistic nod and budget real time, and real stomach space, for an actual midday meal — and don't be alarmed if a colleague orders a beer alongside it. If you're moving from Czechia to Austria, learn the word before you learn the menu; "Mahlzeit" will do more daily social work for you than any specific dish, and saying it back correctly matters more than anything you actually eat.

Either way, you'll leave both offices at roughly the same conclusion: lunch is not an interruption to the workday in Central Europe, it's load-bearing. Treat it that way before it's forced on you.
