# Costa Rica Wants You to Relax About Parenthood. Kazakhstan Wants You Back at Your Desk.

🇨🇷 Costa Rica · 🇰🇿 Kazakhstan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Costa Rica, a country whose national motto might as well be "no rush," gives new mothers four fully paid months off and then can't get enough of its own citizens to have babies — the fertility rate has collapsed to 1.32, among the lowest on earth. Kazakhstan, a country whose labor code reads like a spreadsheet of MCIs and tenge, offers a comparatively unglamorous benefits package and somehow maintains a fertility rate near 2.8, one of the highest outside sub-Saharan Africa and the Middle East. If you were hoping generous leave policy predicts national enthusiasm for reproduction, both countries would like a word.

#### 🇨🇷 Costa Rica

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with the CCSS (social security) the moment you're employed — it's what unlocks paid maternity leave and pediatric care | Assume "Pura Vida" scheduling applies to daycare waitlists — bilingual guarderías in Escazú and Tamarindo book out months in advance |
| Budget for private insurance if you want an English-speaking OB or a faster appointment | Skip formalizing a nanny's paperwork — Costa Rican law requires CCSS registration and a mandatory 13th-month aguinaldo bonus for household staff |
| Lean on the extended-family, everyone's-invited culture — Ticos genuinely welcome children in restaurants, offices, and meetings | Expect paternity leave to change the default division of labor — it's eight days total, not eight weeks |
| Confirm your residency status before assuming maternity pay applies — the CCSS split only covers insured workers, not every foreign employee | Bank on the public CEN-CINAI childcare network for full-time coverage — its budget was just cut and waiting lists are already long |
| Ask your employer in writing whether they extend beyond the legal minimum | Forget that pregnant employees and new mothers have strong job-protection guarantees — use them |

#### 🇰🇿 Kazakhstan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for the one-time state childbirth allowance and monthly childcare payment through egov.kz right away | Expect a firm due date or a Western-style birth plan in a public hospital — protocols are rigid and medication is restricted, but scheduling is famously loose |

| Keep a paper trail of any employer commitments beyond the legal minimum | Plan on the unpaid leave until age three as income — it is leave without salary, full stop |

## Costa Rica: Generous on Paper, Thin on the Ground

Costa Rican law is, by regional standards, unusually protective of new parents. Mothers get 112 to 124 days of maternity leave — a month before birth, three months after — paid in full through a 50/50 split between the employer and the Caja Costarricense de Seguro Social (CCSS), with no minimum tenure required to qualify. Fathers got their own entitlement only in 2022: eight days, taken two at a time over four weeks, also paid at 100 percent. Termination during pregnancy or within twelve months of birth is illegal. On the page, this looks like a country that has thought seriously about parenthood.

Off the page, the picture gets more complicated. According to World Bank/ILO estimates, Costa Rica's female labor force participation rate sits around 47.5 percent — meaning roughly half the country's working-age women aren't in the labor force at all, a gap The Tico Times has reported women themselves cite as harder to close than men's unemployment. The state's flagship childcare program, CEN-CINAI, which exists explicitly to free up mothers for work or study, had roughly $88 million stripped from its 2026 budget even as it already carries waiting lists, according to reporting by The Tico Times. Private daycare fills the gap at $300–$800 a month, a real bill in a country where the median household budget doesn't have much slack. Hofstede Insights scores Costa Rica at just 35 on power distance — the lowest in Latin America — and a strikingly collectivist 15 on individualism, reflecting a culture where extended family, not the state or the market, has traditionally absorbed childcare. That culture is a genuine asset for parents with local family nearby. For everyone else, it's a policy gap wearing a folk tradition's clothing.

## Kazakhstan: High Participation, Low Ceiling

Kazakhstan's formal leave regime is less generous than Costa Rica's in one obvious respect — there is no dedicated statutory paternity leave — but its labor economics for mothers tell a different story. Maternity leave runs 126 calendar days (70 before birth, 56 after), paid and counted toward seniority, and either parent may take unpaid childcare leave until the child turns three under Article 100 of the Labor Code. A state childcare payment, averaging about 86,221 tenge (roughly $170) a month through late 2025, is available until the child turns 18 months, according to Kazakhstan's State Social Insurance Fund as reported by The Astana Times. Female labor force participation is around 66 percent — higher than most OECD economies, and far higher than Costa Rica's.

The catch is what happens to that participation once children arrive. Of the more than 600,000 people receiving childcare-related benefits as of December 2025, fewer than 0.2 percent were men, per the State Social Insurance Fund's own figures — a statistic that renders "either parent may apply" almost decorative. The gender wage gap stood at 25.2 percent in 2022, and researchers describe entrenched occupational segregation, with women concentrated in lower-paid public-sector fields like health and education while men dominate higher-paying extraction and industrial work, per an Asian Development Bank gender assessment. Job interviews routinely include questions about marriage and childbearing plans, according to the same research. A 2025 opinion piece in The Astana Times by public-policy graduate student Assel Gutova noted that more than 235,000 women take maternity leave annually, and that nobody tracks how many return to their previous jobs, are demoted, or leave the public sector altogether — "not charity or benefits," she argued, but a question of institutional sustainability that Kazakhstan has yet to answer.

## The Reckoning

Put the two systems side by side and the intuitive read — generous leave equals a family-friendly country — falls apart almost immediately. Costa Rica has the better-looking statute book: paid time for both parents, ironclad job protection, a cultural aversion to letting babies feel like an imposition anywhere. Kazakhstan has the better-looking labor market: two-thirds of working-age women employed, a state payment system that reaches even the informally employed, benefits assigned by law to either parent. And yet Costa Ricans are having historically few children while Kazakhstanis are having a lot, which suggests that neither country's parental-leave regime is actually the variable driving the decision to reproduce.

What both countries share, despite the very different scaffolding, is a quieter problem: motherhood functions as a career tax almost everywhere the paperwork is examined closely. Costa Rica solves the childcare gap with grandmothers and aunts rather than institutions, which works beautifully until you're the one relocating employee without any. Kazakhstan solves the participation question by getting women into the workforce early and keeping the exit ramp — leave, benefits, reduced hours — almost entirely gendered, which keeps the labor force participation numbers impressive while keeping the wage gap intact. Pick your economy: one asks you to already have family on hand, the other asks you to accept that the leave law and the leave culture are two very different documents.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering a question on whether Costa Rica is good for raising children, one respondent focused less on the country's family-oriented reputation than on the practical asterisks: strong on safety and community warmth, weaker on the "reputation versus reality" gap in education quality, and highly dependent on which town you actually land in.*

> *Expat blog (bloggingmoneylife.com) — An American couple who had their first child in a private San José hospital described the maternity-leave benefit as something that simply didn't apply to them, since they weren't yet permanent residents; they paid roughly $3,000 out of pocket for a C-section with a ventilator stay, then hired a bilingual local nanny because remote work didn't pause for the newborn.*

> *Expat blog (Ersatz Expat) — A longtime expat who had a child while based in Astana recalled being told her due date was simply "some point in September, when the baby feels like coming," being denied antibiotics or painkillers she'd taken without issue during an earlier UK pregnancy, and having Kazakhstani friends genuinely alarmed that she'd worn heels while pregnant.*

> *TripAdvisor Almaty forum — A long-time Almaty-based contributor answering a family's relocation question put it bluntly: international schools were solid but expensive, ordinary local schools were not good, public healthcare was cheap but of uneven quality, and the city was better suited to a long stay with children than a permanent one.*

> *The Astana Times (opinion piece) — A Nazarbayev University graduate student writing on the public sector noted that Kazakhstan tracks exactly how many women go on maternity leave each year but keeps no data at all on how many of them return to their old jobs, get quietly sidelined, or leave the workforce altogether.*

## Conclusion

Neither country hands new parents a system built for someone parachuting in without local family, without fluent Russian or Spanish, and without an employer willing to exceed the legal minimum. Costa Rica's paper protections are real and worth using — register with the CCSS, get everything about pay and residency status in writing before the due date — but its childcare infrastructure assumes a grandmother down the road, and increasingly, budget cuts assume you'll manage without state help at all. Kazakhstan's system will pay you something and mostly let you keep your job, but the moment leave starts, the statistics make clear whose career absorbs the interruption, and it is not going to be an even split just because the statute says "either parent." Whichever country you're weighing, the honest move is the same: ask your prospective employer what they do beyond the legal floor, because the floor, in both places, is set considerably lower than the marketing suggests.
