**When "It's Fine" Means Trouble, and "This Is Wrong" Means Tuesday: Nigeria vs. Germany**

**🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇩🇪 Germany**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Lagos, a supervisor who genuinely dislikes your work may still tell you it's "not bad" to your face, and expect you to read the real verdict from everything they don't say. In Berlin, a colleague will tell you the deck is wrong, specify exactly which slide, and consider the matter closed by lunchtime. Neither approach is rudeness or its absence — they are two different bets about what feedback is actually for.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read tone and context carefully — negative feedback is often softened or delivered indirectly to preserve harmony | Take a mild-sounding comment from a superior at face value; it may carry more weight than the words suggest |
| Show visible deference to seniority and age in review conversations | Publicly challenge a superior's assessment in front of others, even if you disagree |
| Seek a private, one-on-one moment if you need clarity on where you stand | Expect a formal written review process everywhere — many organizations still run informally |
| Build a personal relationship with your manager over time — trust unlocks more candid feedback | Assume silence from your boss means things are fine; it may mean the opposite |
| Bring solutions, not just concerns, when raising a problem upward | Frame disagreement as confrontation; frame it as a respectful question instead |

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect direct, specific criticism tied to concrete outcomes, not vague impressions | Read directness as hostility — it's considered a form of respect, not an attack |
| Come to reviews with data and clear metrics on your own performance | Pad feedback conversations with unnecessary praise before the critique — it reads as evasive |
| Separate the critique of the work from your sense of self — Germans do | Take critical feedback personally or as a signal your job is at risk |
| Expect regular, scheduled check-ins rather than only an annual formal review | Assume no news is good news; German managers will tell you if something's wrong |
| Respond to feedback with a clear plan, not an emotional defense | Over-explain or justify at length — concise acknowledgment is preferred |

---

Nigeria's feedback culture is shaped heavily by hierarchy and the priority placed on harmony over confrontation. Research on Nigerian civil service appraisal systems found that supervisors are often reluctant to discuss negative ratings directly with subordinates, out of concern for the confrontation it might provoke (RSIS International Journal). That reluctance isn't incidental — Nigerian workplaces tend to run on a hierarchical structure where age, seniority, and position command deference, and challenging authority directly is broadly discouraged (Rivermate Cultural Considerations). The practical effect is a feedback economy that runs substantially on inference: what's said matters less than how it's said, and employees are expected to develop the social fluency to read what a superior means rather than simply what they state.

Germany runs on the opposite premise, treating clarity itself as the respectful choice. Feedback is task-focused rather than emotion-focused — the conversation is about how the work performed, not how anyone feels about it (Susanna Kis, "The Hidden Logic of German Feedback"). At companies like Volkswagen, performance feedback is often explicitly quantitative, tied to measurable targets rather than subjective impressions (globalpeopletransitions.com). Gallup's own reporting has noted that German firms have historically leaned on rigid annual review structures that, while blunt, are meant to be fair through consistency rather than warmth — critique delivered without excessive cushioning isn't an oversight, it's the design.

### The Reckoning

The trap for a newcomer moving between these systems runs in both directions. A Nigerian professional arriving in a German office may hear direct criticism and reasonably interpret it as a signal their job is in danger, when in fact it's simply Tuesday's status update. A German professional arriving in Nigeria may take a supervisor's mild, softened comment as genuine approval, and miss that it was, in local terms, a serious warning.

Hofstede's power distance scores frame this well: Nigeria sits considerably higher than Germany on power distance, meaning feedback flows are more carefully managed around status and are less likely to run bluntly upward. Germany's lower power distance, paired with a strong preference for explicit, low-context communication, makes bluntness not just acceptable but expected regardless of who's speaking to whom. The two cultures aren't disagreeing about whether feedback matters — they're disagreeing about whether feedback should protect the relationship or protect the truth.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Medium (Nigerian workplace essay) — A Lagos-based professional wrote that the most common failure in Nigerian offices isn't bad management but the absence of any real feedback loop at all — employees frequently can't tell a superior an idea won't work, so problems get discovered only once they've already gone wrong.*

> *Quora — Someone who transitioned from a Nigerian employer to a European one described the first review under a German manager as unexpectedly clarifying: being told exactly which numbers were missed, without any softening, felt harsh at first but ultimately easier to act on than the ambiguous encouragement they'd grown used to.*

> *Reddit — A foreign hire in a German firm recalled being blindsided the first time a colleague flatly said a proposal "doesn't work" in a group setting, and needing a manager to explain afterward that this wasn't personal criticism — it was simply how disagreement is voiced there.*

> *Internations Lagos — An expat manager working with a Nigerian team noted that direct Western-style critique in team meetings visibly unsettled staff, and that shifting negative feedback into private one-on-one conversations dramatically improved how it was received without changing the substance.*

> *Quora — A German professional who worked briefly for a Nigerian company observed that the hardest part wasn't giving feedback but receiving it — waiting for a clear verbal signal that never came, when the actual answer had already been delivered through tone and hesitation days earlier.*

### Conclusion

The useful question to ask before moving isn't "how good is the feedback culture" — both systems function well on their own terms — but "how is disapproval actually communicated here, and will I recognize it when it happens." In Nigeria, calibrate your ear for what's implied and build enough of a relationship with your manager that they'll eventually tell you plainly. In Germany, calibrate your skin for direct critique and stop hunting for a compliment sandwich that isn't coming. If a friend asked me which is harder to adjust to, I'd say Germany hurts for a week and Nigeria confuses you for a year — pick your discomfort accordingly.
