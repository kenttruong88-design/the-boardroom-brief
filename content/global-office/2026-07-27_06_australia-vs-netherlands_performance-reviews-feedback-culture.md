**"This Section Is Too Long" vs. "Great Work, Though Maybe Consider...": Feedback in the Netherlands and Australia**

**🇦🇺 Australia · 🇳🇱 Netherlands**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Dutch manager will tell you your presentation wasn't good enough and hand you a specific list of what to fix, in the same tone they'd use to tell you the printer is out of toner. An Australian manager, delivering the identical critique, may bury it inside three compliments and a joke — not because the feedback is less real, but because in a culture wary of anyone standing out too sharply in either direction, criticism that's too pointed can read as its own kind of overreach. One in three Australian workers report experiencing "tall poppy" dynamics at work, according to research covered by SEEK and HR Director Australia — a distinctly local pressure that has no real Dutch equivalent, where saying the blunt thing is simply what respect looks like.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read feedback about "communication style" or "team fit" carefully — it sometimes carries more than the literal words | Take vague feedback at face value without probing for the specific behaviour behind it |
| Downplay your own achievements slightly in group settings, even strong ones | Loudly self-promote in team meetings; it can trigger exactly the social correction you're trying to avoid |
| Ask directly for specific, concrete feedback if a review feels too soft to act on | Assume a pleasant-sounding review means there's nothing to improve |
| Deliver critical feedback to others wrapped in context and acknowledgment | Give blunt, unsoftened criticism to an Australian colleague expecting it to land as merely efficient |
| Build credibility quietly through consistent output over time | Expect fast public recognition to translate smoothly into more visibility without some pushback |

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect direct, specific feedback about the work — not about you as a person | Interpret blunt critique as a personal attack or a sign the relationship is damaged |
| Give feedback the same way you'd want to receive it: clear, specific, unpadded | Wrap Dutch feedback in so much cushioning that the actual point gets lost |
| Ask clarifying questions if directness leaves you unsure what to actually change | Stay quiet out of politeness when you disagree with feedback — pushing back is expected and welcomed |
| Treat a blunt performance review as a sign you're being taken seriously | Assume a lack of blunt feedback means you're doing brilliantly — silence isn't necessarily praise |
| Adjust your own directness slightly with international colleagues who aren't used to it | Keep maximum bluntness with newcomers who haven't yet learned to read Dutch communication norms |

**The Netherlands'** feedback culture is built on a foundational belief that honesty is the most efficient form of respect. Multiple guides to Dutch workplace communication describe a "speakability" norm — if something needs to be said, it gets said, without the buffering common in Anglo-American feedback models like the "praise sandwich." A Dutch manager's critique — "this section is too long," rather than "great work overall, though perhaps consider trimming" — is intended as clarity, not coldness, and Dutch professionals broadly report finding indirect, hedged feedback more frustrating than directness, because it leaves them unsure what's actually being asked of them. This tracks with Hofstede's low Dutch score on power distance (38): feedback flows relatively freely in both directions, and pushing back on your manager's assessment is a normal part of the exchange, not an act of insubordination.

**Australia's** feedback culture is shaped by an underlying tension between egalitarian instincts and social conformity pressure that researchers and HR analysts describe as "tall poppy syndrome" — the tendency to cut down anyone whose visible success or confidence exceeds the group norm. This doesn't just affect how success is received; it shapes how feedback itself gets delivered and interpreted. SEEK's workplace research found roughly a third of Australian workers have experienced it directly, with more than two-thirds reporting reduced trust among colleagues as a downstream effect. Practically, this means Australian performance reviews often carry a second, unstated function: not just assessing output, but recalibrating someone's visibility relative to the group. A review that focuses heavily on "communication style" or "team fit" for a high performer is sometimes doing exactly this work under professional-sounding language.

### The Reckoning

The irony is that the culture famous for social equality (the Netherlands, per its low power-distance score) actually produces the more legible, easier-to-act-on feedback, while the culture famous for laid-back informality (Australia) produces feedback more likely to require reading between the lines. Dutch directness assumes adults can handle clear information without it damaging the relationship. Australian softness assumes clear information, delivered too plainly, risks damaging either the relationship or the delicate social balance that keeps nobody standing too far above anyone else.

For a newcomer, this inverts the expected difficulty. You'd assume the blunt culture (Netherlands) would be the harder one to adjust to emotionally, and the friendly culture (Australia) would be the easier one to navigate practically. In practice it's often the reverse: Dutch bluntness stings briefly but leaves you knowing exactly what to do next; Australian gentleness feels comfortable in the room but can leave you genuinely unsure what just happened to your standing.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone answering on the good and bad sides of Dutch directness said the real skill for newcomers wasn't learning to tolerate bluntness, it was learning to stop mentally translating it into an insult, since the emotional charge outsiders add to direct feedback simply isn't there on the Dutch end.*

> *Quora — A response on how Dutch directness affects workplace communication noted that colleagues who hedge and soften their feedback are often seen as harder to trust in Dutch teams, since ambiguity is read as either avoidance or a lack of a clear opinion.*

> *Quora — Someone comparing bluntness between Dutch people and Americans argued Dutch directness is more consistent and less situational — it shows up the same way in a performance review as it does commenting on a haircut — while American directness tends to be more selectively applied depending on social context.*

> *Blind — A "performance review" thread drew a wide mix of experiences across companies and countries, with several posters noting that reviews heavy on soft language about "presence" or "style" were often signaling something the manager wasn't willing to state plainly — a pattern more than one poster linked specifically to workplace cultures uncomfortable with direct criticism.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to the Netherlands, brace for direct feedback and take it at face value — it's about the work, not a verdict on you, and asking clarifying questions is welcomed, not weak. If you're moving to Australia, learn to read between the lines of a review that sounds friendlier than it is, and be deliberate about how visibly you claim credit for your own wins. My honest line for a friend over drinks: in the Netherlands, the feedback will sting for five minutes and then make total sense. In Australia, it might feel fine for five minutes and then take you a week to figure out what it actually meant.
