## In Botswana, the Org Chart Is Sacred. In America, It's a Suggestion.

**🇧🇼 Botswana · 🇺🇸 United States**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Botswana, you will be expected to let the most senior person in the room speak first, even in a meeting about the coffee machine. In the United States, you will be expected to call that same senior person by their first name, disagree with them in a meeting, and still somehow lose the argument to someone named Chad who joined four months ago. Both countries insist they respect their employees. Only one of them means it the way you'd expect.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇼 Botswana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet your manager with a handshake and a few minutes of small talk about family before discussing work | Walk into a meeting and open with the agenda |
| Use a title and surname, or Rra/Mma plus surname, until you're invited to use a first name | Assume a warm manner means first-name terms are already in effect |
| Let the most senior person present speak first | Jump in ahead of someone senior, even to agree with them |
| Expect a decision to take longer than you think it should | Mistake a slow decision for a dead one |
| Dress formally as a default | Treat "casual Friday" as the office-wide norm it isn't |
| Let a pause sit in conversation | Rush to fill silence to look decisive |

#### 🇺🇸 United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Call your boss by their first name unless told otherwise | Default to "Mr./Ms. Surname" and wonder why it reads as frosty |
| Speak up in a meeting even to someone two levels above you | Assume rank earns the last word automatically |
| Test the "my door is always open" line by actually walking through it | Assume the open door changes the decision once you're inside |
| Ask directly for the raise or the title | Wait quietly to be noticed |
| Read the flat org chart as aspiration, not documentation | Assume "flat" means nobody is actually in charge when it matters |
| Expect feedback fast, in writing, and logged in software | Expect it to arrive gently |

Botswana's workplace is, on paper, a textbook hierarchy. A business-etiquette briefing circulated to incoming expatriates puts it plainly: the country "values hierarchy, so acknowledge seniority by allowing the most senior person to initiate discussions," and newcomers are told to wait until invited before using anyone's first name (ProtocolWW). Power distance — the degree to which people expect and accept unequal authority — runs considerably higher than in the United States, where the gap between boss and subordinate is comparatively small, per Hofstede-style comparisons cited in the same briefing.

What makes this interesting is that the hierarchy is largely procedural, not autocratic. Research by Richard Pfau, described as a landmark study on the subject, found that Batswana workers tend to prefer managers who seek group consensus or actively consult subordinates before major decisions, rather than simply issuing them — a finding echoed by a 2014 study from Botho University showing high interdependence among business students (ProtocolWW). The formality is real; so is the consultation happening underneath it. A Gaborone business column, citing MIT Sloan research, notes that toxic workplace culture predicts employee attrition more reliably than pay or industry — a claim that lands differently once you realize the formal hierarchy is partly there to slow things down long enough for everyone to be heard (Business Weekly Botswana).

Gallup's most recent rolling data puts Botswana's employee engagement at 20%, down from 25% three years ago, with 27% actively disengaged. But daily workplace stress sits at 26% — well below the Sub-Saharan Africa average of 46% and the global average of 40% (Gallup, 2025). Whatever else is wrong with the Botswana workplace, it does not appear to be anxiety about where you stand with the boss.

Seniority also tends to track age and tenure more literally than it does in, say, a Silicon Valley org chart, where a 26-year-old can outrank people twice her age on the strength of a good Series B. A newcomer who is technically your junior in years but senior in title will still expect — and generally receive — the deference the title implies, and skipping that step to make a point about meritocracy is the fastest way to be quietly written off before you've said anything useful. The upside, foreign managers report, is that once seniority is acknowledged, the actual working relationship tends to be warmer and more personal than its formal opening suggests.

The American workplace runs on the opposite premise: hierarchy is embarrassing, so everyone pretends it is optional. Employees are told to use first names, speak freely in meetings, and treat the org chart as a light suggestion rather than a legal document. Gallup's 2025 figures show 32% of U.S. employees engaged — comfortably ahead of Botswana's 20% — but daily stress sits at 50%, ten points above the global average and the highest of any region Gallup tracks (Gallup, 2025). An analysis of U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data found that the most closely supervised occupations in the country are not executives debating strategy but nursing assistants, sewing-machine operators, and restaurant hosts, whose managers check in more than once a day and track them against numeric targets (Becker's Hospital Review, citing BLS Occupational Requirements Survey data). The flattening of hierarchy, in other words, has a floor, and it is occupied disproportionately by the lowest-paid people in the building — the median salary across those most closely watched roles clusters well under $35,000 a year, while the people telling you the org chart is flat are usually several pay grades above the supervision.

This is also the culture that invented the open-door policy as a stated value rather than an architectural fact, and American workers have noticed the gap between the two. The door, as a rule, is open; whether walking through it changes anything is a separate and more interesting question, and it is one most new hires only get to test after the decision in question has effectively already been made elsewhere.

**The Reckoning:** Put the two cultures side by side and the irony sharpens. Botswana keeps the formal scaffolding of hierarchy — titles, seniority, deference in the room — while quietly running a consultative process behind it, and its workers report comparatively low daily stress. The United States dismantles the scaffolding in language — first names, open doors, speak-up culture — while keeping the actual authority fully intact, and its workers report the highest stress levels Gallup measures anywhere. One country tells you where you stand and then asks your opinion anyway. The other tells you your opinion matters and then promotes Chad.

Neither system is obviously healthier. Botswana's engagement number has fallen five points in three years, suggesting the consensus-building isn't curing disengagement so much as coexisting with it. America's higher engagement number sits on top of stress levels that have held at 50% for three straight years, which is not what "flat and friendly" was supposed to produce. The honest version for a prospective transplant: Botswana will ask you to perform respect you may not yet feel earned, and reward you with a boss who is actually listening. America will hand you a first-name basis and an open door, and quietly remind you, usually at review time, which one of you still has the final say.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com, Botswana forum — one longtime poster described the local job market bluntly: "It's who you know first, then what you know." Their highly qualified spouse spent over two years job-hunting despite being more skilled than many local peers, eventually taking lower-paid freelance work with a former employer rather than landing a permanent role.*

> *ExpatExchange.com, Botswana forum — a member living there for years said what struck them most wasn't the formality but the "strong sense of community and hospitality," extending from neighbors into the workplace, where colleagues ask after your family before anything else gets discussed.*

> *Blind, posted by a U.S. biotech associate director — six months into a new role, they described a senior director boss who fixated on trivial details, dismissed unfamiliar ideas, enforced a "strict hierarchy," and looked down on subordinates, a sharp contrast to the "flat, flexible" culture the company's recruiting materials had promised.*

> *Ask a Manager, U.S. reader letter — a new team leader asked whether to keep using staff members' preferred first names in front of a boss who insisted on gendered titles and surnames for everyone; the advice, and most of the response thread, sided firmly with respecting what colleagues actually asked to be called, regardless of what the boss preferred.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two on the strength of the management culture alone, choose based on which discomfort you'd rather manage. In Botswana, you will spend real energy learning a formal code — who speaks first, what to call whom, how long to let a silence sit — and the payoff is a manager who, once the forms are observed, is more likely to actually ask what you think. In the United States, you will skip the code entirely and go straight to first names and open doors, and spend the following months discovering, meeting by meeting, exactly how much that door was ever really open. Pack accordingly: in Gaborone, bring patience and a good suit; in any American office, bring patience anyway, and don't bother bringing the suit.
