# Iron Discipline Meets a Brick Wall: The Generation Gap in Taiwan and Malaysia

**🇹🇼 Taiwan · 🇲🇾 Malaysia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Malaysia has one of the highest power-distance scores anywhere in Hofstede's original dataset — 104, near the top of the global ranking — and yet its Gen Z workers report feeling more loyal to their direct manager than to the company employing them. Taiwan's older managers, raised on what one of them calls "iron discipline," are describing that same approach hitting "a brick wall" against a younger workforce that would rather leave than tolerate blind obedience. Two very different hierarchy scores, one shared story: the org chart isn't where loyalty lives anymore, at least not for anyone under 30.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇼 Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Offer real growth and training opportunities, not just a pay bump, to retain younger staff | Assume raising salary alone solves a younger employee's disengagement |
| Expect younger employees to push back on rigid "blind obedience" management styles | Manage a Gen Z team the way a manager from the 70s or 80s generation was managed |
| Take financial independence seriously — many young Taiwanese no longer send money home | Assume traditional family-support obligations still shape every employee's compensation expectations |
| Be ready to discuss flexible hours, remote options, or shorter workweeks openly | Treat a request for schedule flexibility as a lack of commitment |
| Normalize conversations about mental health at work if you want to retain younger talent | Dismiss stated mental-health needs as a generational fad |

#### 🇲🇾 Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest specifically in the direct manager-employee relationship — it's often what actually keeps someone | Assume institutional loyalty to the company will retain a Gen Z employee on its own |
| Provide a visible career development path early | Wait until a review cycle to discuss growth — many will have already decided to leave |
| Offer wellness and mental health benefits explicitly — more than half of Gen Z respondents want them | Assume older-generation benefit packages (pure healthcare, no flexibility) will land the same way |
| Recognize that switching jobs for work-life balance is now a normal Gen Z move, not a red flag | Read early job-hopping as a character flaw rather than a generational norm |
| Lean into cross-generational collaboration deliberately — most workers report it genuinely helps | Silo generations into separate teams assuming they won't work well together |

---

Taiwan's generational rift shows up most clearly in how differently two working generations think about discipline itself. One Taiwanese manager overseeing operations in China, Deng Daohong, described it starkly: the "iron discipline" that once ran unquestioned in Taiwanese companies "runs into a brick wall" when applied to post-1990s employees, while supervisors from the 1970s and 80s generation often still default to "blind obedience" as their core management principle. Other employers are adapting rather than fighting it — Fortune Electric chairman Sun Shiming shifted his retention strategy away from straightforward pay increases toward exchange programs and training that give younger engineers "the feeling of achieving personal growth." The shift extends into family economics too: two Taiwanese employees in their twenties acknowledged sending no money home to their families at all, a real departure from a generation that typically contributed to household income as a matter of course. Layered on top of this is a broader set of Gen Z demands common across the region — flexible hours, remote options, openness about mental health, and a rejection of the old corporate-ladder mentality — landing in a country that scores a moderate 58 on Hofstede's power distance index.

Malaysia's version of the same generational shift plays out against a much steeper hierarchy — a Hofstede power-distance score of 104, among the highest recorded anywhere. You might expect that kind of score to translate into unusually strong deference toward institutions and management broadly. Instead, recent workforce data shows the loyalty has narrowed and personalized: 80% of Gen Z respondents report strong relationships with their direct manager, and 72% say they feel more connected to that individual manager than to the company itself. That attachment cuts both ways — 63% say they'd quit if their manager wasn't supportive of their development, and 58% would leave without genuine interest from leadership in their career growth. Meanwhile, a full 29% of Malaysian Gen Z workers report having already changed jobs specifically to improve work-life balance, compared with Millennials, who tend to negotiate for the same outcome within their current role, and Gen X, who more often just cut back on overtime rather than leave at all. The pressure is real on both ends: separate survey data shows 41% of the Malaysian workforce reporting burnout, with Gen Z citing stress reduction as their top lever for greater happiness at work.

Both countries, in other words, are watching the same institutional loyalty drain out of the org chart and pool somewhere more personal — a specific mentor, a specific growth opportunity, a specific manager willing to treat a 24-year-old as more than a junior body to be disciplined into shape.

**The Reckoning:** The genuinely interesting twist is that Malaysia's extreme hierarchy score didn't produce extreme institutional deference — it produced hyper-personalized loyalty to whoever sits directly above a young employee, since that's the only part of the hierarchy that actually shows up for them day to day. Taiwan's more moderate hierarchy score produced a more values-based generational clash: less about which specific person you report to, more about whether the entire management philosophy (blind obedience versus personal growth) still makes sense at all. If you're managing across either generational divide, the fix looks similar either way — invest in the relationship and the growth path — but the reason it's failing without that investment is genuinely different in each place.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A recurring question about "the overall work culture and hours" in Taiwan draws answers consistently split by generation of respondent: older or more traditional-industry commenters describe long hours as simply normal, while younger and tech-sector respondents increasingly frame the same hours as a dealbreaker they're actively avoiding in their next move.*

> *CommonWealth Magazine (Taiwan, English-language local outlet) — A Taiwanese manager overseeing Chinese operations described the old "iron discipline" style hitting "a brick wall" with post-90s staff, while a university student quoted in the same piece pushed back on the stereotype that her generation is simply lazy, calling it "a double burden being a member of the Post-90 Generation."*

> *GOFLEX (Malaysian workforce research digest) — Its data shows the sharpest generational split isn't about pay at all: 63% of Gen Z respondents said they'd quit a job specifically over an unsupportive manager, a number high enough that the guide's core advice to employers is to "understand the generation, but lead the individual."*

> *The Edge Malaysia (Gen Z workplace pressure survey) — Findings showed 41% of the broader Malaysian workforce reporting burnout, with Gen Z specifically citing reduced stress as the single biggest lever for improving their happiness at work — well ahead of the same concern among Millennial or Gen X respondents.*

### Conclusion

If you're managing or joining a team in either country, stop reading the national hierarchy score as a proxy for how loyal your younger colleagues will be to the institution — in both places, that loyalty has already relocated to something smaller and more specific: a manager, a growth plan, a sense that the hours asked of them add up to something worth having. Show up as that specific person or offer that specific plan, in Taipei or Kuala Lumpur alike, and the generational gap gets a lot more manageable than the headline statistics suggest.
