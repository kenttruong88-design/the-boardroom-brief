**Yes, Uncle; No, Boomer: A Field Guide to Generational Combat, Pakistani and American Style**

**🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇺🇸 United States**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Pakistan's median age is 19, per the 2023 census, which means the average Pakistani workplace is staffed by people who were in diapers when the iPod launched. America's median labor-force age is 41.6, per the Bureau of Labor Statistics, which means the average American workplace is staffed by people who remember when the iPod launched and are still annoyed it replaced the CD. Both countries are currently having the identical argument — does the person who has been here longer automatically know better — just across a twenty-two-year age gap instead of a generation gap, which in Pakistan's case is basically the same thing twice.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use "Sir," "Madam," or an honorific like "Bhai" with senior colleagues until they explicitly invite otherwise | Assume multinational-office first-name culture applies on day one — it's still being negotiated sector by sector |
| Expect a 24-year-old hire to ask about flexible hours and purpose before salary | Dismiss that as entitlement — Dawn's reporting on Gen Z in corporate Pakistan frames it as a values shift, not laziness |
| Accept that a junior employee's parents may have opinions on their career | Act surprised when a 28-year-old wants to "check with home" before accepting an offer |
| Build rapport before delivering criticism | Send blunt written feedback and expect it read as purely professional — indirectness up the hierarchy is the norm |
| Expect your best young performers to be eyeing their own startup | Assume loyalty scales with how good someone is at the job — the Tribune has documented the opposite |

#### 🇺🇸 United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Call your manager, and probably your manager's manager, by first name | Default to "sir" or "ma'am" — it reads as distance, not respect |
| Assume a meaningful share of your colleagues are over 55 — it's roughly a quarter of the workforce now | Treat older coworkers as placeholders until retirement; BLS data shows they're the fastest-growing cohort, not the shrinking one |
| State after-hours email expectations explicitly, in writing | Assume silence on a weekend message means consent — ask a workplace advice column how that usually ends |
| Offer structured mentorship on purpose | Assume younger staff will absorb guidance by osmosis — Deloitte finds half of Gen Z want it and barely a third get it |
| Let people leave at five | Schedule the "quick" Saturday all-hands and expect enthusiasm |

Pakistan's workplace generational story is really a demographic story wearing a culture costume. With roughly two-thirds of the population under 30, per national census data, there is no meaningful "old guard versus young guard" numerically — there's a thin layer of people over 45 managing a very large, very online cohort that learned how to work from YouTube and group chats rather than from the person down the hall. The Express Tribune has reported on the resulting friction directly: managers trained in the 1970s and 80s, when a university degree and formal process knowledge were rare and valuable, now oversee millennials and Gen Z staff who gained skills informally and expect their ideas to be taken seriously regardless of tenure — and who leave, often within a year or two, when they aren't, forcing the same employers to rehire and retrain for the same junior roles on a loop. Dawn's coverage of Gen Z entering corporate Pakistan adds the second half of the picture: today's young hires aren't angling to inherit the hierarchy so much as to exit it, trading the multi-decade climb for shorter hours, remote flexibility, and eventually a business of their own, with internship cohorts reportedly finishing assigned tasks faster than expected and then asking, not always patiently, for more interesting ones.

America's version runs in the opposite direction. The median age of its labor force sits at 41.6, per the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and workers 55 and older have gone from roughly a tenth of the workforce in 1994 to about a quarter of it today, according to Census Bureau figures reported by Dawn. That means the typical American office isn't managing a youth wave — it's managing a long, uneven handoff, concentrated unevenly by industry: utilities and manufacturing are now heavy with veteran staff, while retail and food service stay stubbornly young, and firms skewing older tend to open fewer new locations and create fewer net-new jobs than younger-skewing ones. Layered on top is Deloitte's 2025 Gen Z and Millennial Survey, which finds barely 6% of either generation names a senior leadership role as a primary career goal; what they want instead is mentorship, learning, and work-life balance, and the same survey finds a persistent gap between how much guidance they want from managers — about half, across both generations — and how much they report actually receiving, which hovers closer to a third.

### The Reckoning

Put side by side, the two countries have produced near-identical generational complaints from opposite demographic starting points. Pakistan's elders worry that the young don't want to wait their turn; America's elders are the ones still occupying the turn, in greater numbers and for longer than at any point in decades, while their juniors wonder whether there's a turn left to wait for. Hofstede Insights' long-standing cultural scores help explain why the same complaint lands so differently: Pakistan scores notably collectivist and higher on power distance, so generational friction gets absorbed into existing hierarchy and family structures, while the US scores among the most individualist nations measured, so the same friction surfaces as a direct, often litigated negotiation over flexibility and boundaries.

The counterintuitive part is which country is actually better equipped to adapt. Pakistan's youth-heavy pyramid means employers who resist change are simply out of bodies to run the business with — the Tribune's reporting on hiring attrition makes that arithmetic explicit. America's slower-turning, older-skewing workforce means the generational handoff will take longer, cost more in mentorship infrastructure, and matter more to firms in aging sectors like manufacturing and utilities than to a Karachi startup that was never structured around tenure to begin with. Put bluntly: Pakistan's hierarchy is being outrun by its own demographics, while America's is being outlasted by its own actuarial tables, and neither org chart has caught up to the one it's actually managing.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — a younger manager overseeing staff twenty to thirty years their senior described treating age as irrelevant to output, judging direct reports purely on work quality, while also crediting older colleagues with informally coaching them in return.*

> *Moniepoint company blog — a Pakistani software engineer recalled the shock of being told to call his multinational employer's executives by their first names, comparing it to addressing a school principal as "dude," and said it took roughly nine years of working with North American clients before it stopped feeling wrong.*

> *Expat Arrivals — its guide for foreign workers in Pakistan warns that the culture remains formal and seniority-driven, and that relationship-building with colleagues has to happen before business discussions, not alongside them.*

> *Ask a Manager — a reader whose new boss emailed on weekends and seemed to expect prompt replies was advised to clarify expectations explicitly rather than guess, since commenters noted that unstated norms around after-hours availability are exactly where generational assumptions quietly collide.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving from Pakistan to the US, expect to manage up less and negotiate boundaries more — nobody will tell you the rules, because everyone assumes you already know them. If you're moving the other way, expect the opposite: the rules exist, they're just unwritten, inherited, and occasionally held by someone twenty years younger than the org chart suggests. Either way, the generation gap you're bracing for is really a paperwork problem — nobody has updated the manual, and nobody under thirty is going to wait for them to.
