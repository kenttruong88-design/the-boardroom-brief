# Malaysia Has a Law for Working From Home, Australia Has a Tribunal, and Both Have a Manager Who Would Rather See You

🇲🇾 Malaysia · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In August 2025, 36.2% of employed Australians usually worked from home, according to the Australian Bureau of Statistics, and the Fair Work Commission has since ordered a bank to let a mother work from home five days a week. Malaysia, meanwhile, spent 2026 turning the civil service into a three-days-in, two-days-out operation and monitoring it with a system called SPOT-Me. Both countries have discovered flexibility. They disagree mainly on who gets to grant it, and how much paperwork the grant requires.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Malaysia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask about the flexible work policy at interview; the Malaysian Employers Federation found 58% of surveyed firms have one and 42% do not | Assume that a policy exists, or that it applies to your role |
| Propose a role-specific arrangement, which is the structure MEF itself recommends | Demand a blanket right to work from home |
| Cite the Employment Act amendments if you have caregiving duties for children or aged parents | Expect the formal request right to cover every reason you might want to stay home |
| Expect output to be measured; over 40% of surveyed firms moved toward judging performance by output | Assume that working from home means being unobserved |
| If you work in operations or production, expect to be on site | Expect the policy to look like the one your head office has |
| Mind hierarchy when raising the subject with a boss | Challenge a senior colleague about attendance in front of others |

### In Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Put a written request to your employer once you have 12 months of continuous service | Treat it as a casual hallway question after six months |
| Tie the request to your circumstances, specifically and in detail | Rely on general statements about productivity |
| Expect a written response within 21 days | Accept a refusal without asking for the reasons |
| Get any working-from-home arrangement in writing, as commenters on Reddit advise | Assume company policy will not change |
| Check state rules; Victoria plans a legal right to two days at home from September 2026 | Assume the rules are the same in every state |
| Keep your attendance in line with the team; surveys show most office workers want some days in the office | Disappear for a month and hope the policy is vague |

## Malaysia

Malaysia's flexibility is real, selective and employer-led. The Malaysian Employers Federation survey of 2024, reported by The Star, found that more than 70% of companies had increased flexible work routines since the pandemic and that 58% of respondents had formally adopted flexible working arrangements, against 42% who had not. Non-manufacturing firms (60.7%) were ahead of manufacturers (53.5%). The firms without arrangements were mostly candid about the reason: 86% said their tasks require physical presence. MEF also found that senior management, managers and executives generally maintained or improved productivity at home, while supervisors, operations and production staff struggled more. The federation's president, Syed Hussain Syed Husman, said employers must weigh flexibility against productivity risks and recommended a structured, role-specific approach. A separate piece in The Star quoted him saying that on-site work remains the norm for many employers.

Workers want something different. The Randstad Workmonitor 2024, reported by SAYS, found that two in five Malaysians would resign if required to be in the office more often, and that nearly half of Gen Z (49%) and millennials (47%) opposed inflexible arrangements. The same survey found that 86% of employees would still choose the office for at least three days a week if given the choice, which suggests that the Malaysian worker wants a say rather than the sofa. People Matters Global, cited by The Star, reported that more than half of Malaysian workers said employers expect them in the office more often, 17% above the global average.

The law has begun to catch up, modestly. Amendments to the Employment Act 1955, which MEF says took effect on 1 January 2024, let employees formally request flexible arrangements for caregiving duties for children or aged parents. Budget 2025 added a one-time additional 50% tax deduction on the cost of implementing flexible work, available from 2025 to 2027. And the state set an example: from 1 August 2026, according to Malay Mail, eligible civil servants work three days in the office and two from home, with attendance and output monitored through SPOT-Me, and heads of department able to demand office attendance or revoke the arrangement if conditions are not met. Frontline, security, defence, enforcement, education, healthcare and judicial services are excluded.

## Australia

Australia's version is less a policy than an adversarial process. The ABS reports that 36.2% of employed people usually worked from home in August 2025, down from 37.3% in August 2023, and the gap by occupation is stark: 59% of managers and professionals, against 21% of everyone else. A further 30% of employees had an agreement to work flexible hours, rising to 47% among those who work from home.

The legal route is the right to request. Employees with more than 12 months of continuous service can ask for flexible arrangements, employers must try to reach agreement, and refusal requires reasonable business grounds, with a response due within 21 days, according to SBS. In 2025 that process produced a headline. The Fair Work Commission found that Westpac lacked a reasonable business case to refuse a part-time employee, who lived about 70km from the nearest corporate office, the right to work from home five days a week. HCAMag reports the bank's evidence was described as generalised and insufficient, and that Westpac's chief executive nonetheless reaffirmed a two-day office policy. Victoria will go further, with a legal right for eligible roles to work from home at least two days a week from September 2026, while a Greens bill introduced in November 2025 proposes a national version.

The opposing force is also real. AHRI research cited by HCAMag found almost half of organisations require two or three office days, and only 18% require four or five or offer no hybrid at all. Randstad research reported by The New Daily found that 56% of Australians would consider quitting if required to spend more time in the office. Robert Half data, also reported by HCAMag, suggests that 39% of employees were expected in the office five days a week in 2024, up from 19% in 2023. Australia has a right to ask. It does not have a right to be told yes. The HCAMag analysis adds that surveys suggest around 93% of office workers want to attend the office at least part of the time, with a median preference of two to three days, and that roughly 40% of workers want more work-from-home time than their employer allows. The argument, in other words, is about the last day or two of the week rather than about whether offices should exist.

## The Reckoning

Compared head to head, Malaysia's arrangement is a managed privilege and Australia's is a negotiated entitlement. In Malaysia, the weight sits with employers: they decide whether a policy exists, which roles qualify and when it can be withdrawn, and the state sets the tone by monitoring its own hybrid workers. In Australia, the employee has a statutory way to force a conversation and a tribunal to appeal to, at the cost of a process that can take weeks and requires evidence from both sides. Hofstede Insights, as reproduced by Cyborlink, scores Malaysia at 100 for power distance and 26 for individualism, and Australia at 36 and 90. This is a statistical way of saying that one office takes the manager's word for it, and the other asks for the manager's reasons in writing.

For someone moving for work, the useful distinction is leverage. A Malaysian employee gains flexibility by being valuable, senior or well-matched to a firm that has a policy. An Australian employee can gain it by having 12 months' service and a well-documented personal circumstance. Neither country offers the unconditional home office. Both will ask you to be in the building more often than you would like, and both have survey data showing that you would rather resign than agree to it. The difference is that Australian surveys show 56% would consider quitting, while Malaysian ones show two in five would resign, which suggests that the threat is credible in both places and that employers in both are, for now, ignoring it with equal composure.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (r/Australia), via Pedestrian.tv — in a thread about a job offer paying $20,000 less in exchange for working from home, commenters split. Some pointed to lower commuting and food costs, some said they took the pay cut for reduced burnout and more time with family, and one warned that working from home should be written into the contract because company policy can change.*

> *Hacker News — in a 2019 Ask HN thread about remote teams, commenters described the loss of spontaneous watercooler conversation and a slow slide into isolation. One disagreed, saying the main factor was whether the whole company was committed to remote work, not remote work itself. The thread is global and about neither country.*

> *Lowyat.NET forum — an Australian permanent resident planning a move to Malaysia asked how to work remotely for an overseas employer. A commenter replied that an Australian cannot work in Malaysia without a valid work visa, and another suggested asking digital nomad communities about tax.*

> *Lemmy — an Australian working remotely for a US company from Australia, and considering a trial in Malaysia or Thailand, got a warning from a commenter who had worked for a US firm from Asia: the time zone gap made working hours very difficult.*

> *Expat.com (Malaysia) — a US citizen on a dependent pass asked whether remote work for a US company needed a work permit. The only reply was a guess that it should not be an issue, with no rules cited, which is its own lesson about checking immigration status first.*

## Conclusion

Choose Australia if you want flexibility enforced by someone other than your manager, and are prepared to wait twelve months, write it down and lose some of the time arguing about it. Choose Malaysia if you work in a role and a company where the policy already exists, and are comfortable with a model that depends on the employer's goodwill and monitors your output.

Whichever country you pick, get the arrangement in writing, check what your visa permits before you open the laptop, and read the policy before you read the brochure. The home office is a privilege everywhere. In Australia, at least, you can ask a tribunal why.
