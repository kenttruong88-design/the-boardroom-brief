# Sweden Trusts You to Work From Home. Brazil Wants It Notarized.

**🇧🇷 Brazil · 🇸🇪 Sweden**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Sweden, asking to work from home on Thursday is a Slack message. In Brazil, working from home at all is, legally speaking, a contract amendment — Articles 75-A through 75-E of the Brazilian Labour Code require remote arrangements to be written into your employment contract, with the employer notifying you in writing at least 15 days before assigning it. One country treats flexibility as a norm you opt into quietly. The other treats it as a status you formally acquire.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your remote arrangement written into your contract or a formal addendum | Assume a verbal okay from your manager is enough to legally work remotely |
| Ask who covers equipment and home-office expenses — employers are required to specify this | Assume your employer will absorb costs without it being addressed in writing |
| Expect a strong in-person, relationship-driven office culture even where hybrid exists | Expect a remote-first culture just because remote work is legally regulated |
| Build face time deliberately if you're mostly remote — it still matters for advancement | Assume output alone will be visible to leadership without some in-person presence |
| Clarify your notice period rights if your employer wants to change your work location | Assume you can be shifted to remote or office-based work with no formal notice |

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take flexible hours and remote days as a baseline expectation, not a favor | Ask permission for routine flexibility that colleagues already treat as standard |
| Trust that being offline doesn't damage your standing — Swedish management culture assumes competence | Overcompensate with visible "always online" behavior; it can read as insecure, not diligent |
| Use consensus-based decision-making even in remote settings — input still matters broadly | Push decisions through quickly without input just because a meeting is virtual |
| Respect that flexibility runs both ways — your manager expects the same trust from you | Assume flexibility means no accountability for deliverables |
| Keep work chat within reasonable hours even remotely — boundaries are culturally reinforced | Message colleagues late at night expecting a response; it isn't the norm even informally |

---

Sweden's low Hofstede power-distance score (31) and famously low masculinity score (5, among the lowest in the world) shape a workplace where flexibility is assumed rather than negotiated. Roughly 45 to 46 percent of Swedish employees aged 20 to 64 report working from home to some degree, among the highest rates in Northern Europe according to labor statistics, and the arrangement tends to run on mutual trust rather than close monitoring — a natural extension of a management culture that already favors consensus over top-down control. Swedish flexibility policy isn't really a separate HR initiative; it's downstream of a broader belief that autonomy produces better work than supervision does.

Brazil's picture is shaped by both a higher power-distance score (69) and a formal labor code that predates the remote-work era but has been retrofitted to govern it in detail. Brazil's CLT amendments on teletrabalho require the work arrangement to be explicit in the contract, spell out who provides equipment, and mandate written notice before an employer can unilaterally move someone into or out of remote status. According to workforce data, remote work in Brazil and much of Latin America remains modest in daily practice compared to Northern Europe, even where it's legally well-defined — the paperwork caught up faster than the culture did, and many Brazilian firms still treat physical presence as the default marker of commitment and promotability, remote clauses in the contract notwithstanding.

The reckoning is a paradox: Brazil has the more detailed remote-work law, and Sweden has the more genuinely remote-friendly culture. A Brazilian employee moving to a Swedish employer may be surprised that flexibility requires almost no paperwork at all — it just requires trust, which either exists in the relationship or doesn't. A Swedish employee moving into a Brazilian office, even one with a technically compliant remote policy, may find that working from home is legally protected but culturally penalized — visible in who gets tapped for the next promotion, and it usually isn't the person nobody sees.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone describing Swedish company culture said the most disorienting part wasn't the flexibility itself but how little anyone commented on it — no one asked where you'd been or why you were logging on at an unusual hour, because the assumption was always that the work would get done.*

> *Quora — A respondent explaining remote work from Brazil for a foreign employer noted that while remote arrangements are completely legal, day-to-day life still often required navigating a Brazilian entity's payroll and tax registration — regulatory clarity didn't always translate into administrative simplicity.*

> *teamblind.com — Reviews of a major Swedish tech employer's work-from-home policy described being able to opt into fully remote status with limited office access, though some employees noted that career visibility still quietly favored people who showed up, a tension between formal flexibility and informal advancement.*

> *teamblind.com — A separate review of the same company's Swedish operations praised the work-life balance and described colleagues as kind and low-pressure, while also flagging that promotions and raises had become harder to secure in recent reorganizations — flexibility and career mobility were not the same lever.*

> *Quora — Someone who researched working remotely for a Swedish company while living outside Sweden explained that the employer withholding Swedish income tax was often the deciding factor in whether the arrangement was even legally straightforward, a detail that mattered more in practice than any stated culture of trust.*

### Conclusion

If you're negotiating remote work in Brazil, negotiate the paperwork first — the culture around presence won't bend just because the law does. If you're negotiating it in Sweden, the paperwork barely matters; the real question is whether you can handle being trusted without being watched, because nobody there is going to check. Both countries will let you work from home. Only one of them will make you feel strange for doing it.
