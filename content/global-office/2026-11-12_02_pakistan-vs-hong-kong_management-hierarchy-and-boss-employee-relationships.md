# Pakistan Will Make Your Boss a Patron; Hong Kong Will Merely Make Him Your Boss and Hope You Notice

🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Both Pakistan and Hong Kong treat the org chart as something close to scripture, but they disagree on what it is for. In Pakistan, a senior figure is often also a patron, whose favour governs pay, promotion and sometimes a good deal else. In Hong Kong, a manager is a role, to be deferred to correctly, briefed carefully and never kept waiting. According to a Michael Page study of nearly 3,000 current and former employees of Chinese firms, about 70 percent of Hongkongers who left those employers cited office culture and low pay. Dawn, citing the International Labour Organization, reports that women hold under 8 percent of senior and middle management posts in Pakistan. Hierarchy, in other words, is not a quaint backdrop. It is the job.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address seniors by title and surname until invited otherwise | Use first names with older or higher-ranking colleagues on day one |
| Spend time on conversation and family questions before business | Open a first meeting with the agenda and nothing else |
| Greet the oldest and most senior person in the room first | Let rank order be a guess at the door |
| Raise concerns privately and with a proposed fix attached | Contradict a senior in front of the team |
| Agree in writing what you were asked to deliver and by when | Assume a verbal instruction means the same thing next week |
| Build a relationship with a respected mentor in the company | Mistake flattery of the boss for a career plan |

### In Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Defer to seniors in meetings and keep disagreement for afterwards | Challenge a manager publicly, which costs both of you face |
| Deliver bad news early and in private, with a plan | Hide a problem because the upward channel feels delicate |
| Come to the boss with progress and a willingness to solve problems | Tell a boss a task is impossible or that you are too busy to talk |
| Observe small rituals, such as holding your glass lower than your boss's at a toast | Treat a team dinner as off the record |
| Confirm instructions and the information behind them before acting | Assume silence from a subordinate means agreement or good news |
| Learn who actually decides, which may be at head office | Spend effort persuading a manager who cannot say yes |

## Pakistan

Pakistani workplaces run on rank and relationship in roughly equal measure. Expat Arrivals notes that those who are older, more experienced and higher in position should be greeted and addressed first, that people are addressed by title and surname, and that trust is built through informal conversation before business is discussed. This is warm, and it is also an investment: the relationship is the contract. Deadlines are often flexible, according to the same guide, and meetings can run long or be cancelled at short notice, so the person who defines urgency is, once again, the boss.

The darker side of the arrangement is documented by Pakistani writers themselves. Faisal Bari wrote in Dawn that in a weaker organisation he knew, approval from owners and senior managers was the main measure of success, and being in their good books determined pay and promotion. Personal connections steered hiring, which in his account produced suspicion, low loyalty and infighting. In The Friday Times, Farhan Akhtar described darbari culture, the habit of flattering superiors with what they wish to hear, and argued that capable staff who see a flaw in a decision often keep quiet for fear of retaliation. In Dawn, Rakhshanda Abbas complained of employers whose moods decide outcomes, so that a serious error is overlooked on a good day and a small one inflated on a bad day. These are opinion pieces and not surveys, but they agree with one another, which is its own kind of evidence.

Authority also has a gendered shape. Dawn, citing the ILO, puts women at under 8 percent of senior and middle management, and describes women being asked to take minutes, circulate notes and carry follow-ups that men are rarely handed. Presence is measured as well as output: a Tribune writer on Karachi argues that managers judge commitment by hours at a desk, with late-arrival deductions applied to anyone caught in traffic. None of this makes Pakistani offices uniform. Dawn contributors describe sharply different cultures between firms, with one writer contrasting a British multinational where promotion followed connections and an American plant where staff worked extra shifts because pay and grievance handling were good. A ProPakistani poll cited an undated 58 percent of respondents perceiving gender discrimination at work, though its sample is not described. The advice is to find out which kind you have been offered before you sign.

## Hong Kong

Hong Kong hierarchy is quieter and more procedural. World Business Culture describes managers as often paternalistic, giving instructions that are expected to be carried out without much discussion, with respect owed to the job title rather than to the individual. Upward communication suffers for it: employees may find bad news hard to deliver, so managers can receive incomplete information. Expat Arrivals adds that saving face matters, that bad news should be delivered when others are not present, and that senior staff are often expected to work well beyond standard hours, which tends to set the tone for everyone beneath them.

The Michael Page study, reported by the South China Morning Post, shows what this looks like when the scale is large. Former employees of Chinese firms in Hong Kong described rigid workplaces where the atmosphere tightens when senior management is in the room, with key decisions made at headquarters and little local say. Power distance between top executives and other staff was described as larger than at British firms. Forty-four percent of those Chinese firms reportedly struggle to retain talent, and 67 percent of departing employees said pay was lower than they had expected. Deference, in short, is not free.

The system is also unforgiving about small things. Human Resources Online, drawing on posts in the Hong Kong Discussion Group, collected phrases that bosses reportedly use about overtime, including a blunt reminder that the employee is paid a salary, and noted that "Anyway" signals that your input is over and the boss's way is final. Another post there weighed a toxic boss against bad colleagues and concluded, among other things, that you can ignore a colleague but not the person who controls your pay and promotion. None of this is dramatic. It is the accumulated weight of ranks observed correctly, forever. Expat Arrivals notes that juniors are generally expected to defer to seniors in meetings, and Human Resources Online has recorded complaints that bosses treat staff as personal assistants, from collecting forgotten wallets to booking restaurants for private occasions. Some staff reportedly treat such favours as a route to advancement, which the writer called a shoe-shining culture.

## The Reckoning

The two cultures are hierarchical in different ways, and the difference is best summed up as personal versus procedural. Hofstede Insights scores Pakistan at 55 for power distance and 14 for individualism, against 68 and 50 for Hong Kong, figures I reached only through secondary compilations of Hofstede data. On distance alone, Hong Kong is the more hierarchical country, which fits its title-bound, headquarters-driven structures. The low Pakistani individualism score fits what Pakistani writers describe: loyalty to a person, a family or a faction, and an office where the group around the boss matters more than the rule book.

For the person moving, this changes what you are managing. In Pakistan you manage a relationship: you learn the boss's expectations, the mentor network and the unspoken order of favour, and you accept that personal goodwill can move a decision the process would not. In Hong Kong you manage a role: you learn the correct channel, the correct moment and the correct level of deference, and you accept that a personally friendly manager may still be unable to override a decision made elsewhere. Pakistan can be arbitrary where Hong Kong is rigid. Which of these you tolerate better is a matter of temperament.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *All Things Pakistan (Pakistaniat), 2008 comment thread — the author described bank employees feeling unable to leave before the boss, who stays late to look efficient. A commenter returning to Karachi after years away found professionals working until nine in the evening and rarely taking leave because of workload and peer pressure. This voice addresses long hours, an adjacent theme, and is dated.*

> *ProPakistani reader comments, 2015 — one commenter argued that high unemployment leaves workers feeling lucky to have a job, so they rarely challenge poor conditions, while another said employers expect staff to be reachable at all hours. The article itself described an authoritarian management style in which employees fear for their jobs if they voice opinions.*

> *Hong Kong Discussion Group, via Human Resources Online, 2017 — posters swapped lines bosses use about overtime, and others said they would rather endure a toxic boss than bad teammates, though some noted the boss, unlike a colleague, controls your pay and promotion.*

> *Dcard, via The Star, 2023 — a young Hong Kong office worker believed he angered his manager by raising his glass higher than the boss's at a team toast. Most commenters called the manager unfair, a few suspected his performance was the real issue, and several shared their own etiquette mishaps.*

## Conclusion

If you take a job in Pakistan, invest early in people: learn names, titles and who is trusted, and find a senior ally while you are still new. Keep your concerns private and constructive, and be wary of the comfortable habit of telling the boss only what he wants to hear. If you take a job in Hong Kong, invest in procedure: learn the channel, the timing and the expectations of seniority, deliver bad news early and in private, and find out who really decides.

In both places your boss will matter more than the policy handbook, and neither handbook says so. The honest summary is that one country will test your diplomacy and the other your patience, and you will be told which in your first quarterly review, probably in the third person.
