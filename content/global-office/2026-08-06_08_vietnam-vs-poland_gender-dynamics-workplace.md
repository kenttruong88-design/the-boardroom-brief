# Vietnam Hires Women. Poland Pays Them Fairly. Neither Promotes Them.

🇻🇳 Vietnam · 🇵🇱 Poland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Vietnam sends more of its working-age women to work than almost any country the OECD tracks — a labor force participation rate north of 70 percent that would make Scandinavia blush. Poland, meanwhile, has quietly closed its gender pay gap to one of the smallest in the European Union, a few points above zero and an ocean below the EU average. Put those two facts together and you might conclude one of these countries has solved gender equity at work. You would be wrong twice. Vietnam counts women in the workforce and then loses them somewhere between the org chart and the boardroom; Poland pays women fairly for jobs it is still, per its own surveys, quietly reluctant to hand them. Read on before you pack.

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect direct questions about marital status and children in casual small talk — it is rapport-building, not HR overreach | Don't mistake a woman running the family shop or market stall for evidence of boardroom parity — the informal economy and the corporate ladder are different countries |
| Dress modestly and formally, especially in state-linked or traditional offices | Don't schedule mandatory late dinners assuming everyone's evening is equally free — many female colleagues still run the household after six |
| Notice that seniority and age often outrank gender in meeting dynamics | Don't read a management job posting at face value — some still specify a sex preference informally, particularly in technical and engineering roles |
| Ask about the informal economy — women dominate its financial management | Don't assume high female labor participation means equal pay; the gap runs wider than the headline number suggests once rural and urban figures are split out |
| Respect the emphasis on collective harmony over individual assertiveness in meetings | Don't be surprised if the leadership team photo skews heavily male despite a mostly-female floor |
| Build relationships gradually — trust outranks a CV in the first few months | Don't lead with imported "lean in" language — it lands as tone-deaf rather than empowering |

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect blunt, short feedback — it's a national communication style, not hostility aimed at you specifically | Don't assume equal representation at management level means equal treatment day to day — a large share of women report being evaluated on appearance |
| Take parental leave norms seriously — they are generous and widely used by women | Don't expect an automatic fast return to your pre-leave track without asking for it directly — informal penalties for career breaks are well documented |
| Look for strong female representation in law, medicine and education | Don't expect the same in IT or construction — those fields remain heavily male, with roughly one woman for every five men in technical study tracks |
| Use formal titles and surnames until invited to do otherwise | Don't make gender-based jokes at the office — surveyed employees flag this as a persistent, specific complaint |
| Ask about pay bands openly — new EU-driven transparency rules are reshaping norms | Don't assume a low national pay-gap figure means no wage conversation is needed at your specific employer |
| Note that close to half of management roles nationally are held by women | Don't take that statistic as proof of comfort — fewer than one in five employees surveyed say men and women are actually treated the same |

### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

Vietnam's female labor force participation rate sits above 70 percent — by some counts closer to 79 percent of women aged 15 to 64 either working or looking for work, a figure that beats every OECD member except a small cluster of Nordic outliers. On paper, this looks like the gender-equity success story of Southeast Asia. In practice, the participation is not matched by the paycheck: the ILO and Vietnam's own General Statistics Office both track a persistent gender wage gap, with GSO estimating the earnings gap as high as 29.5 percent overall — 21.5 percent in urban areas, 35.2 percent in rural ones — even though women work comparable hours and have comparable education levels to men.

The mechanism is occupational, not academic. Women are concentrated in lower-paid segments of the labor market — textiles, garments, retail, agriculture — while men are steered toward technical and supervisory roles. The ILO has documented that as many as 83 percent of management job postings in Vietnam specify a male candidate, a statistic that does not require much decoding. Layer on unpaid domestic work, which women perform at roughly twice the rate of men, and the arithmetic of the "double burden" becomes clear: formal employment plus informal second shift, for less pay than the man doing the first job alone.

The irony, per Hofstede Insights, is that Vietnam scores 40 on the masculinity dimension — a comparatively feminine, consensus-oriented culture that in theory values negotiation and quality of life over competition and hierarchy. The World Bank's Women, Business and the Law index gives Vietnam a respectable 78.8 out of 100, with near-perfect marks on legal freedom of movement and the right to work. The laws are, largely, fine. It's the org chart that hasn't caught up.

### 🇵🇱 Poland

Poland's gender pay gap, by contrast, is the kind of number an EU commissioner puts on a slide with pride: roughly 4.5 to 8 percent depending on the year and methodology, well under half the EU-27 average of around 11 to 12 percent, according to Eurostat. Poland also places among the top handful of EU member states for women in management, with women holding somewhere between 43 and 49 percent of managerial positions — ahead of the EU average of 35 percent, trailing only Latvia and Sweden by some counts.

And yet, ask Polish women how it feels rather than what it measures, and the picture sours. Fewer than one in five respondents in recent workplace surveys say men and women are actually treated the same. The specific grievances are concrete: evaluation by appearance, being handed simpler or less creative assignments, a documented slowdown in career progression after motherhood, and a steady undercurrent of jokes that HR departments have not managed to legislate away. The gap between Poland's statistics and its lived experience is, in its own way, more interesting than Vietnam's — the numbers say "solved," the surveys say "not quite."

Structurally, Poland scores 64 on Hofstede's masculinity dimension — a competitive, assertive, achievement-oriented culture, a near-mirror image of Vietnam's 40. Fields like IT and construction remain overwhelmingly male, with roughly 19 women per 100 men in relevant study tracks, even as women dominate education and healthcare. Poland began transposing the EU's Pay Transparency Directive into its Labor Code in mid-2025, with a fuller draft act following in December — regulatory infrastructure catching up to a reputation the country has arguably outrun.

### The Reckoning

Put side by side, Vietnam and Poland are running two different experiments that arrive at the same disappointing footnote. Vietnam optimized for getting women into the workforce and got there decades ago, but never built the promotion pipeline to match — women are everywhere on the floor and nowhere in the room where headcount decisions get made. Poland optimized for closing the pay gap and largely succeeded, and stacked its management layer with women in the process, but has not managed to convince the women in question that any of it feels equitable day to day.

The counterintuitive lesson for anyone relocating: neither country's headline statistic tells you what your actual Tuesday will look like. Vietnam's high participation rate says nothing about whether you'll be one of the 17 percent of management candidates a job ad was actually written for. Poland's small pay gap says nothing about whether a colleague will comment on your outfit before your quarterly numbers. Read the small print, not the ranking.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit, r/expats — a newly arrived employee in Warsaw described spending her first month convinced her manager's one-line, unembellished feedback emails meant she was being singled out for criticism, until a Polish colleague pointed out that everyone, regardless of gender, gets the same terse treatment; it was a communication style, not a verdict on her performance.*

> *Reddit, r/AskAsia — a foreign worker in Ho Chi Minh City's retail sector was surprised at how quickly young women were promoted to store and team-lead roles, crediting real energy and independence on the floor, but noted the regional head office team in her company photo was, without exception, male.*

> *Quora — a respondent answering a thread on gender attitudes in Vietnam described being asked directly in job interviews about marriage plans and future children, framing it as standard practice rather than an exception, and linked it to a broader pattern where women's career trajectories are informally pre-negotiated around domestic expectations before a contract is even signed.*

> *Blind — an engineer at a Polish tech office described being the only woman in most technical planning meetings despite her company's management statistics looking respectable on paper, noting that formal parity in headcount numbers did not translate into being looped into informal technical discussions that happened before the meeting officially started.*

> *InterNations forum — an expat in Vietnam pointed out the gap between the country's high overall ranking in global expat surveys and its far weaker ranking specifically for career prospects and local work culture, advising newcomers to negotiate role scope and title explicitly before accepting an offer, since the informal ceiling turned out to be real even for foreign hires who assumed expat status would exempt them from it.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two purely on gender dynamics, the honest answer is that you are choosing between two different flavors of the same ceiling. Vietnam will get you hired fast and will not pretend the leadership team looks like the rest of the company. Poland will pay you close to what your male colleague earns and will still let you find out, eventually, that the joke in the meeting was about you. Pick Vietnam if you want a job market that is honest about its hierarchy from day one; pick Poland if you want your payslip to reflect fairness even when the room doesn't quite.

What I'd actually tell a friend over a drink: ask about the last three promotions on the team you're joining, not the national statistics — in both countries, the brochure has better numbers than your actual office will.
