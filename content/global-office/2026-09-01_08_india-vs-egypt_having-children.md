# So You Want to Have a Baby in India or Egypt? A Career Survival Guide

🇮🇳 India · 🇪🇬 Egypt

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries, two labor markets, and one shared national hobby: writing generous leave policies on paper and then quietly expecting women to disappear from the org chart anyway. India offers new mothers six months of paid leave — more than most of Scandinavia. Egypt offers four, plus a legal right to on-site childcare that almost nobody has ever seen in person. Both countries have female labor force participation rates that would make a McKinsey partner reach for the smelling salts. Here is what actually happens once the baby arrives.

## Do's & Don'ts for the First Weeks

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm your 26-week maternity leave in writing before you sign — it's law (Code on Social Security, 2020), but HR onboarding decks conveniently forget it | Don't assume paternity leave exists at your company; only about 14% of employers offer it, and it's rarely more than two weeks |
| Budget for full-time household help — a nanny or daycare typically runs ₹6,700–15,000/month, a fraction of Western equivalents | Don't expect your in-laws' offer of childcare help to come without commentary on your parenting choices |
| Negotiate a phased return (part-time or work-from-home ramp) before you leave — it's far easier to arrange pre-leave than post | Don't assume "flexible policy" on the careers page survives contact with an actual pregnancy; ask departing colleagues what really happened |
| Line up backup childcare early; joint-family support is common but increasingly thin in cities as nuclear households become the norm | Don't wait until week 30 to loop in your manager on your return-to-work plan — re-entry conversations that start late tend to end in a demotion by omission |
| Ask specifically about the return-to-role guarantee, not just the leave itself | Don't take silence on paternity leave as neutral — it usually means the default expectation is that childcare is your problem alone |
| Research your building's proximity to reliable daycare before signing a lease | Don't assume a "generous" 26-week policy means anyone actually takes it without a pay or promotion penalty later |

#### 🇪🇬 Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your employer's headcount — companies with 100+ women must legally provide a nursery or partner with one under Labor Law 14/2025 | Don't assume that legal nursery obligation is enforced in practice; many companies quietly don't comply |
| Budget for a full-time nanny or housekeeper — around $1,000/month is standard among expat and professional households, and most families with children have one | Don't expect public nurseries to be a serious option; quality and availability are patchy outside major compounds |
| Ask about your reduced-hours entitlement in the sixth month of pregnancy and the two years of daily breastfeeding breaks after birth — both are statutory | Don't budget for daycare alone; extended family (mothers, mothers-in-law) still provides most childcare, so factor in whether you have that network locally |
| Get the maternity leave terms in writing — four months, at least 45 days of it after delivery, usable up to three times in a career | Don't assume returning to your "same role" is automatic in smaller, less formal workplaces despite the legal guarantee |
| If relocating with kids, budget seriously for international school fees — they start around $3,000/year and climb past $15,000 | Don't assume the low cost of living offsets childcare costs; nursery and school fees are where expat budgets actually break |
| Build a relationship with other working parents early — informal childcare-sharing arrangements are common and often essential | Don't rely on your own instincts about "normal" work hours around a new baby; ask what colleagues actually did, because policy and practice diverge |

## India: The Policy Is Generous. The Org Chart Disagrees.

On paper, India's maternity leave is the envy of the industrialized world: 26 weeks of paid leave for a first or second child under the Code on Social Security, 2020, with eligibility kicking in after just 80 days of work in the preceding year — a remarkably low bar (India Briefing, greytHR). Paternity leave is a different story: no statutory entitlement exists for private-sector fathers, leaving roughly 14% of companies to offer anything at all, usually five to fifteen days, occasionally more at outliers like Zomato's 26-week policy (Omnivoo, employerrecords.com). A March 2026 court ruling has flagged the absence of paternity leave as a gap in India's social security framework, which is judicial-speak for "someone should probably fix this."

The generosity, though, does not survive the return to work. Roughly 73% of Indian women quit their jobs after childbirth, and nearly half of those who do return leave again within four months (The Swaddle). Of women who left the formal workforce for family reasons, 70% report struggling to re-enter, and 69% expect a pay cut when they do (The Swaddle). Female labor force participation, by the more comprehensive PLFS measure, has climbed to roughly 41.7% as of 2023–24 — real progress — but international comparisons using narrower urban/formal-sector metrics still rank India among the lowest in the G20, ahead of only Saudi Arabia (Foreign Policy, Policy Circle). Nearly 20 million women exited the workforce between 2004 and 2012 alone. The six-month leave, in other words, functions less as a bridge back to work and more as a dignified off-ramp.

## Egypt: Shorter Leave, Harder Ceiling

Egypt's new Labor Law No. 14 of 2025 grants four months of paid maternity leave, usable up to three times across a career, with legal protections against dismissal during leave or shortly after return (Lexology, Africa-HR). It also requires employers with 100 or more female staff at one site to provide a nursery or share one with neighboring businesses — a rare instance of a childcare mandate written directly into labor law rather than left to corporate goodwill (Lexis Middle East). Pregnant employees get reduced hours from the sixth month, and new mothers get two years of paid breastfeeding breaks.

The trouble is what happens around the law, not within it. Egypt's female labor force participation rate has fallen from 23% in 2012 to roughly 18% today — already low, and moving the wrong direction (World Bank, 2025). Childcare, or the absence of affordable formal childcare, is a primary reason: among working mothers, family members handle the bulk of care (27% grandmothers, 18% mothers-in-law, 13% other relatives), while only 19% use a nursery or nanny at all (J-PAL/IZA study). Mothers who do work still average a reported 11 hours a day on childcare on top of household chores — a figure that suggests "working mother" and "full-time caregiver" are, in practice, the same unpaid job with an office attached.

## The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the paradox sharpens: India hands out more paid leave than almost anywhere in the world and still can't keep women in the workforce past their thirties, while Egypt has actually legislated employer-funded childcare and still watches participation shrink. The common thread isn't the law — it's what Hofstede Insights' cultural comparison would predict from two high-power-distance societies (India scores 77, Egypt 70): decisions about who raises the child are made by family hierarchy, not HR policy, and no statute yet written overrides a mother-in-law's opinion on daycare.

The practical difference for a relocating employee is where the safety net actually sits. In India, it's extended family and paid domestic help, arranged privately and unevenly across cities. In Egypt, it's nominally the employer, per Article 60 — except enforcement is inconsistent enough that you should treat the legal nursery guarantee as a nice bonus if it materializes, not a plan.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *TeamBlind — a tech professional who relocated to India described the move as smooth for the household — grandparents nearby, full-time help easily arranged — but noted his wife found the adjustment far harder, particularly in the first six months, largely due to social expectations placed on mothers specifically.*

> *Quora — a respondent answering "how do working couples manage kids in India without parental support" pointed out that the joint-family safety net people assume exists is thinning fast; migration for careers means many young parents are improvising without it, contrary to the outside assumption that grandparents are always on call.*

> *Quora — someone describing expat family life in Alexandria noted that daily costs were manageable, but that international schooling and reliable childcare were the two line items that actually determined whether the move worked financially.*

> *Talesmag (diplomatic community post report) — a Cairo-based expat noted that hiring full-time household help is close to universal among working families with children, at roughly $1,000 a month, and that most newcomers underestimate how quickly this becomes a necessity rather than a luxury.*

> *TeamBlind — one parent visiting India with young children admitted they'd made no formal childcare arrangements at all, assuming family would absorb it, and instead spent the whole trip managing both childcare and aging in-laws simultaneously — advice they wished someone had given them beforehand: arrange care before you land, not after.*

## Conclusion

The honest comparison isn't leave-weeks or nursery mandates — it's who is actually expected to absorb the gap between "policy" and "practice," and in both countries, the answer is still overwhelmingly the mother, just with different institutions declining to help her. India outsources the problem to family networks that are quietly dissolving; Egypt legislates a solution that many employers simply don't build. If you're relocating for work and planning a family, the real due diligence isn't reading the labor code — it's asking three departing employees, honestly, what happened to their careers after they used it.

Bring a nanny budget either way; the law was never the plan.
