# 1.1 Years vs. 2.9 Years: Generational Tenure in Canada and Singapore

**🇨🇦 Canada · 🇸🇬 Singapore**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Canadian Gen Z workers stay in a first job an average of 1.1 years, compared to 2.9 years for Boomers — a gap wide enough that a Boomer manager and a Gen Z hire can be operating on entirely different definitions of what "settling in" even means. In Singapore, 45% of Gen Z professionals openly expect to stay with an employer for no more than two years. Neither country's older managers are fully caught up to what these numbers mean for how a team actually functions.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| If you're early-career, expect frequent job changes to be normalized — nearly a third of Canadian Gen Z workers plan to change jobs in a given year, versus just 5% of Boomers | Assume short tenure reads as instability to every manager; attitudes vary sharply by generation and industry, so read your specific workplace |
| Raise flexibility and mental health support directly in interviews if they matter to you — younger workers are notably more likely to say employers under-support well-being | Assume older colleagues share the same openness about stress or mental health at work; generational comfort with these conversations still varies widely |
| Ask what a company's stance is on purpose-driven work if that matters to you — 61% of Canadians overall, and higher among younger cohorts, prioritize industries that "help people" | Assume every older colleague is indifferent to purpose; the gap is one of degree, not total absence, across generations |
| Use generational differences as a discussion topic, not a grievance — most Canadian workplaces are actively trying to bridge four active generations at once | Write off an older manager's preference for in-office presence as simply outdated; it often reflects a different, equally genuine model of how trust gets built |
| Bring documented career-growth expectations to reviews — younger Canadian workers are reshaping what loyalty even means, and managers are adapting review structures accordingly | Expect a Boomer-era manager to instinctively understand rapid job-hopping as ambition rather than as a red flag; you may need to make that case explicitly |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Be upfront about tenure expectations if you're early-career — a large share of Gen Z professionals in Singapore now openly plan for two-year stays, and this is increasingly normalized rather than penalized | Assume older Singaporean managers universally share that view; the meritocratic, loyalty-adjacent culture still runs deep in more traditional firms |
| Prioritize belonging signals early — 67% of Gen Z workers in Singapore say they'd consider leaving a job over a weak sense of belonging | Underestimate how seriously employers now take retention risk tied to culture fit, not just compensation |
| Push for transparent growth and learning opportunities — a quarter of Gen Z workers don't trust employers to invest in non-technical skill development, and it's a common area of friction | Assume stated L&D programs are automatically credible; ask for specifics, since skepticism here is measurably high and well-founded in many firms |
| Factor cost of living directly into compensation discussions — it's the top concern for Gen Z workers in Singapore at 49%, ahead of most other issues | Assume salary alone addresses retention concerns; purpose and stability now compete directly with pay in what younger workers weigh |
| Watch for trust-gap dynamics with leadership — Gen Z is the generation least likely to trust senior leadership and the most likely to leave over it | Assume silence from a younger report means agreement; low trust often shows up as disengagement rather than open pushback |

### 🇨🇦 Canada

Canada's workplace is currently juggling four active generations with meaningfully different expectations, and the data shows the gaps are wide and growing rather than narrowing. Randstad Canada's tracking finds Gen Z's average tenure in the first five years of a career sits at just 1.1 years, against 1.8 for Millennials and 2.9 for Boomers — and looking forward, 32% of Gen Z workers plan to quit or change jobs in a given year, compared with just 5% of Boomers, according to reporting cited by Randstad and corroborated by broader Canadian workplace surveys.

The generational split runs deeper than tenure alone. Ipsos polling finds 61% of Canadians overall consider it important to work in an industry that "helps people," a sentiment that peaks among Millennials (68%) and Gen Z (65%) and trails among older cohorts. Mental health openness follows a similar generational curve — Gen Z workers, especially women, report the highest rates of work-related stress severe enough to affect attendance, reflecting a generation that came of age with far more public discourse around burnout than their Boomer counterparts did. Canadian employers, per CBC reporting, increasingly describe managing this range of expectations as one of the defining HR challenges of the current moment.

### 🇸🇬 Singapore

Singapore layers its generational shift on top of an unusually intense meritocratic tradition, producing a distinctive tension. Deloitte's 2025 Gen Z and Millennial survey found these generations in Singapore are actively redefining career success around stability, wellbeing, and purpose — even as cost of living pressure (the top concern for 49% of Gen Z respondents) pushes hard in the opposite direction. Tenure expectations have shifted dramatically from the traditional loyalty-first model: 45% of Gen Z professionals expect to stay with an employer no more than two years, and Singapore's average Gen Z tenure of 2.9 years is notably shorter than counterparts in Malaysia (3.7 years) or China (4.6 years), according to regional data reported by HRM Asia.

Trust is the sharper fault line. Research cited by Randstad Singapore found 67% of Gen Z workers would consider leaving over a weak sense of belonging, and Gen Z ranks as the generation least likely to trust leadership teams — with that distrust directly predicting attrition. This sits uneasily against Singapore's historically hierarchical, results-first meritocracy (Hofstede Insights scores the country at 74 on power distance), creating friction between an older management style built on clear top-down authority and a younger workforce that explicitly conditions its loyalty on trust and belonging, not just performance-linked reward.

### The Reckoning

Both countries are watching the same underlying phenomenon — younger workers trading long tenure for faster movement and clearer alignment with personal values — but they're layering it onto very different cultural baselines. Canada's shift plays out against a relatively egalitarian, already-informal workplace culture, so the generational gap shows up mostly as a difference in stated priorities and stress tolerance. Singapore's shift plays out against a historically hierarchical, loyalty-and-merit-coded system, so the same generational shift reads as more disruptive — a genuine challenge to how authority and trust have traditionally worked.

The counterintuitive detail is that Singapore's Gen Z tenure figures, while short by the country's own historical standards, are still longer than Canada's. A newcomer assuming Singapore's meritocratic intensity would produce more loyalty than Canada's flatter culture would be wrong — cost of living pressure and trust deficits are proving to be stronger forces than either country's traditional cultural script.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A respondent describing generational friction in a Canadian office said the hardest adjustment for older managers wasn't the shorter tenure itself but learning that a Gen Z report leaving after 14 months wasn't a personal rejection of the team — it was simply the new normal being treated as a rational career strategy.*

> *Fortune (widely cited in Canadian and Singaporean HR circles) — Reporting on generational workplace friction found that Gen Z and Millennial workers report struggling to collaborate with older colleagues at notably higher rates than Boomers report struggling with younger ones, suggesting the discomfort is asymmetric rather than mutual.*

> *teamblind.com — A worker reflecting on quiet disengagement patterns observed across Asian tech hubs noted that younger employees increasingly disengage quietly well before formally resigning, meaning managers who rely on visible complaints as an early warning system are often blindsided by resignations that had been building for months.*

> *gulfnews.com — Analysis on generational tension warned employers against framing the dynamic as a "Boomer vs. Gen Z war," noting that most surveyed workplaces found the friction resolved faster when younger and older employees were paired directly on cross-generational projects rather than segregated by assumption.*

> *Quora — Someone comparing four-generation Canadian workplaces described the most effective managers as the ones who stopped trying to make every generation work the same way and instead negotiated explicit, individual expectations — a small shift that reportedly cut a lot of unspoken resentment.*

### Conclusion

If you're managing across generations in either country, the practical move is the same: stop assuming shared context and start asking directly what tenure, trust, and growth mean to the specific person in front of you, because the averages hide real variation underneath. If you're the younger arrival in either market, be upfront about your timeline rather than letting a manager assume long-term commitment by default — clarity beats surprise in both cultures. My honest take: the generational gap isn't really about age, it's about which decade taught you that job security was a promise worth trusting — and neither Canada nor Singapore has fully decided what to do about the fact that fewer people believe it anymore.
