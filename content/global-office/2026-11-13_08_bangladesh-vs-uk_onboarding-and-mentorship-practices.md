# Bangladesh Throws You In the Deep End, Britain Hands You a Buddy and a Laptop That May Not Arrive

🇧🇩 Bangladesh · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Fifty-four percent of English and Welsh office workers say they were dissatisfied with their onboarding, a figure that a country with an induction pack for everything might prefer to leave out of the pack. Bangladesh, meanwhile, recorded 13.5 percent unemployment among its tertiary-educated young in 2024 and a mentoring culture that its own commentators describe as missing. One country has the process and loses the people in it; the other has the people and is still drafting the process. For anyone moving between them, the first ninety days will be a lesson in which kind of institutional neglect suits you.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Spend the first month listening, taking notes and learning how your manager works | Criticise office rules or the work environment on your first day |
| Think through possible solutions before taking a problem to your manager | Bring your manager trivial problems |
| Find an informal mentor among experienced colleagues yourself | Wait for a formal programme to assign one |
| Warn your manager early if you might miss a commitment | Hide a problem out of fear of small mistakes |
| Ask for feedback regularly and report what you finished and how it could improve | Report "done" and expect seniors to volunteer a review |
| Be punctual and seek the trust of the whole team | Rush to prove yourself or court only the boss |

### United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Email your manager before day one to confirm start time, dress code and equipment | Assume a laptop and a desk are waiting |
| Ask in your first one-to-one what success looks like at 30 and 90 days | Wait for a review to find out what was expected |
| Take everyday questions to your buddy | Take objectives, working patterns or HR matters to them; buddies are not line managers |
| Book short coffees with colleagues in your first week | Decline invitations to lunch or drinks repeatedly |
| Ask questions in a public team channel | Suffer in silence on the theory that you will be expected to know everything |
| Read your contract for the probation terms | Assume the length of probation is set by law |

## Bangladesh

Bangladesh's onboarding is less a programme than an unwritten examination. A Financial Express opinion piece in January 2026 by Hisham Khan and Tasnima Zerin, citing Bangladesh Bureau of Statistics data, reports that unemployment among the tertiary-educated young reached 13.5 percent in 2024, and that unemployed graduates' share of total unemployment rose from 9.7 percent in 2013 to 27.8 percent in 2022. The authors argue the gap is practical know-how, and call for a formal mentoring culture to replace the ad hoc one. Employers, they say, expect to retrain fresh graduates for six to twelve months, though that claim rests on unnamed industry surveys.

Hierarchy shapes how that learning happens. Hofstede Insights scores Bangladesh 80 on power distance, against 35 for the United Kingdom, and local commentary reads accordingly. Writing in The Daily Star, Soud Bin Faisal, a business professional with more than 13 years in multinationals and corporate groups, argues that in some organisations strict hierarchy discourages employees from sharing ideas, asking questions or giving feedback. Prothom Alo's guide to the first 90 days advises newcomers to listen, ask fewer repeated questions, think of solutions before approaching a manager and avoid rushing to prove themselves, a very different tone from Western advice to ask early and often. It cites a 2025 Journal of Management review of 256 studies showing that newcomers adapt faster by seeking support from experienced colleagues and mentors.

The advice is not uniform: The Daily Star's guide for newcomers says asking many questions is unlikely to hurt and urges them to clarify the scope of their role early, which sits awkwardly beside the counsel to ask fewer. The multinationals soften the picture; a Global Graduate at British American Tobacco Bangladesh credited patience and actively seeking help from peers for his settling in, which describes a formal scheme that still depends on the newcomer's initiative. Mentorship elsewhere tends to be informal, personal and unreliable. A Huawei solution architect told The Daily Star that unwritten corporate norms are hard for newcomers to navigate and that seniors can overlook juniors. The pay-off for failing to build such relationships is mobility: according to Gallup's State of the Global Workplace 2025, as cited by The Business Standard, 67 percent of the Bangladeshi workforce is actively seeking better opportunities, one of the highest rates in South Asia. Whether people leave because they are not mentored, or are not mentored because they may leave, is a question the survey does not settle.

## United Kingdom

Britain's onboarding is formal on paper and uneven in practice. According to a Cognexo report based on a Research Without Barriers survey of 2,000 office-based employees in England and Wales, 54 percent were dissatisfied with onboarding. The reasons were a lack of structure (31 percent), differences between the advertised role and the actual one (21 percent) and confusion about daily tasks (20 percent), while 14 percent felt isolated. A separate KPMG-commissioned survey of about 1,000 students and new joiners, reported by The HR Director, found 84 percent enjoying work and 82 percent reporting strong connections with colleagues, so the experience is evidently a lottery with a decent jackpot.

What Britain does reliably is the buddy. The government's GOV.UK team describes a buddy as a temporary guide who answers questions, points to documentation and helps newcomers feel welcome, with a catch-up in the first week or two and a feedback discussion after the first month. Hays suggests short ten- to fifteen-minute coffees with key colleagues in week one, which is the British idea of a welcome ceremony. Explicitly, buddies are not line managers and do not handle objectives or HR processes. At the Office for National Statistics, a content design team pairs each new starter with an experienced colleague for weekly conversations. The scheme exists to take everyday questions off managers, which is a design choice that a high-power-distance office might struggle to copy.

The legal backdrop is lighter than newcomers assume. Acas says employers are not legally required to have a probation period and that it can run for any length, although from 1 January 2027 protection from unfair dismissal begins after six months' service. The CIPD notes that induction kicks off probation and that changes under the Employment Rights Act 2025 mean employers need to take more care with the process. The effect is that your ability to ask a question without penalty is partly a matter of contract and partly a matter of whether your manager read the guidance.

## The Reckoning

The difference is not that Britain mentors and Bangladesh does not. Britain systematises small, low-stakes help, such as a buddy, a one-to-one and a probation review, and leaves the larger questions about career and promotion to the individual. Bangladesh withholds the structure but often provides the loyalty: a senior who adopts you may do more for your career than any programme, though the senior chooses and you do not. A newcomer who relies on a formal scheme in Dhaka may be waiting for a train that has not been built.

The practical conclusion depends on temperament. If you learn by asking, Britain is gentler, provided you are not among the 54 percent who find the structure absent. If you learn by watching, and can read a room, Bangladesh rewards patience and punishes eagerness. In both countries the best mentor is the person you find yourself, which is the sort of career advice that is cheap to give and expensive to follow.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — a woman who spent four years in Dhaka says her colleagues at her first advertising agency were non-judgemental and an eclectic bunch, and one even joked about the attention she attracted on the street. The harder part was outside the office, as her next agency sat in a more crowded area where the stares were more intense.*

> *ExpatExchange — an anonymous contributor with four years in Bangladesh, after living in Singapore, says that outside multinationals there is effectively no benchmark for standards. Colleagues may say they are on their way before leaving, traffic can limit you to one meeting a day, and the advice is to arrange every detail before and after a task.*

> *The Daily Star — a Huawei solution architect says fresh graduates struggle to gain acceptance, describing the unwritten corporate norms as difficult to navigate. He also thinks senior professionals can overlook juniors because of bias and compatibility issues.*

> *ONS Digital blog — a new starter at the Office for National Statistics joined during a remote induction and felt isolated until she met her buddy about a week in. She says the relationship shifted from teacher and pupil to a reciprocal partnership, and made her feel her experience mattered.*

> *Times Higher Education — a new lecturer at Kingston University regrets not accepting colleagues' invitations to drinks and lunch sooner, because seeing them socially made them much more approachable. Another contributor reassures newcomers that nobody expects them to know everything.*

## Conclusion

If you are weighing the two, ask what you would rather lose: structure or sponsorship. In the UK, request the buddy and the thirty- and ninety-day expectations in writing before you start, check your probation terms, and accept the lunch invitation. In Bangladesh, do your homework before approaching your manager, find a senior who will vouch for you, and treat patience as a professional skill, not a personality flaw.

Either way, you will be trained by someone who was never trained to train you.
