**Call Him "Herr Magister" in the Hallway, "Hans" at the Christmas Party: Austria and Canada on Who's in Charge**

**🇦🇹 Austria · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Austria looks, from the outside, like the most hierarchical office on this list: use the wrong professional title, address your boss with the informal "du" before invited to, and you will know about it immediately. And yet Hofstede Insights scores Austria at just 11 on power distance — one of the lowest figures the index records anywhere in the world, formality and all. Canada, by contrast, looks almost aggressively informal, and still expects its managers to visibly justify every decision to a team that will read silence in a meeting as disengagement. Formality, it turns out, is not the same thing as actual hierarchy, in either direction.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use the correct professional title (Austria recognizes more formal titles than most English-speaking countries) until told otherwise | Switch to the informal "du" with a boss or senior colleague before they explicitly invite it — default to formal "Sie" |
| Expect the highest-ranking person to enter a meeting room first, and let that order play out | Assume formal address means a distant or inaccessible boss — many Austrian managers are genuinely warm once the formality is cleared |
| Expect clear, defined roles and a decisive, top-down meeting style, often from a detailed agenda | Expect decisions to be made by committee — in most Austrian firms, leadership makes the actual call |
| Let your expertise and individual achievement, not just your title, earn you visibility — rank tracks competence here more than seniority alone | Be surprised if the "boss tells you what to do, but not how to do it" — autonomy within a clear mandate is common in practice |
| Save genuine informality for after-work drinks or the office Christmas party, where even reserved bosses visibly relax | Assume your workplace's formality rules are fixed — many Austrian companies have shifted toward "du" policies recently; check early |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect direct access to leadership and expect your input to be genuinely weighed, not just collected as a formality | Make a unilateral decision without visibly consulting your team — it can cost you trust fast, regardless of the decision's quality |
| Speak up in meetings — silence is often read as disengagement, not respect | Deliver criticism bluntly and without softening — Canadian feedback is typically framed diplomatically, often as a question or suggestion |
| Expect your manager to explain the reasoning behind a decision, not just announce it | Assume consultation means your manager has to adopt your suggestion — being heard and being obeyed are different things here |
| Recognize regional variation — Ottawa's federal public service, Alberta's energy sector, and Quebec each carry distinct norms | Treat "Canadian workplace culture" as one uniform thing across every province and industry |
| Expect a manager who checks in personally during real hardship — accessible, hands-on support from leadership is a noted strength | Assume flat-feeling hierarchy means no real authority exists — decisions still ultimately sit with someone specific |

### Austria

The puzzle at the center of Austrian management is that its visible formality and its actual power distribution point in different directions. Expatica and The Cultural Atlas both describe a business culture of strict role clarity, correct titles, and formal "Sie" address that persists until a superior explicitly signals otherwise — the kind of etiquette that reads, to an outsider, as deeply hierarchical. But Hofstede's own measurement tells a different story: Austria's power-distance score of 11 is among the lowest recorded globally, reflecting a workplace where authority is earned through individual expertise and achievement rather than inherited status, and where, per The Cultural Atlas, "hierarchy matters less in informal settings" — exactly the kind of after-hours softening that formal daytime etiquette would never predict.

A first-person account published by Welcome to the Jungle captured this contradiction directly. Author Axelle Gasne, raised in a culture she describes as "hard-wired to be cold and formal," expected Austrian management to match — and was instead struck by being greeted with "Hey Hans, what's up?" by her own senior boss, and by what she called "horizontal management... in the way employees were listened to." She described her manager's actual governing style in a single line: "the boss tells you what to do, but not how to do it" — a mandate-with-autonomy model that let her propose and run her own internal seminar series on the strength of one simple response: "The boss said yes." Within months of starting, she found herself meeting Austria's own Minister of the Interior — a degree of access she hadn't expected the formal exterior to permit at all.

### Canada

Canada's management style looks, by comparison, almost deliberately anti-hierarchical from the outset — Hofstede's power-distance score of 39 sits in a similar low range to Austria's, but the cultural performance around it is nearly opposite. Where Austria signals hierarchy through titles and formal address while quietly practicing flat authority underneath, Canada signals flatness constantly and explicitly, through consultation rituals that are, per workplace guidance from Berlitz, treated as "not weakness in Canadian corporate culture" but as the actual expectation. Employees are described as wanting direct "access to leadership," meetings where silence gets read as disengagement rather than deference, and feedback that arrives "framed as a question or a suggestion" rather than a blunt directive.

Crucially, consultation in this model doesn't mean a manager must change course — Berlitz's own framing is explicit that "the consultation does not obligate you to change your decision" — but skipping the ritual of visible consultation entirely is treated as a credibility risk serious enough to undermine an otherwise sound call. A first-person account from Capco's "Be Yourself at Work" series, describing a move into a Toronto-based role, offered a concrete illustration of what that visible, personal management style looks like in practice: during a serious illness, the author recalled receiving "calls from senior management daily," having groceries sent, and being "continuously told to rest" — direct, personal engagement from leadership rather than a policy delegated to HR. The regional caveat matters too: Berlitz notes that Ottawa's federal public service, Alberta's energy sector, and Quebec's distinct communication norms each bend this general pattern differently, meaning "Canadian management style" is really several adjacent styles sharing a passport.

### The Reckoning

Both countries land in roughly the same low-power-distance range by Hofstede's numbers, yet they get there by nearly opposite theatrical routes. Austria performs hierarchy on the surface — titles, formal address, a strict meeting entrance order — while quietly running a workplace where competence, not rank, decides who actually gets heard, and where a mandate-with-autonomy model gives individual employees real latitude once trust is established. Canada performs flatness on the surface — first names, open-door access, constant consultation — while still expecting a clear decision-maker to exist and be accountable for explaining their reasoning afterward; the openness is real, but it is not the same thing as an absence of authority.

The trap for an incoming hire is reading either performance at face value. Treat Austrian formality as proof of real distance from your boss, and you'll miss the mandate-with-autonomy relationship Gasne describes, where the formal title and the "boss said yes" flexibility coexist easily. Treat Canadian informality as proof that hierarchy has dissolved, and you'll miss that staying silent in a meeting, or skipping the visible ritual of being consulted, can cost you credibility in ways a more openly hierarchical culture would never even test.

The deeper point is that both cultures have simply decided which signal gets to lie. Austria lets its formality lie about the actual distance between ranks, while keeping the substance of authority, once earned, genuinely flexible and personal. Canada lets its informality lie about the actual existence of a decision-maker, while keeping the ritual of consultation serious enough that skipping it has real consequences. Knowing which signal to discount, in each place, matters more than knowing the etiquette rules themselves.

### Conclusion

If you're trying to read your new boss correctly in either country, don't trust the surface signal. In Austria, the "Sie" and the formal title are not a measure of how much real autonomy and access you'll actually get — judge that by what happens once trust is established, not by the greeting. In Canada, the first-name basis and the open-door policy are not a sign that no one's really in charge — judge that by who ends up explaining the "why" behind a decision you didn't get a vote on. Either way: watch what the hierarchy does, not what it calls itself.
