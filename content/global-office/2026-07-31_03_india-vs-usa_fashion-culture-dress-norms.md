**India vs. USA: One Country Legislates the Hemline, the Other Just Wears Whatever Was Clean**

**🇮🇳 India · 🇺🇸 USA**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In 2018, Bihar's labour department issued a formal circular telling government staff not to wear jeans and T-shirts to the office. In the same year, roughly four in five American employers told researchers they now prefer an informal dress code, and by last count only 3% of US workers say wearing business-professional clothing is their daily norm — the lowest figure Gallup has ever measured. Somewhere between those two facts sits every relocation packet's most-ignored paragraph: what you're actually supposed to wear.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether you're joining a legacy sector (banking, law, government) or IT/startup — the dress code gap between them is enormous | Assume "IT company" automatically means casual; some still mandate formal wear Monday–Thursday |
| Default to business casual on day one — collared shirt and trousers, or kurta and churidar/salwar — until you see the room | Wear jeans to a government-adjacent office; several states have formally banned them in circulars |
| Keep a formal outfit ready for client-facing days even at relaxed employers | Wear leather accessories without checking norms first — it can read as culturally insensitive in some settings |
| Ask HR directly if a written dress code exists — several companies still enforce one literally, down to hair being tied back | Assume regional or state-level dress rules are jokes; some carry real disciplinary weight in public-sector jobs |

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to business casual for your first day, then calibrate down once you see what everyone else wears | Show up underdressed on day one — you can dress down after, but a bad first impression is hard to undo |
| Expect wide variation by industry — finance and law still expect real formality; tech treats a hoodie as a compliment | Assume the whole country is casual; roughly a quarter of US workers still wear a uniform or formalwear daily |
| Budget for wardrobe turnover if you're client-facing — plenty of professionals still spend hundreds of dollars refreshing work clothes each year | Overdress dramatically at a tech company; it can read as trying too hard rather than professional |
| Ask directly about the dress code before day one — it varies so much between employers that guessing is genuinely risky | Assume "business casual" means the same thing everywhere; definitions vary company to company |

### India

India's workplace dress code is really two different countries wearing two different outfits. Traditional sectors — banking, law, government offices, older family-run businesses — still expect conventional formal wear: suits or shirts and trousers for men, saris, salwar kameez, or conservative Western formalwear for women. Several state governments have gone further and made this explicit: Bihar's labour department circulated a formal notice discouraging jeans and T-shirts in 2018, and Maharashtra introduced a rule requiring khadi on Fridays while banning jeans and T-shirts outright in government offices. On the other end, India's IT and startup sector — the dominant employer of the country's younger professional class — has moved firmly into smart-casual territory, with jeans, polo shirts, and Indo-Western combinations (kurtis paired with trousers) now standard.

Hofstede Insights scores India at 77 on Power Distance, among the highest in the world, and this shows up directly in how seriously dress-code hierarchy is enforced: some companies still specify exactly how hair should be tied and whether shawls need to be pinned, rules that read, to a Western hire, less like a workplace policy and more like a school uniform code.

### USA

The American story is one of fairly rapid, fairly recent collapse of formality. Gallup's most recent measurement found that 72% of US workers describe their typical workplace attire as business casual or fully casual street clothes, while just 3% wear business-professional clothing daily — the lowest share Gallup has recorded. The share of companies allowing casual dress all week rose from 32% to 50% in five years, according to Qz/Indeed research, and FashionUnited reports that 79% of US employers now say they prefer an informal dress code outright. The shift has teeth: one in three US workers surveyed said they'd quit or turn down a job that required formal attire.

This isn't classless, though — it's industry-stratified. Finance and law remain comparatively buttoned-up; tech has swung hardest toward the casual end, to the point that some employees still budget real money for clothes even in "casual" jobs — sales-facing roles average roughly $240 a month on wardrobe, customer-facing roles closer to $150, because "casual" doesn't mean "no effort," it means a different, subtler set of unwritten rules.

### The Reckoning

The two countries look, from a distance, like polar opposites — India formalizing dress code by government circular, the US abandoning it by cultural drift — but the underlying logic is closer than it appears. Both countries run two dress codes at once, split cleanly along the same fault line: legacy institutional sectors (banking, law, government) stay formal almost everywhere in the world, while the newer, faster-growing tech economy sets its own looser rules wherever it lands.

What differs is enforcement. India's formality is sometimes written into literal government policy with disciplinary consequences; America's formality is almost entirely social — nobody will write you up for a hoodie, but everybody will notice, and it will shape how seriously you're taken in a way no memo admits to.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (r/IndianWorkplace) — A poster shared a friend's IT-company offer letter with a dress code so strict it required shirts tucked in, jeans only with shirts tucked in, and for women, kurtas and churidars with shawls "properly pinned" and hair tied back with a ribbon — and asked, genuinely, what any of it had to do with writing code. The thread's outrage centered specifically on the surprise: IT was supposed to be the casual industry.*

> *Quora — Someone answering "How strict is the formal dress code in Deloitte?" described the real policy as formals Monday through Thursday with business casual allowed on Fridays, but noted enforcement varied enormously by manager — some teams effectively went casual daily and nobody said a word, others policed it closely.*

> *Blind — A new hire preparing for their first day at Google, coming from a bank background, asked whether they should "dress down" from their instinct to wear formalwear. The most-upvoted reply distilled Silicon Valley's actual rule: if an outfit would get you served at a fast-food counter, it's appropriate for the office — and that you can't be overdressed on day one, but you can absolutely be underdressed.*

> *Blind — A separate thread from an engineer asking about dress code at a major US bank's tech division got a similarly split response: sneakers were fine, jeans were fine, but the poster was warned that client-facing days still meant a collared shirt at minimum — the same legacy-versus-tech split showing up inside a single company.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to India, the practical move is to ask which India you're joining — legacy formal or startup casual — because guessing wrong reads as either overdressed and stiff or underdressed and unserious, with very little grey area between. If you're moving to the US, default sharp on day one and dress down once you've actually seen the room, because underdressing is the more expensive mistake in both directions. The line I'd give a friend packing for either move: nobody's handing you a rulebook, so read the room before you read anything else.
