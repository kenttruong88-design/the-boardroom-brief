# Nepal vs Singapore: Where the Boss Is God, and Where the Boss Is a KPI

🇳🇵 Nepal · 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Kathmandu, you add "ji" to your manager's name as a matter of course, and a decision that could be made in an afternoon is instead deliberated, respectfully, for a week. In Singapore, the deliberation takes ninety seconds and the decision arrives by Slack at 11 p.m. Roughly half a million young Nepalis leave the domestic labour market yearly, mostly for the Gulf and Malaysia, per the ILO — while in Singapore, only 14 percent of employees report feeling engaged at work, per Gallup's 2026 Singapore Workplace Report. Neither country, in other words, has quite cracked the problem of making people want to be at the office. They have simply arrived at that failure by opposite routes.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇵 Nepal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address seniors with "ji" and greet the most senior person in the room first | Walk straight past a senior colleague's desk without a greeting |
| Expect decisions to move up the hierarchy before they move at all | Push a manager for an on-the-spot yes in a group meeting |
| Budget real time for tea, small talk, and relationship-building before business | Treat the first meeting as purely transactional |
| Dress formally and conservatively, even in warm weather | Assume casual Fridays are universal — many firms have none |
| Expect fixed, long hours with little flexibility around them | Assume you can quietly work from home without asking first |
| Learn a few lines of Nepali; effort is noticed and rewarded | Rely solely on English and assume it covers every office interaction |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up on time, every time — punctuality reads as competence | Treat meeting start times as approximate |
| State your point directly; Singaporean colleagues are candid and expect the same | Bury disagreement in vague language and hope it's understood |
| Respect the reporting line even in an outwardly flat-looking org chart | Skip your manager to raise an issue with someone more senior |
| Expect long hours to be normal, especially in your first year | Assume a 6 p.m. logout is standard practice everywhere |
| Ask about flexible work arrangements only after three months' service, per MOM guidelines | Request remote work in week one and expect an automatic yes |
| Lean into meritocratic self-promotion — modesty won't get you noticed | Wait quietly for someone to recognise your unstated contributions |

### Nepal: Deference First, Deadlines Eventually

Nepali corporate culture runs on a moderately high power distance common across South Asia: junior staff address seniors with careful formality, rarely interrupt, and expect authority to flow visibly downward before a decision is final. Titles matter, "ji" matters, and a specific hierarchical system is followed not to demean anyone but as a straightforward matter of respect, according to reporting on the country's business culture. The upside is a genuinely collectivist spirit: colleagues cover for each other, milestones are celebrated communally, and personal time is routinely sacrificed to help a teammate hit a deadline.

The downside shows up in the numbers. Youth unemployment sits near 19 percent, against 2.7 percent for the population overall, per the ILO — a gap wide enough that roughly 70 percent of Nepal's labour migrants are under 30. For those who stay, the private sector remains mostly family-run and slow to modernise its policies: only 28.5 percent of working-age women are in formal employment, and roughly 70 percent of organisations deny statutory maternity leave in practice by citing "inconvenience," despite a Labour Act that mandates 98 days, per reporting from OnlineKhabar and World Bank gender data. Flexible work exists mostly as an aspiration rather than a policy line item.

### Singapore: Meritocracy, Measured Precisely

Singapore's workplace culture presents as thoroughly modern — efficient, English-speaking, rule-bound — which is why its power distance score of 74 on the Hofstede Insights framework surprises newcomers expecting a flat Silicon Valley org chart. Confucian-inflected hierarchy sits underneath a language of meritocracy and KPIs: reporting lines are respected, but promotion is genuinely earned rather than inherited, and the Ministry of Manpower caps contractual hours at 44 a week, 8 a day, even if the "usual hours worked" figure has hovered closer to 44.5, and paid overtime adds a couple more on top, according to MOM's own statistics.

What the efficiency doesn't buy is enthusiasm. Gallup's 2026 report puts engagement at just 14 percent, trailing the regional average of 25 percent and the global average of 20 percent, while 43 percent report significant daily stress — above the 40 percent global average. Workers under 35 fare worse still: only 10 percent engaged, and 53 percent stressed daily. Singapore did not build a broken system. It built a highly functional one that a large share of the people inside it would rather not be in.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the counterintuitive part is how similarly unhappy the outcomes are, arrived at from opposite cultural instincts. Nepal's hierarchy is explicit and personal — you know exactly whose approval you're waiting for, and the wait itself is treated as a form of respect. Singapore's hierarchy is procedural and impersonal — dressed in meritocratic language, delivered through a KPI dashboard, and no less rigid for being invisible. One culture asks you to be patient with people; the other asks you to be efficient with outcomes. Neither, per the data above, is currently making its employees feel especially good about either approach.

The practical difference for a newcomer is speed of consequence. In Nepal, a cultural misstep — skipping the tea, pushing too hard for a yes — mostly costs you relationship capital you can slowly rebuild. In Singapore, a missed deadline or a badly read hierarchy costs you a performance rating, which costs you a bonus, which costs you the flat you were saving toward. Nepal will forgive you for being slow. Singapore will simply route around you.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A contributor described Singapore's office culture as thoroughly workaholic, with the workplace functioning as a second home and colleagues visibly competitive about who logs the longest hours, framed locally as simply "getting the job done."*

> *TeamBlind — A software engineer recounted an entire team staying until 9 p.m. most nights for no operational reason beyond the fact that their manager hadn't left yet, and noted a quieter, harder-to-prove ceiling on advancement for employees who weren't Singaporean or Caucasian.*

> *Expat forums (Expat.com) — A newcomer observed that government-linked and long-established local firms in Singapore ran far stricter, more hierarchical operations than foreign multinationals, which tended to be less political and more openly results-driven — a distinction no orientation packet mentioned.*

> *OnlineKhabar (Nepal) — A workplace commentator noted that although Nepal's Labour Act guarantees 98 days of maternity leave, a large share of employers quietly discourage women from taking it in full, citing scheduling "inconvenience" rather than contesting the law outright.*

> *Merokalam (Nepal returnee blog) — A professional who moved back to Kathmandu after years abroad found that formal job postings mattered far less than personal referrals, and that foreign work experience earned respect in interviews without translating into a foreign-equivalent salary.*

### Conclusion

If what you want is warmth, communal effort, and a workplace where your effort is noticed even when it's slow, Nepal will give you that — provided you can also live with a formal job market too thin to hold its own graduates, and a bureaucracy that moves at the pace of consensus. If what you want is efficiency, clear rules, and a salary that clears on time, Singapore will give you that — provided you're prepared to discover that "meritocracy" and "hierarchy" are not, in practice, opposites. Pick the version of tired you'd prefer: tired from waiting, or tired from delivering.
