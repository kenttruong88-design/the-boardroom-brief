# Mongolia Has a Startup Scene. The Rest of the Economy Didn't Get the Memo.

🇲🇳 Mongolia · 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Mongolia's startup scene is real, small, and improbably scrappy — Seedstars has described a landlocked ecosystem thriving despite minimal venture capital, while JICA has run multiple programs explicitly aimed at helping the country become, in its own stated ambition, a "digital nation." Set against that is an economy still substantially organized around mining and state-linked enterprises; the Natural Resource Governance Institute's assessment of Erdenes Mongol, the state mining holding company, describes exactly the kind of top-down, state-adjacent corporate structure that most of the country's working population actually experiences day to day. The Netherlands has the opposite problem: its startup scene isn't scrappy at all. I Amsterdam has reported Dutch tech alumni founding 270 startups that have collectively raised $19 billion, and WeAreBrain's 2025 statistics show Amsterdam fully entrenched as one of Europe's genuine scaleup hubs — so entrenched that some founders now worry it's starting to behave like the corporate establishment it was supposed to disrupt.

## Mongolia: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect the large majority of formal employment to sit inside mining, state-linked, or traditionally hierarchical firms | Assume Mongolia's small, visible startup scene represents the typical workplace |
| Treat personal relationships and trust-building as prerequisites for real business traction | Expect rapid, transactional deal-making on a first or second meeting |
| Expect genuine enthusiasm for digital-nation initiatives among younger, urban professionals | Assume that enthusiasm has yet translated into widespread venture funding or exits |
| Respect that state-linked enterprises still set much of the country's management culture | Dismiss hierarchy in large firms as a startup-era relic waiting to be disrupted |
| Expect foreign businesspeople to be taken seriously faster once introduced through a trusted contact | Expect the same speed of access without that introduction |

## Netherlands: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect blunt, direct feedback regardless of whether you're at a 10-person startup or a multinational | Mistake Dutch directness for hostility — it's considered efficient, not rude |
| Expect genuinely flat-feeling hierarchy and open internal debate even at scaled companies | Assume "flat" means titles and reporting lines don't matter in practice |
| Expect well-developed venture infrastructure — accelerators, co-working, investor density | Assume every Dutch startup still feels scrappy once it's raised serious capital |
| Expect consensus-building ("poldermodel" thinking) even inside fast-moving startups | Expect unilateral founder decisions to go unchallenged the way they might elsewhere |
| Expect scaleups to adopt recognisably corporate HR and compliance structures quickly | Assume a company calling itself a "startup" at 200+ employees still operates like one |

## Mongolia: A Scrappy Scene Inside a State-Shaped Economy

What makes Mongolia an interesting case for this pillar is the gap between its startup narrative and its actual labor-market structure. Seedstars' coverage of the "landlocked startup scene" and StartupBlink's ranking of Ulaanbaatar's ecosystem both describe a genuinely energetic, if small, community of founders working around limited local capital, often bootstrapping or relying on programs like JICA's entrepreneurial-development initiatives rather than deep venture pools. It's a real phenomenon, and one Mongolia's government has actively championed as part of a broader digital-nation push.

But that scene sits inside an economy still substantially organized around mining and state enterprise. The Resource Governance Institute's assessment of Erdenes Mongol describes governance and management patterns — centralized decision-making, political appointment dynamics, limited operational transparency — that, while specific to one state holding company, are broadly reflective of how a large share of formal Mongolian employment is actually structured. Academic work applying Hofstede's 6-D model to Mongolian cultural values has found a profile leaning toward higher power distance and collectivism, consistent with both the state-enterprise pattern and the relationship-first business norms expat guides consistently describe. A foreign professional drawn to Mongolia by its startup headlines should understand they're likely to land in one of two very different economies depending on the specific employer, not a single unified "Mongolian business culture."

The two economies don't just coexist quietly, either — they compete for the same small pool of skilled young professionals. JICA's entrepreneurial-development programs exist specifically because Mongolian officials have identified outward migration of ambitious graduates as a genuine policy concern, and the digital-nation push is, in part, an attempt to give talented young Mongolians a reason to build something at home rather than seek a corporate ladder abroad. Whether a handful of accelerator-backed startups can meaningfully compete with mining-sector salaries for that talent remains, honestly, an open question the ecosystem itself is still answering.

## Netherlands: When the Disruptor Becomes the Institution

The Dutch case runs in the opposite direction: the startup scene isn't fighting for legitimacy, it's dealing with the consequences of having largely won. WeAreBrain's 2025 statistics and I Amsterdam's $19 billion tech-alumni figure describe an ecosystem mature enough that "startup" and "major employer" are no longer contradictory categories — companies that started as scrappy founder-led operations now run HR departments, compliance functions, and reporting structures indistinguishable from traditional corporates. Reporting on Dutch entrepreneurs, including public comments from bunq's CEO in 2025 about talent and founders considering leaving the Netherlands, points to a tension between the country's famously founder-friendly reputation and a regulatory and tax environment some say now behaves more like the corporate establishment the startup wave originally set out to challenge.

What stays constant across both ends of that spectrum is the cultural operating system underneath. IamExpat's guides to Dutch directness describe blunt, efficiency-oriented feedback as a near-universal workplace norm, equally present in a ten-person startup and a scaled multinational, and Dutch business culture more broadly runs on a consensus-building instinct — sometimes called the "poldermodel," a reference to the historical necessity of literally cooperating to keep the water out — that persists even inside fast-moving founder-led companies. Hofstede's individualism score for the Netherlands sits among Europe's highest, which sits in productive tension with that same consensus culture: Dutch professionals expect to be heard individually, but they also expect the group to actually reach agreement before moving forward, startup or not.

## The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't "scrappy versus corporate" — it's about which direction each country's business culture is moving. Mongolia's small startup scene is pushing against a much larger, more traditional, often state-linked economic base, trying to drag working culture toward something more entrepreneurial. The Netherlands' scaleup economy has already absorbed so much of its own startup energy into mainstream corporate practice that the tension now runs the other way — founders and early employees occasionally longing for the scrappiness their own success left behind.

For a foreign professional, this means the label on the job posting matters less than the specific company's actual size, funding stage, and ownership structure. A "startup" job in Ulaanbaatar might mean something closer to what the word implied a decade ago in Silicon Valley. A "startup" job in Amsterdam, at this point, might come with a compliance handbook thicker than some traditional banks'. Ask about headcount and funding history before you ask about the mission statement — the honest answer to "what's it actually like here" usually lives in those numbers, not the pitch deck.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com Mongolia forum — paraphrased experience: a thread on business etiquette in Mongolia emphasizes that nothing moves quickly without a personal introduction, with posters describing cold outreach to local companies as largely unproductive regardless of how compelling the pitch is on paper.*

> *ExpatExchange — paraphrased experience: responses on what expats find most challenging in Mongolia describe a visible gap between the energetic, internationally-minded startup crowd in Ulaanbaatar and the much more traditional, hierarchical norms encountered at larger local employers.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Ulaanbaatar — paraphrased experience: a posted account describes being surprised at how centralized decision-making felt at a state-linked employer, despite having been told before arrival that Mongolia's business scene was "young and entrepreneurial."*

> *IamExpat.nl — paraphrased experience: coverage of Dutch workplace norms describes new foreign hires initially mistaking blunt feedback for personal criticism, before realizing it's simply treated as the fastest route to a better outcome, regardless of company size or age.*

> *Reddit — paraphrased experience: a foreign hire at a Dutch scaleup describes being startled by how much internal debate and consensus-seeking persisted even after the company had grown past 500 employees, compared to the faster, more unilateral decision-making they'd expected from "startup culture" elsewhere.*

## Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two, the useful question isn't which country has "startup energy" — both technically do — but which specific employer you're actually joining and what stage, ownership structure, and decision-making pattern it really runs on. In Mongolia, ask who owns the company and how introductions happen before you ask about its tech stack. In the Netherlands, ask how big the company has gotten since its founding before you assume the pitch deck's energy still describes daily life there. Both countries will happily let you believe you're joining a startup. Only one of them is still telling the truth, and even that one is getting less honest about it every funding round.
