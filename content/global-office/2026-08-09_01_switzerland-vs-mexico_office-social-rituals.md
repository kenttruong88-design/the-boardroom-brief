# The Apéro Clock vs. The Two-Hour Table: Switzerland and Mexico at Lunch

**🇨🇭 Switzerland · 🇲🇽 Mexico**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Zurich, the office empties for lunch at 11:45 sharp and refills by 12:30, a break as punctual as the trains. In Mexico City, lunch does not really begin until the trains — metaphorical and literal — have long since stopped worrying about the clock, often starting at 2 p.m. and running past 4. Both countries will tell you their approach builds trust among colleagues. Only one of them will make you late for your next meeting on purpose.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to the office apéro, even briefly — skipping every one reads as disengaged | Assume a warm lunch chat means you're now friends outside the office |
| Keep weekends and evenings free of work messages — colleagues genuinely won't check | Linger past the agreed meeting time; punctuality is a form of respect here |
| Bring your own lunch or eat at the canteen without guilt — solo eating isn't antisocial | Ask coworkers personal questions about salary, family finances, or age |
| Use direct, calm feedback — it's meant constructively, not as an attack | Expect promotions or bonuses to be discussed casually at the apéro |
| Learn the informal "du" vs. formal "Sie/vous" rules before switching to first names | Cancel on a planned team lunch without notice — it's noted, quietly |

#### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the invitation to a long lunch — it's often where real decisions get made | Rush straight into business before asking about family or weekend plans |
| Expect the office to run past 6 p.m. and plan your day around that reality | Assume a lack of formal RSVP means an event isn't happening — show up anyway |
| Use last names and titles (Licenciado, Ingeniero) until invited to switch | Book back-to-back meetings around the 2–4 p.m. lunch window |
| Join birthday celebrations and coffee-break chats — they matter for cohesion | Treat the first year's limited vacation allowance as negotiable |
| Bring a genuine interest in colleagues' personal lives — it reads as respect | Confuse warmth and hospitality with an immediate, binding commitment |

### Switzerland: The Apéro Is Mandatory Fun, Politely

Switzerland runs one of the most tightly bounded office social calendars in Europe. The average workweek clocks in at 41.5 hours, and according to OECD data, only 0.4% of Swiss employees work 50 hours or more a week, versus an OECD average of roughly 10% — a statistic that helps explain why Switzerland regularly ranks among the top five countries globally for work-life balance. Lunch is short, often 30 to 60 minutes, and frequently solitary: bringing your own food to eat at your desk or in the canteen is normal, not a social failure.

Where Swiss office culture does socialize is the apéro — a standing tradition of after-work drinks and finger food for nearly any occasion: a birthday, a promotion, a Friday. It looks casual. It is not optional in the way it looks. Missing every apéro reads as disengagement, even though no one will say so directly. What the apéro is not, however, is a fast track to friendship. Colleagues remain colleagues for years; the professional and personal spheres stay separated in a way that can feel cold to arrivals from more blended work cultures. Hofstede Insights scores Switzerland at just 34 on Power Distance, reflecting a genuinely egalitarian office, but that flatness doesn't translate into informality — feedback is direct, schedules are respected, and contacting a colleague outside working hours is mildly taboo.

### Mexico: The Table Is the Meeting

In Mexico, the workday is long, and the lunch inside it is longer. Business hours nominally run 9 a.m. to 6 p.m., but staying visibly late is itself a marker of commitment, and lunch — genuinely built into how business gets done — regularly stretches two hours or more, frequently starting as late as 2 or 3 p.m. Mexico's Hofstede Power Distance score of 81 and its startlingly high Indulgence score of 97 — the highest of any country in Hofstede's global dataset — line up with what shows up at the table: hierarchy is respected, and enjoying life, food, and company is not treated as time stolen from work but as part of how work gets built.

That long lunch is not a break from business; it is a delivery mechanism for it. Deals and decisions move forward once a personal relationship has been established, and skipping the small talk to get to the agenda can read as brusque or even untrustworthy. New arrivals should also note a less charming structural fact: Mexican labor law grants only six vacation days after a full year of service, meaning the generous, unhurried lunch culture coexists with one of the thinner statutory leave allowances among the countries The Global Office has covered.

### The Reckoning

Both countries build cohesion through food, but they've optimized for opposite things. Switzerland treats time as the scarce resource to be protected — lunch is short so evenings can be genuinely off-limits. Mexico treats relationship-building as the scarce resource — lunch is long because trust cannot be rushed, and the workday absorbs the difference. A Swiss employee transplanted to Mexico City will likely feel, for the first few months, that no work is getting done at 3 p.m.; a Mexican employee transplanted to Zurich may feel that colleagues have quietly locked the door on real connection.

The irony is that both systems produce loyalty, just through different instruments. Switzerland does it by protecting personal time so thoroughly that work never colonizes it. Mexico does it by making work itself feel personal enough that the boundary barely needs protecting. Neither is more "social" than the other — they've simply decided where the socializing belongs.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who relocated from the U.S. for a nonprofit role in Mexico City wrote that they assumed the work culture would mirror what they'd known back home and were, in their words, definitively wrong — the biggest adjustment wasn't the language but realizing that a 6 p.m. "close of business" was aspirational, not actual.*

> *r/switzerland — A software engineer who moved from London described the apéro as the single hardest Swiss custom to read correctly: nobody explains that skipping three in a row quietly recategorizes you as "not really on the team," even though the invitations keep arriving as if nothing changed.*

> *InterNations Mexico City — A European consultant noted that trying to start a client lunch with the agenda, rather than twenty minutes of unhurried personal conversation, visibly cooled the room — and that the deal only moved forward after a second lunch spent almost entirely off-topic.*

> *Quora — A respondent working across both cultures observed that Mexican colleagues regularly ask direct, warm questions about family and private life that would read as intrusive in a Swiss office, and that learning to answer them (rather than deflect) was the actual key to being trusted with real work.*

> *r/switzerland — An expat engineer wrote that the most disorienting realization was how genuinely private Swiss coworkers stayed even after years side by side — pleasant, reliable, and essentially unknowable outside the office walls, which took longer to accept than any language barrier.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing a move, the practical question isn't which culture is friendlier — both are, by their own internal logic. It's which currency you'd rather bank your career relationships in: time, protected and finite, or table conversation, unhurried and central. Move to Switzerland and your evenings stay yours, but plan to wait years for a colleague to become a friend. Move to Mexico and colleagues may feel like family within months, but that family meets at the office past dark.

Priya's honest read: if you need hard boundaries to function, Switzerland will hand them to you gift-wrapped. If you build trust through conversation rather than calendars, Mexico will reward you faster than your inbox expects — just don't schedule anything important before 4 p.m.
