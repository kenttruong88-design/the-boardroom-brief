**Spain Will Tell You Nothing About Your Salary. Norway Will Tell the Whole Country.**

**🇪🇸 Spain · 🇳🇴 Norway**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Spain, asking a colleague what they earn is roughly as welcome as asking about their confession. In Norway, you don't have to ask — the tax authority publishes everyone's taxable income once a year, searchable by name, for anyone curious enough to look. Both countries will tell you they value fairness. They have simply chosen opposite strategies for being coy about who actually collects it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇪🇸 Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm whether a quoted salary is paid in 12 installments or 14 ("pagas extra" in summer and December) before comparing offers | Assume the national average salary (€26,949, INE) describes your job — the median (€22,383) and the modal figure (around €14,586) are the more honest benchmarks |
| Ask about meal vouchers, transport vouchers, and private health cover — flexible retribution can add €1,500–3,500 a year in net value | Volunteer your previous salary unprompted — new EU rules bar employers from asking, but the old habit of disclosing it anyway dies slowly |
| Take the customary three to seven days to review a written offer rather than answering on the spot | Expect an annual merit raise — many sectors set pay through collective bargaining agreements (convenios colectivos) that apply the same percentage regardless of your performance review |
| Build rapport over a long lunch before naming a number | Lead with an ultimatum — Spanish negotiators call overplaying your hand the "dos pulpos" mistake, after the diner that outlasts the deal |
| Check whether the job ad now lists a salary band — mandatory disclosure arrives under the EU Pay Transparency Directive, due by June 2026 | Expect colleagues to compare notes on pay — Spain has no equivalent of Scandinavian openness, and asking directly reads as a faux pas |

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Quote and negotiate everything in gross, pre-tax terms — that is the universal convention | Expect a national minimum wage to set a floor — pay is set sector by sector through collective agreements, and only a few trades carry a legally binding minimum |
| Expect the larger raise to come from changing employers, not staying loyal — a theme that recurs across Norwegian salary forums | Assume the public tax list (skattelister) shows your colleague's base salary — it shows taxable income, which can include other jobs, investments, and benefits |
| Ask your union representative about the annual "lønnsoppgjør," the sector-wide wage norm that anchors most raises | Ignore your tax bracket when sizing up an offer — a flat ~22% plus a bracket tax up to roughly 39.5% combined (or a flat 25% PAYE rate for newcomers in year one) changes take-home meaningfully |
| Factor in feriepenger, holiday pay of roughly 10–12% of the prior year's earnings, paid out the following June | Picture Norwegian pay as flat across the board — mining and finance roles average close to double what hospitality work pays (SSB) |
| Push for a renegotiation after your probation period, typically around six months, or when your duties change (NITO) | Bring a Spanish-style charm offensive to the table — Norway's workplace culture rewards understatement and consensus over performance |

Spain's numbers are, by most European measures, modest. INE puts the average annual salary at €26,948.87 and the median at €22,383.11, with the single most common salary closer to €14,586 — a spread wide enough that "average" barely describes anyone (The Local Spain). OECD comparisons cited in the same reporting show salaries roughly 56% higher in Germany, 43% higher in France, and 8% higher in Italy, gaps that outpace the difference in living costs. Economist Ignacio De La Torre's verdict, as quoted there, is blunt: Spanish salaries are low "even when adjusted for the differential in the cost of living."

Part of the explanation is structural. Unemployment sat at 10.4% in early 2025 against an EU average of 6.1% (Eurostat), which keeps bargaining leverage with employers rather than candidates. Union membership, once above 40% under Suárez, had fallen to about 14% by 2023, thinning the collective muscle that used to push wages up sector-wide — fittingly, the Basque Country, which still pays the best, is also known as Spain's strike capital. What Spain is gaining instead is paperwork: the EU Pay Transparency Directive, due for transposition by June 2026, will force employers to publish salary ranges in job ads, ban questions about salary history, and require public reporting of gender pay gaps — currently estimated around 25%. A country famous for never discussing numbers at the table is about to be legally obligated to put them on the job posting, whether it wants to or not.

On the ground, compensation is still assembled from pieces rather than quoted as one clean figure. Contracts commonly split pay into 12 or 14 installments — the extra two "pagas extra" land in summer and December — and employers sweeten the base with meal vouchers, transport subsidies, and private health insurance, none of which show up in a headline number but all of which affect what you actually bank. A written offer is rarely the final word: the custom is to take several days to review it, counter politely over a shared meal rather than by email, and expect the figure to move by single or low double digits rather than dramatically.

Norway runs the opposite experiment. Average monthly earnings reached NOK 62,070 in 2025, up from NOK 59,370 the year before, though the median of NOK 55,800 is the more representative figure, since high earners in finance and mining pull the average upward (SSB, via Life in Norway). There is no national minimum wage; pay is set through sector collective agreements, with an annual negotiation round — the lønnsoppgjør — that produces a general percentage norm rather than individual raises. Individual negotiation still happens, mainly when you change jobs or finish probation, and NITO's own guidance states plainly that "it is normal practice to negotiate your own pay when you are offered a new job."

Then there is the skattelister, Norway's public ledger of who earned and owns what, a transparency mechanism built on the theory that visibility breeds trust rather than resentment. It is less revealing than it sounds — taxable income folds in investments and side earnings, and since lookups themselves are logged and visible to the person searched, casual browsing has quietly declined. Combined with a relatively flat ~22% income tax plus a bracket tax (up to roughly 39.5% total), and a simplified 25% flat PAYE rate for first-year foreign workers, the system trades secrecy for a kind of institutional honesty about the numbers, if not full clarity on where they come from.

The averages also hide who is left out of them. Women's average monthly pay sits at 87.9% of men's (SSB), a gap the agency attributes largely to occupation and seniority rather than identical roles paid differently. Immigrants average NOK 55,180 a month against NOK 64,150 for the rest of the workforce, a reminder that Norway's famous equality is a national average, not a guarantee extended evenly to every new arrival on day one. Newcomers typically discover this gap not in a spreadsheet but in the first few payslips, once the optimistic gross figure from the job offer meets actual rent in Oslo or Bergen.

The reversal worth noting: Norway's reputation is consensus and equality (Hofstede Insights scores it near the bottom globally on "masculinity," a measure of competitiveness, versus a middling score for Spain), yet it explicitly rewards individual hustle — job-hopping and post-probation renegotiation are the accepted paths to a raise. Spain, more hierarchical by the same metric and famously more combative at the negotiating table, often leaves the real wage-setting to sector-wide agreements that render individual haggling close to decorative. The country that talks tougher negotiates less; the country that talks softer negotiates harder.

Transparency flips too. Norway's skattelister looks like radical openness but delivers a muddied number, filtered through investments and side income. Spain's incoming pay-transparency law, by contrast, will require an actual salary band printed on the job advert — a level of candor the "closed" country may soon practice more literally than the "open" one.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/Norway — a university researcher roughly five years past their PhD, earning around 620,000 NOK, described themselves as "the king of underpaid," while another on a permanent post at 730,000 NOK still called it thin pay for the years of training behind it.*

> *r/Norway — more than one commenter warned that loyalty doesn't pay: the real raises come from switching employers, since internal annual negotiations tend to track an agreed sector-wide percentage rather than individual merit.*

> *Expat.com (Norway forum) — a family relocating from Canada watched their household income double on paper, yet still felt squeezed some months after downsizing from a house to a one-bedroom flat, concluding that "on minimum wage there is no chance you would save money."*

> *Expat.com (Oslo contractor thread) — a developer offered $15 an hour to relocate to Oslo for three months was told flatly by other posters that the rate was "extremely less" for the city, with estimates for fair pay landing closer to $30–40 an hour once local costs were factored in.*

> *SpainExpat.com forum — longtime members put a typical salary around €1,200 a month, roughly a third below the EU average, while one poster pushed back that lower rent and food costs can offset the gap, framing the real test as whether it adds up to "a nice life" rather than a direct euro-for-euro match.*

### Conclusion

If you are moving for the number alone, Norway wins on paper and mostly in practice — the median wage, the structured annual bargaining, and the job-switch premium all favor people willing to ask. If you are moving for the number relative to what you actually keep and spend, Spain's gap narrows considerably, provided you read the small print on pagas, vouchers, and the sector agreement that will quietly set your raise for you.

Negotiate hard in Norway, read the fine print in Spain, and in neither country mistake what's public for what's true.
