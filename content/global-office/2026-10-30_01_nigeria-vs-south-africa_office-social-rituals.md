**Lagos Will Make You a Friend by Lunchtime. Johannesburg Will Make You Wait for the Braai Invite.**

**🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇿🇦 South Africa**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Lagos, the office and your social calendar are the same document, and pretending otherwise is the first mistake a transferee makes. In Johannesburg or Cape Town, the social calendar exists, but it has a gatekeeper, a meat course, and an unspoken probation period before you're allowed to bring your own drinks to it. Both countries will tell you, cheerfully, that work and life aren't really separate. They mean two different things by it, and the distance between those two things is where a new arrival's first few months quietly go wrong.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the invitation to "step out" for food or drinks after a deal closes — it's where the next deal starts | Assume a dinner invitation is purely social; expect business to come up, often explicitly |
| Greet people properly before any ask, even a one-line email — skipping it reads as rude, not efficient | Rush straight into the agenda in a meeting without a few minutes of pleasantries first |
| Let someone cover a bill once in a while and return the favor later — it's relationship bookkeeping, not debt | Keep a running tally out loud; generosity is meant to look effortless |
| Expect senior colleagues to work very late and treat the office as a second home | Be the first to leave every day without building a reputation for working hard elsewhere first |
| Use the office food-run system — someone is always collecting orders — rather than eating alone at your desk | Skip the group lunch order repeatedly; it reads as standoffish |
| Dress sharply; Lagos corporate style skews formal and image-conscious even in heat | Default to the "humble founder" undress code you may have picked up elsewhere |

#### 🇿🇦 South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Go to the braai when invited, even if you don't drink or grill — attendance matters more than appetite | Decline twice in a row; a second decline is read as a quiet "no" to the relationship |
| Ask "should I bring something?" — the answer is usually meat, salad, or drinks ("bring and braai") | Show up empty-handed to a colleague's home event |
| Greet first, in English or a local-language hello if you know one, before getting to business | Open with the ask; skipping small talk reads as cold, not brisk |
| Clarify deadlines in exact times — "now now" is not "now," and "just now" can mean hours from now | Take time-related phrases literally and then be quietly furious when nothing happens "now" |
| Voice disagreement with a senior colleague privately, afterward | Contradict a manager in front of the room, even gently |
| Dress one notch more formal than you think you need to for the first week, then recalibrate | Assume "smart casual" culture at a tech startup extends automatically to a client-facing finance or legal office |

Nigerian corporate life, especially in Lagos, runs on a principle a departing American entrepreneur once summed up bluntly: there is no separation between work and pleasure, because everyone you meet socially is also someone you might eventually do business with. Hours are long by habit as much as by requirement — informal office lore has long celebrated the colleague who's still at their desk at nine at night, holding the keys, treating the building like home. The structure beneath the warmth is real, though: large Nigerian employers tend toward formal hierarchy, title matters, and a junior staffer learns early that deference to seniority is not optional even inside a culture that otherwise prizes charm and relationship-building.

That same warmth has a sharper edge that rarely makes the expat brochures. Zikoko, the Lagos culture outlet that functions as something close to a national diary of workplace grievances, has published first-person accounts of a chief financial officer sending a young on-air personality home over a crop top ("the mindset of people working in my office is archaic," she said afterward), and of junior law-firm staff being pulled from their own seats to serve food at a partner's party. These aren't universal, and Zikoko trades in the vivid case rather than the statistical average — but the pattern they point to, a workplace where informality and hierarchy coexist without much tension, tracks with what more sober guides describe.

South Africa runs on a gentler but no less binding social contract, and its totem is the braai — not merely a barbecue, but a ritualized, usually weekend-adjacent gathering that doubles as the unofficial HR department. Guides for new arrivals are explicit that accepting the invitation matters more than anything that happens once you're there, and that "bring and braai" — bring your own meat, bring your own drinks — is an expectation, not an offer. According to data compiled by Oxford's Our World in Data project, South Africans worked roughly 2,209 hours in 2017, about 42.5 hours a week, placing the country among the longer-working nations tracked; the International Labour Organization's 2018 figures found about 21% of the 16.4-million-strong labour force logging more than 49 hours a week, even though the Basic Conditions of Employment Act caps "normal" time at 45 hours. The gap between legal ceiling and lived practice is its own small cultural lesson.

### The Reckoning

The real divergence isn't warmth versus coldness — both cultures are, by most Northern European standards, intensely relational. It's where the warmth is staged. Nigerian office sociability happens mid-week, mid-hustle, often at the exact moment a deal is being discussed; it is loud, frequent, and largely improvised. South African office sociability happens on a schedule, usually on someone's actual property, around actual fire, and missing it without a good excuse costs you more socially than missing a dozen smaller, spontaneous Lagos outings would.

There's a further irony: the culture that looks more bureaucratic on paper — South Africa, with its codified maximum hours and sector-specific punctuality norms — is also the one where time itself is spoken of loosely, where "now now" requires a translator. The culture that looks looser — Nigeria, all hustle and informal charm — is the one where hierarchy is rigid enough that correcting a senior colleague publicly can end a career trajectory on the spot. Flexibility and rigidity aren't opposites here; they're just applied to different things.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Zikoko — Temi, who works in a Lagos office, said colleagues and even her male boss regularly comment on what she eats at her desk, including whether she looks "fat enough" after having a burger. "I am legit scared of eating at work," she said — a specific, ongoing discomfort that no onboarding document mentions.*

> *expat.com forum (Africa/South Africa board) — One contributor pushed back hard against the idyllic picture other posters had painted of South African life, writing that "the reality about SA is far away from this idyllic picture" and specifically flagging that evening outings many expats assume are safe and casual are not treated that way by people who actually live there.*

> *How We Made It In Africa — An American entrepreneur who spent years running a company in Lagos described the absence of a work/life line directly: "There is no separation between work and pleasure," he said, noting that dinners, clubs, and casual nights out all double as professional networking whether or not anyone announces it as such.*

> *Zikoko — A junior associate at a Lagos law firm, Adesuwa, recalled being pulled away from her own seat at a managing partner's party to serve senior guests, recounting her superior's reasoning as: "She asked how they could eat when her guests had not eaten." It was the moment she decided not to stay long at the firm.*

> *Intergate Immigration relocation guide (South Africa) — Its onboarding notes flag a specific linguistic trap for new hires: "now" can mean soon, "just now" can mean later today, and only "now now" means immediately — advice the guide frames as essential, not cultural trivia, for anyone agreeing to a deadline in a South African office.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two moves on social grounds alone, ask yourself which kind of obligation you'd rather carry: frequent, informal, slightly exhausting sociability that blurs into every weeknight in Lagos, or infrequent, highly structured sociability that quietly judges your two-time absence in Johannesburg. Neither is lighter duty — they just bill you on different schedules. The one universal: in both places, skipping the unofficial gathering is never read as neutral.

Here's what I'd actually tell a friend taking either job: in Nigeria, say yes to the dinner, because the dinner is the meeting. In South Africa, say yes to the braai, because the braai is the performance review nobody put on your calendar.
