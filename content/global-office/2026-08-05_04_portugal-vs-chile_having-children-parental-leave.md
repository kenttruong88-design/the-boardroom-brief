# Portugal Gives New Fathers a Month Off by Law. Chile Gives New Mothers Seven Months and Then Quietly Prices Them Out of a Job.

🇵🇹 Portugal · 🇨🇱 Chile

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Chile will hand a new mother up to thirty weeks of paid leave and a legal shield that makes her nearly impossible to fire for a year afterward. Portugal will hand a new father twenty-eight mandatory days off, one of only four OECD countries where that isn't optional generosity but statute. And yet Chile's fertility rate fell to 0.99 children per woman in 2025 — a record low, and the first time it has dropped below one — while Portugal's own parents spend years on creche waiting lists the government does not bother to measure. Two very different theories of state support for parenthood, both arriving, by different roads, at the same national shrug.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇹 Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register at your local centro de saúde as soon as pregnancy is confirmed — public prenatal care is free and thorough | Don't wait to apply for a creche vaga; waitlists run months to years and the government publishes no national wait-time data |
| Decide as a couple whether to take 120 days at 100% pay or 150 days at 80% before the baby arrives | Don't assume flexible or remote work will be offered after leave ends — it's negotiated, not standard |
| Take the full 28 mandatory paternity days — the first 7 must be consecutive and immediate | Don't skip pre-registering for a private creche as backup, even if you're counting on the public system |
| Line up grandparent or after-school nanny coverage early | Don't expect much sympathy for formula-feeding; attitudes remain fairly traditional outside the big cities |
| Ask about the additional 30 shared days available when both parents split leave | Don't assume long Portuguese work hours will bend around school pickup — plan the logistics yourself |

#### 🇨🇱 Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand the fuero maternal: you're protected from dismissal through pregnancy plus one year after postnatal leave ends | Don't expect much from paternity leave — fathers get just 5 paid days |
| Check whether your employer has 20+ female staff, which legally obligates them to fund a sala cuna | Don't assume smaller employers offer sala cuna funding — the threshold exempts most workplaces |
| Budget roughly CLP 504,000 a month for daycare nationally, more in wealthy Santiago comunas | Don't expect a sala cuna to accept a child with even a mild cold — mismatched sick-leave rules leave parents scrambling |
| Consider the part-time postnatal parental option — 18 weeks at 50% pay on a half-day schedule | Don't ignore the hiring-bias research tied to the sala-cuna mandate before negotiating your offer |
| Ask about extending postnatal leave to 18 weeks if the birth was preterm (before week 33) | Don't assume your career resumes where you left it — the earnings penalty for mothers persists a decade or more |

### Portugal

Portugal's parental leave architecture is, on paper, close to a model citizen. The OECD's "Paid Leave for Fathers" report notes that Portugal is one of only four member countries — alongside France, Italy, and Spain — where paternity leave is mandatory rather than merely offered, and at 28 days paid at 100% of salary, it is second only to Spain's 16 weeks among OECD fathers. Parents can additionally take 120 days of shared leave at full pay, or 150 days at 80%, and UNICEF's Innocenti research office ranks Portugal in the top five of 31 rich countries for family-friendly policy, one of just fifteen nations globally that guarantees all three of its benchmark protections: two years of free pre-primary education, paid breastfeeding breaks through the first six months, and substantial paid leave for both parents.

The gap between legislation and lived experience opens at the creche door. Portugal's subsidized public daycare program now covers roughly 126,000 children, but the spaces vanish fast, and — tellingly — the state does not publish national wait-list figures, leaving families to ring individual centres and hope. Parents who can't get a public spot are looking at private creche fees running €300 to €600 a month against a country where the minimum wage translates to roughly €665. The practical result, reported consistently across expat and local parenting communities, is that Portuguese mothers tend to return to paid work within four to six months regardless of what the leave calendar technically allows, because someone has to be home and the creche isn't ready yet.

### Chile

Chile's system runs on the opposite logic: legislate the mother's absence generously, then leave the economics of her return to sort themselves out. Since a 2011 reform, Chilean mothers are entitled to 18 weeks of maternity leave at 100% pay (capped around 66 UF a month) followed by an additional 12 weeks of postnatal parental leave — or 18 weeks at half pay on a part-time schedule — pushing total paid leave close to thirty weeks, among the longest in Latin America. Job protection, the fuero maternal, extends a full year past the end of postnatal leave. Fathers, by contrast, get five paid days, among the stingiest allotments the OECD's family database tracks.

The catch sits inside the sala cuna law itself: employers with 20 or more female employees must fund infant daycare, a threshold researchers and Chilean labour economists have long flagged as an incentive to simply hire fewer women of childbearing age, or to keep female headcount just under twenty. Layered onto that, a CEPR-cited study of Chilean labour markets found the birth of a first child triggers a sharp, durable decline in mothers' employment and earnings that persists more than a decade, while fathers' outcomes are statistically untouched; female labour informality jumps 38% after a first birth. None of this has bought demographic dividends: Chile's fertility rate crashed to 0.99 in 2025, a nearly 60% fall since 1993 and, per World Bank series, the first time it has dropped below the replacement of even a single child per woman.

### The Reckoning

Line the two systems up and the irony sharpens. Portugal, the more gender-neutral leave design on paper — mandatory paternity days, shared leave pools — nonetheless leans on an unspoken assumption that grandmothers and nannies will absorb whatever the creche system cannot. Chile, the more mother-centric leave design, generous almost to a fault in duration, has built a legal incentive structure that plausibly makes employers warier of hiring the women it's trying to protect. Both countries are, in their own ways, subsidizing the idea of parenthood more enthusiastically than the practice of it.

The most counterintuitive contrast is length versus consequence: Chile's leave is nearly twice as long as Portugal's, yet its motherhood earnings penalty is the more severe and better documented of the two. More paid weeks off did not translate into a softer landing back at work — if anything, the sala cuna mandate's employer-side calculus suggests the opposite. Portugal's shorter, shared leave, messier as its childcare access is, at least distributes the interruption across both parents by law, even if reality still routes most of the logistics to mothers.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

*One expat parent in Santiago, posting to an Expat.com forum thread on childcare, described her son's jardín infantil with something close to relief: full-day coverage from early morning to early evening, staff organized into clear tiers of qualification, and — unprompted — a level of physical warmth and affection from caregivers that surprised her after moving from a colder, more clinical daycare culture elsewhere.*

*A member of Lisbon's largest international parents' community, interviewed about family life in the city, put the childcare math bluntly: almost no public creche spots exist, the subsidized ones have year-long waits, and private options can run €500 or €600 a month in a city where wages don't stretch that far — a fact she said surprised newcomers who'd read Portugal's parental leave stats and assumed the rest would follow logically.*

*The same interviewee flagged a smaller but recurring cultural misread: Portuguese strangers, especially older generations, tend to touch and coo over newborns far more freely than northern European or American parents expect, which some new mothers find charming and others find mildly overwhelming in the first few overstimulated weeks.*

*A single American mother who relocated to Lisbon, recounting her experience in a long-form expat interview, described being asked for her ex-husband's written consent to open her own daughter's bank account and tax file — despite holding sole custody — a bureaucratic reflex that had nothing to do with the parental leave system and everything to do with how deeply certain assumptions about two-parent households are baked into Portuguese paperwork.*

*A Chilean mother quoted in a Radio Universidad de Chile feature on the postnatal parental leave reform said the return to work came faster than she was ready for, and that she wished she had known to negotiate a hybrid schedule before going back rather than after — along with a warning other parents in the piece echoed: daycare centres routinely refuse children with even minor cold symptoms, while employers just as routinely decline to grant paid leave for those same minor illnesses, leaving working parents to improvise the gap themselves.*

### Conclusion

If the choice is between systems, it is less "which country loves parents more" than "which failure mode you'd rather manage." Portugal will hand your family a matter of legal principle — mandatory paternity leave, shared days, breastfeeding protections — and then leave you to hunt for a creche spot largely on your own initiative and your own euros. Chile will hand a mother nearly seven months of paid absence and airtight job protection, then let the market quietly factor that generosity into who gets hired in the first place. Neither government has figured out how to make having a child compatible with keeping a career on the same terms it was on before, and both countries' collapsing or stagnating birth rates suggest their citizens have noticed. The honest advice for new parents in either country is the same: read the labour code for what it promises, then talk to someone who has actually tried to use it.
