# Agbada or Pinstripes: What Nigeria and Belgium Actually Expect You to Wear to Work

**🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇧🇪 Belgium**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Lagos, a ministry can run on a diesel generator and institutional faith, yet a civil servant showing a bare knee will trigger an actual memo. In Brussels, you can greet a minister in flawless French and still be privately filed as unreliable for wearing brown shoes to a Monday meeting. Nigeria and Belgium disagree about almost everything administrative - currency stability, traffic laws, the correct number of regional governments - but they agree, with unexpected intensity, that what you wear to the office is a referendum on your character. The only disagreement is which direction the uniform points: outward, toward visible success, or inward, toward disciplined restraint.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep one well-tailored native outfit (agbada or kaftan) ready for Fridays and events | Assume "casual Friday" means jeans everywhere - banks and government stay formal |
| Dress slightly more formal than you think necessary on day one | Pack your entire "good" wardrobe from home - heat, dust, and traffic ruin it fast |
| Use a local tailor; fit reads as more professional than imported labels | Wear visible tattoos or piercings without first checking your employer's stance |
| Polish your shoes - scuffed footwear is noticed before anything else | Wear shorts, flip-flops, or workout clothes outside your own compound |
| Watch how the person two ranks above you dresses, and match it | Underestimate the social cost of being "the badly dressed one" in the room |

#### 🇧🇪 Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to a dark suit or conservative dress for any client-facing meeting | Treat casualness as a neutral choice - here it reads as unreliable, even rude |
| Keep shoes, bags, and accessories well-kept but understated | Wear flashy, branded, or attention-seeking pieces to signal success |
| Expect Wallonia to run slightly more formal than Flanders | Assume one regional dress code applies everywhere in the country |
| Arrive early and prepared - punctuality is part of the "outfit" | Dress for "the Eurovision Song Contest" when the room expects the European Parliament |
| Let informality develop gradually, on your colleagues' timeline | Expect the office to loosen up quickly just because you have |

In Nigeria, formality is not the default condition of the economy - it is a deliberately maintained island inside one. According to Nigeria's National Bureau of Statistics and the Nigerian Economic Summit Group, roughly 93 percent of the workforce sits in informal employment, and formal wage jobs account for under 8 percent of total employment nationally. Which makes the intensity of dress-code enforcement inside that small formal sliver - banks, government ministries, law firms - almost poignant: the fewer the formal jobs, the more fiercely their outward signals are policed. Gallup's State of the Global Workplace data adds a less flattering footnote, finding Nigerian employees reporting daily stress at 55 percent, well above both the Sub-Saharan African and global averages, even as engagement sits near 17 percent. A sharply pressed shirt, in other words, is frequently doing emotional labor that has nothing to do with the shirt.

What softens this is Friday, which in Lagos functions as a sanctioned pressure valve. Conservative employers who enforce suits Monday through Thursday routinely encourage traditional attire - the agbada, the kaftan, a gele for the women in the office - heading into the weekend, according to local commentary on Lagos office culture. The effect is a style of formality that still permits, even celebrates, visible personal success: a well-cut native outfit signals arrival in a way a Western suit rarely does locally. One Nigerian career-advice column counsels newcomers to default to full business formal "until you observe your workplace's actual standards," warning that banking, law, and government remain the strictest sectors while tech and creative firms have loosened considerably. Regional variation compounds this: wage employment, the Nigerian statistics data shows, runs at roughly 34 percent in Lagos and 27 percent in the Federal Capital Territory, but falls below 5 percent in several northern states - meaning the entire conversation about "office dress codes" is really a conversation about a handful of cities, not a national condition. There is also a harder edge to the enforcement. Dress-code disputes occasionally escalate into genuine controversy, as when a woman was turned away from an immigration office over a knee-length hemline, or when port authority staff were issued detailed bans on tight jeans and bare midriffs - incidents Nigerian commentators have repeatedly flagged as policing of women's bodies dressed up as professionalism.

Belgium runs the same instinct toward formality through an almost opposite mechanism. Business guides describe Belgian offices as rewarding "understated professionalism rather than flashy self-presentation" - dark suits, polished but plain shoes, minimal jewelry, and a near-total absence of visible status display. The OECD/AIAS ICTWSS database, via Eurofound, puts Belgian trade union density around 49 to 54 percent over the past decade, among the highest in Western Europe - a workforce accustomed to institutional structure translating fairly directly into dress that is itself institutional. Casualness, one guide notes bluntly, "is equated with being unreliable or even rude." Meetings are formal, structured, and punctual, and a late arrival damages a reputation for reliability more than a bad suit would.

That rule-bound instinct is not evenly distributed. Flemish colleagues tend toward brisk informality and minimal small talk once rapport is established, while Walloon colleagues keep more ceremony around job titles and the size of one's office. Brussels complicates things further by hosting the European Parliament and Commission, where decades of firsthand accounts - menswear forums among them - describe dress as conservative to the point of dullness: grey and navy suits, mostly black shoes, junior staff dressing more cautiously than senior ones. It is a culture dressing for institutional permanence, not personal flair, which tracks with Hofstede Insights' cultural data: Belgium scores 94 on uncertainty avoidance, among the highest in the world, versus Nigeria's considerably more moderate 55, while Belgium's individualism score of 75 sits well above Nigeria's 30. Translated out of the jargon: Belgians dress to minimize ambiguity and institutional risk; Nigerians dress, in part, to be individually read and ranked. Business etiquette guides covering Belgium are near-unanimous on one further detail worth packing for: genuinely casual dress - sweatpants, flip-flops, anything that reads as leisurewear - is read not merely as unprofessional but as a minor social insult, a category of offense that barely registers the same way in Lagos, where heat and traffic routinely soften what counts as acceptable daytime wear outside the office itself.

Here is the irony neither country would volunteer: Nigeria, an economy running overwhelmingly on informal, undocumented hustle, enforces some of the most visually legible status hierarchies in global business dress - the suit, the agbada, and the shoe all working as a résumé you wear. Belgium, a wealthy, heavily unionized, rule-dense economy, uses dress to erase status signals almost entirely, on the theory that visible ambition is itself a faux pas. One culture dresses up to be noticed; the other dresses down to not be. A Nigerian manager arriving in Brussels in his sharpest agbada risks reading as theatrical. A Belgian executive arriving in Lagos in a tasteful, deliberately unremarkable grey suit risks reading as somebody who did not try. Both countries would insist, in the same dry tone, that they are not being superficial - that the clothing is simply a proxy for competence, trust, or seriousness - and both would be describing the same anxiety wearing a different uniform.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Talesmag, Abuja post report - one longtime resident advised against bringing nice clothing at all, saying the heat and dust make it "not a place to bring nice clothing," and that she gets by on three pairs of shoes and inexpensive pieces she gives away before leaving.*

> *Talesmag, Lagos post report - a spouse who occasionally tried wearing Nigerian attire says his wife jokes that he looks like he is dressed for a circus, so the outfit mostly stays in the closet while he defaults to light cotton clothing for the heat.*

> *StyleForum.net, European Parliament wardrobe thread - a contributor advising a prospective intern put it plainly: dress for the European Parliament, not for the Eurovision Song Contest, and expect junior staff to dress more conservatively than senior ones.*

> *Olusegun Osifuye's Substack, "A Day in My Life as an African Living in Africa" - the writer notes that even strict Lagos employers such as banks and government offices will actively encourage traditional wear on Fridays, treating it as a legitimate alternative to Western formalwear rather than a relaxation of standards.*

> *Expat Exchange, Belgium forum - one expat described the adjustment bluntly, writing that Belgian culture is "quite formal" and that newcomers often find the formality itself, more than any single rule, the hardest thing to get used to.*

### Conclusion

The practical decision point is this: in Nigeria, dress upward and visibly, because formality there is a performance of individual arrival inside an informal economy, and looking the part matters as much as any credential. In Belgium, dress downward and quietly, because formality there is a performance of institutional trust inside a densely regulated one, and looking too deliberate reads as a liability rather than an asset. Neither approach is more "professional" than the other; they are simply answering different anxieties - one about status, the other about risk. Budget for a local tailor in Lagos and a very boring dry cleaner in Brussels, and you will have solved most of the actual problem before your first week of meetings even starts.

If a friend asked which was the easier wardrobe to pack for: Belgium, every time - a handful of dark, boring, well-made items will serve you for years, whereas Lagos will quietly test your tailor, your shoe polish, and your tolerance for being evaluated on sight before you have said a word. Pack accordingly, and under-pack your assumptions about which country is the "formal" one - on the numbers, you would be wrong either way you guessed.
