# Iceland Will Let You Wear Yesterday's Sneakers to a Pitch Meeting. Senegal Will Judge You for Yesterday's Shirt.

**🇮🇸 Iceland · 🇸🇳 Senegal**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two small, proud, famously well-dressed nations, two completely opposite theories of what dressing well at work actually means. Iceland's version is "look like you didn't try, several thousand krónur of effort later." Senegal's is "look like you tried, because not trying would be rude." Pack accordingly, and pack more than you think either place needs.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇸 Iceland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to jeans, a dress shirt, and a jacket for most office settings — tie strictly optional. | Assume this casualness extends to banking, finance, or government roles, where suits and ties remain standard. |
| Ask directly if you're unsure what a specific meeting calls for — Icelanders expect and welcome the question. | Overdress dramatically for a first meeting outside the formal sectors; it can read as trying too hard. |
| Invest in good quality basics; Icelanders are famously fashion-conscious even when dressed down. | Be surprised by worn sneakers on otherwise sharply dressed colleagues — footwear gets a pass that clothing doesn't. |
| Keep a slightly smarter option on hand for client-facing or cross-border meetings, just in case. | Assume "casual" means low-effort; Icelandic casual is curated, not careless. |

#### 🇸🇳 Senegal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a shirt and trousers at minimum for the office — suit and tie for genuinely formal settings. | Wear shorts, short skirts, or low necklines in professional or non-tourist settings. |
| Keep a jacket within reach even on hot days; appearance signals respect, and Dakar takes that seriously. | Let heat talk you out of dressing properly — locals manage the same climate while staying sharp. |
| Treat Fridays as the most dressed-up day of the week, when colleagues often wear their best boubous. | Wear traditional dress (boubou, grand kaftan) casually or carelessly — do it elegantly, or not at all. |
| Pack for embassy functions, national days, and other formal occasions — they come up more than expected. | Assume "business casual" from home translates directly; Senegalese formal is more consistently formal. |

**Iceland**

Iceland's office dress code runs on a paradox familiar to anyone who's spent time in Reykjavik's startup scene: the baseline is casual, but the casual is expensive-looking. Business etiquette guides note that "most Icelanders dress smartly" even in informal settings, and that visiting professionals are generally expected to lean formal by default, adjusting down once they've gauged the room. The practical reality, per locals describing the capital's business culture, lands somewhere more specific: jeans, a dress shirt, and a jacket cover the overwhelming majority of professional situations, tie fully optional — with the notable exception of banking, finance, and government, where traditional formality still holds. One telling detail from people who actually work there: visibly worn sneakers are an accepted, almost default footwear choice, even alongside an otherwise put-together outfit — a small inconsistency that sums up the country's whole approach to professional appearance, which values looking deliberate far more than it values looking conventional.

The underlying logic tracks with Iceland's broader workplace culture, which — like its Nordic neighbors — leans flat and low-ceremony; Iceland doesn't appear as an individually scored country in Hofstede's original cultural-dimensions study, but independent academic research applying the same methodology (VSM94) places it squarely alongside the rest of the low-power-distance, high-individualism Nordic cluster. A workplace that doesn't lean on hierarchy for authority has correspondingly less use for clothing as a hierarchy signal.

**Senegal**

Senegal runs the opposite calculation. Guides written for relocating professionals describe Senegalese workplaces as combining "a strong formal hierarchy" with "marked relational warmth" — and dress code is where both show up at once. Professional attire for men typically means a shirt and trousers at minimum, suit and tie for genuinely formal environments; for women, a suit, dress, or trouser-blouse ensemble, consistently on the conservative side. Appearance isn't treated as a neutral personal choice. Being "clean, well-dressed, and coordinated is perceived as a sign of respect for oneself and others" — a framing that turns getting dressed for work into a small daily act of social courtesy rather than a matter of individual preference.

Fridays carry particular weight: multiple firsthand accounts from Dakar describe the day as the most elaborately dressed of the week, with colleagues turning up in their finest boubous (the flowing traditional robe, often in richly patterned bazin fabric) rather than treating it as a casual-Friday wind-down. Formal Western-style events — embassy functions, national day celebrations, diplomatic and corporate functions — come up with enough regularity that packing light on formalwear is a genuine mistake. Foreign workers do get some latitude on strict modesty norms outside professional and conservative neighborhoods, but the baseline expectation, in and out of the office, sits well above what "business casual" means in most Western contexts.

**The Reckoning**

The irony here isn't that one country is formal and the other isn't — both are, by most global standards, quite put-together. It's that the two countries have inverted what formality is for. In Iceland, dressing well is largely a personal aesthetic statement in a culture that doesn't ask clothing to communicate rank; the country simply isn't in Hofstede's original dataset, but its Nordic-cluster-consistent, low-hierarchy social structure explains why a banker needs a suit and a software engineer very much does not. In Senegal, dressing well is a social signal of respect owed to other people, embedded in a culture that — per the few comparative academic sources that do cover West Africa — combines real hierarchy consciousness with intense relational warmth, where showing up underdressed doesn't just look unprofessional, it reads as a small discourtesy to everyone else in the room.

For a relocating professional, the trap runs in both directions: overdressing in Iceland outside banking and government can look try-hard, while underdressing in Senegal — even just once, even just a slightly wrinkled shirt — registers as something closer to a personal slight than a fashion miss.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Tales Mag Real Post Report, Dakar — A contributor described the baseline office expectation as "a suit or a jacket," with many men keeping a spare jacket on hand throughout the day, while noting that Fridays are the exception that proves the rule: "everyone is dressed to the nines with their nicest boubous."*

> *Tripadvisor Reykjavik forum — A local responding to a visiting professional's dress-code question summed up the city's baseline as "jeans and jacket w. a dress shirt will do the trick for the majority of businesses, tie optional" — while flagging banking, finance, and government as the clear exceptions that still expect a full suit.*

> *jarniascyril.com (expat guide) — A guide for newcomers to Senegal warned against assuming a Western "business casual" wardrobe would translate directly, noting that locally, "being clean, well-dressed, and coordinated is perceived as a sign of respect for oneself and others," and that even occasional traditional dress needs to be worn elegantly to read as genuine cultural integration rather than costume.*

### Conclusion

If you're packing for Iceland, bring fewer suits than you think you need and better casual basics than you think you need — the country will judge your taste, not your formality level, outside a short list of conservative industries. If you're packing for Senegal, bring more than you think you need, full stop, and don't let the heat talk you out of a jacket; looking put-together there isn't vanity, it's manners.

Either way, watch what people actually wear on your first Friday in the office. In Iceland, it'll tell you how casual you're really allowed to be. In Senegal, it'll tell you how much effort everyone else is about to make — and you'll want to match it.
