# Lithuania Eats the Dumpling, Ireland Eats the Vegetables, and Both Will Feed You Anyway

🇱🇹 Lithuania · 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Eurostat survey of EU eating habits, run in 2017, produced one of the more useful relocation statistics available: 84 percent of people in Ireland ate vegetables daily, joint highest in the Union, while in Lithuania the figure was 45 percent, among the lowest five. This is old data and diets move slowly, but it frames the practical question for anyone taking a job in either country. Food is where a new colleague either joins the group or is quietly left at their desk. Here is what to expect at the table in each.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Lithuania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Visit a covered market such as Halės turgus in Vilnius on a Saturday morning, with cash | Expect every traditional stall to take cards |
| Try cepelinai, kugelis and cold beet soup when colleagues offer | Decline the first dish as if it were a negotiating position |
| Say į sveikatą before drinking and wait to be seated | Start eating or drinking before the host does |
| Treat bread with respect | Leave it lying about or waste it |
| Join the cake, birthday and Friday-brunch traditions at work | Eat lunch alone at your desk every day and wonder about integration |

### Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn that tea can mean the evening meal, not a drink | Turn up at six for a cup and be surprised by a roast |
| Budget for restaurants, since dining out is expensive | Assume a casual dinner out is a casual expense |
| Try the local butcher, fishmonger and small shops | Rely entirely on pre-made or frozen meals |
| Take the pub invitation, even if you drink little | Expect an immediate dinner invitation in return |
| Take your lunch break away from the screen | Treat the desk sandwich as a national policy |

## Lithuania: Hearty, Hospitable and Short on Greens

Lithuanian cooking leans on potato, rye bread, smoked fish and sausage, and the staples a newcomer will meet are cepelinai, kugelis and the famous cold beet soup, šaltibarščiai. An expat food guide describes the cooking as lightly spiced and simple, and says a bite is traditionally kept on the table for unexpected visitors as a sign of hospitality. Hospitality is real. It does not quite translate into vegetables.

According to Statistics Lithuania, in 2019 47 percent of people ate fruit daily and 54 percent ate vegetables daily, with an average of 4.3 servings of fruit and vegetables a day against the WHO recommendation of at least five. Eurostat's 2017 survey put Lithuania at 37 percent for daily fruit, joint second lowest in the EU, though that figure is old and the two surveys are not strictly comparable. The newer picture is not flattering either. LRT reported in December 2024 that Lithuania ranks fifth in Europe for the share of overweight adults, citing a Eurostat poll, with a Vilnius University professor saying Lithuanians like fatty food and eat too little fibre, and a dietitian arguing that supermarkets are dominated by frozen and canned goods. Cost is part of it, since the dietitian suggested that many people simply cannot afford better food. Statistics Lithuania agrees that money and schooling matter: 65 percent of the highest-income group ate vegetables daily, and 61 percent of people with high educational attainment did, with women eating fruit and vegetables more often than men. A healthy diet, in short, correlates with a good salary, which is a useful thing to know before negotiating one.

Price also shapes how Lithuanians eat out. An LRT report from April 2025 found that 34 percent of respondents had not eaten out in the previous six months, though the survey organisation was not named, while restaurant owners said margins were close to zero. Hofstede Insights can quantify national differences in values, but live scores could not be retrieved here, so none are offered. At the workplace, the evidence is warmer: one employer blog describes company traditions such as shared monthly brunches, Pastry Fridays, an annual Cake Day for charity, and colleagues eager to introduce newcomers to cold beet soup. The lunch table is a more reliable integration tool than the onboarding deck, and rather cheaper than the offsite.

## Ireland: Greener, Pricier and Oddly Hurried

Ireland's meal structure comes with a vocabulary test. According to Remitly's guide, breakfast runs from about seven to ten, lunch from noon to two, and dinner from six to nine, with seven common. Many Irish people, especially older and rural ones, call the evening meal tea, so an invitation to tea may mean a full dinner. In the countryside the main meal may still happen at midday. Sunday lunch, usually a roast, remains the family fixture.

On vegetables, Ireland comes out ahead of its Baltic counterpart on the evidence available, although the Eurostat survey is from 2017. Lunch is where the picture dims. An RTE essay in 2024 cites a survey finding that 22 percent of Irish workers eat lunch at their desk during the week, and argues that workers treat walking and waiting for food as wasted time. Its other figures are British, so the case is suggestive rather than proven. For context, a 2004 Eurest survey reported by The Irish Times found an average lunch break of 35 minutes in the Republic and sandwiches as the top choice for two in three workers. That is two decades old, and the sandwich appears to have outlasted several economic cycles.

Expat.com forum users add that home cooking carries much of the load: one resident bakes bread and cooks from scratch, noting that local food seemed to contain fewer preservatives than American food, and another grows fruit and vegetables and prefers hosting friends to a night out. The pattern suggests that the Irish table is a domestic institution that visitors reach by invitation. Dining out is another matter. Expat.com puts a three-course meal at a mid-range Dublin restaurant at roughly 70 to 100 euros for two, which concentrates the mind. The traditional repertoire is hearty, with Irish stew described as the national dish, bacon and cabbage as a classic and a full Irish breakfast of sausages, rashers, eggs, mushrooms and tomatoes, and the city also offers fine dining such as the two-Michelin-starred Chapter One. The reward for paying is a lively scene, with brunch and craft beer both prominent, and a food culture that rewards visiting the local butcher.

## The Reckoning

On the evidence, the two countries fail in complementary ways. Lithuania has food culture in abundance, with the hospitality, the market and the workplace cake, and a diet that its own experts describe as heavy and short on fresh produce. Ireland has the better vegetable record, a vocabulary that confuses visitors, and a working lunch that often seems to happen at the keyboard. One country is generous with the table and the other is generous with the greens.

For an employee, the practical difference is social. In Lithuania, food is the entry point: accept the offer, try the soup, and attend the cake. In Ireland, the pub and the shared cup of tea do the work, and the dinner invitation may take longer to arrive. Neither is a hardship, but they point toward different budgets. Lithuania will cost you in cardiovascular goodwill. Ireland will cost you in euros.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum, a Canadian in Ireland, paraphrased: buys from local butchers, fishmongers and small shops rather than relying on pre-made or frozen meals, found portions smaller, and is warmly greeted at the local pub*

> *Expat.com forum, a former Galway resident, paraphrased: Irish people rarely accepted or returned dinner invitations, which made shared meals with locals hard to arrange, though a pint and traditional music helped start conversations*

> *Expat food guide testimonial, paraphrased: Saturday visits to the Halės market in Vilnius have become a ritual, and a stallholder now recognises the visitor*

> *Expat food guide testimonial, paraphrased: Kaunas market traders let the writer sample smoked cheeses, which felt much warmer than the supermarket*

> *WorkInLithuania.com employer blog, paraphrased: colleagues are keen to introduce newcomers to cold beet soup, and new joiners often get an informal lunch or bar snack as a welcome*

## Conclusion

If eating well is a priority, Ireland has the better vegetable record and a more expensive menu, while Lithuania has the cheaper markets, the better-stocked hospitality and a population whose experts keep asking for more greens. Either way, plan your own groceries, find the nearest market or butcher in your first week, and accept the first invitation to lunch you receive.

The practical advice is to bring a fork to both countries and an open diary. In Lithuania the dumpling will find you. In Ireland the invitation might, eventually, and it will probably be for tea.
