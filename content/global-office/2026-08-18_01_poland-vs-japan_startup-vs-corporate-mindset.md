# So You Want to Skip the Hierarchy: A Foreigner's Guide to Poland's "Corpo" World and Japan's Startup Escape Hatch

**🇵🇱 Poland · 🇯🇵 Japan**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Poland invented a word — "korpo," or "corpo" — specifically to describe the experience of working somewhere that isn't a startup, and it is not a compliment. Japan, meanwhile, has spent decades building an employment system so devoted to permanence that "startup" was, until recently, a mildly embarrassing career choice, the kind of thing you explained to your parents rather than announced to them. Anyone moving between these two countries for work is not choosing between two flavors of office life. They are choosing between two entirely different theories of what a career is for.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the difference between a "korpo" (corporate) job and a "startup" job before your first interview — Poles will assume you know which world you're entering | Assume "startup" means informal in every sense — Polish startups still expect punctuality and clear deliverables |
| Expect English-heavy jargon even in Polish-language meetings — terms like "deadline," "feedback," and "onboarding" are used natively | Be surprised when a "flat" startup hierarchy still has a very clear decision-maker in the room |
| Negotiate your contract type carefully — B2B contracts (common for IT roles) mean you're technically self-employed, with different tax and benefit implications than a standard employment contract | Assume a lower base salary at a startup will be offset by meaningful equity — Polish startup equity culture is still maturing |
| Take advantage of Warsaw, Kraków, Wrocław, and Gdańsk's genuinely distinct startup scenes if you're choosing where to live | Expect the same career mobility norms as Western Europe — many mid-sized Polish firms still prize tenure |
| Ask directly about actual working hours — posted hours and real hours can diverge quietly | Badmouth a previous "corpo" employer in an interview — it reads as inexperience, not honesty |

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand that a "shinsotsu" (new graduate) hire and a mid-career "chuto saiyo" hire are treated as genuinely different tracks, even at the same company | Expect a startup's flatter structure to mean no hierarchy at all — someone is still very much in charge |
| Practice "ho-ren-so" — frequent, proactive reporting and check-ins — even at fast-moving startups; the habit runs deeper than any org chart | Assume long hours are a traditional-corporate-only problem — sprint culture at Japanese startups can be just as demanding |
| Ask about promotion timelines explicitly at traditional firms — seniority-based advancement is still real, if weakening | Job-hop the way you might in the US or Poland without understanding it can still raise eyebrows in more conservative sectors |
| Consider a "gaishikei" (foreign-capital company) as a genuine middle path between salaryman tradition and startup chaos | Expect Tokyo's new four-day workweek pilot for metro government staff to reflect private-sector norms yet |
| Budget serious time for the job search if you don't speak Japanese — expect to apply broadly and often | Assume startup casualness extends to how you address seniors — respect language persists even in jeans |

### Poland: The Word for "Not a Startup" Is Also an Insult

Poland's IT and business-services sector has, in a single generation, become one of the most consequential in Central Europe — Warsaw, Kraków, Wrocław, and Gdańsk each host distinct clusters of homegrown startups sitting uneasily next to R&D outposts for Google, Microsoft, and Samsung. But the culture that dominates the popular imagination isn't the scrappy founder story. It's "korpo" — a term Poles use, often with an eye-roll, to describe the world of open-plan offices, English-language jargon bolted onto Polish sentences, and the particular flavor of bureaucratic caution that comes from working inside a large multinational's satellite office. According to Careers in Poland, the corporate vocabulary is so entrenched that fluency in workplace English idioms functions almost as a status marker independent of actual English fluency.

The country's startup scene, by contrast, is smaller and considerably more volatile. VC investment in Polish startups reached roughly PLN 3.4 billion (about EUR 800 million) across some 180 deals in 2025, according to Polish VC market data — respectable, but a fraction of the scale seen in Western Europe, and the country counts just one unicorn to date per Tracxn's tracking. What complicates the calculus for anyone weighing a move is that the old assumption — that engineers accept lower pay at startups in exchange for culture and equity — has weakened considerably now that Google, Microsoft, and Samsung are competing for the same graduates with corporate-scale compensation. The trade-off that once defined the choice is quietly disappearing.

### Japan: Lifetime Employment Is Declining, Slowly, on Its Own Terms

Japan's traditional corporate model still runs on an annual ritual: batches of shinsotsu, or new university graduates, are hired simultaneously each April, trained as a cohort, and — historically — retained until mandatory retirement. This isn't nostalgia; it remains the operating system for a meaningful share of large Japanese employers, built around seniority-based promotion and consensus-driven decision-making that can make even routine approvals feel glacial to newcomers. Average annual working hours in Japan have fallen to roughly 1,633 per worker as of the most recent OECD-adjacent figures, well below the OECD average of 1,736 — a genuine, if under-advertised, improvement from the overwork crises of past decades.

Japan's startup sector, meanwhile, is not the scrappy underdog its Polish counterpart is. Japanese startups have collectively raised around $105 billion in venture capital and private equity, per Tracxn, with $4.25 billion raised across 376 rounds in the first half of 2026 alone — a 129.6% jump year over year — and the country now counts ten unicorns. Startups here genuinely do operate differently: flatter structures, more English in the room, daily scrums, and a hiring calendar that doesn't wait for April. But the deeper cultural habits — the instinct toward frequent reporting, the discomfort with standing out, the layered forms of respect language — tend to persist even inside companies that look, on an org chart, nothing like Toyota.

### The Reckoning

Here is the counterintuitive part: Poland's "startup vs. corporate" divide is mostly about money and structure, while Japan's is mostly about time horizon and social contract. A Polish engineer choosing between korpo and startup is largely negotiating salary stability against equity risk — a fairly legible, Western-style trade-off. A Japanese professional making the same choice is negotiating something closer to identity: whether their career will be organized around a single, patient institutional relationship, or around a faster, more individualized trajectory that still doesn't fully escape the cultural gravity of hierarchy and reporting norms.

The other irony is who ends up more "traditional" in practice. Hofstede Insights scores Japan at 92 on uncertainty avoidance against Poland's 93 — both cultures are, structurally, deeply uncomfortable with ambiguity, which is part of why "flat" startups in both countries still tend to have unmistakable chains of command. The flattening is real, but it's cosmetic before it's structural, in both directions.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — A finance professional comparing a large-bank offer against a startup role, both around $220–235K total comp, was told by another Blind user not to expect the roles to be comparable at all: a startup demands more flexibility and fewer guardrails, and if you're taking the pay cut in structure, you should be taking more in equity to compensate.*

> *Quora — Someone who had worked at both a gaishikei (foreign-capital company) and a traditional Japanese corporation described the gaishikei environment as meaningfully more merit-based, with faster career mobility and more international exposure — but warned that the trade-off shows up in job security, which traditional firms still protect much more fiercely.*

> *Quora — A foreigner who tried to found a startup in Japan described the practical grind more than the cultural one: expect to send out 100+ applications or pitches before traction, and expect every regulatory filing and much of the paperwork to exist only in Japanese, regardless of how international your team feels day to day.*

> *Blind — Someone considering a move to a Warsaw JPMorgan Chase office found the actual, lived answer to "what's the culture like" surprisingly hard to get — multiple posts asking the same question went largely unanswered beyond requests for total comp and years of experience, suggesting corpo culture in Poland is discussed more in salary terms than in day-to-day texture.*

> *Careers in Poland — An overview of Polish corporate slang noted that even in fully Polish-speaking meetings, English terms like "deadline," "feedback," and "onboarding" are treated as load-bearing vocabulary — using the Polish equivalents instead can read as slightly out of step with the office.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two moves, ask yourself what kind of risk you're actually comfortable with. In Poland, the risk is financial and reversible — a startup role that doesn't work out is a resume line, and the corpo world will generally take you back. In Japan, the risk is more structural and slower to unwind — traditional firms still reward patience in ways startups can't replicate, and startup culture, however flat it looks, hasn't fully shed the deeper reporting instincts and hierarchy of the wider society around it.

My honest read, over a drink: in Poland, pick based on the paycheck, because the culture gap is smaller than people pretend. In Japan, pick based on how much institutional patience you actually have, because that gap is real, and no amount of casual Friday will close it.
