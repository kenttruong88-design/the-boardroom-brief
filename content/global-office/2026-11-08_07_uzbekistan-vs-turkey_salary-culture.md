# Two Currencies, One Universal Rule: Never Say the Number First

🇺🇿 Uzbekistan · 🇹🇷 Turkey

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Tashkent, the average worker takes home the equivalent of about $490 a month and watches it rise by double digits every year, which sounds thrilling until you remember that "double digits" of very little is still very little. In Istanbul, the minimum wage has been raised so many times in recent years that payroll departments now budget for inflation the way other countries budget for rent. Neither country publishes its salary bands. Both expect you to simply know what you're worth, and then to be wrong about it.

## Do's & Don'ts: Uzbekistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask local colleagues, discreetly, what "market rate" actually means in soums, not percentages | Don't assume a foreign employer's stated salary already accounts for Tashkent's rapidly rising cost of living |
| Negotiate the full package — housing, transport, and bonus structure — since base pay is often deliberately modest | Don't bring up pay in a group setting; it reads as either boastful or accusatory |
| Expect sector to matter enormously: IT and finance pay well above the national average, teaching and healthcare well below it | Don't expect a published pay scale; almost nothing is publicly benchmarked |
| Treat a verbal raise promise as provisional until it appears on a payslip | Don't compare your salary to Tashkent's capital-city averages if you're working in a regional city — the gap is real and widening |
| Budget for a wage that rises annually, often by double digits, as part of the deal | Don't assume rapid headline wage growth means rapid real-income growth; regional and sectoral gaps are widening at the same time |

## Do's & Don'ts: Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Track the minimum wage announcement every December — it resets the entire pay conversation nationwide | Don't sign a lira-denominated contract without asking how (or whether) it adjusts for inflation |
| Negotiate hard and early; Turkish hiring managers broadly expect a counter-offer | Don't assume your quoted salary is fixed once you've accepted; renegotiation after a few months is a normal, almost expected, ritual |
| Ask peers in your field what a "good" salary looks like this specific year, not last year | Don't rely on published numbers for long — they age out within months in a high-inflation economy |
| Use sites like Levels.fyi or Paylab for a rough technical-sector benchmark before any interview | Don't treat silence on pay during interviews as a bad sign; many employers wait to see if you ask first |
| Factor severance and overtime rules into total compensation, not just the headline figure | Don't be surprised if your "annual salary" conversation has to happen again before the year is out |

## Uzbekistan: Rising Numbers, Rising Questions

The headline looks good: Uzbekistan's average monthly wage climbed roughly 17–19% year-on-year through 2025 and into 2026, reaching about UZS 6.4 million nationally, according to reporting from kun.uz drawing on the State Statistics Committee's own figures. In Tashkent specifically, the average nears UZS 12 million — nearly double the national figure, which tells you most of what you need to know about where the growth is actually concentrated. Finance and IT remain the standout sectors, while teachers and doctors, per the same reporting, continue to earn below the national average even as the headline number rises around them.

What the growth figures understate is distribution. One kun.uz analysis from mid-2026 found that roughly two-thirds of the workforce has fallen behind the pace of average wage growth, meaning the "average" is being pulled upward by a narrower band of well-paid sectors and regions than the topline percentage suggests. For a foreign hire, this translates into a negotiating environment where the number on offer can be genuinely generous by local standards and still feel thin against actual costs in the capital, where rents and imported goods price in dollar terms while salaries are quoted in a currency still finding its footing.

Salary culture itself runs quiet. There is no tradition of transparent bands, no public expectation that a job posting will name a figure, and a layered, Soviet-inherited discretion around money that Central Asian workplace researchers studying Hofstede-style cultural dimensions consistently code as high power distance and collectivist — decisions, including pay decisions, flow from the top, and asking too directly about someone else's number is treated as a faint social transgression rather than due diligence.

This has practical consequences for how a package actually gets built. Since base salary is treated as a fixed, modest anchor, the real negotiation in Uzbekistan happens around the edges: housing allowances, transport, a signing bonus, sometimes even tuition support for a trailing family member's children. A foreign hire who fixates on the headline number and ignores everything wrapped around it is, by local standards, negotiating the wrong line item entirely. Local HR practitioners quoted in regional business press describe this as less a negotiation than an assembly process — you don't haggle over the number so much as you request the pieces that make the number livable.

## Turkey: Negotiate Today, Renegotiate by Spring

Turkey's defining salary-culture fact is that the number is never finished moving. The Labor Ministry's minimum wage announcements, covered in detail by outlets like Bloomberg Tax and Mondaq heading into 2026, function as a national recalibration point: net and gross minimum figures reset, employer costs shift, and every salary conversation in the country effectively restarts around the new floor. This is less a quirk than a structural response to an inflation rate that has made static annual contracts impractical for employer and employee alike.

That instability has, somewhat counterintuitively, produced a far more negotiation-forward culture than Uzbekistan's. Expat.com's Turkey forum carries a long-running thread specifically on negotiating salary and benefits as a foreign hire, alongside others on what a livable wage looks like for a family in Istanbul and how senior technical pay scales hold up against the city's cost of living — the sheer volume of these threads is itself a data point: in Turkey, talking about pay with strangers online is normal, because talking about pay with your employer is also normal, and arguably necessary just to keep real income from eroding.

Academic work applying Hofstede's framework to Turkish business culture consistently finds a high power-distance score (66) paired with comparatively low individualism (37) — hierarchical, relationship-driven, but with enough built-in appetite for direct negotiation that Turkish HR professionals, per industry career-guidance sites like Kariyer.net, routinely coach candidates on how to counter an initial offer rather than simply accept it.

The technology sector sharpens this further. Benchmarking sites like Levels.fyi now publish Istanbul-specific technical compensation data precisely because enough candidates demand a reference point before walking into a negotiation — a level of transparency infrastructure that simply doesn't exist yet for Tashkent. The net effect is a labor market where the inflation problem that erodes everyone's real wage has, paradoxically, produced a far more informed and vocal workforce than a more stable currency might have. People who have watched their salary's purchasing power shrink twice in one year stop being polite about asking what it's actually worth.

## The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the real contrast isn't the exchange rate, it's the emotional posture toward the number itself. Uzbekistan treats salary as something you receive and are grateful for, discussed sparingly and negotiated mostly through perks rather than base pay; Turkey treats salary as something you actively defend against a currency that is quietly working against you, discussed loudly and renegotiated on a rolling basis because standing still means losing ground.

For the foreign hire, that means two entirely different skill sets. In Tashkent, the job is figuring out what nobody will tell you and then negotiating the softer edges — housing, transport, bonus — around a base figure that probably won't move much. In Istanbul, the job is negotiating early, negotiating often, and building a habit of checking whether your contract's protections against inflation are real or decorative. Neither approach guarantees comfort. Both reward people who do their homework before the interview, not during it.

## Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two salary cultures on the basis of stability alone, Uzbekistan currently offers the more legible trajectory — wages climbing steadily, a system that works for you more than against you, provided you're in the right sector and the right city. Turkey offers a far noisier negotiation but a culture that, usefully, expects you to participate in it rather than simply accept whatever number lands in your inbox.

Either way, bring a spreadsheet, not a hunch: in one country the real number is hidden behind etiquette, and in the other it's hidden behind inflation, but in both, nobody is going to volunteer it for you.
