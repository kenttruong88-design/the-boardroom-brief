# Switzerland Will Onboard You Into a System Built Over Decades. Argentina Will Onboard You Into a Person — Usually Over Shared Mate.

**🇦🇷 Argentina · 🇨🇭 Switzerland**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Switzerland's entire economy runs partly on a formalized onboarding philosophy: nearly two-thirds of Swiss youth combine school and paid work through the country's vocational education and training system, assigned a designated "Apprenticeship Mentor" whose job is to guarantee both supervision quality and structured learning. It's onboarding as national infrastructure. Argentina has no equivalent formal system — instead, a new hire's actual onboarding often happens through repeated face-to-face contact, personal introductions, and, if the team drinks mate together, the expectation that you'll accept the shared herbal tea when it's offered to you, because declining it isn't really about the tea. Switzerland onboards you into a process. Argentina onboards you into a relationship.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect formal, structured onboarding with clearly defined mentorship roles | Assume onboarding is casual or improvised — Swiss workplaces treat it as a serious, designed process |
| Respect hierarchy visibly, especially with senior managers who hold real decision-making power | Push for informal shortcuts around structured process, even if you come from a more relationship-driven culture |
| Show dependability early — consistency is a core value that gets noticed and rewarded | Expect quick promotion to inner social circles; professional respect and personal friendship follow different timelines |
| Take the apprenticeship/mentor model seriously if it's offered — it's a genuine career accelerator, not a formality | Expect your onboarding mentor relationship to translate into a close personal friendship quickly |
| Budget real time for integration — Quora accounts describe Swiss workplace and social integration as notably slower than neighboring countries | Get discouraged if colleagues remain professionally warm but personally distant for months |

#### 🇦🇷 Argentina

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept shared mate when offered — it's a genuine bonding ritual, not just a beverage | Decline repeatedly; it can read as standoffish even if unintended |
| Expect a blend of informal mentorship and formal training, often through repeated personal contact | Expect a codified, HR-department-run onboarding process comparable to a Swiss apprenticeship system |
| Let your manager get to know you personally — approachability and presence from leadership is the norm | Expect a purely transactional relationship with your manager; presence and personal interest are expected of them too |
| Use first names quickly if invited, even while still respecting title and seniority in formal settings | Assume fast informality means hierarchy has disappeared; both operate simultaneously |
| Expect trust, and therefore real onboarding, to build through repeated contact over time | Expect to be "fully onboarded" after a single orientation session; integration is ongoing and relationship-driven |

---

Switzerland's approach reflects a country that has effectively industrialized the concept of structured career entry. Its vocational education and training system is frequently cited internationally — including by the Urban Institute in post-pandemic labor policy discussions — as a model worth emulating precisely because of how deliberately it pairs paid work with mentorship and classroom learning simultaneously. Onboarding research more broadly confirms that formal mentoring accelerates cultural assimilation and long-term retention, and Switzerland has essentially built this principle into its national economic architecture rather than leaving it to individual employers. But this formality has a social cost for adult newcomers arriving through the normal hiring process rather than the apprenticeship pipeline: Quora accounts consistently describe Swiss integration, both social and professional, as harder and slower than in neighboring Germany, with one respondent noting bluntly that "your friends will probably all be foreigners" and that the Swiss are "friendly but not looking for friendship with foreigners" in any fast sense.

Argentina's onboarding runs almost entirely on personal contact instead. Business culture guides are consistent on this point: no encounter, including a professional one, starts with business itself — time spent on family, sports, and social conversation is treated as the actual mechanism by which trust and working relationships get built, not a delay before the real work starts. This extends specifically into onboarding practice, where new hires are described as experiencing a blend of formal training and informal mentorship, with managers expected to be personally approachable and invested in wellbeing, not simply present to assign tasks. Sharing mate, the traditional herbal tea passed among a group, functions as a small but real signal of acceptance into a team — accepting it when offered is a low-cost, high-signal way of participating in that relationship-building rather than standing apart from it.

The reckoning: Switzerland's onboarding is stronger on structure and weaker on speed of personal connection — you'll know exactly what's expected of you and who's responsible for teaching you, but genuine social integration may lag well behind professional competence. Argentina's onboarding is stronger on personal connection and weaker on formal structure — you'll feel invited into the team quickly, but the actual mechanics of "how this job works" may be transmitted informally, through relationships, rather than through a defined process. A Swiss transplant in Buenos Aires might feel oddly under-onboarded, missing a formal manual for a job everyone else seems to have learned through osmosis and conversation. An Argentine transplant in Zurich might feel formally well-trained but strangely isolated, professionally set up for success while still eating lunch alone months in.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone asking whether it's easier to integrate in Switzerland or Germany was told Switzerland is generally harder: expect your closest friends to remain fellow foreigners for a long while, since the Swiss are described as friendly in professional settings but not quick to extend that warmth into personal friendship.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — An employee describing their onboarding and adjustment experience after switching jobs noted that a new manager explicitly told them to "take things slow," which read initially as a lack of urgency but turned out to reflect a genuine, structured, paced onboarding philosophy rather than disorganization.*

> *Quora — A respondent addressing whether Switzerland is welcoming to immigrants distinguished sharply between economic welcome and social welcome: highly educated, high-earning newcomers who don't culturally deviate much from Swiss norms are welcomed readily into the workforce, but that welcome doesn't automatically extend into personal social integration.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A worker describing a remote onboarding experience at a large tech company noted that even a well-resourced, formally structured onboarding program couldn't substitute for organically built relationships with teammates, which took months longer to develop than the technical onboarding itself — a dynamic that maps closely onto the Swiss pattern of strong process, slower personal connection.*

> *Quora — A poster researching how to find a job in Switzerland without prior local connections was told it could take months or even years without a network, underscoring how much of Swiss professional entry, despite its formal apprenticeship reputation, still quietly depends on relationships you're expected to build yourself.*

### Conclusion

If you want a job where the path to competence is clearly mapped out for you from day one, Switzerland's structured onboarding and mentorship system is genuinely excellent — just don't expect the social side to move at the same pace as the professional side. If you want to feel personally welcomed into a team quickly, with real investment from your manager as a person and not just a supervisor, Argentina delivers that almost immediately, even if the actual "how things work here" knowledge arrives more gradually, through conversation rather than documentation. The honest trade is structure versus speed of connection, and you can't fully have both. If a friend asked me over a drink: in Switzerland, be patient and let the system teach you; in Argentina, just take the mate when it's offered and the rest follows from there.
