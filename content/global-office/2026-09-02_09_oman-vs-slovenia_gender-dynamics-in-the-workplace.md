# Oman Lets Her In. Slovenia Just Never Locked the Door.

🇴🇲 Oman · 🇸🇮 Slovenia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Oman closes 63.7 percent of its gender gap, according to the World Economic Forum, the lowest score of any high-income economy on the planet — and yet, per the World Bank, women make up a striking share of Omani university graduates and hold visible ground in medicine, banking, and the civil service. Slovenia, meanwhile, closes 77 percent of its gap, ranks 29th globally, and still cannot get women past 34 percent of managerial jobs or 26 percent of corporate boardrooms. One country restricts the on-ramp; the other builds a six-lane highway that mysteriously narrows to one lane right before the executive suite. Neither has solved anything. Both would like a word with the other's HR department.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇴🇲 Oman

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wait for an Omani woman to extend her hand before offering a handshake | Assume mixed-gender socializing after hours works like it does at home |
| Address female colleagues by professional title, not first name, until invited otherwise | Schedule casual one-on-ones in private, closed settings early in a working relationship |
| Expect to see women running hospital wards, bank branches, and ministry departments | Mistake modest dress or reserve for professional disengagement |
| Ask HR about family leave policy before assuming a colleague's caregiving load | Comment on a colleague's abaya, hijab, or dress choices, even as a compliment |
| Build relationships slowly; trust precedes business here for everyone, not just women | Interpret a hand-to-chest gesture instead of a handshake as a personal snub |
| Note that maternity leave now runs 98 days, with a paid nursing hour after return | Assume every workplace is mixed; some remain functionally segregated by sector |

#### 🇸🇮 Slovenia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take blunt, unembellished feedback from a female manager at face value | Read Slovenian directness as hostility; it is neither personal nor political |
| Expect near-equal representation in the room at mid-level meetings | Assume equal presence in the room means equal presence at the top of the org chart |
| Ask about the new 33–40 percent board quotas taking effect from 2026–2028 | Assume the quotas mean the boardroom problem is already solved |
| Respect the strict divide between office hours and personal time for all colleagues | Schedule evening calls assuming anyone, of any gender, will pick up |
| Note women already lead in senior public-sector posts, per OECD data | Assume private-sector Slovenia mirrors the public sector's gender balance |
| Use formal titles and surnames until a colleague signals otherwise | Bring up salary; Slovenia's pay gap is famously narrow, but nobody discusses it |

Oman's case is the more counterintuitive of the two, and worth sitting with. The World Bank's Gender Data Portal shows Omani women's overall labor force participation trailing well behind men's, and the World Economic Forum's 2025 report puts Oman's economic-participation subscore among the weakest of any wealthy nation. But research published in the journal Equality, Diversity and Inclusion complicates the tidy narrative: once inside professional fields such as medicine, banking, and education, Omani women report that gender discrimination bites hardest at entry and mid-level rungs, with markedly less friction at senior levels — the women who make it through report "gliding," not grinding. The bottleneck, in other words, sits at the door, not at the ceiling.

That door is also being redesigned. Royal Decree 53/2023 lifted paid maternity leave to 98 days, added a paid daily nursing hour for a year after return, and — a first for the Gulf — introduced seven days of paid paternity leave for fathers of any nationality, according to the ILO. Omanisation policy, meanwhile, is quietly reshuffling who benefits from any of this: several roles once filled by expatriate women, from secretarial to service-sector work, are now reserved for Omani nationals, per Gulf News reporting, meaning the labor-market story splits cleanly along a citizen/expatriate line as much as a male/female one.

Slovenia's story is almost the photo negative. Its overall gender gap is one of the narrower ones in Europe, its unadjusted pay gap is famously among the EU's smallest, and Eurostat data show its labor force participation rates for men and women sitting close together. The trouble starts higher up. Women held just 33.7 percent of managerial roles in 2024 — a decline of 3.8 percentage points since 2014 — and only 25.6 percent of board seats, per Eurostat and OECD figures, prompting Slovenia to legislate mandatory board quotas of 33 to 40 percent, phased in for large listed companies from 2026 and state-owned enterprises from 2028. The public sector tells a rosier story: Slovenia posted the OECD's highest share of women in senior public-sector roles as of 2021. It is a country that solved gender parity everywhere except the places that set strategy and sign the checks.

### The Reckoning

Put the two head to head and the irony sharpens. Oman's formal statistics look like a cautionary tale, yet its professional women describe an environment where, once admitted, advancement is comparatively unimpeded by gender alone — the barrier is getting hired, not getting promoted. Slovenia's formal statistics look like a model economy, yet its professional women describe an environment thick with mid-career and boardroom ceilings that legislation is only now attempting to shatter by decree. Hofstede Insights' cultural dimension data offers a partial explanation: the Arab cluster that includes Oman scores around 53 on masculinity, a moderate figure, while Slovenia scores just 19 — one of the most "feminine," cooperation-oriented cultures Hofstede measures. A low masculinity score predicts consensus and modesty in daily interaction, not equal representation at the top; Slovenia proves those are two entirely different achievements.

The handshake, fittingly, captures the whole asymmetry. In Oman, a foreign woman waits for an Omani man to offer his hand, and a polite hand-to-chest gesture in its place is a cultural norm, not a rebuff. In Slovenia, the handshake happens without a second thought, from either side, and the feedback that follows it will be blunter than either party expected. One culture negotiates the terms of contact; the other skips straight to the terms of the argument.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A woman who worked several years in Muscat's private sector described the job itself as straightforward, but noted the real negotiation happened before she was hired: convincing a hiring manager that a foreign, unmarried woman would stay professionally "safe" to employ, a hurdle her male colleagues never faced.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a thread on whether women can work in Oman pointed out that the honest answer depends entirely on sector: hospitals and banks feel unremarkable, but a handful of smaller family-run firms still quietly prefer not to hire women at all, a preference nobody states outright.*

> *Connect the Cultures (expat interview blog) — A longtime female expat described Oman as the safest place she had ever lived and worked, adding that the caution she'd been warned about from home turned out to be more about her own assumptions than anything Omani colleagues ever did.*

> *Expat.com forum — A new arrival in Ljubljana described mistaking a female manager's blunt one-line feedback for anger, only to learn weeks later that the same manager gave identical unvarnished notes to every employee regardless of gender or seniority.*

> *Sorcha Coyle (personal travel/work blog) — A visitor who later worked in Oman advised that the single most useful thing she wished someone had told her in advance was to let Omani women set the pace of every introduction, professional or social, rather than performing the confident Western handshake-and-eye-contact routine by default.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway for anyone landing in either country is to stop grading gender dynamics on a single scale. In Oman, watch how a woman is treated once she is in the room, not just who got let into the building. In Slovenia, watch who sits at the head of the table, not just how many women sit around it. Both countries are, in their own ways, mid-renovation: Oman widening the door, Slovenia raising the ceiling. Neither has finished the job, and both would benefit from comparing notes, though it is hard to imagine either admitting it needs the other's blueprint.
