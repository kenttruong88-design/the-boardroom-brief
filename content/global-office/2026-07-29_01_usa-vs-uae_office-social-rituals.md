**Lunch Is the New Happy Hour, Unless You're in a Country Where Lunch Might Disappear Entirely: A Guide to Surviving the Break Room in America and the UAE**

**🇺🇸 USA · 🇦🇪 UAE**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In America, the modern office ritual is a $14 catered salad eaten at a communal table while someone asks if you've seen the new show everyone's seen; in the UAE, it is a three-course iftar with a client you have known for six days, and skipping it will be remembered longer than anything you say in the actual meeting. Both are, technically, "networking." Only one of them can get you fired for showing up with a coffee in hand during Ramadan.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to the catered team lunch — 53% of companies now offer one weekly, and skipping it reads as disengaged | Assume the holiday party is optional just because HR called it "optional" |
| Keep small talk to sports, weekend plans, or the weather | Bring up salary, religion, or politics at the lunch table |
| Say yes to the occasional happy hour, then leave after one drink if you want | Get visibly drunk at a work event — it will outlive your tenure in office lore |
| Use the lunch break as an actual break — 94% of workers report feeling happier when they take it | Eat at your desk every day and assume nobody notices |
| RSVP promptly to team events, even to decline | No-show without a message — 51% of workers admit to ducking out of events, but doing it without notice damages trust |

#### 🇦🇪 UAE

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept invitations to lunch or dinner with colleagues and clients — relationship-building happens over food, not in meetings | Rush straight to business talk before the meal is served |
| During Ramadan, avoid eating, drinking, or chewing gum in public spaces during fasting hours, even if you're not Muslim | Eat at your desk in view of fasting colleagues — use the designated break room |
| Accept an Iftar invitation if offered — it's a significant gesture of goodwill | Decline without a good reason; it can read as a rejection of the relationship, not just the dinner |
| Dress modestly for work social events, even informal ones | Assume after-work socializing looks like a Western happy hour — alcohol is restricted and context-dependent |
| Expect team events to lean toward family-inclusive daytime activities rather than nightlife | Schedule meetings or team events during prayer times without checking first |

**The United States** treats the office lunch as a productivity tool with a smile on it. According to a 2023 ezCater survey, a majority of American workers now prefer bonding with colleagues over a free catered lunch rather than an alcohol-centered happy hour — food outperformed drinks five to one as the thing that actually gets people into a conference room. CaterCow's 2025 Workplace Food Habits Report found 53% of companies now cater lunch at least weekly, a quiet admission that free food is cheaper than a consultant's report on "culture." But the ritual has teeth: Gallup research consistently links employees who feel socially connected at work to lower burnout and higher retention, meaning the sandwich platter is doing real institutional labor.

Still, Americans have limits on how much bonding they will tolerate. A separate ezCater finding shows 64% of workers have felt obligated to attend an outside-office event they didn't want to go to, and 51% have lied or quietly left to avoid one. The performance of enthusiasm, in other words, is part of the job — just not a part anyone discusses in the exit interview.

**The UAE**, by contrast, treats the shared meal not as a bonding exercise bolted onto the workday but as the actual mechanism through which business gets done. Socializing over lunches and dinners is described by Expatica and other business-etiquette guides as central to doing business in the Emirates, where small talk about family and life is expected before any shop talk begins. This is not incidental warmth; it's how trust gets established in a business culture the region's own consultants describe as "high-context," meaning much of what matters goes unsaid and has to be read from behavior rather than stated in an agenda. The workforce itself makes this more complicated than it sounds: with professionals from more than 200 nationalities in the country, per multiple UAE workforce reports, the "team dinner" your Emirati manager expects, your British colleague assumes, and your Filipino teammate is used to may be three different events with three different rules.

Ramadan adds a hard reset to the calendar. UAE labor regulations shorten the workday by a minimum of two hours during the holy month, eating and drinking is restricted to designated areas during fasting hours, and Gulf Business's etiquette guides note that even virtual meetings carry the expectation that non-fasting employees won't eat or drink on camera. None of this is optional cultural color — it is enforced, and getting it wrong is the kind of mistake colleagues remember.

**The Reckoning:** Both countries have converted the shared meal into a load-bearing piece of office infrastructure, but they've wired it to opposite ends of the org chart. American lunch culture is bottom-up and largely transactional — it exists to make an eight-hour day more bearable and is tracked by HR as an engagement metric. Emirati meal culture is top-down and relational — it exists because the deal, the promotion, or the trust you need from a manager is often decided at the table, not in the meeting that follows it. The irony is that the more "optional" American event — the happy hour — is the one people most readily skip, while the more obligatory Emirati one — the iftar, the client dinner — is the one you skip at your professional peril.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A professional who moved from the US to Dubai for a finance role wrote that the hardest recalibration wasn't the heat or the tax-free paycheck, but realizing that declining a dinner invitation from a manager was read as a professional signal, not a scheduling conflict — something no orientation packet mentioned.*

> *Internations Dubai forum — A German engineer described being confused in his first month that "team building" in his Dubai office meant a family-friendly desert brunch rather than the beer-and-bowling nights he'd known in Frankfurt, and that bringing his kids was not just allowed but expected.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — An engineer discussing a move to a Dubai tech office noted that official work hours looked reasonable on paper but that after-hours messages from managers were treated as normal, and that colleagues who didn't respond within the hour were quietly noted as less committed.*

> *r/dubai (via secondary reporting) — One British expat described a typical day running from 8 a.m. to 6:30 p.m. with work continuing after hours via messages from colleagues, adding that he'd accepted the pace because the job market rewards visible availability more than it punishes long hours.*

> *Quora — An American who relocated from a Midwest office job described the opposite shock in reverse: returning to a US team lunch culture after years abroad and being surprised at how quickly colleagues left the table the moment food was finished, calling it "efficient to the point of feeling cold."*

### Conclusion

If you're moving from the US to the UAE, the practical shift isn't learning new small talk — it's learning that the meal is the meeting, and treating it as filler will cost you more than a bad quarterly review. If you're moving the other way, the adjustment is smaller but sneakier: American office bonding looks casual and low-stakes, right up until you realize skipping it repeatedly is quietly logged against you too. Either way, keep your calendar loose around lunchtime — in both countries, it's doing more work than the job description admits. My honest advice, over a drink or, more appropriately, over dinner: eat the meal, ask about their family, and save your opinions about the job for after dessert.
