**The Bonus Is the Real Salary in Gaborone; In Lima, Ask Twice Whether That Number Is Gross or Net**

**🇧🇼 Botswana · 🇵🇪 Peru**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Quote a base salary in Botswana without asking about the annual bonus and language premiums, and you've priced yourself against a number nobody who actually works there uses. Quote a base salary in Peru without asking whether it's gross or net, and you may have just accepted a pay cut you won't notice until the first payslip. Two countries, two entirely different ways for the headline number to lie to you.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇼 Botswana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask about the full package — annual bonuses (typically 1–3 months' pay) can be a fifth or more of total compensation | Anchor your expectations to base salary alone |
| Factor in that benefits can add 20–40% to total compensation value | Compare Botswana offers to home-country offers using base pay only |
| Expect a premium for Setswana fluency, advanced degrees, or specialized certification (10–30% typical) | Assume your foreign credentials alone set the number |
| Budget extra negotiating room if relocating to Gaborone specifically — relocation allowances are common | Assume the same offer applies uniformly outside the capital |
| Confirm your work permit status before salary talk gets serious — permits can take a long time to clear | Accept a verbal offer as final before the permit and paperwork are sorted |
| Ask directly about equal-pay compliance — it's a genuine legal and policy priority, not just marketing language | Assume pay equity is self-enforcing without asking how it's tracked |

#### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clarify gross versus net before agreeing to any number — this trips up more foreign hires than any other single issue | Assume a quoted salary figure is what lands in your account |
| Expect mandatory extras baked into total compensation: CTS, biannual gratificaciones bonuses, profit-sharing | Treat those legally mandated extras as a bonus on top of a "real" market-rate base |
| Get everything in writing — formal payroll status and full package terms, especially as a foreign hire | Rely on a verbal understanding of a "fully compliant" package |
| Expect real salary growth with tenure — roughly 35% higher pay is typical between 2 and 5 years' experience | Expect entry-level Peru pay to resemble mid-career pay elsewhere |
| Understand that negotiations and paperwork often move slower than in more deadline-driven cultures | Push hard for a rushed timeline; it can read as distrust rather than efficiency |
| Ask how a role's pay compares within Lima specifically — most qualified roles and best pay concentrate there | Assume national averages reflect what a Lima-based professional role actually pays |

---

Botswana's compensation culture is built around a real, if under-advertised, minimum-wage floor — statutory minimum wage sits at roughly BWP 2,596 a month, translating to a standard 45-hour workweek that in Gaborone offices typically runs conventional 8-to-5 hours, per Rivermate's guide, though mining-sector roles commonly run rotation schedules instead. What matters more for anyone actually negotiating a professional package is how far the real number moves from that floor: gross salaries in 2026 range roughly BWP 6,000–22,000 a month depending on industry and seniority, and base pay is explicitly described across multiple salary guides as only one component of the total. Annual bonuses of one to three months' salary are common, benefits can add 20–40% to total value, and specific premiums exist for advanced degrees, professional certifications, and — notably for expatriates — fluency in Setswana. On the policy side, Botswana has made equal-pay compliance and anti-discrimination enforcement an explicit government and NGO priority, which shows up in job postings and HR guidance more consistently than in many peer economies.

Peru's compensation structure runs on a different logic entirely: mandatory statutory add-ons baked directly into what "total compensation" legally has to include. Beyond the base salary — itself subject to a national minimum wage of PEN 1,130 a month as of January 2025 — Peruvian employment law requires Compensación por Tiempo de Servicios (a severance-linked savings benefit), biannual gratificaciones bonuses, and profit-sharing where applicable. None of this is negotiable in the way a Botswana signing bonus might be; it's simply what a compliant Peruvian payroll includes, whether or not a foreign hire's employer flags it clearly. Salary progression is real and measurable — experience commands roughly a 35% premium moving from entry-level to the 2–5-year mark — but so is a persistent gender pay gap, with men earning 20.2% more than women on average nationally. Layered on top of all of it: Peruvian business culture treats deadlines and negotiation timelines with real flexibility, meaning the process of actually agreeing a number, in writing, with all the statutory components clarified, tends to move slower than a foreign hire used to fast compensation cycles will expect.

**The Reckoning:** Both countries hide the "real" salary somewhere other than the headline number, but they hide it in opposite directions. In Botswana, the headline number understates total compensation — the real value is above the quoted base, buried in bonuses, benefits, and premiums a candidate has to ask about explicitly. In Peru, the risk runs the other way: a quoted number can overstate what actually lands, if gross-versus-net and the statutory add-on structure aren't clarified up front. A negotiator who applies Botswana's "ask what's on top" instinct in Lima might walk away satisfied with a number that's actually smaller once deductions apply; a negotiator who applies Peru's "assume the mandated extras are already included" instinct in Gaborone might leave real money on the table by not asking about the bonus at all.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Responding to a thread on whether foreigners can find jobs in Botswana, several answers converged on the same warning: local-market salaries, absent an expatriate package specifically, often aren't worth the wait for a work permit — the permit timeline and the base local pay scale were repeatedly described as the two factors that make or break whether relocating for a "local" (non-expat-package) role actually pencils out.*

> *Talesmag (real post report, U.S. foreign-service community, Gaborone) — A spouse of a posted foreign-service officer, asked directly about local job options and salary scales for expatriate partners, described the market as thin outside a few sectors, with realistic options split between full-time roles in mining-adjacent or NGO work and part-time or telecommuting arrangements that avoided the local salary ceiling entirely.*

> *Expat.com (Lima work guide) — The guide's own compiled advice for foreign job-seekers in Lima repeatedly flagged the gross-versus-net distinction as the single most common point of confusion in an offer negotiated from abroad, alongside a pointed recommendation to get written confirmation of full payroll compliance before accepting.*

> *ExpatPeru forum — In an older but still-referenced thread on work options in Lima, posters compared notes on realistic pay ranges for foreign hires and repeatedly steered newcomers toward clarifying whether an offered number already accounted for gratificaciones and CTS, noting that employers unfamiliar with hiring foreigners sometimes quoted the base figure alone without flagging the difference.*

> *Africarrieres (Botswana salary guide, drawing on regional recruiter data) — Recruiters quoted in the guide's profession-by-profession breakdown noted that candidates who negotiated only on base salary consistently left value on the table compared to those who explicitly negotiated bonus structure and benefits as separate line items — a pattern recruiters said was more pronounced among first-time expatriate hires than repeat ones.*

### Conclusion

The one number worth writing down before any offer conversation in either country: in Botswana, ask "what else is in the package beyond base," and in Peru, ask "is that number before or after deductions, and does it already include the mandated bonuses." Get those two questions backwards and you'll either underprice yourself in Gaborone or overestimate your Lima paycheck by a meaningful margin. What I'd actually tell a friend: never accept a number over email in either country — get it broken down, line by line, before you say yes.
