**Thobe, Abaya, or Blazer: Getting Dressed for Work in Qatar vs. France**

**🇶🇦 Qatar · 🇫🇷 France**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Qatar's government recently felt the need to issue an actual numbered circular — 13/2024 — specifying exactly what public servants may and may not wear to the office, down to a ban on sport shoes. France has no equivalent decree, and needs none, because the entire population appears to have absorbed the rules through cultural osmosis: as one relocation guide puts it, in Paris "it's better to be slightly overdressed than too informal," a sentence that would read as vague anxiety anywhere else and as gospel in the 8th arrondissement.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇶🇦 Qatar

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress conservatively by default: for men, long sleeves and lightweight trousers; for women, below-knee hemlines, modest necklines, and at least half sleeves | Wear short, tight, or sheer clothing to the office, even in a private multinational company with a relaxed internal culture |
| Keep an abaya or headscarf accessible for mosque visits or formal occasions, even if not required daily | Assume you must wear an abaya as a non-Qatari woman — it isn't mandatory, but respectful conservative dress still is |
| Follow your specific employer's guidelines closely — dress norms vary meaningfully by company and industry | Assume one universal Qatar dress code applies everywhere; a bank, an oil major, and a startup can differ significantly |
| Default toward darker, more formal suits for non-Qatari men in client-facing or government-adjacent roles | Wear bright colors, sport shoes, or overly casual fabrics to a meeting with government or traditional Qatari institutions |
| Notice and respect that Qatari colleagues will typically wear a thobe (men) or abaya (women) as standard professional dress | Treat traditional Qatari dress as "formal wear" reserved for special occasions — it's everyday professional attire |

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Aim for "smart casual" as your daily default — polished and understated, not necessarily suited-up | Show up in a hoodie or sweatshirt unless you are, specifically, an unpaid intern; even startups draw this line |
| Keep accessories minimal — one deliberate statement piece (earrings, a bracelet) rather than several | Overload an outfit with visible logos or excessive jewelry; the aesthetic favors quiet quality over branding |

### Qatar: Modesty as Professional Baseline

Qatar's approach to workplace dress sits at the intersection of religious and cultural values and formal government regulation. Everyday professional guidance for women specifies dresses, trousers, or skirts below the knee, modest necklines, and at least half-length sleeves; for men, long sleeves and lightweight trousers are standard, with full suits reserved mainly for important meetings and formal events. Crucially, per expat guides, wearing an abaya or headscarf is not legally mandatory for non-Qatari women — but conservative, elegant dress at formal business gatherings is expected regardless, as a matter of demonstrated cultural respect rather than legal compulsion.

The government took this from cultural norm to written policy in 2024: Circular 13 spells out precise requirements by nationality and gender for public sector employees — Qatari men in traditional thobe, ghutra, and egal; non-Qatari men in dark suits with matching shirts and ties; Qatari women in traditional dress with head covering; non-Qatari women in "appropriate work suits" — and explicitly bans short or tight clothing, bright colors, and sport shoes except for documented medical need. Private-sector norms are less codified but track the same instinct: dress conservatively, default to what your specific employer and colleagues actually wear, and treat traditional Qatari dress not as ceremonial but as ordinary professional attire worn to the office every day.

### France: Understatement as the Actual Rule

French office dress code operates almost entirely without a written manual, because the standard is transmitted through observation rather than policy: "smart casual," per one relocation guide covering Paris offices, meaning "polished, understated, and elegant" rather than formal in the necktie-and-blazer sense. The specific French genius here is restraint — Coco Chanel's famous advice to remove one accessory before leaving the house functions as an actual, cited workplace principle, and one deliberate statement piece is preferred over visible layering of jewelry or logos. Makeup, where worn, stays deliberately understated.

The formality floor is nonetheless real and consistently enforced by social pressure rather than HR policy: hoodies and sweatshirts are treated as intern-only wear even inside French startups that would look casual by American or British standards, and the practical advice given to newcomers is to run slightly overdressed at first rather than risk reading as sloppy. This produces an odd contrast with France's otherwise famously worker-protective legal culture — a country with a legislated right to disconnect from work email has no equivalent formal dress code law, because it has never needed one. Appearance, per multiple guides aimed at foreign hires, is read directly as a signal of how seriously an employee takes their role, not as a superficial concern separate from actual competence.

### The Reckoning

Both countries treat dress as a real, non-optional signal of respect — for religion and tradition in Qatar, for professionalism and taste in France — but they encode the rule completely differently. Qatar increasingly writes its expectations down, in circulars and numbered guidelines, precisely because the multicultural, multi-nationality workforce can't be assumed to share a common cultural default. France writes almost nothing down, because it can assume a shared aesthetic vocabulary that most of its workforce absorbed well before their first job.

The trap for a newcomer runs in both directions. Someone arriving in Qatar from a culture with looser dress norms who treats "business casual" as an American concept — an untucked shirt, sneakers, capri pants — will find themselves conspicuously and specifically out of step with a written government standard, not just an unspoken one. Someone arriving in France assuming "no dress code" means genuine casualness, because no HR document said otherwise, will discover the sweatshirt line is enforced just as firmly as Qatar's circular, purely through the raised eyebrows of colleagues who never had to write it down.

### Conclusion

The honest shortcut for either country: in Qatar, dress modestly, check your specific employer's written or unwritten rules closely, and treat conservative dress as the professional default rather than an occasion-only choice. In France, dress a notch more polished than you think you need to, take off one accessory before you walk out the door, and never, ever wear the hoodie unless you're twenty-two and unpaid. Both countries are telling you the same underlying thing through very different vocabularies: how you dress is read as how seriously you take the room. Dress like you know that.
