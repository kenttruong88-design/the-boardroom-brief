**The Flattest Org Chart in Europe Still Has One Person Who Decides Everything**

🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇦🇹 Austria

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Austria's workplaces look, on paper, like a managerial utopia: Hofstede's cultural-dimensions framework gives it a power-distance score around 11, among the lowest ever recorded, implying a flat, consultative office where everyone's voice counts. Then you sit through your first meeting and discover that decisions are "made at the top of the company, often privately," agendas are followed to the minute, and the highest-ranking person enters the room first (per guidance compiled by businessculture.org). Next door in Czechia, the org chart looks more conventionally hierarchical — and yet the actual friction in a meeting is less about who outranks whom than about who's willing to say, out loud, that the plan everyone just nodded along to is a bad one.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Send an email before requesting an unscheduled meeting | Drop by someone's desk expecting an immediate sit-down |
| Arrive on time, even by a minute or two | Show up late — colleagues notice and remember |
| Expect decisions to land after the meeting, not during it | Push for a verdict in the room — it usually isn't coming yet |
| Join colleagues for lunch, even occasionally | Eat at your desk every day and wonder why no one talks to you |
| Raise a real objection privately, afterward | Say in the meeting that the plan is bad — silence there often *is* the disagreement |
| Accept a beer at a work event without making it a thing | Decline every single time — it reads as standoffish |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Circulate a precise agenda in advance and stick to it, including end time | Wing it, or let a meeting run long without acknowledging it |
| Back claims with data, examples, and case studies | Oversell or hype a proposal without evidence |
| Call immediately if you'll be even five minutes late | Cancel a meeting at the last minute — it damages the relationship |
| Expect the highest-ranking person to speak first and enter first | Jump in with your own opinion before being given the floor |
| Treat a long, detailed review of your proposal as a good sign | Read a drawn-out Q&A as rejection — scrutiny often means they're serious |
| Negotiate your own perks (meal allowance, transit pass) explicitly | Assume standard benefits are automatic — "if you don't ask, nobody offers" |

### Czechia

Czech meetings run on a kind of polite indirection that outsiders routinely misread as agreement. Guidance aimed at foreign hires lists the exact missteps that get people in trouble: proposing a Friday-afternoon meeting nobody will attend, expecting an immediate decision once the meeting ends, or asking for an impromptu sit-down instead of sending an email first (Expats.cz). None of these are really about meetings — they're about a culture that prefers to let a decision mature somewhere upstream before announcing it, rather than hammering it out live in the room.

Where this gets genuinely tricky for newcomers is in reading disagreement. Czech colleagues who object to a plan rarely say so inside the meeting; the real feedback tends to surface afterward, sideways, between people who trust each other — which research on gossip and informal communication in Czech workplaces (Prague University of Economics and Business) has documented as a structural feature of how organizations there actually process disagreement, not a personality quirk of any one office.

### Austria

Austrian meetings are, by contrast, almost aggressively well-organized — and that orderliness is precisely what hides the hierarchy. The power-distance number is deceptively low because Austrian workplaces are consultative about process, not about outcome: everyone gets to weigh in, data gets presented, counterarguments get entertained at length, and then the decision that was probably already made privately at the top gets confirmed (businessculture.org's Austria meeting-etiquette guide). A thorough, drawn-out review of your proposal is a good sign, not a rejection — Austrians treat careful scrutiny as evidence they're taking you seriously.

Punctuality functions almost as a moral category. Showing up late without calling ahead, or cancelling a meeting at the last minute, reads as a real breach, not a scheduling inconvenience — guidance for incoming businesspeople treats a phone call explaining a five-minute delay as table stakes, not an extra courtesy. None of it is cold, exactly; it's precise, in a way that can feel cold to anyone used to more improvisational meeting culture.

### The Reckoning

The irony here is almost too clean: the country with the famously flat hierarchy (Austria) runs the more rigid, top-down meeting process, while the country with the more conventional hierarchy (Czechia) runs on consensus-by-omission, where the org chart tells you less about actual power than who's willing to go off-script. An Austrian meeting will tell you exactly who's in charge by watching who enters the room first and who speaks last; a Czech meeting will tell you by watching who stays quiet when the plan everyone just "agreed" to gets raised again, differently, three days later over coffee.

The practical consequence for a foreign hire: in Vienna, you earn credibility by having your numbers ready before anyone asks; in Prague, you earn it by not making anyone defend a bad plan to your face in front of the room. Both are forms of respect. Neither looks like the collaborative, improvisational meeting culture a lot of expats expect from "European office culture" as a single, interchangeable thing.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Local — A journalist who moved to Vienna wrote about "countless failed attempts to get the attention of Viennese waiters" and a general civic reserve that extended into her working life, including interview requests on summer Friday afternoons that went nowhere because staff had simply left for the weekend — a work-life boundary she came to respect, even when it wrecked her deadline.*

> *Expat.com forum (Austria work-culture thread) — One commenter, going by Vanessa2909, was blunt about the limits of employer generosity: lunch allowances and a subsidized transit pass are common, but, in her words, "if you don't negotiate it nobody will give it to you for free" — a line she offered as a direct corrective to newcomers expecting perks to simply appear.*

> *Expats.cz (reader voices) — A Romanian expat named Sorin described himself as "not that well integrated in the Czech society," saying he mostly socializes with other foreigners and people interested in EU politics — a reserve several other European expats quoted alongside him described experiencing at work as well as socially.*

> *Internations Vienna (community data, via The Local's 2023 Expat Insider coverage) — Vienna ranked last among surveyed cities on "Local Friendliness" and "Culture & Welcome," with 63% of foreigners there calling it difficult to make friends, against a 36% global average — numbers that track closely with how slowly Austrian colleagues are reported to open up at work.*

### Conclusion

If precision calms you down, go to Vienna — just bring your data and never cancel on anyone. If you'd rather work somewhere that keeps its real opinions for the hallway, Prague will suit you better, provided you can live with finding out what everyone actually thought three days after the meeting ended. Either way, stop assuming "European" tells you anything useful about how a room makes a decision — you still have to learn the room.
