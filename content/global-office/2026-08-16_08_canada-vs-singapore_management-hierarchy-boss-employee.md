# In Singapore, Your Boss Gets the Respect You'd Show a Parent. In Canada, Your Boss Gets a Slightly Better-Paid Colleague.

**🇨🇦 Canada · 🇸🇬 Singapore**

<br>

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Singapore, per multiple business-culture guides, the respect an employee shows their boss can genuinely resemble the respect they'd show a parent — first names are avoided in favor of "Sir," "Madam," or simply "Boss," and asking too many clarifying questions is read less as diligence than as a quiet accusation that the boss failed to explain the task properly the first time. In Canada, employees are broadly expected to approach senior executives directly when needed, contribute ideas regardless of seniority, and treat consultation as a two-way, ongoing habit rather than a formal event. Both countries have bosses. Only one of them expects you to earn the right to disagree with yours out loud.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Approach senior leadership directly with a question when it's genuinely relevant | Assume you always need to go through your direct manager first — Canadian offices generally welcome bypassing the chain when appropriate |
| Contribute ideas openly in meetings, regardless of your title | Wait to be explicitly invited to speak — collaboration, not deference, is the default expectation |
| Expect your manager to consult you before major decisions affecting your work | Assume consultation means you get a vote — input is gathered, but the final call still rests with managerial authority |
| Build trust through your qualifications and the terms of your role | Expect the same intense personal loyalty dynamic common in more hierarchical cultures — Canadian managers generally trust the contract and the competence, not a personal bond |
| Note that Quebec offices often run more hierarchically than the rest of Canada | Assume Canadian management style is uniform nationwide — expect more formality and less pre-decision discussion in Quebec, outside tech and startup firms |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use formal address — "Sir," "Madam," or an honorific — until explicitly told otherwise | Default to first names with a senior manager on your own initiative — it can read as presumptuous rather than friendly |
| Frame disagreement or suggestions carefully and, where possible, privately | Challenge a decision openly in a group setting — public pushback against authority is generally frowned upon regardless of how constructive the intent |
| Come prepared with your own answer before asking a clarifying question | Ask a boss to re-explain a task that was already covered — it can be read as implying they explained it poorly the first time |
| Expect senior figures to display visible authority, exercised benevolently | Mistake visible hierarchy for coldness — deference coexists with genuine investment in junior employees' development |
| Understand that final decisions rest with senior management | Expect to be looped into the decision-making process just because you were consulted for input — being asked and being included in the final call are different things |

### The Dominion of the Open Door

Canadian management culture runs on a paradox that trips up a lot of newcomers: hierarchy exists, but it's treated as largely optional in daily practice. Talent Canada's research on global management styles places Canada firmly on the "harmony" and consultation end of the spectrum — authoritarian, top-down directives are genuinely rare, and managers are expected to actively solicit input from across the team before deciding on a course of action. Employees, per multiple newcomer guides, can approach senior executives directly rather than routing every question through a direct supervisor, and doing so isn't read as a breach of protocol.

What Canadian consultation doesn't mean, despite how it can feel, is collective decision-making. Input gets gathered broadly, but the actual call still sits with managerial authority — Canadian managers trust the contract and the person's qualifications more than a personal, loyalty-based bond, which gives the relationship an even, professional texture rather than an emotionally weighted one. The regional exception is worth knowing: Quebec runs meaningfully more hierarchical than the rest of the country, with greater deference to rank and less pre-decision discussion, except at tech and entrepreneurial firms that tend to mirror the national norm regardless of province.

### The Republic of the Correctly Addressed Superior

Singapore's version of hierarchy is neither optional nor primarily procedural — it's cultural, rooted in Confucian values that treat deference to authority as a form of basic social order rather than a workplace policy. Senior figures are expected to lead and guide; junior employees are expected to listen attentively and execute, and open challenges to authority in group settings are broadly discouraged even when the underlying feedback is constructive. The formality extends to something as small as what you call your boss: avoiding first names in favor of a title or honorific is standard enough that skipping it reads as a genuine misstep, not an informal charm.

None of this means junior employees have no voice — ideas do get shared and considered, but the framing matters enormously. Suggestions are typically presented indirectly, carefully worded to avoid appearing to challenge a superior's authority, and disagreement, when it happens, is generally routed through private conversation rather than open meeting debate. Singapore's management culture, sitting at the crossroads of Eastern deference and Western corporate efficiency, ultimately expects authority to be displayed, but displayed benevolently — a boss who is strict but visibly invested in a junior employee's growth is the cultural ideal, not a contradiction.

### The Reckoning

The starkest contrast isn't how much power a Canadian or Singaporean boss actually holds — arguably comparable in both, once you strip away the atmosphere — but how openly that power gets performed day to day. A Canadian manager mostly exercises authority quietly, behind a curtain of open-door consultation that can make the final decision feel almost incidental to the discussion that preceded it. A Singaporean manager exercises authority visibly and formally, which paradoxically makes the boundaries of that authority easier for a newcomer to read correctly, provided they know to look for the formal cues (the honorific, the private channel for disagreement) rather than the informal ones a Canadian office trains you to expect.

The risk runs in both directions. A newcomer from Singapore working in Canada may under-use the direct access they're actually being offered, deferring to a hierarchy that, locally, barely exists in the way they're used to. A newcomer from Canada working in Singapore may over-use directness that, there, reads as a challenge to authority rather than the collaborative contribution it would be back home.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone answering a question about whether staff fearing their manager is good for business argued the effect is entirely cultural: in a hierarchy-averse workplace it corrodes trust and output, but in a hierarchy-respecting one, the same visible deference can coexist with — and even protect — a genuinely warm, mentorship-style relationship underneath it.*

> *Cadogan Tate expat relocation guide — A newly arrived Western manager in Singapore described the adjustment as learning to stop asking his team direct yes/no questions in group settings, since staff would answer what they thought he wanted to hear rather than risk contradicting him in front of colleagues, and switching to private one-on-ones fixed it almost immediately.*

> *Medium (workplace culture essay) — A writer analyzing "boss-culture fear" argued that the healthiest hierarchies weren't flat ones but ones where the hierarchy was explicit and consistently applied, since ambiguous authority — not knowing whether you can go over someone's head — created more anxiety than a clearly defined chain of command ever did.*

> *Psychology Today Singapore (reader-submitted account) — A junior employee described the relief of finally understanding that her manager's formality wasn't personal coldness after watching him quietly extend her deadline and mentor her through a difficult project without ever softening his tone in front of the team.*

> *Expat-forum thread — A Canadian who transferred to a Singapore office described accidentally using his new manager's first name in his first week, and said the silence that followed taught him more about the local hierarchy in three seconds than any onboarding document had managed.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway: in Canada, use the access you're given — speak up, approach leadership directly, and don't wait for permission to contribute, because nobody is going to hand you an invitation. In Singapore, earn the access first — use the correct address, raise disagreement privately, and let deference be the foundation your credibility gets built on before you start pushing against it. I'd tell a friend: in Toronto, walk into your boss's office; in Singapore, knock, use their title, and wait to be asked to sit down.
