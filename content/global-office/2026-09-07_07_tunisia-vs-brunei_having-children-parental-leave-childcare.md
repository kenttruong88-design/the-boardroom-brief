# Tunisia Tripled Its Maternity Leave. Brunei Never Bothered With Paternity. Guess Which Country's Mothers Work More.

🇹🇳 Tunisia · 🇧🇳 Brunei

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In August 2024, Tunisia's parliament voted 111 to 4 to triple statutory maternity leave from 30 days to three months, and, in a fit of generosity unusual for the region, gave new fathers a full paid week off, up from one grudging day. Roughly 9,000 kilometres east, Brunei has never passed a paternity leave law of any kind and shows no sign of drafting one. And yet Brunei's female labour-force participation rate sits at around 54 percent, more than double Tunisia's 25.5 percent, according to World Bank data. The lesson, unwelcome as it is for anyone who assumed generous leave equals mothers actually working: those turn out to be two entirely different policy problems.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇳 Tunisia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget for a nanny — domestic help is plentiful and cheap around Tunis | Assume your nanny works a Western eight-hour day; many treat 8am–2pm as full time |
| Register promptly for the new paid-leave reimbursement | Expect English-speaking childcare outside Tunis's wealthier districts |
| Book a Montessori or French-curriculum kindergarten spot months ahead | Skip written leave confirmation — enforcement of the 2024 law is uneven |
| Lean on extended family; grandparents are expected to help, not just offer | Plan around paternity leave beyond seven paid days |
| Ask if your contract qualifies for the four-month complicated-birth extension | Assume a cheap nanny is a reliable one |

#### 🇧🇳 Brunei

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register early at Jerudong Park Medical Centre or a private clinic; both need driving and planning | Count on statutory paternity leave; anything given is a discretionary favour |
| Find the expat prenatal community immediately — official postnatal support is thin | Expect a midwife-led model; care is hospital- and doctor-centric |
| Check citizenship status before assuming your leave terms — they differ for locals and foreign workers | Assume extended family will be nearby; isolation is the common adjustment |
| Plan mostly indoor activities; heat and humidity make outdoor infant time impractical most months | Skip confirming the employer/government pay split before you need it |
| Confirm your employer covers the first eight weeks, with government reimbursement after | Expect much hand-holding after discharge; you're largely your own case manager |

### Tunisia: A Better Law, Written for a Market That Isn't There Yet

Tunisia's August 2024 reform is, on paper, one of the more progressive family-leave documents in the Maghreb: maternity leave up from 30 days to three months at full pay (four months for twins, disability, or complicated births), up to 15 days of prenatal leave, and paternity leave up from a single day to seven paid working days, financed through social insurance funds rather than left to individual employers, according to WTW and Agenzianova's coverage of the bill.

The trouble is that the World Bank's own diagnosis of Tunisia's labour market identifies childcare access, not leave length, as the real constraint. Female labour-force participation has hovered around 25.5 to 26 percent — among the lowest the World Bank tracks regionally — and analysts point at scarce, affordable childcare, not the leave statute, as the bigger obstacle. Formal daycare exists from two months of age in principle, but clusters in Tunis, rarely comes with English-speaking staff, and thins out fast elsewhere. What actually functions as childcare infrastructure is an informal domestic-labour market: nannies working roughly an 8am-to-2pm day, paid 200 to 500 dinars a month, a rate expat postings on forums like Tales from a Small Planet call remarkably affordable, if inconsistent. Hofstede Insights classifies Tunisia as relatively collectivist and high on power distance, which shows up here practically: extended family is expected to absorb the caregiving gap formal childcare doesn't cover.

### Brunei: Thin Statute, Thick Safety Net for Those Who Qualify

Brunei's system runs almost entirely on citizenship status. Local mothers get 15 weeks of leave, 13 paid, split as two weeks before delivery and thirteen after, with employers covering the first eight weeks and government refunding the remaining five, according to payroll guides from CloudPay and Global Expansion. Foreign workers, a large share of the private-sector workforce, get a leaner deal: roughly nine weeks total, eight paid, split oddly across the pre- and post-birth period. Paternity leave has no statutory basis whatsoever; whatever a father receives, typically around three days by informal accounts, is an employer favour, not a right.

And yet Brunei's female labour-force participation rate runs close to 54 percent, comfortably above the global average of about 51 percent and more than double Tunisia's. Free universal healthcare and education, an oil-wealth-subsidised public sector absorbing women into predictable, unremarkable employment, and a small, tightly networked society appear to matter more than the leave law's fine print. What Brunei conspicuously does not provide, per expat accounts, is much support once you've left the hospital; that gap gets filled informally, through prenatal groups, not policy.

### The Reckoning

Put the two systems side by side and the causal story falls apart. Tunisia wrote the more generous law: three months of paid maternity leave, a real if modest paternity allowance, job protection for a year after return. Brunei's defaults look almost punitive by comparison: no paternity statute at all, a maternity benefit that treats foreign workers as lesser parents. Yet Brunei's mothers work at close to double Tunisia's rate.

The explanatory variable isn't the leave law; it's what happens on day 91, when Tunisian leave runs out and the World Bank's own research points to childcare, not legislation, as the chokepoint. Brunei doesn't win on generosity — it wins on friction reduction: a public-sector job market absorbing women into steady, expected employment, and a compact geography where an informal caregiving network is logistically feasible. Tunisia legislated better leave. Brunei just made staying employed the path of least resistance, for those the system was built around.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A parent answering a thread on the hardest parts of raising children in Tunisia named the language wall as the real shock: state schools run entirely in Arabic, pushing anyone without fluency toward a short list of French-curriculum private schools in Tunis with genuine waitlists.*

> *Foreign Service community forum (Tales from a Small Planet) — A diplomatic spouse posted from Tunis warning that going cheap on household help backfires: their neighbours hired the least expensive nanny available and dealt with no-shows and boundary problems, while paying slightly more for a non-local hire bought reliability worth every dinar.*

> *Expat blog (The Engineer's Wife, Brunei) — A New Zealander who conceived via fertility treatment at Jerudong Park Medical Centre described the postnatal period as the hardest part of the whole experience: appointments were thorough but there was almost no one to call with a small question, so a prenatal yoga class turned informal support group became the thing that actually got her through the first year.*

> *Expat blog (The Engineer's Wife, Brunei) — Coming from a home country where midwives manage low-risk pregnancies, she found Brunei's system reflexively medicalized, a doctor for everything, and admitted her main takeaway was relief that nothing went wrong rather than confidence in the process itself.*

> *Middle East careers forum (Bayt.com) — Working mothers across the region described being quietly passed over for stretch assignments after returning from leave, advising incoming parents to negotiate flexible arrangements before going on leave, not after.*

### Conclusion

For someone weighing an offer letter, the honest read is this: Tunisia's 2024 law makes it a defensible place to have a baby, provided you're prepared to solve childcare privately, the way most Tunisian households already do. Brunei asks less of the government and more of the individual: thin legal protections, but a labour market quietly built to keep mothers employed anyway, if you hold the right passport; foreign workers should read their contracts twice.

Neither country has solved the underlying problem: somebody still has to be home at three in the afternoon. Tunisia and Brunei have simply decided, with admirable clarity and no embarrassment, who that somebody is expected to be.
