# In Ireland You May Request the Spare Room Office; in Serbia You Negotiate It With Your Contract

🇮🇪 Ireland · 🇷🇸 Serbia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In 2025, according to Eurostat, 19.2 percent of employed people in Ireland usually worked from home, against an EU average of 8.8 percent and, in Serbia, a figure below 5 percent. The prospective expat might conclude that Ireland has solved remote work and Serbia has not discovered it. The more accurate conclusion is that Ireland has legislated the conversation and Serbia has left it to the contract, and that both countries are now quietly asking everyone to come back to the office.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Put any remote working request in writing and cite the right to request | Assume the right to request is a right to work remotely |
| Keep a record of the employer's reply; disputes can go to the Workplace Relations Commission | Accept a vague reference to business needs without asking for the reasoning |
| Ask in the interview how many office days are expected and whether that is changing | Take a hybrid offer as a permanent arrangement |
| Check whether your employer is among those tightening attendance | Plan your housing around the assumption of full remote work |
| Learn the national code before the conversation, not after it | Rely on the right if you are unsure whether you are covered |

### In Serbia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get remote working terms, equipment and expense reimbursement into the employment contract | Expect a statutory home-office allowance |
| Ask whether the role is remote, hybrid or office-based before you accept | Assume a remote advert will stay remote |
| Check the visa and company-registration rules before you move | Assume that a foreign employer can simply put you on payroll |
| Budget for an accountant if you register your own company | Treat the tax incentives as permanent |
| Learn what supervision the contract requires | Expect an employer to inspect or insist on your home setup |

## Ireland

Ireland's remote working regime is the most explicit in this comparison. The Work Life Balance and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 2023 gave all employees a right to request remote working, and a Workplace Relations Commission code of practice, approved on 7 March 2024, set the rules for how employers must consider such requests. The emphasis is on the word request. A government statutory review published in March 2026 stresses that the Act provides a right to request, not a right to remote work, and it finds that about 10 percent of employees say they have made a formal written request, that 94 percent of those who used the right received full or partial approval, and that only 48 percent of employees knew the right existed. The WRC has received 70 complaints under the Act. For a statute, this is a modest body count.

The labour market, meanwhile, has been less sentimental than the legislator. According to the Central Statistics Office, as reported by The Irish Times, the number of people working fully on-site in the fourth quarter of 2025 reached 1.866 million, the first time it has exceeded the pre-pandemic figure of 1.861 million. About 956,700 people still worked from home. The government's own review finds 57 percent of surveyed employees fully office-based, 33 percent hybrid and 9 percent fully remote. A William Fry survey of 404 firms, also reported by The Irish Times, expects the share of larger employers fixing specific remote days to rise from 34 percent to 50 percent during 2027, while only 5 percent expect a full return to the office within a year.

The attitude, then, is one of regulated compromise. Hofstede Insights scores Ireland 28 on power distance, which fits a country where an employee is permitted to ask the employer for something and the employer is permitted to say no with a documented reason. The Irish Examiner reports that Amazon wants corporate staff five days a week, Salesforce four and Microsoft at least three, and that AIB is raising its requirement from two days to three. The right exists. It has simply met an employer with a calendar.

Recruiters describe the same drift from the supply side. Trayc Keevans of Morgan McKinley told the Examiner that remote-first roles are probably less than 15 percent of agency-advertised positions, a big drop. When the statutory review looked at refusals reported in its public consultation, the commonest reason was concern about performance or service quality at 30 percent, followed by the view that the role was unsuitable at 15 percent. The review also records criticism of vague references to business needs, which is the polite Irish term for no.

## Serbia

Serbia offers remote work as a private bargain. According to CMS's expert guide, remote work is governed by the Labour Law and can be introduced only through the employment contract or its annexes. Employers cannot force an employee to work from home, employees cannot compel an employer to allow it, and an employer may require a return to the office only if the contract provided for that. The employer must supply the necessary equipment and reimburse agreed expenses, but there is no statutory home-office allowance. It is a tidy framework with one omission: it is entirely silent on what happens if you simply ask nicely.

The data show a sector that is large, export-oriented and no longer certain about home working. The Serbian government reports record ICT services exports of 4.552 billion euros in 2025, up 10 percent, and N1, citing National Bank of Serbia data, puts sector employment at 138,769. Yet an October 2025 survey by Osiguranik, TIM Centar, Resilient and Infostud, of 3,111 employees and 759 decision-makers, found that only 28 percent of employees can work remotely even occasionally, that almost 20 percent said their companies had introduced mandatory office days that year and that 63 percent of managers do not fully trust remote staff. Home working was nonetheless the most valued benefit for the second year running.

The retreat shows up in the advertisements. An analysis of HelloWorld IT job ads, reported by Vreme in September 2026, found remote roles in 40 percent of ads in both 2024 and 2025, falling to 26 percent in the first eight months of 2026, while office-based roles rose from 32 percent to 42 percent. For foreigners the entry route is more formal still. Vreme reports that newcomers wanting a work and residence visa generally must register a local business, and that a decree on incentives for new residents allows a 70 percent reduction in the tax and contribution base for those who qualify. Remote work in Serbia is attractive, but it arrives with an incorporation certificate.

## The Reckoning

The two countries differ less in outcome than in who is supposed to be protecting the employee. Ireland gives the worker a procedure: a written request, a statutory code, a tribunal and a review that admits fewer than half of workers know the procedure exists. Serbia gives the worker a contract, and the contract is only as good as the worker's bargaining position on the day it is signed. The numbers reflect the difference. Roughly one in five Irish employees usually works from home, against fewer than one in twenty in Serbia, and in both countries the pendulum has begun to swing towards the office.

For the expat, this makes Ireland the better place to be a remote employee and Serbia the better place to be a remote entrepreneur. The Irish system lets you ask a large employer for flexibility and be heard. The Serbian system lets you build your own employer, at a cost of accountants and visa paperwork, and keep the arrangement you design. Neither guarantees a spare room with a view.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Irish Times — paraphrased experience: a Los Angeles native who moved to Ireland with his wife two years earlier and runs a vehicle-charging company says employers should judge results rather than presence, and that he overdelivers because he regards remote work as a privilege.*

> *The Irish Times — paraphrased experience: an HR worker at a large insurer, after six years of remote work and a move out of Dublin city centre, says he misses social connection and would now prefer a hybrid role.*

> *Euronews — paraphrased experience: an Austrian who followed a partner to Belgrade runs a remote-only digital agency with clients in Austria, the UK and Ireland, and says choosing where and when he works is the whole point, helped by lower costs than Vienna.*

> *Expat.com — paraphrased experience: a freelance consultant in Belgrade who cites the US exemption for citizens abroad, in a decade-old thread, says he registered a limited company in about fifteen days, pays Serbian taxes only and needs an accountant, and that the drawback is being unable to withdraw money except as salary.*

> *Expat.com — paraphrased experience: a long-term resident says it is genuinely hard to find work in Serbia, that locals struggle too, and that most foreigners are there on work visas rather than the rarely issued partner-based residence.*

## Conclusion

If you intend to remain on an employer's payroll, Ireland gives you a procedure and a fair chance of a yes, but you should assume your employer would also like you on Tuesday and Thursday. If you intend to be your own employer, Serbia offers the low costs and incentives that remote entrepreneurs have found attractive, as long as you accept the incorporation, the accountant and a labour market in which office-based roles are again the majority of IT adverts.

In both cases, ask about the office policy before the relocation and not after it. The brochure promised flexibility. The contract will tell you what kind.
