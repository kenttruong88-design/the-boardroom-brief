# Iran Pays You Twice a Year Whether You Ask or Not. Canada Will Let You Go Your Whole Career Without Finding Out What Anyone Else Makes.

**🇮🇷 Iran · 🇨🇦 Canada**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Iran, your employer is legally obligated to hand you a bonus worth two months' salary before Nowruz, whether you asked for it or not. In Canada, 50 percent of workers have never told even a friend what they earn, let alone a colleague doing the identical job three desks over. One of these countries has built secrecy into the culture; the other has built generosity into the law. Neither one makes it easy to know what you're actually worth.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇷 Iran

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect your Eidi (Nowruz bonus) as a legal entitlement, not a gift | Assume your listed salary is the full compensation picture — allowances matter |
| Budget for monthly inflation adjustments, not annual ones | Treat a quoted rial figure as stable for more than a few weeks |
| Ask about housing, transport, and family allowances separately | Forget that severance is owed per year of service, by law, on exit |
| Expect overtime at a fixed 40 percent premium | Assume private-sector pay matches public-sector pay for the same title |
| Factor sector into expectations — oil, tech, and finance pay far better than education | Compare your rial salary to a foreign one without adjusting for the exchange rate daily |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research the provincial salary range before you interview | Assume a posted "range" means the top number is realistic |
| Negotiate — the tight labor market gives you more leverage than you think | Ask a Canadian colleague directly what they make |
| Push for a credential assessment early if moving from outside Canada | Expect Canadian tech pay to match US tech pay for the same role |
| Use the growing pay-transparency laws in your province to your advantage | Assume everyone else already knows the going rate — most don't discuss it either |
| Get licensing and association timelines in writing before you move for a job | Trust a recruiter's informal salary estimate over your own research |

### Iran

Iranian compensation is a layered, almost architectural thing. A base salary is only the starting point; on top of it sits a housing allowance, a transportation allowance, and a per-child family allowance, all itemized and all expected. Then comes the Eidi — a bonus equal to two months' basic salary, paid before the Nowruz new year, which Rivermate's compensation guide describes as a legal requirement rather than a discretionary gesture. Overtime carries a fixed 40 percent premium, and severance is owed at one month's salary per year of service on termination. It is, on paper, one of the more worker-protective structures going. In practice, the rial's inflation means salaries need near-constant renegotiation just to hold their value, and the sector you land in matters enormously: oil, gas, technology, and finance pay well above what education or public healthcare can offer for comparable seniority.

For Iranians who leave, the gap between credential and compensation can be brutal. IranWire's interviews with Iranian professionals navigating Canada found a pediatric specialist who, after fifteen years in the country, still cannot practice medicine there because Canada only recognizes credentials from a short list of countries that doesn't include Iran. A nurse who arrived in Quebec expecting roughly $4,000 a month instead discovered, in her words, that "up to 45% of your income is deducted for taxes," on top of licensing and retirement fees her immigration lawyer never mentioned — and then spent eighteen months on credential evaluation before she could even start.

### Canada

Canadian salary culture runs on an almost comic level of secrecy for a country that talks constantly about fairness. An Indeed/Hiring Lab survey found just 52 percent of Canadians feel they're paid fairly, yet 50 percent have never discussed their salary with a friend, 64 percent have never discussed it with a colleague of the same gender, and a full 68 percent have never discussed it with a colleague of a different one. Only a third feel comfortable negotiating at all. The upside, slowly, is regulatory: salary-range disclosure in job postings is spreading province by province, and 75 percent of Canadian job seekers say they're more likely to apply when a range is listed.

For newcomers specifically, the advice from settlement services like CIC News is blunt: don't assume you have no leverage. With job vacancies running near a million in a recent quarter, the labor market has historically favored candidates who negotiate with confidence, citing their home-country experience as a credential rather than a liability. The catch, as one Blind thread on Canadian tech pay put it, is that even a rising floor doesn't close the gap with the market everyone is implicitly comparing themselves to: "there was an increase in salaries that last couple of years. The floor is higher but no where near what one gets paid in the US."

### The Reckoning

Iran's system discloses almost everything about structure and nothing about amount — you know exactly what your Eidi and allowances are made of, down to the line item, but the number itself erodes by the week. Canada's system is the inverse: salary ranges are increasingly posted by law, but what your actual colleague earns stays a closely guarded secret even in offices that pride themselves on collegiality. Both countries, in other words, have found entirely different ways to keep an employee slightly in the dark about whether they're being paid fairly.

The credentials story completes the irony. Iran, for all its currency volatility, recognizes professional qualifications earned at home without question. Canada, a country actively recruiting skilled immigrants, routinely fails to recognize the very credentials that got those immigrants selected in the first place — turning pediatric specialists and radiologic technologists into long-term-care aides and rideshare drivers while their paperwork clears. Hofstede Insights data offers a tidy explanation for the secrecy gap: Canada scores a high 80 on individualism against Iran's 41, and a workplace built around individual achievement, it turns out, is also one where your pay is treated as nobody's business but your own.

### Conclusion

If predictability is what you want, Iran's compensation structure is more honest about its own mechanics, even while its currency makes long-term planning nearly impossible — you'll always know exactly what your Eidi and allowances are for. If you want a shot at currency stability, Canada offers that, but you'll be negotiating in the dark, guessing at what your coworkers earn, and quite possibly starting over professionally if your credentials didn't make the recognized list. The honest move for anyone making this jump either direction is the same: ask for the number in writing, in the specific currency you'll actually be living on, before you sign anything that depends on exchange rates or recognition boards you don't yet understand.
