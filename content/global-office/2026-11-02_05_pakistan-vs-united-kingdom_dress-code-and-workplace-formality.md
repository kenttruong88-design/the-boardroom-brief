**The Suit, the Shalwar, and the Lanyard: Pakistan and the UK Disagree on What Counts as Dressed**

🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Islamabad, a government office will not blink at a man in a hand-tailored shalwar kameez chairing a meeting of foreign dignitaries; in London, that same office will spend a working group's entire Tuesday deciding whether shorts are "smart" enough for a client call. Pakistan's formality is a question of modesty and hierarchy, worn into the fabric itself. Britain's formality is a question of vibes, negotiated anew every time someone's blazer collides with someone else's trainers. Neither country has solved dress code. They have simply disagreed, with great conviction, about what the problem even is — and about who gets to decide it for you on any given Tuesday.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep a tailored shalwar kameez or a suit on hand for client and government meetings | Assume a blazer excuses sleeveless or sheer fabric underneath |
| Dress more conservatively in public-facing or field roles than you would in a Western HQ | Wear shorts off a compound, campus, or hotel grounds |
| Carry a shawl or dupatta for women entering offices, mosques, or conservative neighbourhoods | Expect casual Friday energy; dress-down is the exception, not the rule |
| Match your formality to seniority — juniors dress up, not down, around superiors | Shake a woman's hand unless she offers hers first |
| Budget for custom tailoring; ready-made Western sizing is inconsistent | Wear anything see-through, tight, or overtly branded as "loungewear" to the office |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly what "smart casual" means at your specific employer — it varies wildly | Assume finance, law, or client-facing rules apply to your creative or tech job |
| Keep a jacket at your desk for unplanned client meetings | Wear a full suit to a startup and expect to blend in |
| Default slightly smarter than you think necessary in your first fortnight, then calibrate down | Treat the office thermostat as decorative; dress for the actual weather inside |
| Notice who dresses up on which days and follow that rhythm | Panic if colleagues don't comment on your outfit — feedback is rare and indirect |
| Save money by watching for seasonal dress-down periods many firms now permit | Buy a full wardrobe before arriving; norms shift once you're actually at your desk |

Pakistan's workplace is, officially, barely a workplace at all. The Pakistan Bureau of Statistics' Labour Force Survey for 2024-25 found that 80.8 percent of employed Pakistanis work informally, with formal jobs — the kind with a handbook, an HR department, and an actual dress code to violate — held by only 19.2 percent of the workforce. Formality, in other words, is a minority privilege, concentrated in banks, multinationals, and government ministries, which is exactly why those institutions guard it so fiercely. Hofstede Insights scores Pakistan at 70 on uncertainty avoidance, a preference for established protocol over improvisation, and a startlingly low 14 on individualism, meaning dress reads less as personal expression than as a signal of which group — family, firm, faith — you are representing that day.

The result is a formality that is both rigid and flexible in ways a newcomer rarely expects. The suit and the shalwar kameez sit as equals at the same boardroom table, per Cyborlink's business etiquette notes, with neither read as the lesser option. What is not negotiable is modesty: women are expected to cover arms and legs regardless of how senior they are, and expat accounts collected by Talesmag describe conservative norms as the one constant across Islamabad postings spanning more than a decade, even as everything else about the posting changed. One contributor, writing in 2022, found more formal events in Islamabad than anywhere else they'd worked — though, tellingly, they noted these were social, not mandatory.

Public institutions enforce this unevenly but loudly. Karachi's education authorities made national headlines in 2026 for formally banning teachers from wearing jeans, t-shirts, and tight-fitting clothing, following similar directives issued by Pakistan's Federal Directorate of Education years earlier. The policy specifics vary by province and employer, but the underlying message doesn't: formality in Pakistan is treated as a civic good worth legislating, not a matter of personal taste left to HR's discretion. A private bank and a public college might disagree on tailoring, but neither would entertain the idea that dress is nobody's business but the employee's.

The United Kingdom, by contrast, has spent two decades quietly and anxiously dismantling its own dress code, then wondering whether it went too far. A 2,000-plus-respondent survey covered by BM Magazine found that over a third of UK workers feel judged on what they wear, more than a quarter struggle to find office-appropriate clothes at all, and nearly four in ten would actually prefer a compulsory uniform to the ambiguity of "dress appropriately." That ambiguity has a name — smart casual — and almost nobody agrees on what it means, which is precisely the point: it lets employers claim standards exist without committing to what they are.

Formality in Britain now tracks sector more than nationality. DavidsonMorris's 2026 guide to UK dress codes places law and financial services at the formal end and creative, startup, and tech firms at the casual end, with hybrid work adding a further wrinkle: Korn Ferry reported that London's financial district returned to suits even as roughly a third of UK employees kept working from home at least part-time, and that firms increasingly reserve full formality for the two days a week staff are actually in the building. The suit, once a daily uniform, has become something closer to a costume worn for specific scenes — client meetings, board reviews — rather than the whole production.

The pressure, where it exists, is unevenly distributed and rarely about comfort at all. The same BM Magazine survey found recruitment staff felt the most pressure to dress to impress, at 59 percent, followed by advertising and PR at 56 percent and charity work at 51 percent — industries, notably, where the product being sold is often the employee's own credibility. Meanwhile a fifth of UK workplaces now tolerate skinny jeans and trainers, and roughly a tenth allow shorts or band t-shirts, which suggests the "rules" are less a wall than a sliding scale that most employees are left to triangulate alone.

**The Reckoning:** Here is the twist the brochure never mentions: Pakistan's dress code is more prescriptive but more comfortable with tradition, while Britain's is less prescriptive and far more anxious. A Pakistani professional has a clear hierarchy of acceptable formal options — suit or shalwar kameez, both correct — and the real rules concern modesty and context, not the specific garment. A British professional has almost no fixed rules at all, which sounds liberating until you are the one standing in a Monday morning meeting wondering if your trainers just cost you a promotion, because nobody will tell you, only silently judge you, per that same BM Magazine survey.

The other inversion: Pakistan's apparent rigidity is actually more forgiving of individual interpretation within its lanes, while Britain's apparent casualness hides a minefield of unstated sector-specific expectations that newcomers routinely misjudge. Dressing "too formally" in a Karachi bank is never a mistake; dressing "too formally" at a London media startup can read as try-hard. The country with the official dress code is, perversely, less likely to embarrass you than the one that insists it doesn't have one.

Hofstede's framework offers a tidy explanation for why: Pakistan's low individualism score of 14 means dress signals group belonging, a code anyone can learn and follow correctly, while Britain's high individualism score of 89 means dress is read as a personal statement, which makes it both more flexible and far harder to get definitively right.

### Conclusion

If you are relocating for work, the practical advice inverts what you'd expect. In Pakistan, overdress, because the ceiling is high, the floor is modesty, and nobody has ever been penalised for arriving too polished to a country where the Bureau of Statistics itself says four in five workers have no formal dress code to begin with. In the UK, ask — repeatedly, specifically, by department — because the national dress code is a rumour that varies by floor, and the only real sin is assuming last year's answer still holds. Budget accordingly, too: a mid-range UK suit runs toward £500 once shoes and shirts are added, while a Pakistani wardrobe splits its spending between a tailor and a dupatta, and both will feel, at first, like money spent guessing.

The honest version, the one I'd give a friend over a drink: pack one good suit and one good shalwar kameez for Pakistan, and pack absolutely nothing you're attached to for Britain, because within six months some manager who has never once discussed it with you will have silently decided what you should be wearing, and you will only find out by being wrong.
