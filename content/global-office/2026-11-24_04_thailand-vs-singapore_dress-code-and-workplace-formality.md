# Thailand Keeps the Jacket for the Boss, Singapore Keeps the Opinions About Your Sandals

🇹🇭 Thailand · 🇸🇬 Singapore

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries sit within a short flight of each other, share a climate best described as a warm, damp towel, and have reached opposite conclusions about what to put on over it. In Thailand the suit says who you are in the hierarchy. In Singapore it says you may have mistaken the office for a courtroom.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Thailand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress conservatively for client meetings, with a jacket and tie for men | Assume the tie is dead everywhere |
| Choose breathable cotton or silk, and carry the jacket if it is hot | Wear black to a formal occasion, as it is associated with funerals |
| Cover the shoulders and keep skirts around knee length | Treat shorts as acceptable office wear |
| Greet seniors first and return a wai when one is offered | Open a meeting by questioning someone senior |
| Use a title with the first name, or Khun, until invited to drop it | Skip the after-work outing and expect no one to notice |
| Watch what upper management wears before deciding what you will | Judge a commuter's casual look by the boardroom's standard |

### Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to business casual, which is the common standard | Arrive in a heavy suit and jacket for an ordinary day |
| Dress up for finance, law and client-facing roles | Assume the tech sector's rules apply to a bank |
| Wear light fabrics that still look neat | Mistake a relaxed code for no code |
| Keep accessories good but not showy | Wear the weekend's footwear to a client meeting |
| Check the company's own policy, since flexible codes vary | Take flexible dress as a licence for shorts |
| Notice what colleagues of your seniority wear | Confuse comfort with permission |

## Thailand: Formal Where It Counts, Hot Everywhere

Expat.com's guide to the Bangkok workplace describes the business dress code as quite conservative, with men typically in suits and ties and women in dresses or skirts, and notes that Thais are fashion-conscious. The Thaiger's etiquette guide goes into detail: dark suits and long sleeves for men, knee-length skirts and covered shoulders for women, no black because of funeral associations, and breathable fabrics because of the heat. It also says draping a jacket over a shoulder is acceptable in hot weather, which is perhaps the most practical sentence ever written about corporate dress.

Reality is looser at street level. A 2018 letter published by Ajarn, a site for teachers in Thailand, said professional men had drifted from semi-formal dress to business casual over the previous five to ten years, with full suits and ties mostly left to senior executives and formal events. An Expat.com forum thread makes the same point from the other side: ties have become less common, though jackets are still expected for upper management and sales staff. A FlyerTalk thread on Bangkok dress says jackets and ties turn up at meetings but everyday commuters rarely wear them, and one poster felt conspicuous in a jacket on the train.

The dress code is really a seniority code. Expat.com's guide says age and seniority matter as much as job title, that a junior should offer the wai to a senior, and that you should not openly question an older colleague. The Thaiger adds that you should stand when a senior person enters, let them speak first and greet the oldest person first. Hofstede Insights is the usual yardstick for this kind of thing, but its comparison table could not be read in full for this piece, so the argument rests on the etiquette guides, which agree with one another at tedious length. The jacket matters less than who is wearing it.

The Ajarn letter defines business casual with some precision: slacks, polished shoes, and either a short-sleeved button-down or a polo shirt. Its author considered the older long-sleeved shirt and tie look dated for most men, and noted that foreign teachers sort themselves by nationality, with British colleagues favouring semi-formal dress and Americans something more relaxed. Expat.com's guide adds that Thais tend to be reserved with new people and prefer to build a relationship before doing business, so the first meeting is a poor moment to test how casual the office really is. A Reddit thread on Thai workplace hierarchy offers a small, useful caution: one commenter said a published description of the dress code did not match their own workplace. Treat every guide, including this one, as a starting hypothesis.

## Singapore: Casual by Policy, Judgemental by Temperament

Singapore's business dress is, according to World Business Culture, more informal than in many Western countries and than in Japan or Korea. A shirt and trousers without a tie is typical for men, jackets are usually left at home, lighter colours are acceptable, and women wear lightweight suits. Expat accounts in TALES Magazine's post reports are consistent: business casual is the norm, people are seldom dressed up, and open-toed shoes are accepted in many offices.

Formality has not disappeared. It has moved indoors. The CIPD reports that PwC Singapore introduced a FlexDress Everyday policy asking employees to dress responsibly and use judgement, while noting that many firms find full casual dress hard to adopt. Page Personnel's Richa Sharma describes the barrier as largely psychological: some staff feel they do not work effectively when dressed casually. In her words, it is a mental game. Monash University's Fang Lee Cooke told the CIPD that flexible codes may not suit client-facing staff such as those at the Big Four firms, because being well dressed is central to impression management.

The Independent Singapore reports that ManpowerGroup's Linda Teo sees even traditionally formal sectors adopting smart casual or business casual, with finance and law still favouring uniformity. Stomp, meanwhile, recorded a 26-year-old civil servant calling a colleague's Birkenstocks sloppy and saying that dressing up shows pride and self-respect. A podcast joke about a petition to allow shorts at work drew more than 50,000 views on TikTok. The Independent concluded that shorts in meetings are unlikely to be accepted for now. The climate argues for shorts. The culture argues for a second opinion.

The CIPD article lists heat and humidity, and the needs of more working women, among the reasons for looser codes across Asia, and fear of missing business opportunities and of projecting an unprofessional image among the reasons for resisting them. It also notes that some FMCG, retail, media and IT firms already allow casual dress while others struggle with it. That is a fair description of Singapore: a country that has accepted the argument for comfort and is still working out what, exactly, it is being asked to give up. For a newcomer the safest reading is that the written policy tells you the floor, and your colleagues tell you the ceiling.

## The Reckoning

On paper, Singapore is the more relaxed of the two and Thailand the more formal. In practice the difference is one of mechanism. Thai dress follows rank: the senior man wears the jacket, the junior one carries a smaller version of the same expectation, and the heat is acknowledged but seldom treated as an argument. Singaporean dress follows context. The code is casual by default and formal when a client, a regulator or a partner is present, and colleagues enforce it by raised eyebrow rather than by memo.

The newcomer should therefore watch different things. In Thailand, watch the people above you and match their level on formal days. In Singapore, watch your sector, because the code in a software firm and the code in a law firm are separated by more than a few degrees of tailoring. In both places, being slightly overdressed costs little and being visibly underdressed costs more. One country forgives the jacket. The other forgives nothing but is too polite to say so.

## Conclusion

If you are moving to Thailand, pack for conservative first impressions: a good jacket you may carry more than wear, covered shoulders, and respect for age and rank in how you greet people. Then adjust downward once you see what your own office does. If you are moving to Singapore, assume business casual, check the sector and the client, and avoid mistaking a flexible policy for an absence of opinions.

Neither country asks you to be uncomfortable for its own sake. Both ask you to read the room, which in this climate is at least a pleasant place to stand.
