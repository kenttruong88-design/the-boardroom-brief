# Kenya Surveys Its Onboarding, Ghana Recommends a Mentor: Both Expect You to Swim

🇰🇪 Kenya · 🇬🇭 Ghana
*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

When BrighterMonday Kenya asked 2,059 employers and HR practitioners about onboarding, 83 percent called it very important to their talent strategy, and 76 percent said mentoring and coaching was their most common development programme. When Gallup asked Kenyan workers about their own jobs, 81 percent said they were watching for or actively seeking a new one. Both sets of numbers are accurate, and the gap between them is where the arriving professional lives. Ghana, for its part, produces the same gap with a different accent.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Insist the probationary contract is in writing and states that it is probationary | Accept a verbal assurance that you are on probation |
| Ask in week one who your buddy or mentor is | Wait to be assigned one |
| Spend early time learning colleagues, not just tasks | Rush straight into deliverables and skip the introductions |
| Note the probation limit of six months, extendable only with your consent | Agree to open-ended extensions |
| Ask what the first 90 days should produce, and in writing | Assume the induction covered what success looks like |
| Mind the seven-day notice during probation | Treat probation as a grace period for either side |

### Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat the first meetings as a test of whether you are pleasant to work with | Open with the agenda |
| Check that your probation does not exceed six months | Accept a longer probation without a new contract |
| Seek out a mentor yourself among senior colleagues | Assume the HR orientation will supply one |
| Ask for tasks to be assigned explicitly, with scope and owner | Guess what a loosely assigned task means |
| Expect to help train newer colleagues' basics, such as memos and minutes | Assume new hires arrive job-ready |
| Confirm work-permit responsibility before you sign a local contract | Take a local hire on the assumption the employer will handle the permit |

## Kenya

Kenya treats onboarding as a topic of research, which is not the same as a practice, but it is a start. BrighterMonday's 2022 survey separates induction, which teaches the organisation and the role, from onboarding, which helps the newcomer adjust and build relationships. The most common induction methods it found were discussing company mission, goals and values (34 percent), administrative essentials (30 percent), personalising the experience (21 percent) and addressing work culture (14 percent). Assigning a peer buddy or onboarding mentor is listed among the common methods, though without a percentage attached.

The academic evidence is modest but consistent. A 2021 Kenyatta University study of 254 employees across 19 advertising firms found formal, self-directed and mentored induction all significantly related to staff turnover, and recommended self-directed and mentored induction as the most effective at curbing it. That does not prove every Nairobi employer provides one, merely that those who do are on the right side of the data. The legal frame is clear: according to CM Advocates, a probationary contract must be in writing, is limited to six months, may be extended by up to six months with the employee's consent, and cannot exceed twelve months in total, with either side able to end it on seven days' written notice.

The human version is more mixed. Hofstede Insights, in the regional figures relayed by Cyborlink, puts East Africa at a power distance of 64 and an individualism score of 27, which is academic shorthand for a hierarchical workplace in which groups matter. Expat Arrivals similarly notes that Kenyan business culture emphasises relationships and warns against rushing meetings. The newcomer who treats the first month as relationship-building is therefore working with the grain. The one who treats it as a sprint to deliverables may find the grain has opinions.

The outcome data give pause. Gallup's 2024 release for sub-Saharan Africa found that 18 percent of Kenyan employees are engaged and 16 percent thriving, with 34 percent reporting a lot of stress the previous day. Pairing that with the finding that 81 percent are watching for a new job suggests that a good induction is necessary, though far from sufficient, to keep a person past the first year. It may also explain why Kenyan employers care so much about probation: the first six months are when both sides discover whether the brochure was accurate.

## Ghana

Ghana's onboarding record is better documented in the public sector than the private. The Office of the Head of the Civil Service ran a graduate onboarding study, and its Phase Two report in 2021 drew 316 responses from officers on probation across 16 regions, down from 423 in Phase One. It recommended building mentor-mentee relationships to help new officers integrate, and it found that 56.64 percent of respondents agreed that tasks were assigned directly to them while 22.79 percent disagreed, which the report took as a sign that work planning needed to be clearer. A Durham University project, listed by the International Growth Centre, was set up to examine how line managers shape the socialisation of civil service recruits hired in 2020 and 2021, and its page records expected findings rather than results.

The private sector inherits a skills gap as its starting point. Writing for Brookings in 2020, William Baah-Boateng and Eric Kofi Twum reported that managers at twenty manufacturing firms said new entrants lack skills, that employers often have to provide additional training, and that internships at universities are usually not required and loosely organised in polytechnics. Humanities and business graduates, they added, reportedly struggle with memos, minutes and reports. On the legal side, Asanify reports that the maximum probation under Section 14 of the 2003 Labour Act is six months, with a minimum notice of one week.

Engagement is where the picture darkens. Gallup's 2025 figures put 8 percent of Ghanaian employees as engaged, against 19 percent for sub-Saharan Africa, and its 2024 release found that 71 percent are watching for or seeking another job. Expat Arrivals adds a cultural note that first meetings focus on whether the parties will work well together personally, and that Ghanaians value maintaining face. For the new hire this means that asking publicly where the training is may be received as a complaint. Asking privately is received as ambition.

Ghana's own HR press is aware of the problem, in general terms. Jobberman Ghana argued in June 2025 that onboarding begins before day one and can run for several months, that attrition is often highest in the first few months, and that mentorship, early wins and psychological safety make staff more engaged and loyal. The article carries no local statistics and does not mention Ghanaian law, so it is best read as an aspiration with a Ghanaian byline. It is nonetheless the aspiration that a sensible employer will adopt, and a sensible applicant can ask about.

## The Reckoning

Head to head, Kenya has the more visible vocabulary and Ghana the more visible public-sector paper trail. Kenyan employers say they value induction and say they offer mentoring; Ghana's civil service has measured its onboarding and recommended mentors, which is the bureaucratic equivalent of a mentor. Both systems cap probation at six months, though Kenya allows extension to twelve with consent, and both give either side a week's notice, so the legal protection during the awkward first months is similar and thin.

The practical difference is who does the work. In Kenya, the evidence points to a hiring culture that has accepted the case for structured induction and sometimes delivers it, with the buddy or mentor a named option. In Ghana, the evidence points to employers who compensate for thin preparation through on-the-job training, with mentorship a recommendation more often than a rule. In neither country is it wise to assume that a mentor will arrive unasked. The pattern in the accounts collected here is that mentorship often exists as an informal act by a particular senior colleague, and that the newcomer who identifies that person early appears, judging by the accounts below, to do better than the one who waits for a programme to find him.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com (Kenya forum) — a poster advised newcomers that once hired, employers can ask a great deal of new staff, and that foreigners are often expected to work overtime and weekends because they receive higher allowances. Hours and Saturday work depend on the company.*

> *Nation Africa — a young Kenyan presenter who joined a radio station while still a second-year student said veteran journalists mentored him, his supervisor sparked ideas and then gave the team room to run its projects, and everyone could contribute in planning meetings. He was unpaid and accepted that as the price of experience.*

> *The Filipino Expat — a Nairobi-based writer hired to lead a college's growth phase recalled no formal onboarding and no mentor, a strict hierarchy in which faculty seemed uneasy when she ate lunch with them, and an owner who overruled her decisions. She resigned after three months, though she later had better experiences in other Kenyan workplaces.*

> *MyJoyOnline — a graduate on UBA Ghana's management acceleration programme described joining with little banking experience, fearing rejection in his first sales calls, and building confidence by speaking to more customers and learning from experienced colleagues rather than through any formal mentoring arrangement.*

> *Expat.com (Ghana forum) — an experienced poster cautioned that a job arranged abroad with an employer operating in Ghana usually pays an expatriate-level salary, while a local hire typically pays less and omits housing and medical benefits, and that employers often will not pay for the work permit, which can leave you working illegally.*

## Conclusion

If structure matters to you, Kenya offers the better odds of a named induction and a plausible mentor, particularly in larger firms that have read the same surveys as BrighterMonday. If you can supply your own structure, Ghana's institutions have begun to recognise the problem, and its civil service has measured it, though the private sector is still a matter of asking around. In either country, the contract matters more than the welcome speech: get the probation terms in writing, confirm who is responsible for the work permit, and find a mentor in week one rather than month four.

Whichever flag is on the letterhead, onboarding is something you will probably do to yourself, with institutional encouragement. The surveys say everyone agrees it is important, which is the sort of agreement that costs nothing.
