# Office Friendship, Itemized: What Czechia and Austria Actually Charge You For It

**🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇦🇹 Austria**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Two Central European offices, two completely different theories of what your coworkers owe you emotionally. In Czechia, Hofstede Insights scores Power Distance at a middling 57 — a hierarchy visibly inherited from the command economy — yet the actual lunch ritual involves a beer, slippers under the desk, and a studied refusal to ask how your weekend was. In Austria, the same framework records a Power Distance of 11, the lowest figure in that same dataset's entire original run, officially making it one of the most egalitarian workplaces on earth — while employees who have known each other for a decade still address one another as "Sie" unless formally invited to switch. Neither country's org chart tells you anything true about how lonely, or how social, your actual Tuesday will be.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take the roughly 11:30am lunch break seriously — it is where informal business actually happens | Treat a beer with lunch as a red flag about a colleague's professionalism; it is normalized, not a warning sign |
| Keep a pair of indoor shoes under your desk if you plan to stay past your first year | Expect colleagues to ask about your weekend on Monday morning; many keep work and friendship deliberately separate |
| Bring cake or chlebíčky to mark your name day or birthday — reciprocity is expected | Assume the pub-lunch tradition is permanent; rising food costs are pushing roughly half of workers toward desk lunches instead |
| Use titles and formal address with older or senior colleagues, even after years together | Schedule anything urgent during "cucumber season" (okurková sezóna), the slow, auto-reply-heavy stretch from July to early September |
| Watch what your office actually wears before committing to a dress code | Expect your Prague office's working hours to be short — Czech employees log some of the longest hours in the EU |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask early whether your company uses "Sie" or "Du" — conventions vary by employer, even within Vienna | Push for informal "Du" with a superior before it is offered |
| Treat the after-work beer (or table football) as a real, if optional, social institution | Assume everyone wants to attend; roughly a third of employees in one Austrian survey called such outings annoying |
| Host your own birthday with cake or schnapps if your office follows that custom | Skip the office Christmas party without a good excuse; it is closer to semi-mandatory than optional |
| Say "Mahlzeit" to colleagues near lunchtime, even well before noon | Expect quick emotional intimacy; Austrians are frequently described as reserved and slow to open up socially |
| Take Gleitzeit (flexible-hours) scheduling at face value if your employer offers it | Mistake formal titles and "Sie" for a rigid, top-down culture — the same index says otherwise |

### Section: Czechia

The paperwork says Czech employees are long-hours people, and the paperwork is right. According to Eurostat figures, Czech full-time employees average 41.3 hours a week, against an EU full-time average of 40.7, and separate OECD annual-hours data puts Czechia among the EU's three longest-working countries, trailing only Greece and Poland. None of that extra time, however, is spent performing enthusiasm about it. The defining ritual is the lunch break — traditionally a proper sit-down affair, often at a nearby pub, often with a beer that nobody treats as scandalous — but it is a ritual currently under economic siege. Czech media report that rising food costs have pushed roughly 62 percent of workers to cut back on eating out, with the average lunch now running about 197 Czech crowns, and that close to half of employees now eat at their desks instead, in offices where only one in ten actually has a dedicated place to do it.

What survives the inflation is the formality underneath the beer. The same index puts Czech Individualism at 58 and Power Distance at 57 — a workplace where hierarchy and titles still carry real weight, a legacy of decades of centrally managed enterprise that international and tech-sector employers are only gradually softening. Socially, this produces a particular kind of office: warm at the table, correct everywhere else. Colleagues mark name days and birthdays with cake, handshakes, and the ritual phrase "všechno nejlepší," but Monday-morning small talk about your weekend is not guaranteed, because a meaningful number of Czechs treat colleagues and friends as two separate categories that happen to share a building.

### Section: Austria

Austria's official numbers describe a shorter, more fragmented workweek. Statistics Austria's most recent figures put the average across all employed people at 35.4 hours, pulled down by a part-time workforce that now makes up nearly a third of all employment; full-time employees alone average 41.6 hours, almost identical to their Czech counterparts. The social architecture built on top of those hours, though, runs on entirely different wiring. That same dataset gives Austria a Power Distance score of just 11 — the lowest recorded anywhere in the original study, officially the flattest hierarchy on the list — a result that sits oddly next to an office culture where "Sie" remains the polite default and where, per reporting from The Local Austria, the greeting "Mahlzeit" gets deployed well before lunchtime actually arrives.

Where Austria compensates is in scheduled bonding. After-work beers are a standard team activity, table football is taken seriously enough to confer real social status, and birthdays come with an expectation that the person celebrating treats colleagues to cake or schnapps — Sachertorte is a safe, if unoriginal, choice. A 2017 karriere.at employee survey found that only 12 percent of respondents genuinely enjoyed these outings as team-building; 31 percent called them an annoyance, while managers polled separately were noticeably more enthusiastic about their value than the staff actually attending them. The office Christmas party, by contrast, reads as closer to compulsory than optional, spritzer in hand.

### Section: The Reckoning

Set the two countries' full-time weekly hours side by side — 41.3 in Czechia, 41.6 in Austria — and the spreadsheet says you are choosing between near-identical jobs. The social contract wrapped around those hours could not be more different. Czechia's comparatively high Power Distance score (57) predicts a formal, titled office, and then delivers a beer with lunch and slippers under the desk; Austria's famously low score (11) predicts a flat, egalitarian one, and then delivers a decade of "Sie" with your own manager. Neither country's stated hierarchy level is a reliable guide to how it will actually feel to sit in that office.

The deeper divergence is about what counts as bonding at all. Czech colleagues tend to treat friendship and colleagueship as genuinely separate relationships, which means skipping an after-work drink costs you little; Austrian colleagues have built an entire, semi-obligatory social calendar — Christmas parties, birthday schnapps, table football — around proving otherwise, even while a third of the people attending privately consider it a chore. Move for the org chart in either country, and you will be mildly misled; move for the lunch break, and at least Czechia's is honest about what it has become.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — a tech-worker thread on relocating to Prague describes the move paying off mainly for juniors, postgrads, and freelancers serving Western clients, with one commenter warning the appeal fades within about a year and that long-term career growth there can feel limited compared to Western Europe.*

> *Expats.cz reader voices — a reader who relocated to Czechia described the local combination of a heavy midday meal and a work lunch beer bluntly: eat and drink that much at noon, and you need to lie down afterward, a habit they'd never had before moving.*

> *Welcome to the Jungle (named interview, Axelle Gasne) — a French professional who spent nearly eight years in an Austrian R&D department recalled addressing the company's head informally as "Hans" from early on, and having a casual internal seminar idea approved and run for six months, a flatness she hadn't expected going in.*

> *ExpatExchange.com forum — an anonymous expat in Vienna described Austrians as generally private people who take a while to open up, a reserve that newcomers often misread at the office as coldness rather than simply a slower social clock.*

> *karriere.at survey respondents (aggregate, not an individual account) — the clearest piece of advice buried in the data: don't assume an after-work invitation signals real bonding. Nearly a third of Austrian employees surveyed called such outings an annoyance, tolerating only the clearly mandatory ones like the Christmas party.*

### Conclusion

If the thing you actually want from a move abroad is coworkers who become friends, neither country is going to hand that to you for free, and the instruction manuals point in opposite directions. Czechia will be straightforward about the boundary — colleagues are colleagues, the beer at lunch is real but the invitation to your actual life mostly isn't, and that honesty is arguably the nicer gift once you've been there a year. Austria will hand you a packed social calendar of birthdays, table football, and Christmas parties that looks like intimacy and is, per its own employees' surveys, often experienced as homework.

Pick Czechia if you want your coworkers to leave you alone outside working hours and mean it; pick Austria if you want a structured, slightly reluctant simulation of a workplace friend group, complete with its own strange paperwork of who gets to call whom "Du." Either way, bring your own lunch plan — the pub ritual in Prague is getting priced out, and the Viennese one never really included you until someone handed you a schnapps.
