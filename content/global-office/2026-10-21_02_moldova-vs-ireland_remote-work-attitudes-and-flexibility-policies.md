**Dublin Will Give You Two Days a Week in the Office. Chisinau Will Give You a Visa for Never Showing Up At All.**

🇲🇩 Moldova · 🇮🇪 Ireland

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Ireland has the second-highest at-home working rate in the European Union — 19.2 percent of its workforce, trailing only Finland — and is still locked in a slow-motion tug-of-war over whether staff must come in two days a week or three. Moldova, a country Eurostat does not even bother to measure for this metric, has simply issued a two-year visa that lets a foreigner work from Chisinau for a company anywhere else on earth, no office opinion required. One of these countries is negotiating flexibility in committee rooms; the other skipped the meeting.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇩 Moldova

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Apply for the digital nomad visa if you're freelancing for a foreign employer — it's built for exactly this, with minimal local bureaucracy | Expect a thriving domestic remote-work culture to match the visa infrastructure — most of this is aimed at outsiders, not local staff norms |
| Budget for very low local salary scales if you're hired locally rather than remotely by a foreign firm | Assume IT-sector norms (flexible, foreign-facing, well-paid) apply to the rest of the economy — they largely don't |
| Expect embassy work and remote contracts to be the default path for expatriate spouses and partners | Skip dressing properly for in-person meetings — locals are known to dress sharply for work, visa or no visa |
| Take advantage of affordable, full-day childcare if you're balancing remote work with a family here | Expect a remote-first HR department to chase you about where you're logging in from — nobody is set up to police this |
| Treat the Moldova Innovation Technology Park's near-complete virtual onboarding (one visit required) as the real local benchmark for flexibility | Overlook that this flexibility is a tax-and-investment strategy first, not a cultural preference — residency guarantees run to 2037 for a reason |

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clarify your required office days in writing before signing — two or three days a week is now the norm, not a courtesy | Assume remote flexibility is permanent; roughly a third of firms already mandate set days, and half may by 2027 |
| Expect "hushed hybrid" — managers quietly tolerating more remote time than policy states — especially if you're a strong performer | Casually mention working from a café outside Dublin; most firms restrict the physical locations you're allowed to log in from |
| Use productivity and retention arguments, not personal preference, when negotiating remote days — that's what sways employers | Expect universal sympathy for remote work; roughly 40 percent of employers want it scaled back, and HQ decisions abroad can override local goodwill |
| Ask directly how your team handles promotions for hybrid staff — a large share of Irish firms admit progression is harder for them | Treat full remote as risk-free; one in five surveyed workers describe genuine loneliness from missing in-person colleague contact |
| Expect a formal written remote-work policy at larger firms, and informal, manager-by-manager arrangements at smaller ones | Assume Dublin's tech-hub reputation means every sector is this flexible; financial services firms are already formalizing three-to-four office days |

Ireland's number is the headline: 19.2 percent of the workforce usually worked from home in 2025, more than double the EU average of 8.8 percent, according to Eurostat data reported by Euronews — second only to Finland. But the headline is aging badly in real time. A William Fry-commissioned Ipsos B&A survey of 404 businesses found that about 34 percent of larger firms already require specific in-office days, a figure projected to climb to 50 percent by 2027. The CSO's own Labour Force Survey shows roughly 956,700 people worked from home in the final quarter of 2025 — a number that actually dipped 1.6 percent from the previous quarter while office attendance climbed back toward 2019 levels. Ireland's Hofstede Insights profile helps explain the tension underneath the numbers: individualism scores a high 70, meaning employees expect to be trusted to manage their own time, while a moderate uncertainty-avoidance score of 35 means the workforce tolerates the current ambiguity about where policy is headed better than it might elsewhere — for now.

Moldova offers almost no comparable domestic statistic, because almost nobody is measuring Moldovan employees working from home for Moldovan employers — Eurostat's European telework rankings don't include the country at all. What Moldova has instead is an aggressively externally-facing flexibility regime: the IT sector alone contributed 8.3 percent of GDP in 2023, employs 4.4 percent of the national workforce (the second-highest ratio in emerging Europe after Estonia), and the Moldova Innovation Technology Park hosts roughly 1,600 resident companies — including 178 foreign-owned ones — on a 7 percent flat tax guaranteed through 2035. New residents typically need to visit the country only once to register. This isn't a cultural embrace of flexible work so much as an investment-promotion strategy that happens to produce flexible work as a side effect; the country has no individually published Hofstede dimension score, having sat outside the original roughly 40-country study, so there's no official cultural-values shorthand for how locally-employed Moldovans actually feel about any of this.

### The Reckoning

The irony is that Ireland, which talks obsessively about remote work and measures it constantly, is actively tightening it — Eversheds Sutherland found 77 percent of surveyed employers already restrict exactly where staff are permitted to log in from, and "hushed hybrid," informal manager tolerance that quietly exceeds official policy, is now common enough that 43 percent of managers admit to allowing it. Moldova, which barely discusses remote work as a domestic cultural question at all, has built one of the most frictionless remote-work-adjacent legal regimes in Europe, provided you are a foreigner working for someone else's company. Ireland's flexibility is shrinking under its own spotlight; Moldova's exists almost entirely outside the spotlight, aimed at people who were never going to work in a Moldovan office anyway.

Put plainly: if you want a fight about your right to work from home, have that fight in Dublin, where there's a whole apparatus of surveys, solicitors, and union backlash built to have it with you. If you just want to work from home without having the argument at all, Chisinau will happily process your paperwork and never ask.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Irish Times (remote-workers feature) — A woman who relocated to Co Donegal from Australia described the strange double bind of full remote work candidly: working as though she had no family, then parenting as though she had no full-time job, with early days spent logging on while her Australian colleagues were asleep and she was still trying to build a life in a new country.*

> *The Irish Times (remote-workers feature) — A Dublin-based founder was blunt about treating remote flexibility as a privilege rather than an entitlement, saying she overdelivers precisely because she knows what she has, and that she'd be genuinely annoyed by an employer who wanted her in the office purely to be watched working.*

> *The Irish Times (remote-workers feature) — A Dublin professional in a remote HR role admitted the one real cost of the arrangement: he misses the social connection of being around colleagues, enough that he's started attending meetups specifically built for remote workers who feel the same gap.*

> *Talesmag ("Real Post Report," Chisinau) — An expat spouse in Chisinau described embassy postings and remote foreign contracts as simply the default path for partners there, flagging that local salary scales run far below what expatriates earn, that a full working day of affordable childcare is easy to arrange, and that Moldovans dress sharply for work whether or not anyone from head office is watching.*

### Conclusion

If you're an Irish professional sizing up a move anywhere else, the real transferable skill isn't remote work itself — it's the muscle for negotiating it formally, in writing, against an employer who is actively reconsidering the deal. If you're weighing Moldova specifically, understand you're not buying into a domestic remote-work culture so much as a well-engineered side door: a tax regime that happens to let you work from wherever you already are. Ask an Irish employer for their written remote policy before you sign; ask a Moldovan one whether they have any domestic staff working the same way you will be — the silence in the second answer tells you more than the visa paperwork does.
