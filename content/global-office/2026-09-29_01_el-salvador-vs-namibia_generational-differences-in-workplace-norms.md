# El Salvador Still Wants You in the Office. Namibia's Youngest Workers Just Want an Office to Exist.

**🇸🇻 El Salvador · 🇳🇦 Namibia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries, two very different generational anxieties: in San Salvador, a twenty-six-year-old associate is fighting to keep the remote-work privileges her employer grudgingly allowed during lockdown and has been trying to claw back ever since; in Windhoek, a twenty-six-year-old graduate is fighting simply to find an employer. One nation's youth are relitigating the terms of the office. The other's are still waiting for an invitation to it. Anyone weighing a move to either should understand which fight they're walking into before they start negotiating anything else.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇻 El Salvador

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use *Señor*/*Señora* and surnames until told otherwise — first-name informality reads as presumptuous | Don't assume Slack-speak translates; email and phone calls still carry more institutional weight than a DM |
| Arrive on time, especially for meetings above the junior level — punctuality signals seriousness | Don't skip small talk before business; leading with the agenda before asking about family reads as cold |
| Dress formally for office meetings — suits and ties remain the default, not the exception | Don't push hard negotiation tactics; diplomacy outperforms aggression in almost every documented account |
| Expect a in-office-first default even at companies that technically allow remote work | Don't assume a remote-work policy on paper means remote work in practice — managers can and do walk it back |
| Build the relationship before the ask — trust is a prerequisite, not a nicety | Don't be surprised if "yes" in a meeting isn't final; follow-up in writing to confirm specifics |

#### 🇳🇦 Namibia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress neatly and formally — personal presentation reads as respect for the institution and colleagues | Don't treat appearance as optional even in warm climates; khakis and collared shirts are the baseline |
| Budget real time for relationship-building before expecting fast decisions | Don't expect the 45-hour statutory workweek ceiling to mean a fast-paced, transaction-first culture |
| Ask about colleagues' families and communities before diving into agenda items | Don't interpret a slower cadence as disengagement — it's usually the opposite |
| Take youth unemployment seriously as workplace context — junior hires may be supporting extended family on one salary | Don't assume seniority runs on tenure alone; deference to department heads is real but not blind |
| Expect payday rituals — many workplaces treat paydays as informal social occasions | Don't schedule high-stakes deliverables for payday afternoons |

### El Salvador: The Office That Won't Let Go

El Salvador's corporate culture remains stubbornly in-person, and the pandemic barely dented it. One widely cited regional study named El Salvador among the Central American countries with the lowest rate of remote-work adoption, and the accounts bear that out: employees describe employers who kept requiring in-office attendance through lockdown, commutes conducted "with the military and police in the streets," and remote-work requests treated as a favor rather than a default. According to BizLatin Hub's business-etiquette guide, the underlying culture is formal to its bones — honorifics before first names, business cards presented with care, suits expected even at meetings that in most OECD economies would now be conducted in a hoodie over video.

What's notable is what happens when the exception is granted. Workers who did manage to negotiate remote arrangements report outsized rewards: not incremental flexibility but a genuine step-change in salary, safety, and family time, in some documented cases multiples of previous earnings after switching to a remote employer. That gap between what's on offer domestically and what's available to anyone with a laptop and an international client base is precisely why San Salvador's younger professionals increasingly treat remote work not as a perk to request but as a reason to leave the local job market altogether — a dynamic corporate employers in El Salvador have been slow to price into their retention strategy.

Hierarchy here is also generational in the traditional sense: deference to seniority, formality with elders, patience with a negotiating style that prizes relationship over speed. A newly arrived expat manager who tries to run a flat, Silicon-Valley-style org chart will find the org chart nodding politely and then quietly reasserting itself.

### Namibia: A Youth Cohort Still Waiting for the Office

Namibia's defining workplace generational story isn't a clash over remote-work norms — it's the more basic problem of getting hired at all. Afrobarometer's most recent dispatch on the country found Namibian youth naming unemployment as the nation's single most urgent national problem, a finding that tracks with years of prior surveys and with the National Planning Commission's own youth-employment policy briefs. Post-secondary education has expanded, but as The Namibian has reported, it hasn't translated into proportional job creation — a mismatch that leaves a large cohort of qualified young graduates underemployed or informally employed while formal-sector vacancies stay scarce.

For those who do land formal roles, the workplace itself skews relationship-first rather than efficiency-first. Rivermate's compliance guide sets the formal floor — a maximum 45-hour statutory workweek — but the lived texture, per first-person accounts from foreign professionals who've worked inside Namibian institutions, is one where community integration and sustained trust-building outrank speed: colleagues who go to town together on payday, department heads who extend real flexibility once rapport is established, workplaces where showing up and being known matters as much as output. The Peace Corps's own guidance to incoming volunteers underlines the same point from a different angle — personal presentation and neat dress are treated as a form of respect for the community, not mere professionalism-theater.

The generational tension that does exist in Namibia runs less along "flexible young workers vs. rigid bosses" and more along "credentialed, underemployed young graduates vs. an economy still organized around resource-sector jobs that don't require a degree" — a structural mismatch, not a values clash.

### The Reckoning

Put them side by side and the irony sharpens: El Salvador's young professionals have jobs and are fighting over the terms of doing them; Namibia's young professionals are fighting to get a job on any terms. A Salvadoran twenty-something's generational complaint is "my employer won't let me work from home." A Namibian twenty-something's is "there is no employer." These are not the same problem wearing different cultural clothes — they're genuinely different economic conditions that happen to fall under the same headline of "generational friction at work."

Hofstede's power-distance framework offers a small, useful data point here: both societies score on the higher end for hierarchy-acceptance relative to Northern Europe, which explains why an expat manager arriving from a flat, low-hierarchy culture will find both office cultures more formal, more relationship-gated, and more deferential to seniority than they're used to — but for structurally different underlying reasons. In El Salvador that formality coexists with abundant, contested jobs. In Namibia it coexists with genuine scarcity.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *xoandrew's Substack, "Destination El Salvador"* — A remote worker named Gabi described her Salvadoran employer initially refusing pandemic-era remote-work requests outright, forcing her to commute at night past military checkpoints. When she eventually moved to a fully remote international role instead, she reported a threefold salary jump and, in her words, "far less stress and far more safety and security" — a reminder that in El Salvador, the biggest career upgrade often isn't a promotion, it's an employer based somewhere else entirely.

> *namfam, personal WordPress blog on life and work in Namibia* — A volunteer teaching at a Namibian institution described a curriculum culture that was "extremely methodical and prescriptive," with rigid, committee-set requirements and built-in "second opportunity" exams for nearly every assessment — but also department heads who, despite having every formal right to pile on more responsibilities, consistently chose not to. The lesson: authority in Namibian institutions is real, but it's exercised with more restraint than the org chart would suggest.

> *Peace Corps Namibia volunteer guide* — Incoming volunteers are told plainly that "neat and culturally appropriate attire is highly valued, especially in professional settings," and that groups of colleagues routinely "go to town to shop and relax on paydays before heading off to visit their families" — a small detail that tells newcomers more about how Namibian workplaces actually run than any org chart would.

> *BizLatin Hub, El Salvador business-etiquette guide* — Foreign businesspeople are warned that Salvadoran counterparts "openly share business objectives but expect clarity in return," and that skipping the small talk — questions about family, food, the week's news — before getting to the agenda is read as coldness, not efficiency. The advice given to newcomers: budget real time for the relationship before you budget time for the deal.

### Conclusion

If you're weighing El Salvador, the practical question is whether you can tolerate a formal, in-office-first culture that has been slower than almost anywhere else in the hemisphere to normalize remote work — and whether you're prepared to watch your most talented local colleagues quietly defect to remote employers who pay in dollars from somewhere else. If you're weighing Namibia, the practical question is different: whether you're arriving to fill a genuine skills gap in a formal sector that has more jobs than qualified applicants in specific fields, or whether you're another credentialed competitor in a labor market where the country's own youth are telling every pollster who asks that unemployment, not workplace culture, is the crisis. Neither country's twenty-somethings are wrong to be frustrated. They're just not frustrated about the same thing — and if a friend asked me over a drink, I'd tell them: in San Salvador, negotiate for the laptop clause; in Windhoek, don't take the job for granted, because someone very qualified would take it in a heartbeat.
