**Thirty Years at the Same Desk vs. A New Job Every Three: Mexico vs. Norway**

**🇲🇽 Mexico · 🇳🇴 Norway**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Ask a Mexican professional how long they've been at their company and a five-year answer barely registers as a milestone. Ask a Norwegian professional the same question and five years might already be pushing toward "why haven't you moved yet." Both are considered stable, healthy careers in their own countries. The definition of "stable" is doing very different work in each.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat long tenure at one employer as a genuine asset on your résumé, not a red flag | Frame frequent job changes as ambition — it can read as instability to Mexican hiring managers |
| Build a personal relationship with your "patrón" (boss) — loyalty flows through people, not just titles | Expect promotion to move purely on merit and speed; relationships and tenure matter |
| Understand that switching jobs often means starting over on trust, not just responsibilities | Assume your accumulated seniority or reputation transfers automatically to a new employer |
| Expect real wage growth to reward staying — tenure correlates with pay progression here | Undervalue non-cash loyalty signals like informal flexibility your employer grants long-term staff |
| Ask directly about internal growth paths before job-hopping out of frustration | Leave a stagnant role without first testing whether internal mobility is actually available |

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---||
| Treat 2–4 years in a role as a completely normal, even expected, career rhythm | Apologize for job-hopping in interviews — it's read as initiative, not disloyalty |
| Use each role explicitly as a stepping stone and say so openly when it's true | Assume long tenure signals competence; it can just as easily read as low ambition |
| Expect employers to value relevant experience over formal loyalty or credentials | Expect a counteroffer to keep you if you announce you're leaving — it's less common here |
| Leverage Norway's strong job security to move toward better fit, not just better pay | Switch jobs reflexively; with security already high, movement should serve a clear purpose |
| Keep your network active — mobility runs on relationships across companies, not within one | Rely on a single employer's internal ladder as your only path upward |

---

Mexican employment culture still runs substantially on the idea of the patrón — the boss as a figure of loyalty, not just management, an inheritance from a business culture where following direction from an established leader is often more valued than striking out independently (Quora discussions on Mexican business culture). The data backs the sentiment: research on Mexican labor markets found that 38.3% of registered workers experienced job turnover at least once, concentrated heavily among younger workers, while turnover falls sharply with higher education and income — meaning the most stable, best-compensated roles are also the ones people stay in longest (NCBI). Wage growth itself is structured to reward staying: both in Mexico and Brazil, workers who remain longer in a single job see stronger wage growth than those who move frequently, turning tenure into a direct financial strategy, not just a cultural preference.

Norway inverts the incentive entirely. With unemployment low and labor protections strong, job security is close to a given, which means switching roles has to serve a clearer purpose than simply hedging against instability — professionals move because a role genuinely improves career progression or quality of life (Qureos Hiring Trends). OECD data on job-to-job mobility across 17 countries found this kind of voluntary movement tends to reallocate workers toward more productive, higher-paying firms — mobility functioning as an engine of wage growth in Norway the way tenure does in Mexico. Even among older workers, Norway shows comparatively low rates of low-skill-to-low-skill job switching, suggesting mobility there is more often a genuine step up than a lateral scramble.

### The Reckoning

The real divergence isn't about ambition — both cultures reward it — it's about where the ambition is expected to be exercised. In Mexico, ambition is best expressed by rising within a relationship of trust built over years with one employer; leaving too early forfeits the accumulated social capital that makes advancement possible in the first place. In Norway, ambition is best expressed by moving between employers, treating each job as a deliberate rung rather than a home, with trust rebuilt quickly each time because the labor market itself is transparent and secure enough to make that low-risk.

Hofstede's data helps explain the divergence: Mexico scores notably higher on power distance, reinforcing hierarchical, relationship-bound loyalty structures, while Norway sits among the lowest power-distance cultures globally, consistent with a labor market where moving between relatively equal employers carries little social cost. What looks like loyalty in one country would look like a stalled career in the other, and vice versa.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A commenter reflecting on Mexican business culture explained that many professionals genuinely prefer working for an established patrón over striking out independently, describing the arrangement less as a lack of ambition than as a rational trade of autonomy for security and long-term trust.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A tech professional who'd worked across Nordic and Latin American markets noted that changing jobs every two to three years was treated as completely unremarkable in Norway, to the point that staying five years in one role prompted more questions in an interview than leaving frequently ever did.*

> *Reddit — Someone who relocated from Mexico City to Oslo for a corporate role described the biggest adjustment as unlearning the instinct to defer heavily to a single boss's authority — Norwegian colleagues expected direct pushback and treated excessive deference as a communication problem, not respect.*

> *thepeoplepractice.substack.com — An HR consultant writing about job-hopping trends observed that in markets like Norway, treating each job as a deliberate stepping stone toward a stated goal was viewed favorably by employers, provided the candidate could clearly articulate the throughline between roles rather than appearing to drift.*

> *Quora — A long-tenured employee reflecting on staying at one Mexican company for over a decade said the trade-offs were real — slower salary jumps than job-hopping might have produced — but the informal flexibility and trust built with ownership over the years, including leeway during personal emergencies, was something they never saw job-hoppers around them receive.*

### Conclusion

The practical guidance is almost a mirror image depending on where you land. In Mexico, resist the instinct to job-hop for a quick raise — the compounding trust and internal mobility of staying often outperforms it, and frequent moves can quietly work against you in future hiring. In Norway, resist the instinct to dig in and wait for loyalty to be rewarded — the market assumes movement, and staying too long without a clear reason can read as stagnation rather than commitment. If a friend asked me which mindset to adopt, I'd say: figure out which country's assumptions your résumé is being read against, and build your career story to match, because the same five years reads completely differently depending on which office you're sitting in.
