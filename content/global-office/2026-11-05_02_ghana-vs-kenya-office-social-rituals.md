# The Office Is a Family in Accra and a Second Family in Nairobi, and That Is Not the Same Thing

🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇰🇪 Kenya

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Hofstede's research teams, famously thorough about measuring the cultural dimensions of nearly every country on earth, never bothered giving Ghana or Kenya their own individual scores — both are filed under regional clusters, as if one office in Accra and one in Nairobi were close enough to average together. Anyone who has actually eaten lunch at a desk in both cities can tell you they are not, starting with the small but load-bearing matter of who is allowed to ask what you're eating.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet every colleague individually each morning, even ones you just saw in the hallway | Walk past a desk in silence — it reads as a grudge, not efficiency |
| Accept food offered to you at someone's desk, even a small bite | Visibly smell or inspect food before eating it — it insults the cook, not the dish |
| Join the office WhatsApp group — real planning for lunches and outings happens there | Rely on the official calendar invite alone; treat it as a formality, not a commitment |
| Let senior colleagues speak first and defer visibly in group settings | Interrupt a senior colleague's point in a meeting, even to agree loudly |
| Attend the team outing or "outdooring" if invited — declining without a real reason is noted | Treat a colleague's invitation to a family or life event as optional small talk |
| Expect regional personality stereotypes (who jokes, who stays quiet, who manages the boss) to shape office dynamics | Assume every Ghanaian office runs on the same social script — regional micro-cultures vary within one company |

#### 🇰🇪 Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect colleagues to ask about your family and share food without it being a boundary violation | Treat personal questions from coworkers as intrusive — warmth, not nosiness, is the intent |
| Join Friday after-work drinks or "Wine Time" if your office has one — it's a real institution, not optional networking | Skip after-work socializing reflexively to "save time"; you lose standing, not minutes |
| Learn to decline requests for favors gracefully — overcommitment from guilt is a documented burnout driver | Say yes to every colleague's ask just to avoid seeming "cold or arrogant" |
| Budget for genuinely long hours — expatriates in Kenya average over 46 a week by some surveys | Assume the easy, communal atmosphere means the workload is light |
| Bring a packed lunch if that's the office norm in your sector — it's increasingly common even among men | Assume lunch will be a scheduled, uniform break the way it might be elsewhere |
| Clarify directly whether a lunch or dinner invitation is the expected way to build a business relationship — practice varies by contact | Assume one colleague's account of local etiquette (gifts, meals, timing) applies universally |

---

Ghana's office, as one long-running local career column put it, functions as "a mini Ghana" — a building full of regional personalities (the Ashanti morale-booster who praises the boss, the Fante colleague doubling as the office comedian and informal counsellor, the quieter Northern staff, the expressive Ga contingent) who bring football arguments, newspapers, and the occasional visiting relative into the workday. Lunch is less a scheduled event than a running negotiation over who brought waakye today and who still owes jollof from last week. A popular MyJoyOnline opinion piece lays out the unwritten rules bluntly: the office is a genuine social hub, gossip travels fast, and a conspicuously hard worker risks being asked, pointedly, "does the company belong to your father?" — a culture that rewards visible camaraderie more reliably than it rewards visible ambition. None of this is relaxed in the way it sounds, either: Ghana ranked fourth among Sub-Saharan African countries for daily workplace stress in Gallup's State of the Global Workplace findings, a reminder that an office built on belonging rituals is still, underneath the warmth, managing real pressure — just informally, through relationship rather than policy.

Kenya's office runs on a similar engine of communal warmth, with a sharper edge of obligation attached. K24 Digital's reporting on Kenyan work culture names shared food and genuine interest in colleagues' families as the single thing visitors notice first, but cites a 2025 Journal of African Business study (by researcher Esnart Mwaba Tayali) linking that same communal obligation to burnout: saying no to a colleague's request can be read as cold or arrogant, which pushes employees toward chronic overcommitment. The reward for enduring it is real, per InterNations data reported by Kenyans.co.ke: Kenya ranks tenth globally among destinations for female expatriates, fourth for ease of communication, and a majority of surveyed expats report meeting their career expectations — but those same expats also log some of the longest average hours of any top-ten destination, at 46.4 a week. The Standard's own newsroom runs a Friday "Wine Time," explicitly described as a space where work talk is off-limits — a formal acknowledgment that the office knows it needs to build in deliberate off-ramps from its own intensity. Meanwhile, a quieter cultural shift is underway in what people actually eat at their desks: Business Daily Africa reports a rising trend of men carrying packed lunch boxes — ugali, githeri, sandwiches, beef stew — a small but telling sign of how much domestic and office life in Nairobi now overlap.

The two countries also diverge sharply on what actually happens once the workday ends. Ghana's after-work hours are structured by sheer logistics: a popular local lifestyle piece describes the 5pm mass exodus from Accra's office districts as an instant trigger for gridlock, with commutes stretching well past an hour through crowded lorry stations, after which home responsibilities — cooking, childcare, a side business many employees run alongside their main job — begin immediately, leaving little real decompression time between the office and the next obligation. Kenya's evening hours look different again: beyond the now-common Friday "Wine Time" ritual, Business Daily Africa's reporting on the rise of men bringing packed lunch boxes to work — ugali, githeri, sandwiches, beef stew — points to a broader blurring of domestic and office life that cuts against the image of a hard line between work and home. Hofstede's regional cluster data, imperfect as it is for either country individually, backs up the general pattern: both West and East African clusters score low on individualism and moderately high on power distance, which tracks with offices where personal and professional obligations are expected to overlap rather than stay neatly separated, in a way that would feel unusual to anyone arriving from a more individualist corporate culture.

### The Reckoning

Both offices sell themselves on warmth, and both deliver it — but the warmth is structured around different obligations. Ghana's version is built on visible, almost performative camaraderie: greet everyone, eat what's offered, show up to the outdooring, and the social debt is repaid in belonging. Kenya's version is built on genuine personal intimacy — colleagues who know your family, share your food, and expect the same back — but that intimacy comes with a harder-to-decline obligation structure, where saying no costs more socially than simply saying yes and quietly burning out.

The practical trap for a newcomer is assuming either culture's friendliness means the workload will be light or the hours forgiving. Neither is true. Gallup and InterNations data both point the same direction: these are demanding working environments wearing a hospitable face, and the real skill isn't detecting the warmth — it's arrives obvious within a week — but learning where, inside that warmth, the actual limits are, and how to set your own without appearing to reject the culture that's offering it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *expat.com forum, Kenya — a longtime resident working at a large East African media company described office politics as comparable to what they'd experienced in India, and advised newcomers bluntly to "move away from my own nationality and mix with the locals" if they wanted to actually understand how decisions got made.*

> *expat.com forum, Kenya — on a thread about business-trip etiquette, two expats gave contradictory advice about whether lunch or dinner was the better way to entertain a Kenyan business contact, and disagreed on whether gift-giving was customary — a real-time reminder that local etiquette is debated even among people who live there.*

> *MyJoyOnline, Ghana — a widely shared opinion column described a visibly hardworking colleague being asked, half-joking and half-not, whether the company belonged to their father, capturing how performed effort without performed camaraderie can read as suspicious rather than admirable.*

> *The Standard, Kenya — a reporter covering their own newsroom's Friday "Wine Time" described it as "a non-judgmental space" specifically because work talk is banned there, implying that without such a deliberate rule, work talk would otherwise colonize every social hour too.*

> *Jobberman Ghana, 'Office Buzz' column — a real team-outing account described a debate over the "most Ghanaian" road-trip snack turning genuinely heated on the bus, down to a dance-off at the destination, with staff describing a visibly improved mood back at their desks the following Monday.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between postings, the honest difference is this: Accra will absorb you into its social fabric whether you're ready or not, and the discipline it demands is stamina — showing up, eating what's offered, tolerating the late starts. Nairobi will offer you something closer to real friendship faster, but it will expect reciprocity in a way that's easy to underestimate until you're the one everyone assumes will say yes.

I'd tell a friend taking either job the same thing: enjoy the lunch, actually enjoy it, because in both cities it's doing more organizational work than anything on the official calendar — just don't mistake either office's warmth for an absence of cost.
