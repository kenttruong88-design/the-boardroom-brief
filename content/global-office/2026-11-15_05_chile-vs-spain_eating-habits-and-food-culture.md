# Chile Has Once, Spain Has Dinner at Ten: Choose Which Meal Your Colleagues Will Skip

🇨🇱 Chile · 🇪🇸 Spain

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two countries, both Spanish-speaking, both fond of bread and a long sit-down, have arrived at opposite answers to the question of what to do after about 5pm. In Spain, the answer is to wait until roughly 10pm and then eat a full dinner. In Chile, the answer is to have tea, and often to treat that as dinner. A prospective expat who assumes that shared language means shared cutlery timing will spend the first month hungry in one country and politely confused in the other.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat almuerzo, around 1:30 to 2:30pm, as the main meal and book it into your day | Eat a sandwich at your desk and call it lunch |
| Accept an invitation to once; it is a small badge of acceptance | Expect a three-course dinner at 7pm |
| Order the menú del día for the best value out | Ask for coffee first; tea is the national default |
| Bring something to an asado and stay for the whole Sunday | Raise Peru's claim to pisco at the table |
| Read the black warning labels as a shopping guide, not an insult | Assume a packet is healthy because the front is green |

### Spain

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan lunch for 2 to 4pm and dinner for 9 or 10pm | Book a table at 6pm and expect a kitchen |
| Eat the menú del día and let lunch run long | Plan a 30-minute lunch meeting and call it efficient |
| Drop olive stones and napkins on the bar floor where locals do | Tidy up and stand out |
| Bring tápers of home cooking to the office kitchen | Expect butter with the bread |
| Pace the tapas crawl across several bars | Order everything at the first bar |

## Chile

According to Expat.cl, Chilean meal timing is a three-act structure. Almuerzo lands between 1:30 and 2:30pm and is the largest meal of the day. Restaurants fill, and many offer a fixed-price menú del día that the site calls the best value in town. The third act is where the foreigner gets lost. Once, a light spread of bread, avocado, cheese, ham, jam and tea, arrives between 5 and 8pm and in many homes replaces the evening meal altogether. Cena, where it exists, is rarely before 8:30pm and is sometimes skipped. An American exchange student writing for CIEE reported drinking about three cups of tea a day and finding the skipped dinner, plus the absence of snacking, hard to adjust to.

The food itself is generous and carbohydrate-forward: empanadas, pastel de choclo, cazuela, sopaipillas, and the completo, a hot dog with avocado and mayonnaise that no nutritionist has been invited to review. Hofstede's scores, as reproduced in a business communication textbook from GVSU, rate Chile at 23 on individualism, against 51 for Spain, which goes some way to explaining why the national meal is a shared table of bread rather than a plate with your name on it.

Expat.cl also notes that tea is more common in daily life than coffee, that salmon and coastal seafood are cheap by international standards, and that the asado, held for birthdays, football victories or simply a sunny Sunday, is the country's principal social event. Being invited to once, the site says, is a small badge of acceptance, which is a more reliable integration metric than most onboarding surveys.

The public health bill has arrived. The Examination reported in March 2024 that Chilean child obesity rose from 16% in 2009 to over 26% in 2022, according to government data. The state responded with some of the most aggressive food policy in the world. A 2012 law led to black stop-sign warnings on food high in sugar, sodium, saturated fat or calories from 2016, a ban on advertising such foods to children on television between 6am and 10pm, and a ban on selling them in school kiosks. Sales of labelled foods in school kiosks fell from 90% to 15% within six months, per a Santiago study cited in the same piece. The law, notably, did not cover other ultra-processed foods, which have been quietly filling the gap. For the expat, the practical upshot is a supermarket where the packaging is unusually honest and the diet around it is unusually beige.

## Spain

Spain's problem is not the number of meals but the hour. Idealista puts dinner at around 9:30 to 10:30pm, with most restaurants not opening until about 8pm, and weekends later still. The article offers a popular explanation: Spain adopted Central European Time in 1940 and the population kept its original eating schedule, so lunch drifted from 1pm to 2pm and dinner from 8pm to 9pm without the clocks ever being corrected. The Local adds that lunch falls between 2 and 4pm, that the sobremesa, the conversation after the meal, can keep you at the table two hours later, and that sandwiches at desks are uncommon. Plenty of offices have a kitchen where staff heat up tápers of home cooking.

The day around that lunch is also arranged differently. The Local describes breakfast as typically sweet, churros or pastries with coffee, with many workers taking a second breakfast around 11am, often coffee with a pincho de tortilla. Shopping happens at neighbourhood markets, butchers and greengrocers rather than hypermarkets, and vegetables arrive as dishes in their own right rather than as garnish. Jamón is everywhere. Butter is almost nowhere. It is a coherent system, provided you were raised inside it.

The relationship with health is more flattering than the late hour suggests, though not without caveats. A retiree posting on Expat.com said that moving his main meal to between 1 and 2pm, eating a very light breakfast and limiting dinner to a couple of tapas, alongside more exercise, brought his BMI from 30 to 22 in a year. Against this, the World Obesity Federation reported on the PASOS 2022 study that one in three Spanish children aged 8 to 16 live with overweight or obesity, and that adherence to the Mediterranean diet fell between 2019 and 2022. An earlier WHO study, reported by Healthplan Spain, put obesity among six- to nine-year-olds at about 17%, the second-highest in Europe, and blamed the retreat from the Mediterranean diet toward convenience food. The tradition survives mainly among those who can afford to practise it.

## The Reckoning

Head to head, Spain wins on ritual and Chile on frankness. Spain asks you to rearrange your body clock around a dinner that begins when a Chilean has already had tea and gone to bed. In return it offers markets, neighbourhood butchers and a lunch that is treated as a civic obligation. Chile asks you to accept tea as a meal and gives you a state that prints the nutritional truth on the box in black octagons. Neither country has solved the problem of a population eating more processed food than its grandmothers would recognise, but only one has legislated against it with visible results in the school canteen.

For the working expat, the real difference is schedule. In Chile, a late-afternoon once is a social event you can attend and still be in bed by eleven. In Spain, a 10pm dinner is an event you attend because the day has not ended yet. If you are weighing the two on food alone, choose Spain for the lunch, Chile for the labels, and bring a snack to either country for the hour at which your stomach and the local calendar disagree.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — paraphrased experience: A retiree about a year into life in Spain resisted the local schedule for months, then moved lunch to 1 or 2pm, cut breakfast to almost nothing and settled on one or two tapas for the evening. He also noted that Sunday, once his main shopping day, now means closed shops and a bicycle.*

> *GB News (relaying a YouTube expat account) — paraphrased experience: A man who lived in Spain said meals last for hours, dinner is served far later than he expected, and shops shut mid-day. He found the whole rhythm changed his routine more than the language did.*

> *The Local Spain — paraphrased experience: Long-term residents list the surprises as a lunch that cannot be rushed, bars where napkins and prawn heads go on the floor, bread without butter, and a second breakfast around 11am.*

> *CIEE study abroad blog — paraphrased experience: An American exchange student in Chile went from rarely drinking tea to about three cups a day, loves once for its bread and avocado, and found the late or skipped dinner at host homes the hardest part to adjust to.*

> *ExpatExchange — paraphrased experience: A visitor to Chile drove from Santiago to Valdivia without finding organic food, located a few organic shops and a farmers' market in the capital, and asked other expats where to buy year-round outside the city.*

## Conclusion

Food culture is the quietest form of workplace culture. It decides when meetings end, who joins the after-work drinks and whether anyone eats at their desk without an explanation. Spain will test your tolerance for dinner after 9pm, and Chile will test your willingness to accept tea as a complete sentence. Both reward the newcomer who adapts early and stops negotiating with the clock.

Pack an appetite for either, and a snack for the three hours in which neither country has a plan for you.
