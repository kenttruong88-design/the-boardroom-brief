# The One-Company Man and the Serial Optimizer: Botswana and Vietnam on Loyalty

🇧🇼 Botswana · 🇻🇳 Vietnam

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Gaborone, a twenty-year service pin from your employer is still a genuine source of pride, mentioned approvingly at funerals. In Ho Chi Minh City, the same pin would prompt colleagues to quietly ask whether you're all right. According to Navigos Group's Talent Guide 2026, roughly 42 percent of Vietnamese employees are actively job-hunting at any given moment, with another 29 percent open to a better offer, and salary tops every other job-decision factor by a wide margin. Botswana, by contrast, still organizes a meaningful share of its labour market around the government payroll, where a job is less a rung on a ladder than a room you move into and slowly make your own.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇼 Botswana
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a personal network before you need one — hiring runs on referrals | Expect a fast hiring process; budget months, not weeks |
| Bring certified copies of every qualification, and expect to pay for authentication | Assume your foreign credentials will be accepted at face value |
| Show visible deference to seniority and age in early meetings | Push hard for a decision from the most junior person in the room |
| Treat a government or parastatal offer as a long-haul commitment | Signal that the job is a stepping stone to something else |
| Learn to read indirect communication — disagreement is rarely stated outright | Mistake polite agreement for actual consensus |
| Expect a work-permit process even for clearly skilled roles | Assume an employer can hire foreign staff without proving no local candidate qualifies |

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate the 13th-month bonus explicitly before signing | Assume base pay is the whole package — bonuses are structural, not a bonus |
| Expect colleagues to ask about your next move at your own farewell party | Treat job-hopping as a CV red flag — locally it usually isn't one |
| Join after-work team events if you want to be read as invested | Skip company trips and assume performance alone will speak for you |
| Time any resignation with an eye on Tet, when bonuses vest and turnover spikes | Quit in December and forfeit a full year's accrued bonus |
| Match your visa sponsor to your actual employer | Assume your work permit survives a job change automatically |
| Expect directives to be followed without public pushback in meetings | Openly contradict a superior in a group setting, even when you're right |

## Botswana: The Payroll as Extended Family

Botswana's labour market is a study in scarcity shaping behaviour. The World Bank puts national-estimate youth unemployment (ages 15–24) at 45.2 percent in 2024, and Statistics Botswana's Quarterly Multi-Topic Survey found unemployment among 15–35 year-olds running above the national average of 25.9 percent, even though Afrobarometer finds this cohort more educated than its elders. Reliable data on private-sector job tenure is thin here, so the clearest signal comes from where people compete hardest to work: the public service, where employee-review sites peg job security at roughly 90 percent, with pensions and leave allowances private employers rarely match.

That scarcity produces a hiring culture built on relationships rather than resumes. Expat forums are blunt about it: job searches routinely stretch past a year, and getting in the door depends heavily on who already knows you. The upside, once inside, is stability approaching permanence; the downside, per the UNDP's recent report on Botswana's youth employment, is that scarcity locks out the qualified young workers the economy most needs. Loyalty here is less a virtue than a hedge against a market that punishes anyone who leaves a good seat empty.

## Vietnam: The Bonus Calendar as Career Clock

Vietnam's workforce skews younger and moves faster. Generation Y and Z make up roughly 79 percent of employees, per Navigos's 2026 figures, and the ILO's Labour and Social Trends report notes Vietnamese workers rarely struggle to find a next job — the informal sector alone absorbed millions of new entrants from 2011 to 2019 — removing much of the risk that keeps Batswana workers anchored. With unemployment near 2 percent and skilled-worker shortages persistent, according to the ILO and General Statistics Office, employers compete on compensation, and workers respond rationally: Navigos found salary cited by 78.71 percent of respondents as the top reason to switch jobs, with promotion a distant second at 38.36 percent.

The mechanism governing when Vietnamese employees move is almost calendrical. The 13th-month salary — effectively mandatory in practice — plus any performance bonus typically vests only for staff still on payroll at Tet, the lunar new year. HR advisories warn that resigning in December is the costliest timing mistake a jobholder can make, producing a synchronized annual wave of departures each spring. It is job-hopping with a fiscal year attached.

## The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the moral reverses itself. Botswana's job loyalty looks admirable until you notice it is largely a function of unemployment above 25 percent and a hiring culture where burning one bridge can mean burning the only bridge; Vietnam's job-hopping looks reckless until you notice it is a rational bargaining strategy where wages haven't kept pace with sectoral growth, and one employer's loss is simply the next one's gain. Hofstede's cultural-dimensions framework — thinner on Southern African data than on Southeast Asia — still captures something real: Vietnam scores firmly collectivist and high on power distance, yet that collectivism expresses itself as obligation to the group at the current job, not attachment to the employer's name on the building.

The counterintuitive part is who holds the power. In Botswana, the employer does, because the queue for each opening is long and exit costs are real. In Vietnam, increasingly, the employee does, because the queue for talent is long and switching costs are low. Job loyalty, it turns out, is less a personality trait than a bargaining position in disguise.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A contributor describing life in Gaborone noted that the professional circle is small enough that most people eventually work with, or for, someone they already know socially, which cuts both ways: doors open quickly once you're inside, and close just as quickly if you burn one bridge.*

> *Expat.com forum — A UK expat who relocated with his Motswana wife recounted her spending two and a half years searching for full-time work despite strong qualifications, and concluded that hiring in Botswana runs on a "who you know" culture more than merit, making a network established before arrival more valuable than the CV itself.*

> *VnExpress (foreign staff in Vietnam) — A newly arrived manager described being startled during his first week when the entire office pulled out blankets and pillows after lunch, switched off the lights, and slept on the floor for half an hour, only to later concede the practice left the afternoon noticeably more productive than his home country's coffee-and-push-through approach.*

> *Vietnam HR advisories — Recruiters caution that resigning in December, just before Tet, is one of the costliest timing mistakes a new hire in Vietnam can make, since the 13th-month payment and any additional bonus typically vest only for staff still employed when the lunar new year arrives.*

> *Blind — A foreign tech hire transplanted from a Western market admitted his instinct to treat every job as a two-year waypoint, sharpened by a home industry where the standard advice is to job-hop or fall behind, needed active unlearning once local colleagues started asking, not unkindly, why he didn't seem to want to stay.*

### Conclusion

Neither posture is a national character trait so much as a rational response to the incentives on offer. Botswana's loyalty is the loyalty of people who know exactly how long the queue behind them is; Vietnam's mobility is the mobility of people who know exactly how short it is. A newcomer who imports the wrong assumption — treating a Botswana government post as a stepping stone, or expecting a Vietnamese hire to stay put out of sentiment — will find the local labour market patient and entirely unmoved by their confusion. The honest advice, in both directions, is the same: watch what people do with their exit options, not what they say about company culture in the interview.
