# South Africa Offers Four Months and Paperwork; the United Kingdom Offers Thirty-Nine Weeks and a Nursery Bill

🇿🇦 South Africa · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

South Africa's law grants a new mother at least four consecutive months of leave and then leaves the question of pay to a government fund and her employer's generosity. Britain grants up to 39 weeks of statutory pay, 90 percent of earnings for the first six weeks and then £194.32 a week or 90 percent of earnings, whichever is lower. The generous one is not always the one that arrives on time. Both countries will let you have children. They differ chiefly in how long you will wait to be reimbursed for it, and who sends the invoice.

## Do's & Don'ts

### South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm before conceiving that your employer has registered you with the UIF | Assume your employer must pay your salary during leave, since the law requires the leave and not the pay |
| Apply for the UIF benefit early and keep every document | Plan a household budget around the money arriving on schedule |
| Ask whether your employer tops up the UIF, because practice varies widely | Assume four months means four months of full salary |
| Price crèche fees, formula and medical aid before you accept the job offer | Forget that a modest monthly baby budget can pass R7,000 |
| Discuss with your partner how to split the shared leave pool | Assume fathers can claim UIF benefits yet, since they currently go to birth mothers |
| If you hire a domestic worker or nanny, register her with the UIF and pay at least the legal minimum | Join the estimated four in five employers who do not register their domestic workers |

### United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your length of service early, since statutory paternity pay still needs 26 weeks | Assume day-one rights mean day-one pay |
| Budget for the drop to £194.32 a week after week six | Plan on 90 percent of your salary for 39 weeks |
| Ask HR whether enhanced maternity pay exists and what it requires | Assume statutory pay is the only pay on offer |
| Check whether you qualify for the 30 funded hours from nine months | Assume funded hours apply in Scotland or Wales as they do in England |
| Look at tax-free childcare and Universal Credit childcare support before the return date | Treat a nursery place as free because a policy headline said so |
| Plan the gap between children with the childcare calendar in mind | Assume the second baby will be cheaper than the first |

## South Africa

Under the law as it stands, according to WageIndicator, a South African worker is entitled to at least four consecutive months of maternity leave, which she may begin four weeks before the due date and must observe for six weeks after the birth. The statute guarantees the leave, not the salary. Reporting by the Bhekisisa Centre for Health Journalism, published in TimesLive, explains that where an employer has registered the worker, the Unemployment Insurance Fund can cover roughly two-thirds of income for up to four months, but researchers estimate only about 20 percent of employers register their domestic workers, despite the legal requirement. The state, in effect, has built a safety net and then asked employers to attach it. The same reporting cites a BMJ Global Health study of 38 low- and middle-income countries finding that each additional month of paid maternity leave was linked to nearly six more mothers in every 100 breastfeeding exclusively, against a South African rate of 32 percent of children exclusively breastfed for their first six months.

The rules are in motion. On 3 October 2025, in Van Wyk and Others v Minister of Employment and Labour, the Constitutional Court found the parental leave provisions unconstitutional for favouring birth mothers. According to Bowmans, it ordered an interim regime pooling four months and 10 days between two employed parents, with single or sole-earning parents entitled to at least four consecutive months, and gave Parliament 36 months to fix the statutes. BusinessTech notes that UIF benefits still apply only to birth mothers, so fathers gain access to the money only when Parliament amends the legislation. The right to leave has been shared. The payment has not, yet.

The labour market sets the mood. Stats SA reported, as covered by EWN, that female unemployment for women aged 15 to 64 reached 37.5 percent in the second quarter of 2026, against 30.3 percent for men. Childcare is largely private: a Cape Town mother's widely shared TikTok budget, reported by Briefly, listed R3,000 a month for a crèche place for a 14-month-old inside a R7,400 monthly baby budget that included R1,800 of medical aid. The alternative is a domestic worker. According to BusinessTech's reading of Stats SA data, domestic worker numbers have not passed 880,000 in almost four years, and the 2026 minimum wage of R30.23 an hour implies at least R4,834 for a 160-hour month, which exceeds the R3,000 crèche place by a comfortable margin.

## United Kingdom

On paper Britain is the longer, simpler road. GOV.UK sets Statutory Maternity Pay at 90 percent of average weekly earnings for six weeks and then the lower of £194.32 or 90 percent of earnings through to week 39. Baker McKenzie confirms the weekly rate rose from £187.18 to £194.32 in April 2026 and that the Employment Rights Act 2025 makes paternity leave and unpaid parental leave day-one rights, although statutory paternity pay still requires 26 weeks of continuous service and parental leave remains entirely unpaid. The law has therefore moved from asking whether you have worked long enough to take time off to asking whether you have worked long enough to be paid for it.

The cost is in the nursery. The Coram Family and Childcare survey for 2026 found that, since September 2025, eligible working parents in England can receive 30 hours a week of funded childcare from nine months of age, for 38 weeks a year, and that a part-time under-two place is theoretically free during term time for eligible families. Families who do not qualify pay an average of £189 a week for a part-time place, and funding is an England-only matter, with the part-time average at £133.08 in Scotland and £166.33 in Wales, up 5 and 8 percent respectively. Coram adds some perspective: a part-time under-two place in 2024 cost almost three times its 2001 price, and under the expanded funding eligible families' costs in 2025 fell back to 2005 levels. Three-quarters of English local authorities report enough childcare for at least 75 percent of eligible under-twos, which is a statistic that sounds better than it feels in a waiting room. The system is generous to those it recognises and expensive to everyone else.

The penalty comes later. Analysis by the Office for National Statistics, reported by Gingerbread, found that five years after a first child's birth mothers' monthly earnings are on average 42 percent, or £1,051 a month, lower than in the year before the birth, a cumulative loss of £65,618 over five years. That is the part of the system no pay table mentions. Britain gives you time off, and then bills the household for the career.

## The Reckoning

Head to head, the United Kingdom pays for longer at a modest floor and runs it through payroll, which is dull and therefore reliable. South Africa pays for a shorter spell, through a fund whose paperwork is the main risk, with the Constitutional Court now pushing it towards a shared model that Parliament has yet to fund. Where the British parent faces a nursery invoice from the child's ninth month, the South African faces a choice between a crèche, a relative, or a domestic worker whose own rights are poorly enforced. Hofstede Insights scores Britain at 89 for individualism and South Africa at 65, a gap that does not appear in the statutes but may say something about why one country treats childcare as a household purchase to be subsidised and the other leaves a great deal to the family and its helpers.

For the expatriate, the question is therefore less which law is kinder and more which risk you can carry. The UK risk is a predictable bill; the South African risk is an unpredictable payment. A six-figure income handles either. A single salary of modest size handles neither, which is why the forums below are full of people who have done the sums on the back of a payslip.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Mumsnet — A mother of two in Wales, back from maternity leave, with both parents earning about £35k pro rata, described nursery fees as crippling and was considering dropping from four days to three. Replies described extending mortgages, living on credit cards, relying on grandparents, and one single parent earning over £50k living in the cheapest one-bed flat available until the child started school.*

> *Hacker News — A UK-based parent, commenting in 2020 on a thread about parental leave in Seattle and Stockholm, said UK childcare is oversubscribed and expensive, citing about £100 a day for long hours in London outside public nurseries. They noted that shared parental leave lets parents split about twelve months off, but that government pay in the first year amounted to roughly £600 a month plus whatever the employer added.*

> *Mail and Guardian — A South African working mother wrote that her employer paid part of her salary for three months of maternity leave, which looked manageable on paper but not once nappies, formula and medical bills arrived. Her bank refused relief on vehicle repayments, she found the UIF process hard to navigate, and she returned to work just over two months after the birth, finding leaving her baby painful.*

> *The Citizen — A mother who had about five months at home said her previous employer covered a small share of her salary and the UIF paid the rest for four months, after she paid a once-off R550 fee to have her documents prepared. She argued the law favours the employer, that unpaid leave is unrealistic, and that her own sister received the UIF payment only months after returning to work.*

> *TimesLive — A domestic worker identified by a pseudonym, earning R7,500 a month, was paid half her salary for eight weeks of maternity leave, because her employer had not registered her with the UIF. She eventually received a R31,000 payout, and the authors note that many live-in workers are expected to hand a baby over to a nanny rather than breastfeed.*

## Conclusion

If you are seriously planning a family abroad, do the arithmetic for a year, not a month. In the UK, confirm your service length, your employer's enhanced pay policy, your eligibility for funded hours and the nursery waiting list before the pregnancy test, and remember that part-time hours may be the price of staying solvent. In South Africa, confirm your UIF registration, your employer's top-up practice and your savings buffer, and if you hire help, register her properly and pay the legal minimum.

Neither country will make parenthood cheap or your career painless, although each will do its best to describe the outcome as a choice. The statutory entitlements are the brochure; the budget is the document you should read.
