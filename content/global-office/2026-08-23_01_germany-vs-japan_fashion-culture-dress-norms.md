# The Suit Survives in Tokyo. In Berlin, It Surrendered to the Sandal.

## 🇩🇪 Germany · 🇯🇵 Japan

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

German households spent €69.2 billion on clothes and shoes in 2024, the highest clothing bill in the European Union, according to Destatis — and yet the observable output of all that spending, per one Berlin menswear historian, is "blue jeans, some kind of sweatshirt, and an anorak." Meanwhile, in Japan, where 78 percent of fashion consumers say they prize quality over trend, per Statista, the wrong shade of navy on your first day at the office can still read as a small act of rebellion. Both countries agree that clothing communicates seriousness. They have simply never agreed on what seriousness looks like.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask HR or a future colleague about dress norms before day one — they vary wildly by sector | Assume finance, law, or insurance will be casual; they remain suit-and-tie strongholds |
| Buy one well-made coat or weatherproof jacket — outerwear is where Germans spend visibly | Show up in an obviously expensive tailored suit; it reads as compensating for something |
| Wear clean sneakers or plain leather shoes in most tech, engineering, or Mittelstand offices | Wear a short skirt or high heels expecting extra professionalism points; understatement wins |
| Keep jewellery, logos, and makeup minimal | Mention what your clothes cost, even in jest |
| Pack sandals for summer — open-toed shoes at the desk are unremarkable | Expect a tie to be required; most offices have quietly dropped them |
| Default to navy, grey, or black basics that don't stand out | Dress noticeably better than your manager without a client meeting to justify it |

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Start with a recruit-suit-style wardrobe — black or navy suit, white shirt, black shoes — even at an officially "casual" company | Wear anything with visible logos, bright colour, or an unusual cut in your first weeks |
| Watch how senior colleagues dress before copying anyone junior | Loosen your tie or shed your jacket before someone more senior does, even in July |
| Switch to lighter grey suits and half-sleeve shirts once Cool Biz season visibly starts (May–September) | Wear shorts or sneakers on your own initiative, even if the government now permits it in summer |
| Keep a spare jacket at your desk for unannounced client visits | Take casual-Friday photos on the careers page as a literal dress-code promise |
| Iron everything; press and polish substitute for personality | Stand out — coordinated blandness signals trustworthiness, not lack of taste |
| Ask a Japanese colleague to review your outfit before a first big meeting | Assume "smart casual" at a startup means the same thing it means at a legacy trading house |

Germany's relationship with office fashion is best understood as an inheritance dispute it never settled. According to the OECD, the average German worker logs around 1,340 hours a year — among the fewest of any country the organisation tracks — a fact that correlates suspiciously well with a wardrobe built for leaving quickly. Bernhard Roetzel, a Berlin-based menswear writer interviewed by The Local Germany, traces the country's dress-down instinct to a nineteenth-century *Lebensreform* movement that treated sandals as a form of political freedom, and argues the habit never really left: jeans, a non-iron shirt, and "some very cheap, comfortable leather shoes" now cover most German offices outside banking and law.

What makes this more than mere laziness is the moral weight attached to it. Roetzel's central observation — that an expensive, visibly tailored suit will "destroy your image" in a way that an €800 outdoor jacket never will — tracks with Hofstede Insights' data showing Germany scoring 67 on individualism against a comparatively modest 66 on masculinity, a profile that rewards personal autonomy but punishes conspicuous status display. The old Prussian maxim *mehr sein als schein* — being over seeming — still does real work on the shop floor of German HR departments, where a junior employee arriving in bespoke tailoring is more likely to prompt a whispered "is he interviewing elsewhere" than a compliment.

Japan runs the opposite experiment: formality as visible teamwork. The Japan Times reported this summer that Tokyo government officials were, for the first time, showing up in shorts as offices scaled back air conditioning amid high energy costs — a genuinely newsworthy event precisely because it took a national energy crisis and two decades of the government's own Cool Biz campaign, launched in 2005, to get salarymen out of full suits in August. Japan's apparel market remains worth roughly ¥10.6 trillion, per Statista, and its dress conventions are correspondingly durable: dark suits, white shirts, muted ties, with the "recruit suit" — a plain black interview uniform — treated as a rite of passage regardless of industry.

The logic is less about individual taste than group signalling. Hofstede Insights puts Japan at 92 on uncertainty avoidance and 95 on masculinity, against Germany's 65 and 66 respectively — numbers that map neatly onto a culture where visible conformity reduces social risk rather than personal identity. A dark suit in Tokyo is not a fashion statement; it is a way of confirming you understand the rules well enough not to test them, which is precisely why so many foreign hires over-formalise and Japanese colleagues under-explain the unwritten dress code they've absorbed since university job-hunting season.

The Reckoning is where the two systems reveal they are solving the same problem from opposite directions. Germany, ostensibly the more individualist of the two by Hofstede's measure, produces near-total sartorial homogeneity through sheer social pressure not to seem like you're trying — everyone converges on navy, grey, and sensible shoes because standing out reads as insecure. Japan, ostensibly the more collectivist, achieves the same visual sameness through an explicit hierarchy that tells you exactly what to wear and when, down to the calendar month the tie disappears. Both countries end up with strikingly uniform offices; they just get there by German employees independently deciding to underdress and Japanese employees being told, with far less ambiguity, to overdress.

The other reversal is speed of change. Japan's dress code loosens by institutional announcement — a ministry press release, a corporate memo, a government minister photographed in short sleeves to signal official permission. Germany's loosens by nobody asking permission at all; Birkenstocks simply appeared at desks because no rule ever explicitly forbade them. An employee moving from Munich to Tokyo will need to relearn a system where dress-code changes are handed down; one moving the other way will need to relearn a system where they mostly aren't handed down at all.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A German office worker described the confusion of arriving with a full suit on their first day at a mid-sized manufacturing firm, only to find everyone else in chinos and polos; by week two they'd switched to jeans and nobody commented either way.*

> *Quora — A commenter working at a more traditional Japanese company noted that jeans were technically "not banned" in writing, but that in three years no one below department-head level had actually worn them, which functioned as a rule regardless.*

> *The Local Germany — A menswear writer interviewed on German style noted that spending visibly on a tailored suit can actively damage a professional's reputation, while an expensive outdoor jacket carries no such penalty — a distinction that trips up newcomers who assume all visible spending reads the same way.*

> *JoynTokyo — An expat guide for foreign professionals advised starting deliberately conservative and watching how colleagues dress before adjusting, warning that Japanese offices rarely state their real dress code out loud and expect it to be inferred through observation.*

> *Big In Japan (Geinokai blog) — A piece on salaryman uniform culture described the quiet tension some younger employees feel between individual style and the expectation to visually match their department, noting that generational pushback is real but still mostly confined to smaller or newer companies.*

### Conclusion

The practical advice is almost embarrassingly simple: in Germany, arrive dressed one notch more casually than you think appropriate and adjust upward only if forced to; in Japan, arrive dressed one notch more formally than you think appropriate and adjust downward only once someone senior does it first. The deeper lesson is that both countries use clothing to manage the same anxiety — looking like you don't understand the room — they've just built opposite defaults to solve it. Pack the sandals and the suit; you'll need to know which country is watching before you know which one to wear.
