# Jeans Are Sophistication, a Tie Is Respect: Dressing for the Office in the Netherlands and India

**🇳🇱 Netherlands · 🇮🇳 India**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A well-fitting pair of jeans is, by Dutch standards, close to the height of office sophistication. In much of corporate India, the same jeans in a client meeting can read as a quiet failure to take the room seriously. Both countries have entire industries — banking in one, IT services in the other — that break from the local norm in opposite directions, which means the honest answer to "what do I wear on day one" is: it depends which floor you're on, and neither country will tell you that in the offer letter.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Start smart on day one, then match what you observe by week two | Over-dress permanently — it can read as trying too hard, not respect |
| Expect banking and legal sectors to still expect dark suits | Assume every industry follows the relaxed tech/creative norm |
| Wear well-fitted jeans and a clean shirt with confidence in casual offices | Confuse "informal" with "sloppy" — fit and cleanliness still matter |
| Ask HR directly if the dress code is unclear — it's a normal question | Guess and risk being visibly over- or under-dressed in front of clients |
| Read the informality as part of a broader egalitarian workplace ethos | Mistake casual dress for casual hierarchy — managers still hold authority |

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to business casual or formal in client-facing and MNC roles | Assume the "IT sector is casual" stereotype applies to every employer |
| Wear breathable, light fabrics suited to a warm climate while staying formal | Wear heavy Western wool suits without adapting to the heat |
| Recognise sarees and salwar kameez as fully acceptable, respected business attire for women | Assume Western business dress is the only "professional" option |
| Check whether your specific office is a delivery centre, MNC hub, or startup — norms differ sharply | Show up underdressed to a metro-city client meeting on the assumption "tech means casual" |
| Notice how dress signals seniority and occasion more than in more uniformly casual cultures | Ignore that a founder in jeans and a fresher in jeans are read very differently |

### Netherlands: Informal by Design, Formal by Exception

Dutch office culture treats formality as something to actively justify rather than default to. World Business Culture and multiple expatriate guides describe an overall dress code that leans casual — jeans, clean shirts, sneakers in many tech, creative, and consumer-goods offices — grounded in a broader Dutch preference for egalitarianism over hierarchy. Hofstede Insights scores the Netherlands notably low on masculinity, around 14, reflecting a workplace culture that values consensus, modesty, and flatness over displays of status, dress included.

The exceptions are sharply defined rather than blurry: banking, legal, and other traditionally formal sectors still expect dark suits, muted ties, and white shirts, largely unchanged from decades past. The practical advice repeated across expat guides — start smart, then calibrate down based on what you actually see around you — reflects a culture comfortable enough in its own informality that it doesn't need visible signals of it to function, but hasn't fully abandoned formality where clients or tradition still expect it.

### India: Formality as a Function of Floor and Client

India's dress culture resists a single national answer more than almost any other country in this series, because the variance sits inside individual companies rather than between them. Multinational corporate offices, banks, and client-facing delivery centres commonly expect business casual or formal wear — suits, shirts, trousers, or sarees and salwar kameez for women — while pure product-engineering teams at the same company, one floor up, may dress in T-shirts and jeans. India Briefing and multiple business-etiquette guides note that companies like Infosys and Hindustan Unilever have moved toward more casual dress in recent years, mirroring Western tech trends, even as India Hofstede Insights scores the country relatively high on power distance, around 77, and moderately masculine, around 56 — a profile that still rewards visible signals of seniority and occasion far more than the Dutch system does.

Climate does real work here too: full-sleeved shirts and ties remain acceptable and common despite the heat, but breathable fabric choices are a practical necessity that Western wool-suit norms don't accommodate well.

### The Reckoning

The countries invert each other's logic almost exactly. The Netherlands treats casual dress as the cultural default and formality as a deliberate, sector-specific exception that signals tradition rather than power. India treats formality as the safer default and casualness as a deliberate signal — usually of a startup mentality, a specific tech subculture, or explicit company policy — layered on top of a broader culture where dress still tracks hierarchy and occasion closely.

The trap for newcomers runs in both directions. Arrive in Amsterdam in a suit and you may read as needlessly stiff in a flat, consensus-driven office; arrive in Bangalore in jeans assuming "IT is casual" and you may misjudge a client-facing delivery centre that expects business formal. Both mistakes are avoidable with the same tactic — observe before you commit, and ask HR directly rather than pattern-matching from a stereotype about the country as a whole.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who moved to Amsterdam for work said the biggest adjustment wasn't the informality itself but recalibrating what "put together" meant — well-fitted jeans and clean sneakers were treated as a legitimate professional look, not a corner cut, once they saw senior colleagues dressed the same way.*

> *Reddit — An American who joined an Indian MNC's Bangalore office described assuming the IT-sector-casual stereotype would apply everywhere, then showing up in jeans to discover the client-facing floor expected business formal — a colleague quietly suggested they keep a blazer at their desk from then on.*

> *Reddit — A poster reflecting on a Dutch banking internship said the contrast within the same city was jarring — friends in tech roles wore hoodies to work while they were expected in a dark suit daily, and neither office considered the other's norm unusual.*

> *Internations (India expat community) — A European professional working in Delhi noted that female colleagues in sarees and Western-style pantsuits were treated with equal seniority-appropriate respect in the same meeting, which reframed their assumption that "formal" in India meant only Western business dress.*

### Conclusion

If dress code is one of the things keeping you up before a move, the practical fix is the same in both countries: don't trust the national stereotype, trust your specific office floor. In the Netherlands, start slightly smarter than you think you need to and let your colleagues' actual clothes recalibrate you downward. In India, start more formal than the "casual tech culture" headlines suggest and let HR or a direct colleague tell you when it's safe to relax. The thing the brochure never mentions is that in both countries, the fastest way to look like you belong is to simply ask — nobody will think less of you for checking, and everybody will notice if you guess wrong in front of a client.
