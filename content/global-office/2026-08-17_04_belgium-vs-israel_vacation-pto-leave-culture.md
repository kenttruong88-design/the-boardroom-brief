**Thirty Days Off vs. Twelve Plus a War: Leave Culture in Belgium and Israel**

**🇧🇪 Belgium · 🇮🇱 Israel**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Belgium doesn't really ask whether you're taking August off; it assumes you already are, along with most of the country, and quietly shuts down accordingly. Israel doesn't offer nearly as much guaranteed rest — twelve statutory days for a new employee, rising slowly with seniority — but it carries a category of mandatory leave that has nothing to do with rest at all: reserve military duty, which can pull an employee out of the office for weeks with no relation to a vacation calendar. Anyone comparing these two on days-off-per-year alone is comparing the wrong thing entirely.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇪 Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect roughly 20 statutory vacation days plus 10 public holidays — about 30 paid days off total | Don't schedule anything requiring broad internal cooperation in August; much of the country is away |
| Know that leave entitlement is calculated from the prior year's work — a first-year hire may start with less | Don't check work email while on holiday; disconnecting fully is the cultural default, not an exception |
| Plan around the "three long weekends" rhythm many Belgians use to break up the year | Don't treat unused leave casually — it's tightly regulated, not a rollover slush fund |
| Ask your employer directly how prior-year-based accrual applies to your specific start date | Don't assume American-style "unlimited PTO" flexibility exists — Belgian leave is statutory and precise |
| Respect the strict separation between work time and personal time that follows from this structure | Don't be surprised that even senior leadership disappears for stretches — it's not a sign of low commitment |

#### 🇮🇱 Israel

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand that 12 days is a starting point that grows with tenure, up to 20 after enough years | Don't confuse reserve duty (miluim) with vacation — it is legally a separate, protected leave category |
| Ask how your employer handles Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, and Passover scheduling in advance | Don't schedule critical deadlines around major Jewish holidays without checking the calendar first |
| Know that returning reservists have 30 days of legal protection against termination | Don't assume weekend structure matches the West — Friday evening to Saturday evening is the rest period, not Sunday |
| Expect unused vacation from a miluim-disrupted year to roll into the next two years | Don't treat a colleague's reserve callup as a minor scheduling inconvenience — it can mean weeks away with legal protection |
| Check whether your specific industry (tech, finance) offers above-minimum leave, which is common | Don't assume 12 days reflects how much time off Israeli colleagues actually value taking |

---

Belgium's leave culture is a straightforward, generous consequence of EU labor law layered onto a deeply rooted national habit. The statutory floor is 20 vacation days for a standard five-day week, on top of 10 public holidays, which brings a typical full-time employee to roughly 30 paid days off a year — among the more generous packages in a bloc that already sets a 20-day EU minimum. What official leave-law guides don't fully capture is the cultural intensity of how those days are used: Statista survey data on Belgian holiday habits found a majority of respondents said they never check work messages while on leave, and Belgium's well-documented pattern of near-total shutdown in August is less an individual choice than a shared national rhythm that companies build their calendars around.

Israel's framework starts from a much smaller number and a very different logic. The Annual Leave Law of 1951 sets a floor of just 12 paid vacation days for employees in their first five years, rising to 14 in year six, 15 in year seven, and by one additional day per year of tenure after that, up to a cap of 20. That modest baseline sits inside a wider leave system shaped by two forces with no equivalent in Belgium: a full slate of religious holidays — Rosh Hashanah, Yom Kippur, Passover among them — that structure the working calendar around the Jewish lunar year, and compulsory military reserve duty, miluim, which by law is not counted against vacation days at all. Employees called up for at least two consecutive days of reserve service gain 30 days of legal protection against termination afterward, and if miluim service prevents an employee from using vacation days within the year, those days legally roll into the following two years rather than being lost.

**The Reckoning**

The comparison that looks obvious on a spreadsheet — Belgium wins on days off, more than double Israel's statutory floor — collapses once you account for what each system is actually built to protect. Belgium's leave law protects rest, full stop, and the culture around it reinforces that with near-religious consistency: the August closure, the refusal to check email, the assumption that senior leadership vanishing for weeks is normal rather than alarming. Israel's leave law protects something closer to civic obligation layered on top of a thinner rest allowance — the system bends hard to accommodate reserve duty and religious observance, but it was never designed to guarantee the kind of extended, uninterrupted personal downtime that is simply assumed in Brussels.

The practical upshot for anyone relocating: a Belgian employee who takes 12 days off in a year would be considered under-resting by local norms. An Israeli employee with the same 12 days is at their legal floor and, quite possibly, also facing a miluim callup that has nothing to do with the vacation conversation at all. Comparing the two countries by day count alone tells you almost nothing about what either workforce actually experiences.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone answering a question about reserve duty flexibility explained that while soldiers technically have some input into scheduling their miluim service, the practical reality is that call-up dates are frequently set by military needs rather than personal convenience, and employers are legally required to accommodate it regardless of project timing.*

> *Statista-cited survey data — Belgian respondents overwhelmingly reported never checking work email while on holiday, a habit multiple relocating professionals described as taking real, deliberate effort to adopt after arriving from more always-on work cultures.*

> *Substack (relocation blog, Southern Europe) — A writer comparing European vacation habits to the American default described the psychological adjustment of an August where literally nothing gets scheduled — no meetings, no expected replies — as initially disorienting before becoming the single most-valued part of relocating to Belgium.*

> *Route 38 Blog (Israeli employment law commentary) — A piece aimed at employers and reservists alike stressed that many managers underestimate how disruptive an extended miluim callup can be to a small team, since the law protects the individual reservist's job but does nothing to cover the workload left behind.*

> *Reddit — A foreign hire working in Tel Aviv noted that the 12-day statutory minimum felt startlingly low compared to European postings, until a colleague's multi-week reserve callup made clear that Israeli leave culture wasn't really built around long vacations to begin with — it was built to flex around something else entirely.*

---

### Conclusion

If uninterrupted personal rest is what you're optimizing for, Belgium's math does the talking: a legally protected, culturally enforced 30 days a year, including a national August that nobody argues with. Israel offers less guaranteed rest but a leave system built for a different kind of disruption — one a Belgian employer would never plan around and an Israeli one budgets for as a matter of course. Neither is more generous in the abstract; they're generous about different things. If a friend asked me over drinks: move to Belgium for the rest, move to Israel understanding that "leave" there sometimes means something closer to duty than to vacation.
