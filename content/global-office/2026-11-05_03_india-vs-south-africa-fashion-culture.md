# What You Wear to Work in Mumbai and Johannesburg Is Never Just What You Wear to Work

🇮🇳 India · 🇿🇦 South Africa

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

An Indian office can fine you for wearing jeans on the wrong Friday. A South African office cannot agree, city to city, on whether Friday itself is casual. Anyone arriving in either country expecting a single, importable dress code — business casual, say, the great global compromise — is about to learn that clothing at work is doing several jobs at once: status, heritage, climate, and, in at least one documented case, actual payroll deduction.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep one or two traditional outfits (a kurta, a saree) ready for festival or "traditional day" events | Assume a Western business-casual wardrobe alone will see you through the year |
| Cover shoulders and knees in public, especially outside the major metros | Wear shorts or sleeveless tops to a client site without checking local norms first |
| Treat a saree or kurta as a safe, respected professional choice, not a compromise | Avoid traditional dress at work assuming it reads as less "serious" than Western clothing |
| Check your specific office's actual norm — Mumbai and Chennai can differ sharply from Delhi | Assume "business casual" means the same wardrobe in every Indian city |
| Budget for a possible fine or social cost on company "ethnic day" events if you arrive without appropriate clothing | Treat a mandatory traditional-dress day as optional just because it isn't in your contract |

#### 🇿🇦 South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your specific city before packing — Cape Town, Johannesburg, and Durban genuinely differ | Assume "South African office style" is one uniform thing |
| Treat heritage prints, beadwork, or a doek as a legitimate professional style choice, not a casual one | Assume incorporating cultural dress into office wear reads as unprofessional |
| Keep one blazer on hand even in a casual city — it still gets used for client meetings | Show up in full startup-casual to a Johannesburg client presentation without checking the room first |
| Know that courts have limited blanket bans on religious or cultural dress at work | Assume a strict company dress code can override deeply held religious or cultural dress practices without a real safety justification |
| Dress up for errands and outings more than you might expect, especially outside Cape Town | Assume off-hours dress norms are as relaxed as the most casual city you've heard about |

---

India's workplace dress code has a reputation, largely inherited from decades of corporate photography, for formality bordering on uniform. The reality on the ground, per SheThePeople.tv's reporting, has been shifting for years: stylist Neha Gandhi describes women moving away from "dull, stereotypical" office wear toward colour and print, and online apparel shopping among working women has nearly doubled over the past decade by one fashion blogger's estimate. But the shift coexists with real institutional friction over what, exactly, counts as appropriate. Indra Nooyi's own memoir recounts PepsiCo colleagues excluding her from an Indianapolis client meeting because they worried her saree would be "too jarring" — and, separately, a Yale career-services director telling her, ahead of a 1978 admissions interview, to simply wear the saree she was comfortable in rather than force herself into an ill-fitting Western suit, reasoning that "if they do not hire you, it's their loss." That tension between traditional dress as a source of confidence and traditional dress as an imposed, sometimes costly obligation shows up at the opposite end of the pay scale too: Free Press Journal reported on a new hire in Pune, less than a month into her job, fined one hundred rupees for wearing a shirt and jeans instead of Indian wear on a company festival day — she said she simply couldn't afford ethnic clothing that particular month-end. A separate viral case, covered by Asianet News, involved a company reversing its own "casual Friday" into a mandatory "formal or traditional" policy, with one disgruntled employee threatening, only half-joking, to show up in a lehenga out of spite.

South Africa's problem is less about tradition versus modernity than about the country's refusal to agree with itself. Business Report's lifestyle desk describes Cape Town's corporate culture as "comfort first, style second" — jeans, sneakers, hoodies, with a suit jacket deployed only for client presentations, sometimes thrown on over a T-shirt — while Johannesburg runs almost the opposite script, with tailored blazers and "power heels" worn as deliberate personal-branding statements, and Durban's humidity producing its own uniform of linen and sandals. Layered onto this geographic variation is a genuine, documented movement to bring heritage into the office rather than leave it at home: Quartz reported on Johannesburg label Earth Nut building corporate jackets trimmed in African print and Zulu beadwork, on career coach Vumi Msweli wearing Shweshwe-print suits with matching doeks as a statement of professional strength rather than a concession to informality, and on the fallout when a national broadcaster briefly pulled a segment over a presenter's on-air head-wrap before reversing the decision under public pressure. The legal backdrop supports that shift: employment lawyers quoted by BusinessTech note that blanket dress-code bans on religious or cultural clothing are unlikely to survive a challenge in South African courts, though strict safety-driven rules can still apply where a job genuinely requires them.

Climate and geography complicate both countries' dress codes in ways no national guideline fully captures. Talesmag's crowdsourced reports from three different Indian cities make the point almost by accident: a New Delhi contributor describes men dropping ties and jackets from April through September specifically for the heat, while a Chennai respondent recalls wearing a suit and tie only twice in two years, and a Mumbai contributor calls the local code "very lax" by comparison to either. South Africa shows the same regional splinter for a different reason — not heat alone but genuinely distinct civic personalities. Durban's humidity produces a linen-and-sandals default that Business Report's lifestyle desk ties directly to climate, while Johannesburg's much drier, more competitive business culture produces the opposite instinct: dressing up as a form of visible ambition rather than a concession to comfort. For a newcomer packing a single wardrobe for either country, the honest advice from these crowdsourced accounts is consistent — pack for the specific city, not the national stereotype, and expect your first week's wardrobe choices to be wrong at least once.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't formal versus casual — both countries have plenty of each — it's where the authority over dress actually sits. In India, the friction runs between the individual and an institution asserting a specific, sometimes financially enforced, cultural expectation: wear this on this day, or pay for the difference. In South Africa, the friction runs between regions and identities asserting themselves against each other, with heritage dress increasingly winning ground as a legitimate professional register rather than a tolerated exception, backed by real legal protection most Indian employees fining their own new hires would not recognize.

For someone relocating, the practical upshot is almost backwards from the stereotype. Assume India's dress code is more codified and occasionally more costly to get wrong than its reputation for casual tech-office culture suggests. Assume South Africa's dress code is more genuinely contested and city-specific than any single "African business casual" guide will tell you — and that wearing your own heritage, rather than suppressing it, is increasingly the more professionally confident move, not the riskier one.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit, via Asianet News — an employee at a company that switched from casual Friday to mandatory traditional dress joked publicly that she'd show up in a lehenga out of spite if forced to comply, and the resulting comment thread split sharply between people calling the policy petty micromanagement and people insisting standards matter.*

> *Reddit, via Free Press Journal — a new hire less than a month into her job was fined for not wearing ethnic clothing on a company festival day and explained, in her own post, that she genuinely couldn't afford appropriate clothing that month — a detail that turned a minor dress-code story into a wider conversation about unexamined financial assumptions behind "traditional day" policies.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Mumbai — one contributor called the local dress code "very lax," listing sneakers and collared shirts as the norm, while another noted that shorts are fine for both sexes in the city generally but that many women still cover shoulders and knees specifically on public transport.*

> *Talesmag Real Post Report, Johannesburg — two separate contributors, years apart, independently observed that women in the city "dress up more" even for routine errands like grocery shopping, a detail neither expected before arriving.*

> *Business Report / IOL, South Africa — a Cape Town-based contributor described the local norm so bluntly it became the article's own framing: in that city, "every day feels like casual Friday," a line several Johannesburg-based colleagues reportedly found almost unbelievable.*

### Conclusion

The one piece of advice worth packing before either move: find out your specific office's actual rule before you find out the hard way, because in both countries the national stereotype is less useful than the building you're about to work in. In India, that might mean asking HR directly whether "traditional day" is enforced with an actual fine. In South Africa, it might mean asking whether you've been hired into Cape Town's hoodie economy or Johannesburg's power-heel one.

If a friend asked me what to actually bring, I'd say: one good kurta, one good blazer, and the humility to ask a local colleague before assuming either country's fashion sense works the way the brochure — or the last office you worked in — told you it would.
