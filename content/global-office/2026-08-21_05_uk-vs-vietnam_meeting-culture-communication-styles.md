**"That's an Interesting Idea" Means No in Both Countries. It Just Takes Longer to Find Out Why in One of Them.**

**🇬🇧 UK · 🇻🇳 Vietnam**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Executives who take British politeness at face value routinely leave meetings believing they got a yes, when what they actually got was "that might be difficult" — a phrase doing quiet, deliberate work. Vietnamese meetings run on a similar principle from the opposite direction: a "yes" might mean agreement, or it might just mean "I heard you and don't want to embarrass either of us by saying no out loud." Hofstede Insights scores the UK at a low 35 on Power Distance against Vietnam's considerably higher 70, which explains the hierarchy gap — but it's the shared instinct toward indirectness, arrived at for entirely different reasons, that actually trips up newcomers in both places.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇧 UK

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to hear "that's brave" or "interesting" as polite disagreement | Take mild-sounding pushback at face value as approval |
| Arrive on time and expect meetings to start and end on schedule | Assume British punctuality is loose because the tone is relaxed |
| Watch for silence as a signal of discomfort, not agreement | Interpret quiet in the room as consensus |
| Raise real objections privately or in follow-up rather than head-on in the room | Openly contradict a senior colleague in front of the group |
| Use humour to soften disagreement — it's a genuine communication tool, not just banter | Take self-deprecating humour as a lack of seriousness about the topic |
| Confirm action items and next steps explicitly at the end of a meeting | Leave assuming everyone shares your read on what was actually decided |

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask open-ended questions rather than blunt yes/no ones | Corner a colleague with a direct yes/no question in a group setting |
| Expect real consensus-building to happen privately, before or after the meeting | Assume the meeting itself is where the actual decision gets made |
| Follow up verbal discussions with written confirmation | Rely on a verbal "yes" in a meeting as a firm commitment |
| Give junior colleagues room and privacy to express disagreement | Expect junior staff to voice objections in front of senior leadership |
| Treat "yes" as possibly meaning "understood" rather than "agreed" | Take every affirmative response as literal agreement |
| Learn a little Vietnamese — even basic phrases are noticed and appreciated | Assume English fluency signals full comfort with direct Western-style debate |

**UK**

British meeting culture runs on a paradox: it is simultaneously highly structured and deliberately vague. Meetings, per Global Business Culture and Commisceo Global's guides, start and end punctually, with clear agendas and assigned action items — a genuinely low-context, orderly format on the surface. But the content exchanged inside that structure is often the opposite: coded, softened, and layered with understatement. Disagreement rarely arrives as "that's wrong." It arrives as "perhaps we could consider an alternative approach," or the now-famous "that's a brave decision" — a phrase widely understood in British professional circles to mean something closer to reckless. Hofstede's low Power Distance score for the UK (35) supports open dialogue in theory — junior staff are nominally encouraged to speak up — but that flatness coexists with a strong cultural aversion to direct confrontation, particularly with senior colleagues present.

The practical trap for newcomers, especially those from more direct communication cultures, is mistaking politeness for agreement. Silence in a British meeting doesn't necessarily mean assent; per business-etiquette guides aimed at foreign hires, it can signal hesitation, discomfort, or a simple British reluctance to be the one who says the awkward thing out loud. The workaround culturally sanctioned in British offices isn't confrontation — it's the follow-up email, the quiet aside after the meeting, or the raised eyebrow that everyone in the room reads correctly except the newest hire.

**Vietnam**

Vietnamese meeting culture is shaped by two forces working together: a hierarchical social structure — Hofstede's Power Distance score of 70 reflects real, expected deference to seniority and authority — and the concept of "face," which governs communication far more than any org chart does. Per Commisceo Global and Rivermate's cultural guides, disagreement is almost never voiced directly, especially not by a junior employee toward a senior one in a group setting, because doing so risks causing the other person to lose face publicly, a much more serious social cost in Vietnam than an unresolved disagreement.

This produces a specific, learnable pattern: consensus in Vietnamese workplaces is built privately, through side conversations and written follow-ups, rather than through open debate in the meeting room itself. Asking a blunt yes-or-no question in front of a group can put a Vietnamese colleague in a genuinely uncomfortable position, because an honest "no" might read as confrontational while a dishonest "yes" avoids the immediate friction but stores up the actual problem for later. Business guides consistently recommend open-ended questions and private check-ins as the more reliable route to an honest answer — the meeting itself is often closer to a formality that ratifies a decision than the place where the real thinking happens.

**The Reckoning**

Here's the twist: both cultures rely on indirectness for the same underlying reason — protecting a relationship from the damage of open confrontation — but they encode it into completely different parts of the process. The British hide the real message inside the words themselves; you have to learn to decode "interesting" and "brave" and "might be difficult" as a private vocabulary of disagreement, while the meeting's formal structure (punctuality, agendas, assigned actions) stays crisp and legible throughout. The Vietnamese, by contrast, keep the words relatively straightforward but move the real decision-making process outside the meeting entirely — you have to learn to distrust the room itself as the site of the actual answer, and go looking for it in private conversations before or after.

Which means the newcomer's fix is almost opposite in each place. In the UK, listen harder to what's said, because the information is compressed into careful phrasing. In Vietnam, listen less to what's said in the room and more to what happens around it — who's talking to whom afterward, what gets confirmed in writing, what silence from a senior figure actually precedes.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A response explaining British indirectness described it as a deliberate courtesy: criticism is delivered "softly," by taking the long way around a point and letting the listener do the work of interpreting it, rather than stating it outright.*

> *Quora — Someone contrasting American and British professional communication observed that British colleagues have effectively mastered tact and diplomacy as tools, and that mistaking this diplomacy for actual agreement is one of the most common errors newcomers make in their first few meetings.*

> *Quora — A contributor describing Vietnamese communication patterns noted that people frequently say things they don't literally mean, without any specific hidden goal behind it — it's simply the accepted texture of polite conversation, which can read as confusing or even evasive to someone expecting literal precision.*

> *Quora — Someone answering what surprises foreigners living in Vietnam pointed to how warmly and visibly pleased Vietnamese colleagues become when a foreign coworker makes even a modest effort to speak Vietnamese — describing it as a small gesture that measurably changed how quickly they were brought into informal, trust-based conversations at work.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving into a British office, your job is to become fluent in understatement — treat "interesting" as a yellow flag and "brave" as a red one, and do your real negotiating in the quiet follow-up, not the room. If you're moving into a Vietnamese office, your job is to stop trusting the meeting as the decision-making venue at all — build relationships and confirm things privately, and use open questions instead of yes-or-no ones so people aren't forced into a public choice between honesty and face. Both cultures are protecting the same thing, harmony, they've just built entirely different plumbing for it. The line I'd give a friend: in London, read between the lines; in Hanoi, read around the room.
