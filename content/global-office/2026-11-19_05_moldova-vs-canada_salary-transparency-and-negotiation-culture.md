# Canada Prints the Salary in the Ad; Moldova Is Still Working Out How Much of It Goes in the Envelope

🇲🇩 Moldova · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Canada, 57 percent of job postings now tell you what the job pays, a figure that stood at roughly one in five six years ago. In Moldova, the National Bureau of Statistics reports that 5.7 percent of employees held informal jobs and also collected part of their pay unofficially, which is a different kind of transparency, though it does have the virtue of being cash. For the worker weighing a move, the two countries offer opposite opening lines to the same conversation: one begins with a number, the other with a question about which number you mean.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Moldova

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for the gross monthly figure in lei, in writing, before you resign from anything | Accept "the rest in cash" as a pay structure, however warmly it is described |
| Ask whether year-end bonuses are part of the quoted package, since the first-quarter 2026 average dipped after year-end bonuses were paid out | Compare your offer to the national average of roughly 16,000 lei without asking which sector it describes |
| Research the sector, because informal pay clusters in agriculture, construction and trade | Assume the posted ad contains a salary, or that omission means a bad offer |
| Treat the first meeting as a conversation about hierarchy as much as money | Open with an aggressive counteroffer to someone senior in the room |
| Price your own rent and technology costs before judging the lei figure | Assume a lower cost of living applies equally to everything you will buy |
| Verify claims about citizenship requirements for office jobs with an official source | Rely on a forum post from seven years ago to plan your visa |

### Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the posted range in Ontario, British Columbia, PEI and now New Brunswick, where one is required | Assume every province requires one; Quebec and Alberta do not |
| Negotiate total compensation, including vacation, remote days and development budget | Treat the base salary as the only term on the table |
| Give a researched range when asked about expectations, and anchor near the top of it | Volunteer your previous salary where the law bars employers from asking |
| Counter the first offer politely and in writing | Accept it on the theory that a quick yes signals enthusiasm |
| Ask about raise timelines and review criteria if you plan to stay a year or more | Push for the maximum salary at the expense of everything else you need |
| Compare the posted range with the offer, and ask about any gap | Take a very wide range at face value; applicants tend to distrust them |

## Moldova

Moldova's official wage picture is respectable for a country that has been busy. The National Bureau of Statistics put the average gross monthly wage at 15,987 lei in the first quarter of 2026, up 9.7 percent on the year, and down 2.3 percent on the previous quarter, a dip that Logos Press attributes to the passing of year-end bonuses. That is roughly 900 dollars at the 17.8 lei to the dollar rate the same outlet displays. The distribution is wide: the Bureau's September 2025 data show 20.4 percent of full-time employees earning up to 7,000 lei and 32.6 percent earning more than 15,000. The minimum wage rose to 6,300 lei in January, and the government projects an average of 17,400 lei for the year. The Bureau also found that 7.5 percent of employed respondents wanted to change jobs, mainly because of low income, which suggests that the lei figure, however respectable on a chart, is not universally felt.

The unofficial part of the picture is where a newcomer should be careful. According to the same Bureau, informal employment accounted for 17.3 percent of total employment in 2025, and 5.7 percent of employees had informal jobs while also receiving part of their pay off the books. The concentration is telling: agriculture accounts for 58 percent of the envelope cases, construction 12.6 percent and trade 9.9 percent. Expert-Grup's Marina Solovieva told Logos Press that the practice is slowly declining. Informal work is far more common in the fields than in the offices: the Bureau puts it at 61 percent of agricultural employment and 49 percent of construction, against 10.2 percent outside agriculture. A software engineer in Chișinău is unlikely to meet the envelope, though a site foreman in the same city has a fair chance.

Negotiation, meanwhile, happens inside a culture that Hofstede Insights scores at 90 for power distance and 17 for individualism, according to figures compiled in a Moldovan academic working paper. Canada, by the same scale, scores 80 for individualism. The implication is not that Moldovans cannot haggle, merely that the hierarchy in the room matters before the number does. This correspondent found no Moldovan equivalent of the posting rules now spreading through Canada, so the honest advice is to ask early, ask in writing and expect the figure to be a conversation rather than a printed fact.

## Canada

Canada has decided, province by province, that the number should come first. Ontario adds a disclosure of any artificial intelligence used to screen applicants, so the machine, at least, must now be as candid as the salary. According to Employment Hero's survey of the rules, British Columbia has required expected pay in postings since November 2023, Prince Edward Island since 2022, Ontario since 1 January 2026 for employers with 25 or more staff, and New Brunswick since 12 June 2026. Several provinces also bar employers from asking for pay history. Quebec, Alberta and Saskatchewan have no posting requirement at all, so the country is less a single market than a patchwork with a very polite border.

The results are visible. Indeed's Pay Transparency Tracker, reported by Canadian HR Reporter in July, found that Ontario's disclosure rate climbed from 49 percent just before the rules took effect to 71 percent by June 2026, while the rest of Canada outside BC and Ontario sat at 41 percent, essentially unchanged for three years. Only 16 percent of those postings give an exact figure, and the share of human resources postings with pay rose from 30 to 70 percent, which suggests the profession has taken the lesson personally. The rest give ranges, which Ontario now caps at 50,000 dollars width, a rule Law Times notes has been criticised as arbitrary, and which exists because employers had begun publishing ranges wide enough to land a small aircraft.

Negotiation, once a matter of private nerve, remains optional in practice. A Robert Half Canada survey from 2020 found that only 36 percent of professionals negotiated their last offer, against 54 percent in the United States, and that 40 percent of men negotiated compared with 31 percent of women. The same survey found that 59 percent of those who did not negotiate were content with the offer, while 19 percent wanted more money but were uncomfortable asking. A separate Robert Half survey of hiring managers found that 39 percent raise pay at the first in-person interview and 25 percent wait for the second, so the topic arrives sooner than newcomers often expect. Moving2Canada nonetheless advises newcomers that pay is usually negotiable and that benefits, vacation and flexibility belong on the table. Canadians, it appears, have built an elaborate public system of salary disclosure and then declined to haggle over it.

## The Reckoning

Head to head, the two countries are not really offering rival versions of the same thing. Canada offers a legal floor of information, a posted range and in several provinces a ban on questions about your past pay, and leaves you to decide whether to use it. Moldova offers a lower pay scale, an official statistics bureau that publishes the distribution candidly, and a negotiating environment in which the rules are mostly unwritten. The Canadian worker's problem is overconfidence in a printed range. The Moldovan worker's problem is that the range may not be printed at all.

The sensible reading for a mover is therefore asymmetrical. In Canada, the work is to negotiate against transparency, to use the posted figure as a starting point rather than a ceiling and to remember that the first offer is a position and not a verdict. In Moldova, the work is to create the transparency yourself, by asking for gross figures, bonus terms and the contract in writing, and by treating any suggestion of unrecorded pay as a cost, not a perk. Neither country will do the arithmetic for you, though only one of them prints the inputs.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — paraphrased experience of a Canada-based tech worker who said fewer employers and fewer competing offers in Canada mean less upward pressure on pay, and that when they pushed a top-tier employer above its regional range, the offer was simply reclassified into a higher, San Francisco-style market band.*

> *Hacker News — paraphrased experience of a commenter living in Moldova who said that the lower cost of living used to justify outsourcing is overstated, since technology costs about twice as much as in the UK and used cars are far dearer, while rent is cheaper and utilities are about the same.*

> *Hacker News — paraphrased experience of a US engineer who works with colleagues in Moldova, Romania and Serbia and said they work hard and have strong technical skills, with lower salaries reflecting regional macroeconomics rather than ability.*

> *Expat.com — paraphrased experience of a poster who claimed that foreigners can find well-paid work in Moldova in roles such as assembly lines or cashier posts, while better office jobs such as bookkeeping were said to require citizenship, a claim the poster presented as easy to satisfy and another reader then questioned over its cost.*

> *Expat.com — paraphrased experience of a German job seeker who could find mostly Orange-branded listings in Moldova and no salary information for accountant or back-office roles, and who estimated needing about 200 euros a month for a decent apartment.*

## Conclusion

If your priority is knowing what you will be paid before you apply, Canada is built for you, at least in Ontario, British Columbia, PEI and New Brunswick. Read the range, ignore the temptation to treat it as final, negotiate the full package and keep your previous salary to yourself where the law allows. If your priority is a lower-cost environment and you are comfortable in a more hierarchical culture, Moldova can work, provided you insist on gross figures in lei and on a contract that records all of it.

Either way, ask for the number in writing. In Canada it will probably already be in the advertisement, and in Moldova it may be the first time anyone has said it aloud, which is a form of transparency of its own.
