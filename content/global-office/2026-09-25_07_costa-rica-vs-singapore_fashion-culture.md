**Costa Rica vs. Singapore: Flip-Flops Versus Full Air-Conditioned Formality**

**🇨🇷 Costa Rica · 🇸🇬 Singapore**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Both countries sit close enough to the equator that humidity is a daily fact of life, and both have arrived at almost opposite answers to what that means for what you wear to work. Costa Rica's "pura vida" ethos, per multiple cultural guides and Howler Magazine's own reporting on expat wardrobe adjustment, extends into office life as a genuine, sanctioned informality — breathable fabrics, earth tones, and a general suspicion of anything that reads as trying too hard. Singapore, despite sitting almost directly on the equator itself, has built an office culture where business formality survives the climate entirely intact, aided by air conditioning so aggressive that many offices require a cardigan indoors. New arrivals in Costa Rica learn, per Howler's reporting, that "stilettos and heavy makeup don't hold up in jungle humidity." New arrivals in Singapore learn the opposite lesson just as fast.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇷 Costa Rica

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to breathable, lightweight fabrics and a relaxed silhouette — even in professional settings, comfort visibly wins over stiff formality | Overdress for a first meeting; arriving in a full formal suit outside banking, law, or government sectors can read as oddly stiff |
| Take cues from the "pura vida" ethos — practical, unfussy, a little earthy — as a genuine cultural value, not just a tourism slogan | Assume "casual" means sloppy; Costa Rican casual dress is still put-together, just relaxed rather than formal |
| Save heels, heavy makeup, and structured tailoring for genuinely formal occasions — daily jungle humidity and Costa Rica's walkable, outdoor-oriented office culture make them impractical fast | Pack primarily formal business attire expecting to need it daily; most workplaces outside a few formal sectors skew toward smart-casual at most |
| Invest in quality rain-ready layers — Costa Rica's climate shifts fast, and being visibly prepared is read as sensible, not overcautious | Ignore rainy-season practicality in favor of style; functional beats fashionable in Costa Rica's daily office reality |
| Watch what long-term local colleagues wear rather than what a tourist guide suggests — office norms and tourist-zone norms diverge noticeably | Assume beach-town dress norms (seen in tourist areas) apply the same way in San José's actual business districts |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to business or business-casual attire even in Singapore's heat — climate does not relax office dress expectations the way it might elsewhere in the tropics | Assume equatorial heat justifies shorts, flip-flops, or highly casual dress at the office; Singapore's professional norms remain formal regardless of temperature |
| Carry a light jacket or cardigan for the office — air conditioning indoors is famously aggressive and a real, commonly cited adjustment for newcomers | Dress only for the outdoor heat; you will likely be uncomfortably cold indoors within your first week without a layer |
| Observe your specific industry's calibration — finance and law skew more formal, tech and creative sectors allow more smart-casual flexibility | Assume a single uniform Singaporean dress code exists across all sectors; expectations vary meaningfully by industry |
| Keep a spare, presentable outfit at the office for unplanned client meetings — Singapore's business culture places real weight on being visibly put-together | Rely on humidity as an excuse for visible wrinkling or dishevelment; presentation standards remain high despite the climate |
| Ask HR or a colleague directly in your first week if uncertain — it is a normal, welcomed question in a multicultural, newcomer-heavy workforce | Guess based on what tourists or hawker-centre patrons wear; office dress norms in Singapore's central business district are considerably more formal |

**Costa Rica**

Costa Rica's relationship to fashion and dress, per cultural guides like Cyborlink's business etiquette profile and multiple general culture overviews, is inseparable from the country's broader "pura vida" ethos — an unhurried, unpretentious approach to life that shows up in clothing choices as a preference for practical, breathable, unfussy dress over overt formality, even in professional settings outside banking, law, and a handful of formal corporate sectors. Howler Magazine's reporting on expat wardrobe adjustment describes a consistent pattern among newer arrivals: packing for a professional move to Costa Rica the way one might for a temperate-climate capital, only to discover within weeks that heavy makeup, structured tailoring, and heeled shoes simply don't survive daily humidity, let alone the walkable, outdoor-adjacent nature of many Costa Rican workplaces.

That informality is a genuine cultural value rather than mere climate concession — several Quora responses to "How do Costa Ricans dress?" converge on describing local dress as relaxed but still deliberately put-together, a middle register between Western business-casual and something closer to resort wear that outsiders sometimes miscalibrate in either direction.

**Singapore**

Singapore presents almost the inverse case: a tropical, equatorial climate paired with a business culture that has, per trade.gov's Singapore Country Commercial Guide and multiple business-etiquette resources including A Briggs and Ron & Rich's guides, retained formal Western-style office dress norms largely undiluted by the weather. The explanation lies partly in Singapore's built environment — offices, malls, and transit are aggressively air-conditioned, effectively creating a climate-controlled indoor world where formal attire is physically comfortable even as the streets outside sit at near-constant tropical heat and humidity.

Singapore Expats Forum threads on office dress code make the practical adjustment explicit for newcomers: the recurring, genuinely useful advice is to carry a light jacket or cardigan specifically for indoor use, since the gap between outdoor heat and indoor air-conditioned cold is large enough to catch first-time arrivals off guard within days of starting a new job.

**The Reckoning**

Both countries sit near the equator; both have humid, genuinely hot climates outside; and yet they have built almost diametrically opposed professional dress cultures in response. Costa Rica let the climate and the "pura vida" ethos shape formal expectations downward — comfort as cultural value, not just practical concession. Singapore engineered its way around the climate entirely, using architecture and air conditioning to preserve a formal, internationally legible business dress code that owes more to its status as a global financial hub than to its actual weather.

The lesson for anyone moving between the two, or moving from a temperate country to either, is that climate alone is a poor predictor of dress expectations — culture and economic self-positioning matter more. Pack for the office you're actually walking into, not the thermometer reading outside it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A response to "How do Costa Ricans dress?" described locals as consistently more casual and practical than visitors expect, favoring comfortable, weather-appropriate clothing even in white-collar settings, and noted that dressing too formally for a routine meeting could actually come across as overly stiff.*

> *Quora — Someone answering "What's it like being an expat in Costa Rica?" mentioned packing an entire suitcase of business formalwear for a corporate relocation and using almost none of it, eventually giving most of it away, after realizing how little the local office culture called for it.*

> *Singapore Expats Forum — A newcomer's "Dress code in offices" thread asking what was actually expected drew replies emphasizing that Singapore's formal business dress norms hold regardless of the heat outside, with several respondents specifically flagging the indoor air conditioning as the bigger practical adjustment than the tropical climate itself.*

> *Quora — A response to "What is proper business etiquette in Singapore?" noted that even in creative or tech-sector offices with looser dress codes on paper, client-facing meetings still default to a more formal standard, and advised keeping at least one presentable outfit at the office at all times for unplanned meetings.*

### Conclusion

If dress code is shaping your decision between these two, the honest takeaway is that comfort and formality aren't determined by the thermometer in either place — they're determined by culture, and culture points in opposite directions here despite near-identical weather. Costa Rica will let you dress for the actual climate and call it professional; Singapore will expect you to dress for a boardroom in a temperate country and rely on architecture to make it bearable. Pack a cardigan either way — in Costa Rica, for the rain; in Singapore, for the air conditioning.
