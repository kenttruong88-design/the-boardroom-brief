# Bulgaria Is Still Deciding Whether Home Is a Workplace; Germany Decided Years Ago and Would Like a Word on Wednesdays

🇧🇬 Bulgaria · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In 2019, according to Eurostat figures reported by Bulgarian National Radio, 0.5 percent of employed Bulgarians habitually worked from home, the lowest share in the European Union, where the average was 5.4 percent. Germany, meanwhile, now has roughly a quarter of its employees working from home and, in the same breath, no law entitling any of them to do so. One country has too little remote work, the other has plenty of it but holds it on a revocable loan. The person with a laptop and an exit plan should know which condition is worse.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Bulgaria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get remote work written into your contract or an annex; the Labour Code makes it a matter of mutual consent | Assume your employer can be compelled to grant it; outside a declared emergency, neither side can impose it |
| Ask for the employer to supply equipment, software and internet costs, which the Labour Code puts on the employer | Accept an unwritten arrangement and then wonder who owns the router |
| If you have a child under 8, put your request in writing, since a refusal must then be justified in writing | Expect the law to hand you a fixed home-working allowance; it does not mandate one |
| Keep a written record of your working hours and rest | Assume the 12-hour daily rest period will be enforced for you |
| Look first at IT and international firms, which led the move to remote work | Arrive on a visa plan that depends on a fully remote job being easy to find |

### In Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate home-office days into your contract, a company agreement or a collective agreement | Rely on a custom of Thursday-at-home; without a contractual basis, the employer can generally direct you back |
| Ask whether a works council exists; it has co-determination rights over how mobile work is organised | Treat a verbal yes from your manager as a legal position |
| Claim the tax allowance of six euros per home-office day, up to 210 days a year | Expect the employer to pay for your desk if you chose home working while an office remained available |
| Agree a flat rate for internet and phone costs; around 50 euros a month is often considered reasonable | Try to itemise your broadband bill, which is hard to prove |
| Work out which weekday your colleagues come in before choosing yours | Schedule important meetings on a Monday or Friday and expect a full room |

## Bulgaria

Bulgaria's legal framework for remote work dates to 2011 and shows its age. According to the CMS Expert Guide, it is set by mutual consent, recorded in the employment contract or an annex, and an employer generally cannot be forced to accept a request. The exception is a parent of a child under eight, whose employer must justify a refusal in writing. The employer must supply equipment and cover connectivity, and may not deduct commuting savings from pay. This is a fairly civilised package, delivered to a labour market that has barely taken it up.

Nor did the Eurostat number rise quickly. The BNR English service reported a figure of 1.2 percent in a later Eurostat survey, again the lowest in the Union. Todor Kapitanov, a labour-law expert at the Confederation of Independent Trade Unions in Bulgaria, argued that remote work during the pandemic had been as good as office work and often better, so employers' fears had proved unfounded. He added that the working-time rules and the right to disconnect should be written into the law, and noted that the 12-hour rest between workdays had been left largely to workers' discretion. The trade union is, in other words, asking for the employer to behave as though the law had been finished.

The same BNR report listed the case for keeping things as they are. Companies cited better work quality and lower expenses, and experts doubted that the home office was ending even as people drifted back to desks. It also listed the case against: home working is hard for students in dormitories and for parents sharing a room with their children. The union proposed extra financial incentives to cover household costs, a request that the statute, which already obliges employers to pay for internet and equipment, has not yet turned into a general allowance.

The people who can work remotely tend to be few and specialised. Posters on Expat.com, answering an American couple hunting for English-only remote jobs to support a Bulgarian work visa, said call centres already employ many young Bulgarians with excellent English and that pay is modest. A member styled the forum's Bulgaria expert added that remote jobs are common but rarely arrive with full-time contracts, and that Bulgaria has no digital-nomad visa. The flexibility, in short, is real for those who already hold it and conspicuously hard to import.

## Germany

Germany's remote-work culture is established and, according to the numbers, entirely stationary. The Destatis figures reported by IamExpat put 25 percent of German employees at home in 2025, against an EU average of 23 percent and a German pre-pandemic level of 13 percent. The ifo Institute's August 2026 survey found 24.9 percent across the economy and 35.1 percent in services, with 6.2 percent in retail and 5.1 percent in construction. Researcher Jean-Victor Alipour said that for years there had been no significant change. One in five job postings offers home working; in finance and insurance the share is 42 percent.

The law has been less generous than the statistics. The CMS Expert Guide states plainly that there is no general legal right to work remotely in Germany, that a draft bill offering a right to discuss it remains unscheduled, and that an employer cannot unilaterally impose home working but may, where the contract names a workplace, require attendance there. As heise.de reported, a Federal Labour Court ruling of October 2025 points the same way: without a contractual agreement, employers can generally order staff to the office.

Some do. A Hans Boeckler Institute study cited by heise found that about a third of employees have been called back. Supervisors named better collegial exchange as the reason in 86.5 percent of cases; fewer than half cited productivity. Over half of employees suspected a lack of trust, and three in five a desire for control. The same report observed that offices are crowded on Wednesdays and deserted on Mondays and Fridays, which suggests that the compromise has been reached by everyone except the people who drew up the desk plan.

The appeal is not hard to explain. According to heise, citing the Federal Statistical Office, about a third of full-time employees spend an hour or more a day commuting, so a day at home effectively returns time worth roughly a tenth of a working day. Expats seem to have noticed. The InterNations Future of Working Abroad report, as covered by IamExpat in 2021, found that 83 percent of expats in Germany had the option to work remotely, 65 percent enjoyed it, and 40 percent did it all the time, a pandemic-era figure that should be read as a ceiling rather than a promise.

## The Reckoning

On the evidence, Germany offers the more usable form of flexibility and Bulgaria the more sympathetic statute. Germans have a home-working culture that survives without legal protection, supported by a tax allowance and a one-in-five chance that the advertisement mentions it. Bulgarians have an employer-pays-for-equipment rule and a union lobbying for a right to disconnect, but a labour market in which, by Eurostat's count, almost nobody was using it as of the latest figures reported. Neither country gives you a right to a laptop at the kitchen table. They differ in whether the employer is likely to say yes.

Hofstede's framework, cited here once and for decoration, scores Germany at 35 for power distance and Bulgaria at 75 in a Bulgarian comparative study, with individualism at 67 against 49. That ordering fits the evidence: a German manager is more likely to negotiate a home-office day than a Bulgarian one is to offer it unprompted, though the German manager will want it in the contract and the Wednesday attendance figures leave the matter open.

## Conclusion

If your income arrives from outside the country, Bulgaria is the easier place to be remote: the pace is slow, the costs are low, and nobody is going to call you back to a Wednesday. If your income arrives from a Bulgarian or German employer, the position reverses. In Germany, get the home-office days written down, check the works council, and claim the six euros. In Bulgaria, accept that the statute is fine and the market is thin, and verify the job exists before the visa does.

One practical rule applies in both: ask for the arrangement in writing before you sign. In Bulgaria, the Labour Code requires the agreement to sit in the contract or an annex, and the employer must then supply the equipment and cover connectivity. In Germany, the same clause is what separates a right from a favour, because the court ruling cited above leaves the employer free to withdraw an arrangement that was never recorded.

The honest summary is that Germany lets you work from home on a handshake and Bulgaria promises to put it in writing, if someone could be found to ask.
