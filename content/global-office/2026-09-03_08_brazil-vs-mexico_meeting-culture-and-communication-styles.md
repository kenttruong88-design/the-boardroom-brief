# The Meeting Will Start Late in Both Countries. Only One Will Apologize.

**🇧🇷 Brazil · 🇲🇽 Mexico**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Somewhere in São Paulo a 10 a.m. meeting is beginning at 10:22, loudly, with three people talking at once and nobody minding. Somewhere in Mexico City a 10 a.m. meeting is beginning at 10:20, quietly, with everyone waiting for the most senior person in the room to speak first. Both companies will tell head office the meeting "started on time." Neither is lying, exactly — they are simply using two entirely different definitions of the word "start," a distinction that has confused more relocating professionals than the entire Latin American tax code combined.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive on time yourself, even though colleagues routinely won't | Schedule back-to-back meetings assuming the first one ends on schedule |
| Expect 10–15 minutes of small talk about family, weekends, or football before the agenda starts | Jump straight into business — it reads as cold |
| Maintain steady eye contact while speaking | Be alarmed by interruptions or crosstalk — it signals engagement, not disrespect |
| Invest in the relationship before pushing for a decision | Expect a single meeting to produce a firm commitment |
| Use WhatsApp for quick follow-ups — it is near-universal | Rely on email as your primary channel — it is often the slowest one |
| Expect raised voices and animated gestures | Mistake volume or animation for anger |

#### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up on time yourself, even if the room doesn't fill for 20–30 minutes | Publicly correct or challenge a senior colleague in a meeting |
| Use courtesy titles (Lic., Ing., señor/señora plus surname) until invited to do otherwise | Assume "let me think about it" means anything is actually being reconsidered |
| Route requests through the hierarchy and get the senior person's buy-in first | Expect a junior staffer to commit to anything on the spot |
| Treat the comida (a long midday meal, often 2–4 p.m.) as a legitimate business ritual | Book a tight 30-minute lunch meeting expecting it to stay on schedule |
| Listen for a "no" dressed up as "vamos a verlo" (we'll see) | Press for a blunt yes-or-no answer — it can read as aggressive |
| Dress more formally than seems necessary, even at a startup | Treat "business casual" as license for jeans on day one |

---

Brazilian meetings run on relationship time, not clock time. Newly arrived professionals routinely misread the 15-to-30-minute grace period as disorganization; Brazilian colleagues experience it as due diligence — time to ask about your family, your weekend, your football team, before anyone touches the agenda. According to etiquette research compiled by Cultural Atlas and several international business-culture consultancies, Brazilians generally regard personal rapport as more important than the proposal itself, and a negotiation that feels concluded to a visiting executive is, to a Brazilian counterpart, merely warming up.

The data backs the vibe. Hofstede Insights scores Brazil at 69 on power distance — hierarchy matters, but less rigidly than elsewhere in the region — and 38 on individualism, a moderately collectivist profile in which the WhatsApp group chat functions as unofficial company infrastructure and real decisions often get made informally, between meetings, rather than during them. Brazilians also, per multiple accounts, interrupt one another freely and speak with a volume and physicality that outsiders can mistake for conflict. It rarely is one. On the harder numbers, Brazilian workers logged an estimated 1,994 hours in 2023 — comfortably above the OECD average of 1,683, but a notably more restrained figure than their northern neighbor's.

Where Brazil earns its reputation for warmth, it also earns one for loose accountability. Meetings drift off-topic, decisions get quietly revisited, and the follow-up promised "amanhã" arrives, if it arrives, on a schedule best described as aspirational. Even so, Korn Ferry's Workforce 2025 research found more than three-quarters of Brazilian employees report receiving solid AI training at work — a sign the substance keeps pace even where the meeting culture stays loose.

Mexican meetings, by contrast, run on hierarchy time. Everyone may arrive within a tidy 20-minute window, but nobody substantive speaks until the most senior person in the room has set the tone. Commisceo Global and other business-culture guides describe Mexican decision-making as concentrated at the top, with lower-ranking representatives unable to commit to anything on the spot, however reasonable the ask. Hofstede Insights puts Mexico's power-distance score at 81 — among the highest in the hemisphere — against an individualism score of just 30, the numeric signature of a culture where authority is respected first and questioned, if at all, privately and later.

Communication compounds the hierarchy. Mexican colleagues rarely deliver an unadorned "no" — a flat refusal can read as needlessly confrontational — so relocating professionals learn to listen for "vamos a verlo" ("we'll see") and "ahorita" ("right now," which can mean anywhere from five minutes to next Tuesday) as the operative vocabulary of polite decline. Layered onto this is one of the OECD's most demanding labor calendars: Mexican employees logged roughly 2,207 hours in 2023, the longest average annual working hours of any OECD member country, a figure shaped partly by six-day norms in some sectors and a workplace culture where visible presence, or *presentismo*, still substitutes for output in a meaningful share of workplaces.

The result is a paradox familiar to anyone who has worked across the border: courteous, unhurried meetings that produce comparatively little in the room, followed by long days in which the actual decision gets made later, elsewhere, once the hierarchy has quietly signed off.

**The Reckoning.** Put the two side by side and the contrast sharpens. Brazil is informal but slow to close — a relatively flat, relationship-driven culture that nonetheless takes its time reaching yes. Mexico is formal but fast to defer — a steeply hierarchical culture where the yes, once it descends from above, moves quickly. A Brazilian meeting can feel like a gathering that occasionally does business; a Mexican meeting can feel like a court appearance that occasionally serves lunch.

The counterintuitive part is where the actual overtime lives. Brazil, the "relaxed" culture, works close to the OECD average. Mexico, the culture of elaborate courtesy and careful titles, works more hours than any other OECD member — 2,207 a year, according to the OECD, against Brazil's roughly 1,994. Warmth and efficiency are not the same axis, and neither are formality and productivity. Judge the culture by its clock, not by its handshake.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum (Brazil) — A longtime contributor described repeatedly arriving on time for client meetings only to be met with an empty conference room, and warned newcomers that "Brazilian punctuality" is not really a joke: build twenty to thirty minutes of slack into every calendar entry, social or professional.*

> *Quora — A foreign professional working in Mexico recalled being unsettled the first time a meeting grew loud and emotional over a minor disagreement, assuming it signaled a breakdown, until a colleague later explained the passion reflected investment and commitment, not conflict.*

> *Business Insider expat essay (Mexico) — An American who has split her time in Guanajuato for two decades described waiting half an hour for a nonprofit director who was simply "ahorita" — soonish — and advised newcomers not to take Mexican lateness personally, since it is measured against a different clock than the one they arrived with.*

> *Expat.com forum (Brazil) — A separate contributor, comparing notes on workplace norms, noted that a colleague's relaxed attitude toward deadlines turned out to be a function of that specific company rather than a national trait — some Brazilian workplaces run tightly, others on rubber time, and the variation tracks industry and leadership more than any stereotype.*

> *Global Squirrels expat blog (Mexico) — A relocating professional wrote that they had braced for the cliché of the Mexican siesta only to find it barely exists in corporate settings; what replaced it was a genuine two-hour comida block in the early afternoon, treated as a legitimate business ritual rather than a nap.*

### Conclusion

If your tolerance runs toward warm chaos — meetings that wander, decisions made over lunch rather than in the room, a WhatsApp thread doing the work an agenda should — Brazil will feel less like a foreign country and more like an extended improvisation that occasionally files paperwork. If you prefer clear lines of authority, formal titles, and predictable process, even at the cost of longer hours and a "no" delivered as "we'll see," Mexico will suit you better, provided you can also suit up: the dress code, like the hierarchy, is not optional.

The honest version for a friend over drinks: Brazil will waste your morning and Mexico will eat your evening, so pick your poison based on whether you'd rather lose the time in a conference room or at your desk after everyone senior has already gone home.
