# In Turkey the Young Plan Their Exit, in Poland They Negotiate Their Lunch Break

🇹🇷 Turkey · 🇵🇱 Poland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Michael Page asked about 70,000 employees in 37 countries whether they would consider a new job offer, and Turkey came first, with 97% saying yes. Poland's young workers, meanwhile, report in a ManpowerGroup survey that they feel confident in their skills and distrust their managers, a combination that in any other country would be called a normal Tuesday. Both countries have a generation gap at work. In one it is a matter of economics. In the other it is a matter of etiquette, which is cheaper to fix and therefore better tolerated.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show visible respect for seniority and status, whatever your age | Challenge a senior colleague's decision openly in a meeting |
| Allow several meetings for a decision and build the relationship over tea or a meal | Press for a quick answer on the first call |
| Keep communication formal and indirect, especially with older managers | Deliver blunt criticism in front of the team |
| Ask in writing whether overtime is paid, as many young staff report it is not | Assume a standard 9 to 6 will stay that way |
| Open with small talk about family, sports or the city | Raise politics or religion |

### Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use formal titles and surnames with older colleagues until invited otherwise | Switch to first names on day one |
| Ask clearly what is expected and when the day ends; younger staff expect firm limits | Treat unpaid overtime as a loyalty test |
| Be punctual; it is appreciated | Arrive casually late with a good story |
| Raise hybrid or remote options early, since younger staff prefer them | Assume the older manager agrees to remote work |
| Expect to be asked about growth and learning by younger colleagues | Answer a growth question with a reference to your own career in the 1990s |

## Turkey

Turkish workplaces are hierarchical by design and by habit. Expat.com's guide to Istanbul work culture notes that respect for seniority and status is important, that managers are expected to give clear direction, and that decisions can take several meetings. Hofstede Insights scores Turkey at 66 on power distance versus Poland's 68, which makes the two countries nearly identical on paper. The difference is that Turkish young people are being asked to respect a ladder that no longer reaches anything.

The numbers are not subtle. According to the OECD's Education at a Glance 2025, as reported by bianet, 31.3% of Turkish people aged 18 to 24 are neither in employment nor in education or training, against an OECD average of 14.1%. Eurostat data reported by Turkish Minute show 28.2% of Turkish people aged 15 to 29 at risk of poverty or social exclusion, against 24.2% across the EU. A survey from Istanbul Bilgi University found that 67% of young respondents want to move abroad for better job opportunities and 36% cannot see their future in Turkey. The new minimum wage of 28,075 lira net, a 27% increase according to Turkish Minute, sits below the hunger line for a family of four and arrives with inflation of about 31%.

Against this background, the generational divide is not about whether young employees want meaningful work, flexible hours and respect. It is about whether the older generation's formula of loyalty, patience and a single employer for life is still available. A Youthall survey of 1,130 people aged 18 to 35 found that nearly 89% want a four-day week and 88% prefer hybrid work, while about a third work overtime and 80% of respondents are not paid for it. Michael Page's Turkey head said that loyalty has become an exception, and noted that young professionals are less inclined than their parents to follow a saving and investing path in an inflationary economy. Few employers can claim to have been surprised.

## Poland

Poland is hierarchical too, but with a different kind of tension. The generational split is between older workers who read overtime as commitment and younger ones who read it as a billing error. Experts quoted by the Solidarnosc social dialogue project describe exactly this: behaviours that signal dedication to older staff, such as staying late, can look to younger staff like exploitation, and younger workers set clear boundaries and may not accept authority based on age alone, which older colleagues can read as disrespect. Hofstede Insights gives Poland an individualism score of 60 against Turkey's 37, which helps explain why Polish young people expect to negotiate rather than endure.

The data support it. Randstad's 2026 Employer Brand Research for Poland, surveying 4,116 people, found that Gen Z is less focused on salary at 53%, and more on personal growth, learning and fulfilment, and that 25% of Gen Z favour hybrid work while Gen X is the least likely to work remotely. Pay satisfaction is lowest among Gen X at 40%, and 58% cite low pay as the leading reason for leaving a job. According to Statistics Poland, as reported by Polskie Radio, average wages rose 6.7% year on year in the first quarter of 2026 to PLN 9,562.88 gross, while the minimum wage rose by only 3% to PLN 4,806. The young are being paid more and, unlike their Turkish peers, can reasonably afford to be fussy.

Older Poles have the harder deal. Warsaw Business Journal reports that Poland has one of Europe's lowest employment rates for people aged 55 to 64, and that age discrimination in hiring remains widespread. Careers in Poland describes enforcement of age-discrimination law as weak. The generation that once demanded long hours is now sometimes told that its team is young and dynamic. The same experts note that tensions of this kind usually come from a lack of awareness of generational differences, and that managing mixed-age teams takes more effort than managing uniform ones. Poland Insight, reporting on ManpowerGroup's Global Talent Barometer 2025, adds that Gen Z workers in Poland express the lowest trust in managers of any generation surveyed, which gives the older generation a fresh reason to wonder who is supposed to be respecting whom.

## The Reckoning

On paper, the two countries share the same attitude to authority. In practice, they differ on what happens when authority fails to deliver. In Turkey, the young respond by leaving, or planning to, because inflation and weak job quality make the loyalty bargain irrational. In Poland, the young respond by renegotiating, because the labour market gives them leverage: confidence in their skills, a belief they could find a new job within months, and an employer who would prefer a discussion to a vacancy.

For the migrant, this changes what the generation gap costs. In Turkey it is mostly an economic problem handled with etiquette: you will respect your manager, who may well be as exhausted by the system as you are. In Poland it is an etiquette problem handled with data: your younger colleagues will want boundaries in writing, and your older ones will want to be addressed properly. Neither country is easy, but one of them will let you ask about overtime without it being a character reference.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *PolishForums.com — paraphrased experience: in a thread on whether Poles are workaholics, one poster said older workers still are, while millennials and Gen Z are less interested in owning homes, cars or savings and just want to live. The thread quoted an Onet article describing young hires who quit within weeks, unable to accept the eight-hour day or direct contact with managers, and older managers who saw it as a clash of values.*

> *XYZ.pl — paraphrased experience: a 57-year-old Polish manager of 70 people lost his post in spring 2024 and was still unemployed 18 months later, after nearly 200 applications and four interviews. A 56-year-old logistics director said that interviewers kept calling their team young and dynamic.*

> *Turkish Minute — paraphrased experience: a 33-year-old woman in Adana, interviewed for a youth study, said she had worked for years and never had insurance provided. Other respondents described employers wanting two to three years of experience from people who could not get a job to earn it.*

> *Turkish Minute — paraphrased experience: a young Turkish respondent said that asking a father for money had become difficult, and that it was hard whether you were 18 or 25.*

> *Careers in Poland — paraphrased experience: a Turkish professional who moved from Istanbul to Krakow said work-life balance is more valued and regulated in Poland, that Turkey's volatile economy affects daily business while Poland feels calmer and more predictable, and that colleagues were friendly and supportive of newcomers.*

## Conclusion

If you are moving to Turkey, expect a hierarchy that rewards deference and a young workforce under financial pressure; be careful with overtime terms, check that social insurance is actually paid, and read generational frustration as economics rather than attitude. If you are moving to Poland, expect a workplace in which age, titles and expectations around hours are all live topics; use formal address with older colleagues and clear boundaries with younger ones.

Either way, the generation gap is real, and the best response is to ask early what the hours and the pay really are. Both generations will be grateful that someone finally did.
