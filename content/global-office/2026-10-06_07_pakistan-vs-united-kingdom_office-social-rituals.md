# In Pakistan, Office Bonding Means Someone Orders You Tea Whether You Asked or Not. In the UK, It Means Someone Asks If You're Coming to the Pub, and Means It As a Real Question.

**🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In a Pakistani office, you will be handed tea the moment you sit down for a meeting, whether you wanted it or not, because declining would make your host worry about what else to offer you. In a British office, 46 million mugs of tea get poured every working day too — but the real bonding ritual happens after 5 p.m., in a pub, over a round someone expects you to buy back. Both cultures have built an entire social contract around a hot beverage. Only one of them has quietly swapped it for beer by evening.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept tea when it's offered in a meeting, even if you only sip it | Refuse tea outright — it puts your host in an awkward position |
| Expect tea rituals to vary by hierarchy — senior staff often have personalized preferences | Assume one universal "office tea" applies to everyone equally |
| Use the tea round as informal networking time, not just a break | Treat the tea offer as purely functional rather than social |
| Expect genuine warmth and hospitality to extend into daily office rhythms | Rush through a tea-based meeting opener to "get to business" |
| Build rapport before diving into the agenda | Skip the small talk that typically precedes a meeting's real content |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the Friday pub trip occasionally, even briefly, to stay socially visible | Decline every single after-work invitation without ever explaining why |
| Order a non-alcoholic drink confidently if you don't drink — it's increasingly normal | Assume you must drink alcohol to be included |
| Take your turn on the office tea round when it comes up | Dodge the tea round repeatedly — colleagues notice and resent it |
| Ask your employer about prayer space or dietary accommodations directly | Assume there's no formal protection for religious workplace needs — there is |
| Use small talk about weekends as a genuine relationship-building tool | Treat tea-round or pub banter as meaningless time-wasting |

### Pakistan

Tea is less a beverage in Pakistani office life than a social protocol with its own hierarchy. An essay in The Aleph Review describes government office settings where "every single person orders tea as soon as I enter the room," with staff drinking it "syrupy sweet and strong" from enamelware while senior officials maintain individual, personalized preferences — one colleague taking only green tea, signaling status through specificity rather than volume. The social obligation runs deep enough that refusing is genuinely awkward: the essay recalls being taught as a child never to decline tea at someone's home, because "the hostess has to then worry about what to give you instead." That same reflexive hospitality carries directly into the workplace, where a meeting rarely begins with the agenda — it begins with the tea.

The ritual functions as a leveling and a status marker simultaneously. Everyone participates, but who gets asked first, whose preference is remembered, and how elaborately a guest is served all communicate rank and relationship without anyone having to say so directly.

### United Kingdom

British office bonding runs on two parallel beverage economies: tea during the day, alcohol after it. A Travelodge survey of 5,000 UK employees found workers spending an average of 24 minutes daily fetching or sharing tea, consuming roughly 46 million mugs nationwide every working day, with over a quarter of staff actively tracking whose turn it is for the tea round and 14 percent admitting to real hostility toward colleagues who dodge it. It's a low-stakes ritual until someone breaks the unwritten reciprocity rule, at which point it becomes a genuine office grievance.

The higher-stakes ritual is the after-work pub trip, and it carries real inclusion risk for anyone who doesn't drink. Vice contributor Hussein Kesvani's essay on navigating pub culture as a Muslim describes the particular awkwardness of ordering "a pint of water please" in a room built around alcohol, and the social skill gap of being handed a round to buy without knowing the vocabulary everyone else takes for granted. The Muslims in Britain workplace guide makes the structural version of this point explicit: "an astonishing amount of social interaction takes place in British society around alcohol," with informal networking and team bonding frequently centered on pubs in ways that can leave non-drinking colleagues genuinely excluded from relationship-building that happens nowhere else. The same guide notes that UK law has offered formal protection since 2003 — covering prayer space, dietary needs, and religious observance — but legal protection and genuine social inclusion are measured on different scales entirely.

### The Reckoning

Here's the real inversion: Pakistan's tea ritual is built to make inclusion automatic — you're served whether you ask or not, because leaving someone out would be the social failure. The UK's pub ritual is built around opt-in participation that quietly assumes everyone opts in the same way, which means it can exclude people — non-drinkers, the religiously observant, new parents, early risers — without anyone involved intending to exclude anyone at all. Hofstede Insights data frames the backdrop: the UK's individualism score of 89 makes social participation a matter of personal choice with few institutional backstops, while Pakistan's score of 14 makes group inclusion something closer to a collective responsibility, baked into hospitality norms rather than left to individual initiative.

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Pakistan, accept the tea — all of it, every time it's offered — because the ritual is doing real relationship work that skipping it would visibly disrupt. If you're moving to the UK, understand that the real test of office inclusion happens after hours, at the pub, and that opting out entirely, for whatever reason, carries a social cost nobody will openly acknowledge but everyone will quietly notice. Pakistan will hand you connection without asking if you want it. The UK will leave the invitation on the table and let you decide how much that decision costs you.
