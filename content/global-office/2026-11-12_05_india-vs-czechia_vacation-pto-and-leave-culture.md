# India Grants You Leave and Then Asks If You Will Be Online; Czechia Grants It and Forgets Your Number

🇮🇳 India · 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

An Indian employer and a Czech employer will, on paper, offer something that looks like the same thing: a few weeks of paid time off, a handful of public holidays and a form. The difference lies in what happens after the form is submitted. In one country the approval arrives with a request to remain reachable. In the other it arrives with a calendar entry and, from July onward, an autoreply that Czech illustrators have apparently turned into a minor art form. For anyone weighing a move, the statute is the least interesting part of the comparison.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask HR for the state-specific leave policy in writing, since casual and sick leave vary by state | Assume the Factories Act accrual (one day of earned leave per 20 days worked) is your whole entitlement |
| Submit festival leave requests months ahead, especially for Diwali | Request leave a week before a festival and expect warmth |
| Agree on client coverage with your team before applying | Apply for leave during a client deadline without a handover |
| Check carryover caps, since only earned leave usually survives the year | Count on casual or sick leave rolling over |
| Treat "please be online" as a cultural default and decide your own boundary early | Reply to every out-of-hours message in your first month and set the precedent |

### In Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan around the 13 public holidays and bridge days | Spend leave on days the calendar already gives you |
| Expect written notice of employer-scheduled leave at least 14 days ahead | Assume your boss cannot recall you; they can, with compensation |
| Use your leave in the year it accrues | Hoard it, since carryover must be used by the end of the following year |
| Send a medical certificate if you fall ill mid-holiday so sick leave can replace vacation days | Suffer through a fever on a beach and call it a break |
| Schedule summer meetings with the quiet period in mind | Expect Friday afternoons in July to be productive |

## India

India's legal floor is more fragmented than its reputation suggests. According to factoHR, the Factories Act grants one day of earned leave for every 20 days worked, with eligibility at 240 days in the preceding year, a threshold that the new labour codes lower to 180. Four national holidays are fixed (26 January, 1 May, 15 August and 2 October), while festival days depend on the state; Maharashtra alone grants eight. Casual and sick leave also vary: Karnataka gives 12 days of sick leave, Delhi and Tamil Nadu combine 12 days of casual and sick leave, and most of it lapses at year end. Only earned leave is typically carried forward, subject to caps of roughly 24 to 45 days depending on the state.

The difficulty is not the entitlement but the exercise of it. A British Airways survey of 2,006 Indian employees, conducted around the turn of 2017-18 and reported by People Matters, found that 65 percent ended the previous year with unused leave and that nearly 30 percent felt unable to ask for it at all. Sixty percent could not mentally disconnect on days off. The survey is dated, but the anecdotal record has not obviously improved. Business Today reported in July 2025 on a LinkedIn post by a talent acquisition coordinator whose colleague's three-week European holiday earned a cheerful send-off, while his own two-day break prompted questions about whether he would be reachable.

The ILO data, as reported by Business Standard, help explain why. India's average working week is about 46.7 hours, and 51 percent of workers exceed 49 hours, second only to Bhutan in the ILO ranking cited. In an economy where billable utilization is measured and client time zones are not negotiable, a day off is a line item. A leave policy is therefore best read as a ceiling on what is permitted, and your manager as the person who decides how much of it is socially affordable.

Festivals are where this becomes visible. The Free Press Journal reported in October 2025 on an anonymous post by a TCS employee alleging that a manager had announced that no further Diwali leave would be approved. The claim is unverified, and the replies were instructive: some blamed poor planning on both sides, others pointed out that a whole team cannot vanish when a Western client is waiting. Both camps agreed on one thing, which is that the date of Diwali was known well in advance. If you are relocating, book the festival you care about on your first working day, and do it in writing.

## Czechia

Czechia is almost aggressively legible. According to L&E Global, employees are entitled to four weeks (20 working days) of paid leave per calendar year, counted in hours and paid at average earnings. The employer schedules leave, taking operational needs into account, and must give written notice at least 14 days ahead unless both sides agree otherwise. Leave should be used in the year it accrues; if it is carried over, it must be used by the end of the following year, and the Labour Inspectorate may fine employers who let it pile up. Unused days are paid out on termination. Expats.cz adds that employers may recall you from leave, with compensation, and that a medical certificate converts illness during a holiday into sick leave.

The calendar helps. Expats.cz counts 13 public holidays in 2026, of which 11 fall on weekdays; Czechia does not move holidays that land on weekends, which is a small cruelty. Bridge days are an organized sport: one day of leave on 7 May yields four days off, and one on 28 December yields five. The same outlet describes "cucumber season" from July to early September, when long lunches, empty Fridays and unreachable colleagues are standard and an afternoon meeting is a request, not a right.

The caveat is that habits are shifting. A survey cited by Expats.cz found that only about a third of Czechs fully disconnect on holiday and nearly 60 percent occasionally check work messages, though most use nearly all of their days. The law is on your side; the inbox is a separate matter.

There are practical corollaries for a newcomer. Expats.cz notes that large stores over 200 square metres must close on 1 January, Easter Monday, 8 May, 28 September, 28 October and Christmas Day, so a public holiday is an actual holiday and not a trading opportunity. Two-week summer holidays are the most common pattern in the survey data the outlet cites, which means a colleague's absence in August is a fixture and not a signal of disengagement. Plan your own leave around it, and expect your replacement to be equally absent.

## The Reckoning

On the legal numbers alone, the two countries are closer than the mythology suggests: roughly four weeks of paid leave in Czechia against an Indian patchwork that, per the British Airways survey, averages about 17 paid holiday days. The gap is in enforcement and expectation. Czech law schedules your leave, requires you to take it and fines the employer who lets it accumulate. Indian law grants it and then leaves the negotiation to you, your manager and the client. Hofstede-based comparison data scores India at 77 for power distance against 57 for Czechia, and at 24 for individualism against 70, which is a tidy way of saying that in India the request goes up a hierarchy and into a team, while in Czechia it goes into a form.

The practical consequence is that an expatriate in India must learn to read approvals the way one reads a diplomatic cable. "Approved, please be online" is a yes with conditions. In Czechia, the yes is automatic once the notice period is met, and the cultural risk runs the other way: you may find that your colleagues are on leave and you are the only person answering Slack.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/IndianWorkplace — a viral thread contrasted a warm Japanese manager and a terse Indian one who approved the same leave with "approved, be online on Teams and mail." Commenters debated whether low-trust workplaces make managers treat leave as a favour, while others noted that leave suspicion exists everywhere.*

> *Reddit (anonymous TCS employee, as reported by the Free Press Journal) — alleged that a first-line manager announced no more Diwali leave would be approved. Replies split between those blaming poor planning and those noting that Western client demands drive the schedule; the claim remains unverified.*

> *Hacker News — a commenter said vacation is easier to secure in the US than in India because Indian IT managers use billable-utilization targets to refuse requests. (The comment is several years old and speaks to IT services specifically.)*

> *LinkedIn (via Business Today) — a coordinator argued that a two-day break in India invites questions about being on call, while a three-week European holiday drew a friendly send-off, and called the problem mindset, not policy.*

> *Expats.cz — the summer-office guide describes Czech "cucumber season," when afternoon Fridays go quiet, and notes that employers are expected to keep workplaces safe during heatwaves. It is a magazine piece, not a forum post.*

## Conclusion

If your priority is leave you will actually use, Czechia is the safer bet: the entitlement, the notice rules and the carryover deadline all push you out of the office. India offers a more variable arrangement. Large multinationals and a growing number of companies may be generous; client-facing IT services and festival season are the places where entitlement and permission part company. Ask, before signing, how many days the team took last year and which of them were refused.

Either way, read the contract and then read the room. Czech law will not make you take a holiday you do not want, and Indian law will not make your manager like it when you do. Choose the country where your own particular failure of rest is easier to forgive.
