**Right to Disconnect, Right to a Desk: Remote Work in Oslo and São Paulo**

🇳🇴 Norway · 🇧🇷 Brazil

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Norway gave workers a statutory right to request flexible working hours back in 2006, nearly two decades before "flexibility" became a line item in every job posting on earth. Brazil, per OECD pre-pandemic data, sat among the countries with the lowest telework adoption anywhere in the sample — grouped with Hungary and Italy at the bottom of the list — even though its labor code has formally recognized "teletrabalho" as a contract category for years. One country built remote flexibility into its social contract early and quietly. The other legalized it on paper and is still building the cultural and physical infrastructure — reliable broadband, a spare room, a coworking budget — to make it real for most workers.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use your legal right to request flexible hours — it's normal, not a special favor | Treat a manager's approval of flexible hours as exceptional generosity owed back in extra effort |
| Respect the near-total disconnect after hours, including for your manager | Message colleagues after work hours expecting a same-day reply |
| Expect trust-based autonomy — you're judged on output, not visible hours online | Over-communicate your availability as if proving you're working |
| Plan around the long summer holiday period (July) when much of the country logs off together | Schedule critical remote meetings in July assuming full attendance |
| Treat egalitarian norms seriously — a flat structure means everyone's input carries weight remotely too | Assume seniority alone should settle a remote-team disagreement |

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a formal "teletrabalho" clause written into your contract, as required by Brazilian labor law | Assume a verbal agreement to work remotely is legally sufficient |
| Budget for a coworking membership if home space or connectivity is limited | Assume every remote worker has a quiet, reliable home office — many don't and use coworking hubs instead |
| Stay visible and communicative — Brazilian work culture still values relationship-driven check-ins | Go dark for long stretches; it can read as disengagement rather than deep focus |
| Expect some employers to remain skeptical of full remote work, preferring hybrid arrangements | Assume remote-work adoption is uniform — it varies sharply by industry and company size |
| Build informal rapport over video calls — small talk still matters even at a distance | Jump straight into the agenda on every call; it can feel abrupt |

---

Norway's remote-work culture rests on a foundation laid well before "remote work" was a category anyone discussed separately from "work": a 2006 law granting employees the right to request flexible hours, layered onto a broader Nordic workplace culture that OECD and Eurofound researchers consistently describe as high-trust and egalitarian. Norwegian managers tend to evaluate output rather than hours logged, and the cultural expectation of disconnecting after work — reinforced informally rather than by a single rule — means remote arrangements slot naturally into an already-flexible system. The trade-off is less about resistance to remote work than about a slower, steadier institutional culture: Norway wasn't rushing to catch up to a remote-work trend, because in important respects its labor norms were already there.

Brazil tells almost the opposite story. OECD teleworking research places Brazil among the lowest-adoption economies in its comparative sample prior to the pandemic-driven surge, and while the shift since then has been real — one widely cited industry survey found roughly 20 percent of Brazilian companies had adopted home-office arrangements, with the large majority reporting gains in satisfaction and retention — the infrastructure supporting it remains uneven. Brazilian labor law does formally recognize teletrabalho as a distinct contract type, which gives remote workers real legal footing, but the practical experience varies enormously by class, city, and industry: strong home broadband and private workspace are far from universal, which is why coworking spaces have grown into a genuine cultural fixture rather than a lifestyle choice, filling the gap between "legally allowed to work from home" and "actually able to."

### The Reckoning

The counterintuitive divergence is this: Norway's remote-work culture is quiet and structural — nobody markets it, because trust and flexibility were already assumed defaults before remote work had a name. Brazil's remote-work culture is louder and more actively negotiated — codified in contracts, discussed in company surveys, built around visible workarounds like coworking hubs — because it had to be constructed rapidly on top of infrastructure and management habits that weren't originally designed for it. A Brazilian professional moving to a Norwegian firm may be startled by how little remote-work policy gets explicitly discussed; it just happens, quietly, because the underlying trust already exists. A Norwegian professional moving to a Brazilian firm may be startled by how much explicit negotiation, contract language, and infrastructure-planning remote work still requires — flexibility there is a project, not a given.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A software developer working remotely for a São Paulo-based company wrote that the single biggest quality-of-life change wasn't the flexibility itself but finally being able to afford reliable air conditioning and internet by splitting a coworking membership with two friends, something her original home setup couldn't support.*

> *r/expats — A Norwegian consultant posted to a Brazil-based team described being confused when colleagues kept apologizing for slow email replies on weekends — in his previous Oslo job, nobody checked email after Friday at 4pm, manager included, and no apology was ever expected.*

> *InterNations São Paulo — A German expat noted that her Brazilian colleagues were far more willing to hop on a quick video call to resolve something than to write a long async message, the opposite of what she'd expected walking in with a "remote work means async-first" assumption from her previous job.*

> *Substack (expat relocation newsletter) — A Canadian who split a year between Oslo and Rio described Norway's July shutdown as the real test of remote-work trust — entire teams logged off simultaneously, and nothing broke, because the culture assumed in advance that nothing urgent would actually happen.*

> *r/digitalnomad — A remote worker who tried both cities described Brazil's coworking scene as unexpectedly social — cafes and hubs in São Paulo doubled as informal networking events — while Norway's home-office culture felt comparatively solitary, with far less spontaneous professional mingling outside of work hours.*

### Conclusion

If flexibility on paper matters most to you, Brazil already has it — teletrabalho is a real legal category, and the ecosystem of coworking spaces has quietly solved the infrastructure gap for those who don't have a spare room. If flexibility as unspoken cultural default matters more, Norway is the rarer prize — a place where nobody has to ask permission to log off, because the entire system assumes you will. The honest tip for a friend weighing either move: ask not whether remote work is "allowed," but whether it's actually *normal* — in Norway, the answer was already yes before you arrived; in Brazil, you may still be part of building it.
