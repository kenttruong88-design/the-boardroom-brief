# Lunch as Load-Bearing Structure: What Panama and Czechia Actually Put on the Table

🇵🇦 Panama · 🇨🇿 Czech Republic

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Panama, the workday pauses for an hour so that rice, beans, and something fried can be properly appreciated, ideally with a view of the ocean. In Czechia, the workday pauses for an hour so that a plate of meat, dumplings, and gravy can be properly absorbed, ideally followed by a nap no one will admit to needing. Both countries have built entire social contracts around the midday meal; only one of them washes it down with 130 litres of beer per person per year, a volume that, per the OECD, still makes Czechia the most enthusiastic beer-drinking nation in Europe even after a decade of decline. Neither country skips lunch. Both would consider that a personality disorder.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇦 Panama

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Eat your main meal at lunch — many offices still close for it | Expect restaurants full at 9pm; dinner is often light and early |
| Try a mercado (market) for produce and seafood before the supermarket | Assume "seco" (dry) means bland — it just means no broth |
| Say yes to coffee when offered — refusing can read as cold | Rush a meal with hosts; unhurried eating signals respect |
| Carry small bills for street food and market stalls | Expect much English on menus outside tourist zones |
| Ask before assuming a dish is spicy — it's milder than neighbors' cuisine | Confuse Panamanian food with Mexican; tacos aren't a local staple |

#### 🇨🇿 Czech Republic

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Order the "denní menu" (daily lunch menu) — it's what locals eat | Expect a free bread basket or side salad; those are billed separately |
| Use knife and fork for everything, including pizza | Eat with your hands in a sit-down restaurant — it reads as uncouth |
| Say "dobrou chuť" before eating, even to strangers at a shared table | Skip the soup course if it's on a set menu; it's not optional in spirit |
| Tip by rounding up or adding roughly 10%, stated when paying | Assume dinner is the big meal; lunch usually is |
| Try a "malé pivo" (small beer) without embarrassment | Assume tap water is free and automatic — ask, and expect to pay |

## Panama: Rice, Repetition, and the Sacred Lunch Hour

According to FAO's food-based dietary guidelines for Panama, the national diet organizes around five groups — starches and cereals, fruits and vegetables, meats, dairy, and fats — but in practice it condenses to rice, beans, and protein, repeated with quiet conviction across three meals a day. The Global Nutrition Report's Panama profile notes a diet increasingly shaped by processed and fast food in urban areas, a trend a study published via the NIH found starkly among the indigenous Kuna population, where roughly 85 percent reported eating fast food at least weekly and 47 percent reported eating fried food daily — numbers that read less like a cultural footnote and more like a public-health memo nobody opened.

What official statistics undersell is the institution of the lunch break itself. In much of Panama, outside the capital's international firms, the midday meal remains a genuine pause rather than a sandwich eaten over a keyboard — a holdover that Deloitte's Latin America workforce surveys have flagged as a productivity variable multinational employers underestimate. Dinner, by contrast, tends to be lighter and earlier than the Central European norm, a scheduling quirk that catches newly arrived expatriates off guard when they show up to a restaurant at 9pm expecting a full house and find a bored waiter and an empty room.

## Czech Republic: The Hot Lunch, the Cold Dinner, and the Beer That Counts as a Food Group

The Czech Statistical Office's Household Budget Survey puts food consumption at roughly 797 kilograms per capita in 2024, with meat, dumplings, and increasingly vegetables and cheese filling out the plate — modest but real dietary diversification in a cuisine still built on meat, gravy, and starch. What the spreadsheets can't capture is the rigid architecture of the Czech meal day: a hearty, hot lunch treated as the day's main event, followed by a cold, light dinner, a structure so consistent that CZSO's own food-consumption bulletins read almost like liturgy. Beer, meanwhile, is not merely a beverage; the OECD's 2025 alcohol country note for Czechia confirms it remains the highest per-capita beer consumer in Europe even as intake falls from its mid-2000s peak, and a beer at lunch has historically been treated as compatible with an afternoon of office work, though that norm is fading with the generational shift the OECD data tracks.

Hofstede Insights scores Czechia relatively high on power distance (57) compared with more egalitarian Nordic neighbors, and that hierarchy shows up at the table too: set menus, formal courses, and an expectation that soup precedes the main dish are not suggestions so much as sequencing. More practically, restaurants routinely charge for bread, water, and side salads that other European countries bundle in, a habit Prague-based expat outlets have flagged repeatedly as the single most common bill-shock moment for newcomers.

## The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the contrast is architecture versus improvisation. Panama treats the midday meal as a social institution that survives even as fast food chips at the edges of the traditional plate; Czechia treats it as a caloric investment strategy, front-loading the day's real eating so dinner can be an afterthought. Both countries would find the other's evening habits faintly alarming — Panamanians eating light and early would strike a Czech as skipping the point of dinner, while a Czech's beer at a business lunch would read to many Panamanian professionals as a faux pas rather than a birthright.

The counterintuitive finding sits in what people actually eat versus what the brochures advertise. Panama's seafood-and-tropical-fruit reputation obscures a diet the FAO and Global Nutrition Report both flag as increasingly processed among younger, urban populations — the ceviche stand is real, but so is the KFC next to it. Czechia's beer-and-dumplings reputation obscures a Household Budget Survey showing rising vegetable and cheese consumption — the stereotype is real, but it is quietly diversifying while nobody was looking.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A longtime visitor to Panama described the daily diet as chicken, rice, beans, and coffee on repeat, with breakfast often meaning fried corn tortillas piled with eggs and whatever meat is on hand — filling, but not quite what the tourism posters show.*

> *Expats.cz — A reader recounted how Czech coworkers order the heavy lunch special, dumplings and all, wash it down with a beer, then return to their desks, adding dryly that they now need twenty minutes lying down afterward just to function.*

> *Quora — One respondent described the mild culture shock of watching Czechs eat pizza and even fries with a knife and fork in restaurants, realizing eating with your hands there reads as poor manners rather than casual normalcy.*

> *Expat blog (Panama grocery guides) — A newcomer to Panama City described switching from big-box supermarkets to weekly mercado runs, finding seafood bought straight from coastal vendors fresher and cheaper than anything at the chains, and wishing someone had told them sooner.*

> *Expats.cz — A reader new to Prague admitted their biggest early frustration was assuming bread, water, or a side salad came free with a restaurant meal, only to be charged for each one separately — what they called "the quiet tax nobody warns you about."*

### Conclusion

Neither country's eating culture is optimized for the visiting professional's convenience, and that is rather the point. Panama's lunch-centric, unhurried rhythm rewards patience and eating on local time rather than calendar time; Czechia's hot-lunch, cold-dinner structure rewards showing up hungry at noon and pacing the day accordingly. The practical synthesis is unglamorous: in Panama, budget real time for the midday meal and don't expect a lively dinner scene; in Czechia, order the daily menu, keep cash for the extras, and accept that the fork is doing more work than you're used to. Whether either approach produces a healthier office is a question the FAO, CZSO, and OECD data sets gesture at without quite answering — which is, frankly, the most honest thing a spreadsheet has ever said about lunch.
