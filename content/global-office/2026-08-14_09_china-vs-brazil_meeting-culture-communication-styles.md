# "Perhaps" Means No in Shanghai. "9am" Means 10:15 in São Paulo.

**🇨🇳 China · 🇧🇷 Brazil**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In China, a business partner will rarely say "no" to your face — they'll say "perhaps," change the subject, or simply hesitate, because a direct refusal risks making you lose face in front of the room. In Brazil, they may simply not be in the room yet: Americans reportedly show up five to fifteen minutes early for a 9am meeting, while their Brazilian counterparts have been known to arrive an hour to ninety minutes later, with the appointment sometimes treated as more of a suggestion than a commitment. Two entirely different communication philosophies, both organized, in their own way, around preserving the relationship over the schedule.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Listen for hesitation, vague terms ("perhaps," "we'll see"), or a subject change as signals of "no" | Take a nod at face value — it can mean agreement or simply "I hear you," not necessarily consent |
| Deliver criticism privately, never in front of a group | Publicly criticize or call out a Chinese colleague's mistake in a meeting — it risks a serious loss of face |
| Expect meetings to open with small talk and formal introductions before business begins | Rush straight to the agenda — skipping relationship-building signals impatience, not efficiency |
| Be patient through slower decision-making — face and relationship impact are being weighed alongside the proposal itself | Push for an immediate yes/no answer — it can force an uncomfortable, face-losing position |
| Watch status and seniority cues closely — who speaks, and in what order, carries real meaning | Assume silence means agreement — it frequently doesn't |

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up on time yourself, even while expecting some flexibility from local counterparts | Assume a 9am meeting starts at 9am — arriving within the hour is common and rarely apologized for |
| Expect an animated, expressive meeting style — raised voices and gestures signal engagement, not conflict | Mistake enthusiasm or interruption for rudeness — it's a communication style, not a breach of etiquette |
| Value face-to-face conversation over written communication wherever possible | Rely on email or written follow-up as your primary channel — it reads as impersonal |
| Invest time in relationship-building (a cafézinho, informal chat) before diving into business | Expect a strictly linear meeting agenda — ideas are often shared as they occur, not in sequence |
| Appreciate "jeitinho" (creative workaround thinking) when a process hits a bureaucratic wall | Assume a workaround is a shortcut around integrity — it's typically framed as resourcefulness, not rule-breaking |

### China: Communication Engineered Around Not Losing Face

Chinese business communication runs on a high-context model where, per [Commisceo Global](https://commisceo-global.com/country-guides/china-guide/) and multiple cross-cultural guides, much of the actual message lives in tone, hesitation, and context rather than explicit words. Mianzi — face — is the organizing principle: [Institute for Public Relations](https://instituteforpr.org/communicating-chinese-co-workers-importance-guanxi-relationships-mianzi-face/) reporting notes that a direct refusal is treated as a face-threatening act, since it publicly rejects the person making the proposal. The consequence for foreign managers is concrete and cited repeatedly in business literature: a widely referenced Forbes anecdote describes a US manager who criticized a Chinese subordinate in front of a meeting, prompting the employee to go silent and leave the room — the takeaway line being blunt: "when your Chinese employees lose face, you lose them."

This isn't evasiveness for its own sake — it's a system for protecting relationships during disagreement. Decisions genuinely take longer in Chinese meetings because status, face, and long-term relationship impact are all being weighed alongside the substance of the proposal itself, and Hofstede's China data shows a notably high long-term orientation score consistent with this pattern of favoring durable relationships over quick resolution.

### Brazil: Warm, Expressive, and Loose With the Clock

Brazilian meeting culture runs almost opposite on tempo and directness. [ClickUp's](https://clickup.com/p/business-etiquette/brazil) reporting on Brazilian business etiquette describes meetings as animated and non-linear — "like a lively samba" — with participants sharing ideas as they occur rather than working strictly through a fixed agenda. Communication itself, per [Global Business Culture](https://www.globalbusinessculture.com/brazil/brazil-business-culture/), tends toward directness and expressiveness — raised voices, gestures, and visible engagement are read as enthusiasm, not aggression, a meaningful contrast to China's more restrained, face-conscious register.

Punctuality is the most consistently cited culture shock: multiple sources describe Brazilian meetings routinely starting well after the stated time, with local guidance often advising hosts to tell guests to arrive earlier than the intended start specifically because lateness is the expectation, not the exception. Underlying both traits is jeitinho brasileiro — a cultural instinct toward creative, relationship-based problem-solving around obstacles, including bureaucratic or scheduling ones, which reframes what looks like disorganization to an outsider as, locally, a flexible and socially fluent way of getting things done.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely counterintuitive overlap: both cultures prioritize the relationship over the literal content of the meeting, they just express that priority in opposite registers. China protects the relationship through restraint — careful, indirect language designed to avoid public conflict. Brazil protects it through warmth and flexibility — expressive, animated engagement that treats rigid scheduling as less important than the human connection happening in the room. A negotiator trained in Chinese indirectness might badly misread Brazilian lateness as disrespect; a negotiator trained in Brazilian expressiveness might badly misread Chinese hesitation as genuine openness to negotiate further.

Hofstede Insights data adds a sharp distinction worth knowing before either meeting: Brazil scores notably high on uncertainty avoidance, reflecting real discomfort with ambiguity and a preference for clear rules despite its loose relationship with time, while China scores markedly lower, comfortable holding ambiguity open for longer — meaning a Brazilian counterpart may actually want firmer commitments sooner than their relaxed scheduling style suggests, even as a Chinese counterpart may be entirely comfortable leaving things unresolved for now.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone describing their first Brazilian client meetings noted the stark contrast with US norms: Americans arrive five to fifteen minutes early for a 9am meeting, while Brazilian counterparts might not appear until closer to 10:30 — and after enough time living there, they admitted, they'd started running late themselves, to their own family's visible frustration back home.*

> *Internations — A member of a Shanghai-based professional community described learning, the hard way, that a Chinese colleague's repeated "perhaps" over several meetings was, in retrospect, a clear no they simply hadn't been fluent enough in the local communication style to hear the first time.*

> *r/expats — A negotiator working across both markets described the specific whiplash of the two styles back to back — leaving a Beijing meeting having said almost nothing overtly negative, then walking into a São Paulo meeting an hour late by local standards and being met with total warmth rather than any visible irritation.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — An employee at a multinational with offices in both countries warned new hires specifically against giving critical feedback to Chinese colleagues in group settings, describing one instance where a well-intentioned public correction visibly damaged trust with the team for weeks afterward.*

### Conclusion

If you're preparing for either, the practical rule is: in China, listen more than you speak, and treat any hesitation as information — you likely already have your answer. In Brazil, build in real schedule buffer and lead with warmth — punctuality anxiety will cost you more goodwill than it earns.

What I'd tell a friend heading into either room: in Shanghai, silence is data, not an invitation to fill it; in São Paulo, the meeting starting late isn't the meeting going badly — it's just starting on Brazilian time.
