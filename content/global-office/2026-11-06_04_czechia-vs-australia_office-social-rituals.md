**Prague Keeps Its Slippers Under the Desk. Sydney Keeps a Tab Running at the Pub.**

🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇦🇺 Australia

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

The Czech worker clocks roughly 1,824 hours a year, per OECD figures; the Australian worker clocks 1,633. Both describe their office culture, when asked, as relaxed. Neither is lying, exactly, but one of them means "I take a proper lunch and nobody asks what I did this weekend," and the other means "I will absolutely ask what you did this weekend, and I expect a full account by Friday drinks."

The hours gap has narrowed over time rather than widened — Czech figures sat at 1,705 as recently as 2020 before climbing back up, while Australia's crept from 1,683 over the same period, per OECD's annual series — but the social architecture built around those hours has stayed remarkably stable. Czech offices organize sociability around a handful of recognized occasions (a name day, a birthday, the arrival of summer); Australian offices treat sociability as a near-continuous background hum that a newcomer is expected to tune into almost immediately, not wait for a calendar prompt to join.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take your lunch break seriously — stepping out for 30–60 minutes around 11:30am is standard, not slacking | Eat at your desk out of guilt — though note roughly half of Czech workers now do this anyway as restaurant lunches get pricier |
| Keep a pair of indoor slippers under your desk | Be surprised when colleagues swap street shoes for slippers the moment they sit down |
| Follow your older colleagues' lead on formal versus informal address (vykání vs. tykání) | Force first-name familiarity just because you've worked together for years |
| Bring cake or chlebíčky for your name day or birthday | Skip the office birthday ritual — not participating reads as standoffish |
| Expect slower replies and more auto-replies during "cucumber season" (July–early September) | Schedule anything requiring a fast answer for a Friday afternoon or Monday morning in midsummer |
| Dress neatly and ironed — business casual is a safe default | Push for office air conditioning without reading the room first — temperature is a genuine, recurring dispute |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to at least one Friday drinks or morning tea in your first few weeks | Treat after-work drinks as mandatory or as a venue to prove you work hard |
| Join team lunches, birthdays, and farewell celebrations early | Skip every social event and expect trust to build on deliverables alone |
| Order a soft drink at after-work drinks if you don't drink, and leave when you're ready | Assume you'll be pressured to drink — you won't, but also don't assume nobody will be drinking at all |
| Keep small talk about weekends, sport, or weather brief and warm | Over-answer casual questions — brevity is valued even in friendly small talk |
| Downplay your own achievements rather than promoting them | Boast about success — "tall poppy" sensitivity is real, and others notice who doesn't practice it |
| Expect occasional swearing and blunt humour as completely normal office register | Mistake directness or crude humour for hostility |

Czech office culture runs on a kind of structural reserve that Eurofound's own 2008 survey of workplace relations — admittedly dated, but still the clearest institutional data point available — describes in granular terms: 53% of Czech workers characterize relations at work as merely "proper" (good cooperation, no personal closeness), against roughly 30% who report a genuinely "friendly" atmosphere. That reserve shows up anecdotally too: expat accounts collected by Expats.cz describe colleagues who are warm once trust is earned, but who do not treat a shared office as a shortcut to friendship. Hofstede's figures put Czechia's Long-Term Orientation at 70 and Uncertainty Avoidance at 74 — a profile consistent with a culture that plans carefully and keeps its guard up, including, apparently, about room temperature; one labor-code provision is reported to send staff home once the office hits 27°C, which tells you something about how seriously the dispute is taken on both sides of the thermostat.

Lunch itself has quietly become a contested resource rather than a given: with sit-down restaurant lunches now averaging around 197 koruna, roughly half of Czech office workers reportedly eat at their desks instead of stepping out, even as the cultural expectation that lunch is a real, separate event persists undisturbed alongside the behavior that increasingly contradicts it. Only about one in ten Czech workplaces is reported to have a dedicated lunch room at all, which leaves most of this tension to resolve itself quietly, sandwich by sandwich, rather than through any formal policy.

Australia's Gallup figures put daily workplace stress at 50%, above the global average of 40% despite comparatively modest weekly hours, a tension that shows up socially rather than administratively: Australians manage it by making the office genuinely sociable rather than merely tolerable. Hofstede scores the country at 90 on Individualism and 71 on Indulgence, numbers that read, in office terms, as "do your own job well, then go enjoy yourself with everyone else afterward." The much-cited "tall poppy syndrome" statistic about Australian workplace morale is worth a direct caveat here: the underlying study was actually Canadian (Dr. Rumeet Billan's research for Thomson Reuters and Women of Influence), rhetorically applied to Australia by the local press — a genuinely fitting bit of irony for a culture this wary of self-promotion, since even its signature insecurity turns out to be imported.

The drinking culture itself is also quietly less universal than its reputation suggests. Australia's own National Drug Strategy Household Survey found the share of Australians in their twenties who don't drink at all rose from 9% in 2001 to 22% by 2019, and multiple local commentators now describe workplace drinking culture as visibly declining rather than holding steady — which means the stereotype a newcomer packs in their suitcase (mandatory beers, every Friday, no exceptions) is already several years out of date by the time they land.

### The Reckoning

The counterintuitive finding is that Czechia, for all its reserve, is quietly become less formal in its own way, with drinking at lunch widely acceptable and roughly a third of surveyed workers reporting a genuinely friendly office atmosphere — while Australia, for all its backyard-barbecue reputation, runs on an unwritten social obligation that one expat guide bluntly calls "effectively mandatory": skipping the Friday drinks isn't against any rule, but it is noticed, filed away, and occasionally held against you come promotion season. Both cultures use food and drink as the delivery mechanism for trust. They have just chosen opposite defaults for how loudly that trust-building has to happen.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expats.cz Expat Voices — A reader using the pseudonym Thanos said that despite making plenty of contacts at his large Prague employer over three years, he still had only a handful of real friends and none he'd call close — a quiet, specific frustration about how slowly Czech workplace friendship actually compounds.*

> *Expats.cz Expat Voices — A reader using the pseudonym Fernanda described sharing a hospital room with two Czech women after surgery and building a genuine friendship from that single encounter — a reminder that off-hours chance meetings sometimes do more social work than years of shared office space.*

> *Expats.cz Expat Voices — An anonymous non-drinking reader reported that abstaining from alcohol meaningfully narrowed their social options in the Czech workplace, more so than it had in other countries — a piece of advice they said they wished someone had given them before arriving.*

> *ExpatsBlog.com, "Expat's Guide to Australian Working Culture" — The guide describes after-hours socializing as effectively mandatory in practice, singling out "Friday afternoon open bars" as something Americans and other newcomers consistently underestimate, mistaking a strong social norm for an optional extra.*

> *Expat.com Melbourne forum — Several newcomers, including a French au pair and a long-term Senegalese resident, reported that building a social circle beyond a single fixed base in Australia is genuinely harder than expected, even once workplace relationships are going well — a frustration less about the office itself than about the city around it.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two on social rituals alone: Czechia will let you keep your evenings, your opinions, and your privacy, provided you accept that genuine warmth takes years rather than weeks. Australia will hand you warmth almost immediately, provided you show up often enough to earn the right to be quietly self-deprecating about it later. Bring slippers to Prague. Bring stamina to Sydney. Don't mix up which city needs which — the Czechs will not understand the enthusiasm, and the Australians will absolutely notice the slippers.

A final note on timing, since it trips up more newcomers than anything in the do's-and-don'ts tables: arriving in Prague in July means arriving into "cucumber season," when half your new colleagues are effectively unreachable, and arriving in Sydney in late December means arriving into a near-total office shutdown for the summer holidays. Check the calendar before you check the dress code — it will tell you more about your actual first weeks than either country's etiquette guide will. Either posting rewards patience over performance: show up consistently, let the slower rituals do their work, and the warmth both cultures genuinely have on offer will arrive on its own schedule, not yours. In Prague that schedule is measured in years and shared cake; in Sydney it's measured in weeks and shared rounds — pack accordingly, and don't mistake either country's pace for indifference, because underneath it, both offices are quietly keeping score of who showed up.
