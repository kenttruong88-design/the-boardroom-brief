**Morocco Dresses for a Boardroom in Casablanca. Uganda Dresses for a Boardroom in London, Regardless of the Kampala Heat**

🇲🇦 Morocco · 🇺🇬 Uganda

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A modern Moroccan office, per Expat Focus's own coverage, generally expects a Western-style suit blended with a modest, conservative sensibility — covered shoulders, tailored lines, nothing ostentatious — a dress code shaped jointly by French administrative heritage and Islamic cultural norms around modesty (Hofstede scores Morocco at a relatively high power distance, around 70, consistent with the formality this implies). Uganda, like many countries outside Hofstede's original 40-country study, has no individually published Hofstede score, so its formality is better read through its own business-etiquette guides than through a numeric comparison. A Kampala office, by contrast, expects you to show up in the same heavyweight wool suit a London banker would wear, in a city that sits almost exactly on the equator — a detail so specifically absurd that a local style blog has published an entire guide to choosing wool suits for Kampala's climate, as though that combination of fabric and latitude were simply a fixed cost of professionalism rather than a genuinely strange inheritance worth questioning.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇦 Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to a conservative, well-tailored suit for any client-facing or formal office role | Wear anything sleeveless or notably short in a professional setting, regardless of the heat |
| Keep grooming polished — fit and presentation are read as a genuine effort signal | Assume a "relaxed" Mediterranean climate excuses visibly casual office dress |
| Carry a light jacket or blazer even in warmer cities like Marrakech for client meetings | Dress identically for a beach-town office as you would for Casablanca's financial district |
| Expect French-influenced formality (structured blazers, muted palettes) in business settings | Mistake traditional dress (djellaba, kaftan) for everyday office wear; it's occasion-specific |
| Dress modestly in mixed and more traditional settings, even outside explicitly religious contexts | Assume Western fashion norms transfer directly without modest adjustment |

#### 🇺🇬 Uganda

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in a genuine, well-made suit — Kampala's office culture expects it, climate aside | Assume equatorial heat justifies skipping a jacket in a client-facing role |
| Expect formal dress in government, legal, banking, and most corporate settings | Treat NGO or startup informality as the office-wide default across Ugandan employers |
| Keep shoes and overall grooming sharp — it's noticed and remarked on | Neglect footwear while otherwise dressed formally; it undercuts the whole look |
| Pack lighter-weight formal fabrics specifically suited to the heat rather than assuming you'll adapt on arrival | Bring only casual or outdoor-adventure clothing assuming "Africa" means informal dress everywhere |
| Note real variation between Kampala's formal corporate core and more relaxed upcountry or NGO-field postings | Assume one dress code applies uniformly across every Ugandan work setting |

### Morocco

Moroccan office dress runs on a genuinely layered formula: French administrative-era formality, Mediterranean business norms, and Islamic-influenced modesty all shape the same outfit simultaneously. Expat Focus's dedicated guide to Moroccan office culture describes expectations that will feel familiar to anyone who's worked in a French corporate environment — structured blazers, muted color palettes, polished shoes — with an added, non-negotiable modesty layer: covered shoulders and longer hemlines are expected norms in professional settings, not special-occasion exceptions, independent of how international or secular a given workplace otherwise feels.

Traditional Moroccan dress — the djellaba, the kaftan — remains culturally significant but occupies a different register than daily office wear; Commisceo Global's business guide and several Morocco-focused lifestyle sites are consistent that these garments mark specific occasions (religious holidays, family events, certain client-relationship contexts) rather than functioning as everyday corporate attire, a distinction foreign hires sometimes blur on arrival, assuming "traditional" and "professional" are more interchangeable than they actually are.

### Uganda

Ugandan corporate dress culture runs notably formal, and not loosely so: TalesMag's "Real Post Report" entry on Kampala specifically confirms that formal dress is expected across government, legal, banking, and much of the broader corporate sector, and local fashion-business blogs have built entire explainers — the "5-suit rule," the "4 dress codes" guide — around helping Kampala professionals navigate wool-suit selection specifically adapted for equatorial heat, a genre of content that itself tells you how seriously the formality expectation is taken locally despite the obvious climate mismatch.

The Muse's "Notes from the Field" piece on what to wear in Uganda, along with Peace Corps's own packing guidance for volunteers, both flag the same practical tension newcomers hit immediately: Kampala's core professional culture expects a genuinely formal, often suit-and-tie standard inherited substantially from British colonial administrative and legal tradition, while field, NGO, and upcountry postings can run considerably more relaxed — meaning the "right" Ugandan work wardrobe depends heavily on which Uganda, professionally speaking, you're actually walking into.

### The Reckoning

Both countries' office formality traces back to a colonial-era administrative inheritance rather than the local climate — Morocco's French-shaped structured formality, Uganda's British-shaped suit-and-tie standard — and both have simply kept that inherited formality running well past the point the weather would naturally suggest. The genuine point of divergence is the additional layer each culture has built on top of that shared colonial scaffolding: Morocco adds an Islamic-influenced modesty requirement that operates independently of formality itself (a very formal outfit can still be culturally inappropriate if immodest), while Uganda's additional layer is sectoral rather than religious — the sharp formal/informal split between its core corporate-government-legal sector and its large NGO and field-work economy.

The practical lesson for anyone packing for either posting: don't let the heat talk you out of the suit in either country. Both will read genuine professional seriousness through the same inherited formal-Western lens, climate be damned — Morocco will simply also be quietly assessing whether your version of that formality respects modesty norms your European tailoring never had to consider.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A contributor relocating to Uganda for work asked specifically about safety and daily life, and a response volunteered, unprompted, that showing up underdressed to a first meeting did more reputational damage than any other early mistake the respondent had personally witnessed among new foreign hires.*

> *TalesMag Real Post Report (Kampala) — The report itself notes that formal dress expectations hold firm across government and most private-sector offices regardless of season, directly contradicting a common assumption among newcomers that Uganda's warm climate implies correspondingly relaxed professional norms.*

> *The Muse, "Notes from the Field: What to Wear in Uganda" — The piece specifically warns that packing primarily safari-style or outdoor-adventure clothing, a common instinct among first-time visitors to East Africa, leaves newcomers visibly underdressed the moment they're in an actual Kampala office setting rather than in the field.*

> *Quora — A Moroccan business-etiquette respondent noted that foreign women in particular sometimes default to assuming a "modern" or internationally-oriented Moroccan company has relaxed the modesty expectations entirely, only to find themselves quietly, politely out of step with colleagues' own consistently covered-shoulder norm regardless of the company's international branding.*

### Conclusion

If you're heading to Morocco, pack structured, modest, well-tailored pieces and treat traditional dress as occasion-specific rather than an everyday office alternative. If you're heading to Uganda, bring the suit you think the equator should have talked you out of, in a lighter fabric if you can manage it, and reserve judgment about which sector's dress code applies until you know exactly which Ugandan workplace you've landed in. The shared moral for both: the climate map was never going to predict the dress code. The old colonial administrative org chart, it turns out, still is.
