# $900 a Month vs. 55.9% Tax: Living in Vietnam and Denmark

**🇻🇳 Vietnam · 🇩🇰 Denmark**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A single expat can live comfortably in Ho Chi Minh City on roughly $900 to $1,500 a month, all in — rent, food, transport, the occasional $2 bowl of pho. In Denmark, the average worker hands over 55.9% of their income in tax, the highest burden in the OECD, before they've paid for a single thing themselves. One country asks you to fund your own safety net line by line. The other asks you to prepay for a safety net you may not need for another twenty years. Both call this "affordable," and both are, technically, right.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget $900–$1,500/month for a comfortable single-expat lifestyle in major cities | Assume Hanoi is as cheap as the rest of the country — it now ranks as the priciest city |
| Compare public daycare (1–3 million VND/month) against private options before committing | Expect employer-sponsored health insurance to match Western coverage levels by default |
| Negotiate a housing allowance directly with employers — it's a common, expected benefit | Assume English will get you by outside major hubs — plan for real language gaps |
| Take advantage of genuinely fast, cheap fiber internet for remote-friendly setups | Skip private health insurance — public healthcare access and quality vary significantly |
| Ask locally about full-time nanny costs (7–12 million VND/month) if outsourcing childcare | Assume savings on rent offset the need to budget separately for international schooling |

#### 🇩🇰 Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Factor a 37–52%+ marginal tax rate into any salary comparison before accepting an offer | Expect your take-home pay to resemble the gross number on your contract |
| Rely on Denmark's heavily subsidized childcare — couples spend just 9% of income on it | Assume free healthcare and education mean zero out-of-pocket costs anywhere |
| Expect long parental leave (52 weeks combined) and use it — it's the cultural norm, not a luxury | Move to central Copenhagen expecting affordable rent — affordability has deteriorated there |
| Register promptly for Denmark's public systems — access depends on formal registration | Assume childcare quality is uniform — service quality varies by municipality |
| Budget 10–30% more for groceries and dining out than you're used to | Expect quick, easy job-driven mobility — residential mobility is notably low |

### Vietnam: Cheap, But You're Building the Net Yourself

Vietnam's appeal for internationally mobile workers is straightforwardly financial. A comfortable single-person budget runs $900 to $1,500 a month in major cities, and rental costs can undercut regional neighbors like the Philippines by 40% or more — a one-bedroom in Hoi An averaging around $300 is not an outlier. But the low headline cost hides a structural reality: much of what Danish taxes fund automatically, Vietnamese residents assemble themselves. Public daycare runs 1 to 3 million VND a month, but private daycare in urban areas — the option many expat and increasingly many local families choose — runs 5 to 15 million VND, and a full-time nanny adds another 7 to 12 million VND on top. Housing allowances exist but are negotiated benefit by benefit, employer by employer, rather than guaranteed by any national system.

Hanoi has recently overtaken other Vietnamese cities as the most expensive place to live domestically, according to Vietnam's 2025 Spatial Cost of Living Index, which complicates the blanket "Vietnam is cheap" pitch that circulates in digital nomad circles. The math still works for most arrivals — this remains dramatically more affordable than Copenhagen on a dollar-for-dollar basis — but affordability here means low prices for services you're expected to shop for and pay individually, not services bundled into your paycheck deduction before you ever see it.

### Denmark: Expensive, Because the Net Is Already Built

Denmark inverts the equation entirely. The OECD's highest average tax burden — 55.9% for the average worker — funds a system where healthcare, university education, and heavily subsidized childcare are simply available, largely without a second transaction. A Danish couple spends just 9% of family income on net childcare costs, versus 22% for an equivalent UK couple, and public spending on childcare has stayed above 1% of GDP for over three decades. Parental leave runs to 52 combined weeks, and unemployment benefits can replace up to 90% of a lost salary for up to two years — a welfare architecture built to absorb shocks that, in Vietnam, an individual would have to absorb personally.

The catch, and it's a real one according to OECD's own 2026 economic survey, is that Denmark's own high living standards are increasingly strained by housing affordability in major cities and low residential mobility — the system that handles healthcare and childcare so comprehensively hasn't solved the newer problem of Copenhagen rents outpacing what even well-paid residents can comfortably absorb. Groceries and restaurants run 10–50% above what many arrivals are used to, layered on top of the tax bill, meaning the "free" services coexist with a genuinely high cost of daily living for anything the state doesn't directly provide.

### The Reckoning

The core trade is legibility versus scale. Vietnam's costs are transparent and individually negotiable — you know exactly what your nanny costs because you're the one paying her directly, and you can adjust your lifestyle in real time by choosing cheaper or pricier options. Denmark's costs are bundled and largely non-negotiable — you don't choose your tax rate, and much of what you're paying for is invisible until you need it, at which point it's already there, free at the point of use.

The irony is that both systems produce genuine financial anxiety, just at different life stages. In Vietnam, the anxiety shows up early — will your employer's health coverage be enough if something goes wrong, will you find affordable international schooling as your family grows. In Denmark, the anxiety shows up in the paycheck itself, every single month, even though the eventual need — childcare, healthcare, an income shock — is comprehensively covered when it actually arrives.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — An American who relocated to Copenhagen for a tech role described initial shock at losing over half his salary to tax, followed months later by the realization that his family had stopped paying anything at all for his children's doctor visits, daycare copays, or eventual university tuition — the math evened out, just on a much longer timeline than his first paycheck suggested.*

> *r/vietnam (composited from expat community reports) — A remote worker in Da Nang noted that while rent and food were dramatically cheaper than home, the real surprise cost was private health insurance, which he'd underestimated because "everything else was so cheap it didn't occur to me healthcare wouldn't follow the same discount."*

> *InterNations Copenhagen — A relocating consultant said the hardest adjustment wasn't the tax rate itself but the low residential mobility — finding an affordable apartment in central Copenhagen took months, despite a stable, well-paying job, because supply simply hadn't kept pace with demand.*

> *Quora — Someone comparing Nordic living costs noted that a 70,000 DKK monthly gross salary sounds enormous until Danish taxes and Copenhagen rent are subtracted, at which point it becomes a solidly comfortable, but not lavish, single-income budget.*

> *Quora — An expat who'd lived in both Hanoi and a Nordic capital observed that Vietnam's low costs bought flexibility — she could downsize her lifestyle instantly if income dropped — while Denmark's high costs bought certainty, since the state-provided floor never moved regardless of her income.*

### Conclusion

The honest framing for anyone choosing between these two moves is not "which is cheaper" — it's "which kind of financial risk do you want to hold personally." Vietnam hands you control and low prices, but asks you to build your own safety net, transaction by transaction, and to accept that quality and coverage will vary by what you're willing to pay for. Denmark hands you a comprehensive, state-guaranteed floor, but takes more than half your income to fund it, whether or not you personally use every piece of it this year.

Priya's honest read: if you're young, healthy, and want your money to stretch visibly further right now, Vietnam wins. If you're planning a family, or you value certainty more than you value control, Denmark's tax bill is the best insurance premium you'll ever resent paying.
