# Fired at 58, Hired at 24: France and Australia Are Aging Their Workforces From Opposite Ends

**🇫🇷 France · 🇦🇺 Australia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In France, the French Ministry of Employment's own research arm found that the average age at which employers privately consider a worker "old" is 58.5 — a full seven years before the legal retirement age most of those same employers spent 2023 protesting to raise. In Australia, millennials and Gen Z now make up 67% of the entire workforce, per the Great Place to Work Insights Report 2024, which means the "generational clash" headlines are increasingly a clash between two groups of young people who disagree about what a decent job owes them. One country is quietly discarding its older workers years before pension eligibility catches up; the other has almost run out of older workers to discard. Neither has fully admitted it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a CDI (permanent contract) to still matter enormously to workers over 45, for whom it represents real security | Assume it carries the same weight for a 26-year-old who has already cycled through two CDDs and a freelance stint before 30 |
| Raise concerns about hiring or promoting workers past 50 as a real, documented issue — Dares research confirms the bias, it isn't paranoia | Treat age discrimination complaints from French colleagues in their late 50s as exaggerated — 58.5 is the researched average "cutoff," not a folk statistic |
| Expect younger staff to be more comfortable negotiating scope and hours upfront | Assume this reflects less commitment — French labor precarity has simply made explicit negotiation the rational default for anyone under 35 |
| Respect the formality that still governs meetings with senior staff | Extend that same formality automatically to a junior team of 20-somethings who increasingly expect flatter, faster communication |
| Discuss retirement-age politics carefully | Assume every generation in the room agrees on the 2023 pension reform — the fault lines run almost exactly along age |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to direct, informal communication across all levels | Assume informality means indifference — Australians, including Gen Z, still expect punctuality and follow-through |
| Offer a genuine 2–3 day hybrid split if you can | Push for five days on-site without a stated reason — it's now the single least popular arrangement across every generation surveyed |
| Talk to younger staff face-to-face where possible | Default to Slack-only communication with Gen Z hires — 42% of Australian Gen Z workers actually prefer face-to-face over digital channels, reversing the usual stereotype |
| Frame culture and values explicitly in retention conversations | Assume older and younger staff clash the most — Gen Z reports more friction with each other than with boomers, who they find comparatively easy to work with |
| Be explicit about job security and recognition when managing boomer staff | Assume boomers are indifferent to workplace culture — the real gap is that fewer of them cite it as a reason to stay, not that it doesn't matter to them |

### The Republic of the Quietly Discarded

France's generational story is really a story about who gets to keep working. Dares' research places the average "too old to hire" threshold at 58.5 — younger than the legal retirement age, which means a meaningful stretch of a French career now exists in a kind of employment no-man's-land: too experienced to be cheap, too "old" to be hired fresh, and not yet eligible to leave gracefully. Hofstede Insights scores France at a high 86 on uncertainty avoidance and 68 on power distance, describing a system genuinely built around formal hierarchy and job security — the CDI a French worker over 45 still treats as sacred is not nostalgia, it's the system functioning as designed for their cohort.

What's changed is that the system stopped extending the same deal to anyone under it. French labor precarity has grown steadily over the past two to three decades; workers who once expected to be hired into an enterprise and climb it are now more likely to cycle through fixed-term contracts (CDDs), freelance gaps, and negotiated terms before qualifying for the security their parents got automatically. The 2023 pension reform protests, which dominated French streets for months, were in large part a generational argument disguised as a policy one — a workforce nearing the "too old to hire" line fighting to delay the one date that still guaranteed it an exit with dignity.

### The Commonwealth of the Numerically Dominant

Australia's generational dynamic runs almost the opposite direction: there simply aren't as many older workers left to clash with. With millennials and Gen Z now 67% of the workforce, the loudest workplace tension in Australia isn't cross-generational — it's Gen Z with itself. Per Korn Ferry's research, 64% of Gen Z workers report clashing more with fellow Gen Z colleagues than with any older cohort, and they report the least friction of all with baby boomers, a finding that inverts the standard "boomers vs. zoomers" narrative most employers still plan around.

Hofstede's scores back up what expats consistently report on arrival: Australia's power distance sits at a low 36 against France's 68, and its uncertainty avoidance runs a comparatively relaxed 51 against France's 86 — a flatter, more improvisational culture where a junior hire genuinely can walk up to a director with a question. But the flatness doesn't mean the generations agree on what a job is for. Fewer than 30% of boomers say culture influenced their decision to stay with an employer, against 40% of Gen Z and millennials who name it directly — not because older Australians don't value their workplace, but because they were never asked to weigh it as a retention factor the way younger hires now openly do.

### The Reckoning

Both countries are dealing with the same underlying Deloitte finding — that Gen Z and millennials globally are chasing a "trifecta" of money, meaning, and well-being, with 55% of Gen Z and 52% of millennials delaying major life decisions over financial insecurity — but the local mechanics differ sharply. France's older workers are being pushed toward the exit years before they're eligible to take it; Australia's older workers are a shrinking minority in rooms full of twenty- and thirty-somethings who are more likely to argue with each other than with them. A French manager's generational headache is keeping a 55-year-old employable; an Australian manager's is keeping two different flavors of 27-year-old from irritating each other into resigning.

The irony is that both are, in Hofstede terms, moving toward the same practical compromise from opposite structural starting points — France loosening a rigid hierarchy just enough to retain younger talent it can't otherwise negotiate with, Australia formalizing a flat hierarchy just enough to give older staff the job security and recognition Gen Z takes for granted asking for.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone answering why younger French workers appear more selective about job offers explained that precarity, not pickiness, was the driver: with permanent contracts harder to land straight out of school, negotiating scope and pay upfront had simply become the rational move rather than a generational attitude problem.*

> *Expat.com Sydney — A relocating professional described being surprised that Australian managers gave direct, unprompted feedback on a first project within the first two weeks, something she said would have taken months to earn in her previous, more hierarchical workplace back home.*

> *LinkedIn Pulse — An HR consultant working across both markets wrote that the biggest misread by foreign managers in Australia was mistaking the country's informality for a lack of standards, when in practice colleagues who showed up late or missed a deadline lost credibility just as fast as anywhere else, just without the ceremony around saying so.*

> *r/AskEurope — A French contributor described watching their father's generation treat the 2023 retirement-age protests as an existential fight, while colleagues under 30 in the same office viewed the same reform with a kind of resigned detachment, since most didn't expect to reach a traditional retirement age on the current system's terms anyway.*

> *Reddit workplace thread — An Australian team lead recounted that the real generational flashpoint on her team wasn't boomer versus Gen Z, exactly as the research suggests, but two Gen Z hires disagreeing sharply over whether flexible hours meant "work whenever" or "work less," a distinction that took an uncomfortable team meeting to resolve.*

---

### Conclusion

The practical difference for anyone managing across these two workforces: in France, protect your older staff from a documented, researched bias against hiring and promoting past 50, and expect your under-35 hires to negotiate harder than their CDI-era predecessors ever needed to. In Australia, don't assume informality means low standards, and don't assume the generational conflict runs where the headlines say it does — it's more likely sitting inside a single age bracket than across one. If a friend asked me which country to bring a 55-year-old career into, I'd say neither confidently — but I'd tell them to bring a 25-year-old to Australia and a very good CDI lawyer to France.
