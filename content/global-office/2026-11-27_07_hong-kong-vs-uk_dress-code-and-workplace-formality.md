# Hong Kong Wears the Jacket, Britain Wears the Anxiety

🇭🇰 Hong Kong · 🇬🇧 UK

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In a city where summer routinely turns the pavement into a sauna, a bank teller in Hong Kong is still expected to look as though a suit is a reasonable idea. In Britain, a recent YouGov poll found that 93 percent of adults consider a missing tie acceptable in an office, and that 68 percent say the same of men in shorts. One country has abolished the dress code. The other has merely stopped writing it down, which is a different and more demanding arrangement.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress one notch smarter than you would in the UK for your first weeks, and watch what colleagues wear | Assume a relaxed policy document means a relaxed office |
| Keep a blazer or cardigan at your desk, because the air conditioning runs cold | Dress for the street temperature and then shiver through a four-hour meeting |
| Have lightweight wool suits made locally if your sector expects them | Wear heavy British tweed or thick cotton into the humidity |
| Wear a jacket for client meetings even if the team has loosened up | Turn up in sneakers or flip-flops, which Citigroup's Hong Kong guidance reportedly lists as unsuitable |
| Treat casual Friday as a defined category with limits | Read casual Friday as permission for shorts, slogan T-shirts or ripped jeans |
| Ask HR or your manager what applies to your floor | Rely on a single colleague's example, since the finance floor and the media floor differ |

### UK

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Aim for smart casual, which is the most common code | Wear a full suit daily unless your sector, such as law or finance, insists on it |
| Dress up for the first week, then calibrate to colleagues | Arrive in jeans on day one just because the offer letter said casual |
| Bring a neat pair of loafers, boots or flats | Assume trainers are universally accepted |
| Keep a jacket for client days or senior visits | Mistake a bare office for a bare hierarchy |
| Ask a recruiter what interviewers wear | Guess, since the answer varies hugely by employer |
| Respect uniform and safety rules in the many roles that have them | Treat dress as a luxury of desk workers alone |

## Hong Kong: The Suit Defends Itself

Hong Kong's business dress has long been conservative. HR Online reported in 2019 that InterNations describes local businesspeople as dressing fairly conservatively, usually in black suits, shirts and ties. A column in the South China Morning Post by Stephen Vines, undated but apparently from the middle of the last decade, noted that formal dress was standard across the city's finance industry, bank tellers included, and that even when Goldman Sachs relaxed its code for technology staff, its large Hong Kong team was still expected to wear formal business clothing. That source is old and should be read as history, though the 2019 Goldman memo ending mandatory suits suggests the direction of travel.

Official nudges have been gentler than they sound. According to the Hong Kong Government, civil servants were invited to Dress Casual Fridays from August 2022, partly as a health and exercise measure, with uniformed staff and those attending formal events excluded. The Standard noted a precedent from 2006, when the head of the civil service asked staff to dress casually in summer because offices were air conditioned to 25.5 degrees. Citigroup's Hong Kong offices, according to a Business Insider report relayed by inkl and left undated, circulated pictures of acceptable and unacceptable outfits, banning shorts, sneakers and slogan T-shirts. A relaxed code, in other words, arrives with a diagram.

There is a rationale beyond habit, and the climate makes it harder to defend than to explain. The official Hong Kong line has long been that formality and comfort can coexist, which is the sort of claim that is easiest to make from a corridor with the air conditioning turned up. ExpatWoman, a long-running expatriate site, says Hong Kong has no official dress code at all, that weekday streets show a mix of business and smart casual outfits, and that offices and malls are often kept very cold. The rule is therefore social rather than legal, which in practice makes it more durable. Dr Fang Lee Cooke of Monash University told the CIPD that Asian corporate cultures are often hierarchical and that smart dress signals respect for clients and colleagues, although her comments concerned the region rather than Hong Kong alone. Hofstede Insights rates Hong Kong at 68 for power distance and 50 for individualism, which is compatible with a workplace where visible rank matters. The same source scores uncertainty avoidance at 29, which is low, so the city tolerates improvisation in many areas. Clothing, evidently, is not one of them. I could not fetch comparable UK scores, so I will not guess them.

## UK: The Suit Left Quietly

British office dress has loosened on a scale that would unsettle a 1990s banker. YouGov reported in November 2023 that only 7 percent of workers wear business attire, 34 percent wear smart casual, which is the most common category, 26 percent wear casual clothes, and 19 percent wear an employer-provided uniform. Among those who never work from home, 7 percent still wear a suit, compared with 11 percent of part-time home workers, which is a small irony worth savouring.

The July 2026 YouGov poll reported by ITV News shows how far attitudes have moved: 68 percent of respondents consider shorts permissible for men, up from 37 percent in 2016, and 69 percent say the same for women. Sandals are acceptable to 69 percent, whereas only 28 percent tolerate flip-flops, so even Britain's revolution has a boundary.

None of this makes the UK informal in the Hong Kong sense of the word. It makes the rules implicit. The CIPD and the polling firms agree on the trend, though not on the destination. Goldman Sachs, a bank not known for sentiment, told its roughly 36,000 staff in March 2019 that suits were no longer mandatory, and Virgin Atlantic had loosened its own uniform rules a few weeks earlier, according to HR Online. When a bank and an airline agree that the old rule has gone, the rule has gone. The ordinary British sentence is that the dress code is smart casual, which no one defines and everyone enforces by raised eyebrow. New starters who read it as casual find out otherwise on a Tuesday.

## The Reckoning

Hong Kong asks for formality and tells you what it means. The UK asks for smart casual and leaves you to work it out. A newcomer from London will probably find Hong Kong easier on the nerves in the first week, because the answer is a blazer, and harder on the shoulders in August, because the answer is still a blazer. A newcomer to the UK will enjoy the comfort and lose some sleep on the question of what counts as smart.

The practical gap is smaller than the headlines imply, and it runs in an unexpected direction. Hong Kong's rule is stricter but legible, whereas Britain's is looser but opaque, so the person who most needs guidance is the one who has been told that nothing is required. A Hong Kong employer can hand over an illustrated guide. A British employer can only say that you will know it when you see it, and then watch you not see it. Both places still sort people by sector, client exposure and seniority, and both punish misjudgement quietly. The difference is whether the rule is a visible garment or a social inference. If you dislike ambiguity, Hong Kong suits you, literally. If you dislike ties, Britain has moved on, though it will not announce when.

## Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two, pack by sector rather than by country. A finance role in Hong Kong will want a jacket in the meeting room and a second one at your desk for the air conditioning. A technology or creative role in London will probably accept jeans, but only if they are the right jeans, which no one will specify.

In either city, spend the first week observing, ask HR directly, and buy a wardrobe afterwards rather than before. Hong Kong will tell you what the rule is. Britain will tell you that there is no rule, which is the more expensive answer. Employers on either side of the divide would do their new starters a kindness by writing the unwritten code down, preferably before the first Monday rather than after it.
