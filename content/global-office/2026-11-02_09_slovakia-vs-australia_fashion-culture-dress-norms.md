**Pressed Trousers, Pressed Opinions: Slovakia and Australia Cannot Agree on What "Getting Dressed" Means**

🇸🇰 Slovakia · 🇦🇺 Australia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Bratislava, a job candidate who arrives for an interview in trainers has already lost the interview, whatever the quality of his CV. In Melbourne, a job candidate who arrives in a full suit has not lost the interview, exactly, but has made everyone in the room quietly wonder whether he has wandered in from a different, more formal appointment down the hall. Both countries are small, prosperous, trade-dependent economies with blunt-spoken populations who would each insist, if asked, that they do not much care about clothes. Neither is being entirely honest, and the data on what actually counts as acceptable attire, from Eurostat spending tables to a regional survey about flip-flops, tells two very different stories about what "not caring" is permitted to look like. One country has written its expectations down and expects you to follow them; the other has left them unwritten on purpose, which turns out to be its own kind of demand.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇰 Slovakia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy one genuinely good dark suit or dress before you need it, since weddings, funerals, and client pitches tend to arrive with little warning | Assume "casual Friday" is universal; many Slovak employers expect smart attire five days a week regardless of the calendar |
| Keep shoes polished and in good repair; scuffed footwear reads as carelessness faster than an inexpensive suit does | Wear shorts, flip-flops, or visible gym kit to anything resembling an office, even in August heat |
| Overdress slightly for your first weeks in a new job; it is easier to relax later than to recover from a sloppy first impression | Show up to theatre, opera, or a client dinner in denim; several venues still treat it as borderline inappropriate |
| Default to muted, conservative colours in a corporate setting until you know the room | Wear deliberately attention-seeking colours to a first meeting if you want to be taken seriously |
| Ask a Slovak colleague what "smart casual" actually means at your specific employer | Mistake a small company's relaxed daily dress for licence to under-dress a client-facing meeting |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy at least one pair of genuinely smart sandals; "dressy sandals" is a real wardrobe category here, not a compromise | Assume thongs are welcome everywhere; schools, hospitals, kitchens, and most client-facing finance roles still say no |
| Research the specific employer rather than the national stereotype; casual-dress mentions vary enormously by industry | Wear a full suit to an interview unless the firm is banking, law, or has explicitly said formal |
| Dress for heat, sun, and the bike commute; practical fabrics beat polish in most workplaces | Assume casual means careless; a clean, considered T-shirt and a scruffy one read very differently |
| Keep one smart-casual outfit ready for client meetings and the occasional unannounced formal event | Take remote-era dress norms as universal; retail, healthcare, and trades roles still enforce uniforms and strict codes |
| Expect real regional variation before you generalise | Assume Canberra, Darwin, and Brisbane dress the same just because they're all "Australian" |

Slovak dress culture rests on a fairly old-fashioned premise: that appearance signals seriousness, and seriousness is the entire point of being at work. According to Business Culture's guide to Slovak business etiquette, professionals are expected to appear "clean and tidy" and dressed in a "professional and conservative manner," with dark suits and ties still the default for men at larger firms and tailored suiting common among female managers. Formality tracks company size more than industry: big organisations tend to codify a strict policy out of visible respect for partners, clients, and the public, while small and mid-sized firms often skip the written rule, default to business casual day to day, and then summon the suits back out the moment a meeting actually matters.

The country's enduring formality shows up in unexpected corners, too. Reporting in The Slovak Spectator on theatre dress codes found that Slovak National Theatre opera and ballet performances still call for a dark suit and tie on men and a cocktail dress or evening suit on women, with denim flagged as inappropriate even at smaller venues. The money tells a quieter version of the same story: Eurostat data puts clothing and footwear's share of Slovak household spending at a record low of 3.9 percent as of late 2024, down from roughly 7.4 percent in the late 1990s. Slovaks are not buying less because they care less; they are buying fewer, better things and expecting them to last through every formal occasion life throws at them.

None of this means Slovaks actually enjoy the arrangement. A survey of 508 people conducted by the agency 2muse for Postova banka, reported by the wire service SITA, found that more than half of respondents view strict dress rules negatively and almost two-thirds think the rules should loosen, with eight in ten saying comfort matters when choosing work clothes and over 90 percent wanting their outfit to match the day's actual activities rather than a blanket rule. Tellingly, employees at the largest firms, over 250 staff, were most likely to call formal dress unimportant to success, which suggests the suit is less a mark of ambition than a habit nobody has yet found a polite way to drop.

Australia's approach starts from the opposite premise, and the labour market is tracking the shift in real time. According to Indeed Hiring Lab Australia, mentions of relaxed dress standards in job postings reached 0.41 percent in the June 2025 quarter, roughly 3.2 times more common in highly remote-friendly occupations than in medium-remote ones, and concentrated unevenly: insurance postings mentioned casual dress in 11.6 percent of listings, versus 0.9 percent for IT infrastructure roles. The ACT led the country at a 0.9 percent mention rate; the Northern Territory recorded none at all. Indeed's researchers attribute the trend to pandemic-era habits, a workforce increasingly dominated by millennials and Gen Z, and a tech sector that normalised dressing down well beyond its own offices.

The looseness has limits, and a regional YouGov survey reported by Ragtrader found Australians drawing a surprisingly fine line: 56 percent considered sandals acceptable office wear, but only 35 percent extended the same approval to flip-flops, a gap that suggests "casual" is doing more quiet policing than it lets on. Even the national mood toward spending on clothes has cooled; broader household spending data has shown clothing and footwear outlays among the sharpest-falling categories in recent years, down roughly 7.5 percent year on year at one point, with the steepest drops in the Northern Territory and the ACT. Australians are not dressing down because they cannot afford to dress up. They are dressing down because dressing up, for most jobs, has stopped being the point.

The irony sitting underneath both systems is that the stricter one produces less daily anxiety, not more. Slovakia's rules are explicit, which means a newcomer who learns them once can simply follow them; Australia's rules are unwritten and shift by employer, suburb, and season, which means getting dressed correctly requires constant, low-grade social judgment that no handbook will ever fully cover. Hofstede Insights' cultural dimension data captures a version of this split in its individualism scores, with Australia ranking far higher than Slovakia, and it tracks neatly onto the clothes: Slovaks dress to signal they understand and respect the room's shared standard, while Australians dress to signal they are comfortable enough in themselves not to need one.

The second irony is sharper. Slovakia, the more rule-bound of the two, is also the cheaper one to dress for correctly, since the rules themselves limit how much wardrobe you actually need, and the 2muse survey's own respondents say so by voting for comfort while still largely keeping the rule in place. Australia, the more relaxed of the two, quietly demands a larger and more varied wardrobe than its reputation suggests, because "smart casual," "business casual," and "casual casual" are all live categories that a single outfit cannot cover, and guessing wrong reads less like a fashion mistake than like a failure to read the room. A new arrival who treats either country's informality as the whole story will dress wrong at least once in the first month; the only difference is which direction the error runs.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Tales from a Small Planet — a Foreign Service family posted in Bratislava described daily office wear there as business casual, but warned new arrivals to pack at least one properly formal outfit, since Slovak evening events, especially in winter, can call for it on short notice.*

> *SBS Voices — a Romanian-born writer who relocated to Melbourne recalled feeling like a costumed outsider in her tailored trousers and structured accessories, until she realised her new neighbours reserved their effort for almost anywhere other than the supermarket, and eventually swapped her heels for a pair of Ugg boots of her own.*

> *Blind — a tech worker comparing offices across regions noted that the most relaxed dress codes often came with the lowest pay, citing an insurance employer that let engineers drop the tie but offered roughly a third less salary than a comparable formal-dress role.*

> *Fashion Journal — an Australian reader working in media said thongs were a genuine office staple at her workplace, acceptable as long as they were the dressier, square-toed kind rather than a basic rubber pair, and still off-limits in schools, hospitals, and kitchens.*

### Conclusion

The honest comparison here is not about which country dresses "better," because that question has no stable answer across two such different rulebooks. It is about which kind of pressure you would rather carry around all year: a strict, explicit code that tells you exactly what to buy once and then leaves you alone, or a loose, shifting one that never tells you anything directly and expects you to read the room correctly every single day. Slovakia asks for a bigger upfront investment and a smaller ongoing one; Australia asks for the reverse, dressed up as freedom. Pack accordingly, and pack less confidently than the stereotypes suggest, because both countries' own residents are visibly ambivalent about the rules they have each settled into.

If you want someone else's rules, written down, move to Bratislava and buy the suit early. If you would rather argue about whether the rules even apply to you, move to Melbourne and buy the sandals. Either way, spend the real money on shoes first, because in both places, quietly, that is the part everyone is actually judging.
