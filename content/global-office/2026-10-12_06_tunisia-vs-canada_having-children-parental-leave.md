**Tunisia Just Tripled New Mothers' Leave by Law. Canada Will Give You Nearly a Year Off and Still Quietly Dock You for Taking It.**

**🇹🇳 Tunisia · 🇨🇦 Canada**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In August 2024, Tunisia did something most legislatures spend decades failing to do: it tripled statutory maternity leave overnight, from 30 days to three full months, and multiplied paternity leave sevenfold, from a single calendar day to a full week. Canada, meanwhile, offers new parents up to 69 combined weeks through Employment Insurance — more time off than almost anywhere in the English-speaking world — and still produces surveys in which nearly three-quarters of mothers say taking it visibly hurt their careers anyway. One country just made a historic leap forward on paper. The other has had generous paper for years and still can't fully convert it into a workplace that doesn't quietly punish you for using it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇳 Tunisia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Know the current law: 3 months' maternity leave (4 for multiples or complications), 7 days paternity (10 for multiples) | Assume the old rules still apply — this changed substantially as recently as August 2024 |
| If you're in the private sector, apply for your social security allowance within one month of the birth | Miss that filing window — the allowance isn't automatic |
| Expect public-sector pay to stay at 100% during leave | Expect the same if you're private-sector — the allowance caps at two-thirds of wages, capped again at 2x minimum wage |

| Treat the father's 7 days as a floor, not a ceiling, if your employer offers more | Expect most caregiving to shift to fathers just because paternity leave exists now — advocates say 7 days is nowhere near enough for that |

### Tunisia: From One of the Weakest Leave Laws in the Region to One of the Most Rewritten

Before August 2024, Tunisia's maternity leave sat well below international norms: 30 calendar days for a standard birth, 45 for complications, with fathers entitled to a single day — one of the thinnest parental-leave regimes in the region, falling well short of the ILO's own 2000 benchmark of 14 weeks at two-thirds pay. Law No. 44 of 2024 rewrote that almost entirely: three months of maternity leave for a standard birth, four months for multiples, disabilities, or complications, plus up to 15 pre-birth days on a doctor's request, and a full week of paternity leave, extendable to ten days — a change advocates note finally brings basic paternal recognition into a legal framework that had essentially ignored fathers at childbirth since the law's modern origins in the 1950s.

The reform is genuinely significant, and it is also genuinely uneven. Public-sector parents keep full salary throughout; private-sector mothers instead draw a social security allowance worth two-thirds of their average wage, capped at twice the minimum wage, which policy analysts at the Friedrich-Ebert-Stiftung's regional office have flagged as leaving many private-sector mothers "financially vulnerable" relative to their public-sector peers doing the same job. The same analysis argues seven days of paternity leave, while a sevenfold improvement on paper, is still "insufficient" to meaningfully shift caregiving away from mothers — and private-sector employer resistance to the added leave cost remains a real, unresolved friction point the law itself doesn't fully address.

### Canada: The Most Generous Leave on Paper, A Persistent Career Tax Underneath

Canada's Employment Insurance parental benefits are, numerically, extraordinary by global standards: up to 40 combined weeks at 55% of average earnings under the standard option, or up to 69 weeks at 33% under the extended option, split between parents with a portion effectively reserved for whichever parent doesn't take the bulk of it. Quebec runs its own, separate and in some ways even more generous system through QPIP, with dedicated paternity benefits up to five weeks at 70% pay. On paper, it's hard to find a wealthy country offering new parents more structured time.

The career math underneath tells a less generous story. A UK-based "Boardrooms and Babies" survey, widely cited in Canadian HR trade press, found nearly three-quarters of mothers said maternity leave had negatively affected their careers, seven in ten worried their job was more vulnerable afterward, and a third believed they'd been passed over for promotion specifically for being of childbearing age — findings Canadian outlets have run without much pushback because the pattern matches local human-rights caseloads. One British Columbia software company paid more than $11,000 in damages after dismissing an employee the day after she disclosed her pregnancy, with a tribunal finding the pregnancy was a contributing factor despite the employer's denial. Meanwhile, the federal government's flagship $10-a-day child care program, launched in 2021 with a 2026 target, is set to miss that deadline in several provinces — Ontario, Alberta, Nova Scotia, British Columbia, and New Brunswick among them — with one economist calling the policy's "average" wording a "get out of jail free card" that lets real fees stay well above $10 for plenty of families even after the headline target is technically met. Tunisia has no individually published Hofstede Insights score, having never been part of the original research sample, but Canada's own score of roughly 39 on power distance suggests a workplace culture that, on paper, should make it easier for a new parent to push back on quiet discrimination than the data above actually shows.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast is that Tunisia's problem is still mostly about the law catching up to decency, while Canada's problem is that the law got there years ago and the culture hasn't fully followed. Tunisia tripled its maternity leave and multiplied paternity leave sevenfold in a single legislative act — real, measurable, dramatic progress, even with private-sector pay gaps and a paternity allotment advocates still consider too short. Canada has had close to a year of combined, genuinely well-funded leave available for years, and still produces discrimination tribunal cases, promotion gaps, and a childcare affordability program that can't hit its own deadline. More weeks on the books clearly isn't the same thing as more career safety once you're back at your desk.

The practical lesson for anyone actually relocating for work is to stop reading the headline number entirely. A newcomer comparing "three months" against "up to 69 weeks" would reasonably assume Canada is the obviously better deal, and in raw time, it is. But the Tunisian reform comes with an explicit legal bar on dismissal or demotion for pregnancy, a protection that reads as more enforceable, if more recently written, than Canada's longer-standing but unevenly honored equivalent. Time off is only worth what your employer actually lets it be worth when you return.

### Conclusion

If you're planning a family around a relocation, the honest framing isn't "which country gives you more time" — it's "which country's formal protections actually survive contact with your specific employer." Tunisia just handed new parents a dramatically better legal floor than it had eighteen months ago, with real teeth against dismissal, even if private-sector pay still lags public-sector pay and fathers still get comparatively little. Canada has had a famously generous floor for years and still can't fully stop promotion bias, pregnancy-based firings, or a childcare-affordability program from missing its own deadline. Ask about the last person at your specific employer who took leave and came back. Their experience will tell you more than either country's statute book.
