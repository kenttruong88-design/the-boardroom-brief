# Turkey Earns Fourteen Days. Germany Guilts You Into Taking Thirty.

🇹🇷 Turkey · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A new hire in Istanbul spends their first year accruing exactly zero vacation days by law, then graduates to fourteen. A new hire in Berlin starts with twenty on the books before their coffee machine badge even works, and by the time August arrives, half the office has vanished so thoroughly that emailing anyone feels like shouting into a well. Both countries call this "annual leave." Only one of them means it as a compliment.

#### 🇹🇷 Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm your leave tier in writing (14 days for years 1–5, 20 for 5–15, 26 for 15+) before signing | Assume entitlement carries over cleanly if you switch employers mid-career |
| Request the legally provided extra unpaid travel days (up to 4) when your leave destination isn't your work city | Book Kurban Bayramı travel less than two months out — the whole country moves at once |
| Get leave approvals confirmed over email, not just verbally | Expect a multinational's global leave policy to survive contact with local middle management intact |
| Track religious holidays each year — Ramazan and Kurban Bayramı shift on the lunar calendar | Assume the public holiday calendar looks the same as last year's |
| Ask about the 270-hour annual overtime cap and 150% pay rate before agreeing to "flexible" hours | Sign a contract that quietly waives overtime pay for "management-track" roles |

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the sick-during-vacation rule: a doctor's note pauses your Urlaub clock and restores the days | Email colleagues during their Urlaub expecting a reply — many auto-delete incoming mail, not just defer it |
| Submit summer leave requests by Q1 — good August slots go early | Try to look dedicated by not using your days; HR will force the issue before March 31 forfeiture |
| Check your specific state's public holiday count separately | Assume Bavaria and Berlin get the same number of days off (they don't — it's a 13-versus-10 gap) |
| Register with a Krankenkasse early; sick leave and vacation interact legally | Treat "unlimited vacation" as a real category here — Germany runs on hard statutory minimums, not slogans |
| Expect your entire team's productivity to dip in August and plan around it | Schedule critical launches for the last two weeks of August |

### Turkey

Turkish labor law is not stingy on paper — it is simply patient. Under the İş Kanunu, a worker earns nothing in year one, then unlocks 14 days annually for years one through five, 20 days through year fifteen, and 26 days after that, with a flat 20-day floor for workers under 18 or over 50 ([Turkish Labor Law](https://turkishlaborlaw.com/news/legal-news/annual-vacation-principles-in-turkey/); [Justworks](https://www.justworks.com/global-hiring-guides/turkey/leave)). Add roughly 14.5 days of national and religious public holidays — the exact count shifts annually because Ramazan and Kurban Bayramı follow the lunar calendar — and the leave calendar looks generous enough on a spreadsheet ([CottGroup](https://www.cottgroup.com/en/blog/work-life/item/public-holidays-and-working-hours-calculation-in-turkiye)).

The trouble is what surrounds it. The OECD puts average annual hours worked in Turkey at roughly 1,855, well above the OECD average of 1,765, with 43 percent of Turkish employees clocking more than 50 hours in a typical week. Germany, by contrast, logs about 1,343 hours a year — the shortest stack in the bloc. That is a gap of roughly 500 hours annually, or about twelve standard workweeks, between two economies that both claim to observe an eight-hour day (OECD Hours Worked; Statista/HiveDesk analysis). The standard Turkish workweek runs 45 hours with a legal 11-hour daily ceiling, and while overtime is capped at 270 hours a year and paid at 150 percent, a Quora respondent working inside a multinational operating in Turkey noted that even foreign-owned firms often import local management habits — after-hours messages, informal expectations to stay late — that outpace what the contract technically requires.

Then there is Kurban Bayramı, the Feast of the Sacrifice, which functions less like a holiday and more like a nationwide traffic event. With a modest allotment of official days stretched by weekends, the holiday period can approach nine days, and the law obliges employers to grant up to four additional unpaid days specifically so employees can travel to see family elsewhere in the country — a tacit admission that "leave" in Turkey is substantially a family-logistics operation, not a solo beach fantasy (eskimo.travel; C&B Law Firm). Hofstede Insights scores Turkey noticeably higher than Germany on power distance and considerably more collectivist — which tracks with a leave culture organized around extended family and hierarchy rather than individual discretion.

### Germany

Germany's Bundesurlaubsgesetz sets a legal floor of 24 working days on a six-day week — effectively 20 days for the five-day week almost everyone actually works — but that floor is treated as a starting offer, not a ceiling. Most employment contracts land between 25 and 30 days, and the Federal Statistical Office reported German employees took an average of 32.2 vacation days in 2021, once public holidays are folded in ([ZMI](https://zmi.de/en/lexikon/minimum-vacation/); Destatis via multiple secondary sources). Public holidays themselves are a patchwork of federalism: nine are nationwide, but individual states add between one and five more, so Bavaria's Catholic calendar delivers up to 13–14 total days off while secular Berlin, Hamburg, and Bremen settle for around ten (Transparent Hiring; Geography Worlds).

What distinguishes Germany is not the number but the enforcement culture around it. Unused leave forfeits by March 31 of the following year absent a documented exceptional reason, which means HR departments actively chase employees to use their days rather than the reverse. American expats interviewing for German roles consistently report an adjustment period: an initial guilt reflex about booking three consecutive weeks off, followed by the discovery that entire departments empty out in August and nobody raises an eyebrow (iamexpat.de). One structural quirk regularly trips up newcomers — if you fall ill during a booked vacation and obtain a doctor's note, German law pauses the vacation-day countdown and restores those days, a protection that simply does not exist in most leave regimes. Hofstede scores Germany low on power distance and high on individualism, plus a notably high pragmatism score of 83 and a "restrained" indulgence score of 40 — consistent with a culture that treats rest as a scheduled, almost administrative right rather than a spontaneous indulgence.

### The Reckoning

Line the two systems up and the irony sharpens: Turkey's leave law is built around tenure and family obligation, yet its workers spend more hours at their desks than almost anyone in the OECD; Germany's leave law is comparatively modest on paper — just 20 statutory days — yet its workers take the most time off in practice and treat not using it as faintly embarrassing. The country with fewer guaranteed days produces the stronger culture of actually disconnecting, while the country with more guaranteed days produces a workforce that logs 500 extra hours a year anyway.

The other divergence is what leave is *for*. In Turkey, annual leave is substantially a mechanism for reuniting with extended family during bayram, complete with a legal travel-day allowance and predictable nationwide gridlock. In Germany, leave is closer to a personal maintenance ritual, scheduled early, defended by HR, and protected even from your own illness. Neither is more "correct" — but a newcomer who plans a Turkish leave request like a German one, or vice versa, will spend their first year quietly confused about why nobody else seems to be following the brochure.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — a respondent working inside a multinational's Turkish office described how, despite home-office policies promising European-style balance, local management culture still pushed staff to answer messages and stay late, leaving the legal entitlements mostly theoretical in practice.*

> *iamexpat.de — an American transplant to Germany described the odd guilt of booking three uninterrupted weeks off for the first time, only to discover the entire department was doing the same thing in August and treating it as entirely unremarkable.*

> *Quora — a poster asked whether falling sick mid-vacation in Germany meant losing those days, unaware that a doctor's note legally pauses the countdown and restores them, a protection that consistently surprises people who've worked under other systems.*

> *eskimo.travel / Living the Turkish Dream — travel writers described colleagues finding it genuinely strange when a foreign employee didn't leave the city for Kurban Bayramı, since the holiday is culturally assumed to be spent with extended family regardless of how the leave is legally structured.*

> *Hacker News (Ask HN threads on unlimited PTO) — repeated commenters warned that vague "unlimited" vacation policies, common in US tech, tend to produce less actual time off than a fixed accrued entitlement, urging people evaluating offers to look for a hard legal floor rather than a marketing term.*

### Conclusion

If the single number you should ask about before relocating is "how many days do I get," you are asking the wrong question in both directions. In Turkey, ask instead how enforceable those days are against your specific manager, and budget mentally for a workweek that runs meaningfully longer than the contract implies. In Germany, ask instead how fast you'll be expected to actually take the leave you're owed — because the pressure there runs opposite to almost everywhere else: not toward working through it, but away from being the one person still at their desk in August.

The honest version, for a friend deciding between the two: Turkey will teach you to fight for your day off, and Germany will teach you to feel vaguely irresponsible for skipping one.
