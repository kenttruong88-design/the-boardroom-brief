# New Zealand and Ireland Both Think They Invented the Chilled-Out Boss. One of Them Is Lying to a Twenty-Six-Year-Old.

🇳🇿 New Zealand · 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Both countries have built a national identity around being the easygoing alternative to somewhere grimmer — New Zealand to Australia's hustle, Ireland to Britain's stiffness — and both are now discovering that "easygoing" means something different to a 58-year-old manager than it does to the 24-year-old he keeps calling "mate" in a tone that suggests he read about it. According to Hofstede Insights' country comparison tool, both nations score low on power distance relative to global norms, which should, in theory, make generational friction a non-issue. In practice, a flat hierarchy just means everyone has equal standing to be passive-aggressive about it in the group chat.

## 🇳🇿 New Zealand: Do's & Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Let younger staff set up the Slack/Teams integrations no one over 40 understands | Assume "she'll be right" from a 23-year-old means the deadline is fine |
| Ask about someone's weekend before the standup, genuinely | Schedule a "quick sync" that is actually a 90-minute meeting |
| Trust a Gen Z hire's instinct on social media and employer-brand questions | Dismiss a younger colleague's work-life boundary as "not being a team player" |
| Let older staff mentor on institutional memory, not just technique | Assume tenure alone earns deference in a flat-structure office |
| Normalise asking for feedback in writing, not just over beers after work | Treat the Friday pub trip as mandatory bonding rather than optional |

## 🇮🇪 Ireland: Do's & Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect directness dressed in a joke — the joke is still the point | Mistake the craic for a lack of seriousness about deadlines |
| Let Gen Z staff push back on "always-on" culture out loud, not just by quiet-quitting | Assume a 25-year-old asking about a four-day week is being lazy |
| Give younger hires real ownership early — Dublin's labour market rewards poaching, not patience | Expect loyalty from staff who can get a 20% raise by walking across the street |
| Acknowledge the housing-cost elephant in every salary conversation | Pretend a Dublin graduate salary stretches the way it did for their manager's generation |
| Let "the pub after work" stay optional without making it a career tax | Confuse informality with an absence of hierarchy — it's there, just unspoken |

## New Zealand: She'll Be Right, Eventually, For Someone

New Zealand's workplaces run on an informality that reads, to outsiders, as a lack of structure, but is better understood as structure with the edges filed off. The friction isn't that younger and older Kiwis disagree about hierarchy — both broadly dislike it — it's that they disagree about what informality is supposed to buy them. Research from Downer Group's "New Zealand's Future in the Hands of Generation Z" whitepaper finds younger workers reading the famously laid-back Kiwi management style as permission to ask for flexibility, mental-health accommodation, and clearer career pathing, while older colleagues, per the same body of workplace commentary circulating through outlets like IT Brief and the Wellington City Council's own internal "Bridging the Gap" guidance, experience the same requests as evidence that nobody under thirty wants to simply get on with it.

What actually distinguishes the New Zealand case is how small and tight the labour market is. In a country of five million people, reputational effects travel fast, so the generational stand-off rarely escalates into the kind of public, structural conflict seen in larger economies — it just simmers as low-grade mutual bewilderment, managed, per the Retirement Commission's own ageism-in-the-workplace research, by putting boomers and Gen Z on the same project and hoping proximity does the rest. It mostly works. New Zealand's problem is less a generational culture war than a generational communication gap dressed in the same four words — "she'll be right" — that each cohort has quietly decided to mean something slightly different by.

There's also a demographic undertow most workplace commentary skips past: New Zealand's population is ageing faster than its immigration settings can offset in every sector, so older workers are staying on past the point previous generations retired, not entirely by choice. The Commission for Financial Capability's own ageing-workforce research found businesses simultaneously worried about losing institutional knowledge and reluctant to formally invest in retaining it, which leaves a lot of generational goodwill resting on individual managers rather than policy. A Gen Z hire walking into a well-run Kiwi office will find genuine mentorship; one walking into an indifferent one will find a boomer counting down to retirement and a vacancy nobody has budgeted to backfill properly.

## Ireland: The Craic Is Still Mandatory, the Hours Are Negotiable

Ireland's generational divide is sharper because it's attached to money in a way New Zealand's isn't. Dublin's cost-of-living crisis has made the traditional Irish career bargain — put in your years, earn your seniority, eventually get the corner office and the good car-park spot — look less like a plan and more like a bet a 24-year-old graduate can't afford to place. Deloitte's 2026 Gen Z and Millennial Survey found cost of living is the single largest source of anxiety for Ireland's youngest workers, and the Irish Examiner reported more than half of Gen Z and millennial respondents describing themselves as living paycheck to paycheck despite being, on paper, the most credentialed workforce the country has ever produced.

That economic squeeze has reshaped loyalty. The Irish Times has reported on Gen Z staff openly telling managers they want the nature of work itself to change — more flexibility, less performative presenteeism, clearer boundaries around after-hours contact — not as a generational request for leniency but as a negotiating position backed by a tight graduate labour market where a competitor firm is always hiring. Older Irish managers, many shaped by the post-2008 scarcity mentality that made any job feel precious, read this as entitlement; younger staff read the older cohort's instinct to simply absorb bad conditions as a failure of nerve. Both are, in their own way, correct about the economy they came up in, which is exactly why the disagreement doesn't resolve.

It's worth noting that Ireland's Gen Z, per the Irish Examiner's coverage of Deloitte's survey data, are also meaningfully less naive about this than the millennials who preceded them into the same squeeze — they arrived already fluent in the maths of Dublin rents against graduate salaries, which makes them harder to manage with the vague loyalty appeals that worked, imperfectly, on the generation before them. A manager telling a 24-year-old to "pay their dues" in a market where dues now cost more than the salary covers isn't dispensing wisdom so much as revealing how out of date the wisdom is.

## The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the real contrast isn't informality versus formality — both countries are informal — it's what each generation believes the informality is owed to. In New Zealand, the generational gap is cultural and interpersonal: a disagreement about tone, dressed up in a shared national vocabulary of understatement that both sides still use, just to mean different things. In Ireland, the generational gap is economic and structural: Gen Z isn't asking for a vibe shift, it's asking for a compensation model that reflects the actual cost of living in Dublin, and management's generational discomfort is really discomfort about the P&L.

If you're the kind of professional who reads "flexible, informal culture" in a job posting and takes it at face value, New Zealand will mostly deliver what it promises, with the caveat that "informal" doesn't mean "fast" — decisions still move through consensus, just a quieter one. Ireland will deliver the informal tone and then hand you a cost-of-living reality that no amount of good craic will offset, which is precisely why so many of its most ambitious young workers end up informal with their Dublin colleagues and formal, eventually, with an immigration lawyer somewhere else.

## The Bottom Line

If you're weighing a move for the culture alone, both countries will give you an office where nobody salutes the boss, which is a genuinely rare thing globally and worth something. But "nobody salutes the boss" is not the same as "nobody disagrees," and the disagreement you'll actually inherit depends on which problem you'd rather argue about: New Zealand's is a vocabulary problem between generations who are otherwise getting along fine; Ireland's is a housing market wearing a vocabulary problem as a disguise. Pick the argument you can actually win.
