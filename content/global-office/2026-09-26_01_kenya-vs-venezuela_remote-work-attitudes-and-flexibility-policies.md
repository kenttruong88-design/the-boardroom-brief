# Remote, But Not Equal: What "Work From Anywhere" Really Means in Kenya and Venezuela

**🇰🇪 Kenya · 🇻🇪 Venezuela**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Nairobi wants to be Africa's next digital-services capital, complete with a new digital nomad visa and coworking spaces marketed to the laptop class. Caracas, meanwhile, has become an accidental case study in remote work as economic triage: hundreds of thousands of Venezuelans now earn dollars from foreign employers precisely because the domestic economy gives them no other option. Both countries have populations logging into Zoom from home. Only one of them is doing it by choice.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇪 Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get a payment platform that actually serves Kenya (Payoneer, Wise, or a local bank with USD capability) before you need your first paycheck | Assume PayPal will work — it still doesn't support receiving funds in Kenya |
| Budget for backup power and a second internet connection (mobile data as failover) | Treat Safaricom fiber as bulletproof; outages happen and clients notice |
| Build a portfolio and apply directly to international job boards rather than waiting to be discovered | Expect competitive hourly rates to match US or European benchmarks right away |
| Clarify working hours in writing before accepting a role with a foreign manager | Assume "flexible hours" means the same thing to a Nairobi-based team as to a Lagos or London one |
| Join a coworking space in Kilimani or Westlands for the community and the reliable power backup | Work in total isolation if you're new — the digital nomad scene here is organized and worth tapping |

#### 🇻🇪 Venezuela

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Set up at least two payment rails (crypto wallet plus Payoneer or a Zelle-linked account) | Rely on a single payment method — banking restrictions and platform access shift often |
| Plan your workday around the power grid, not the client's calendar, and communicate that upfront | Promise uninterrupted availability during announced maintenance windows or blackout-prone hours |
| Negotiate and receive pay in USD wherever possible | Accept payment in bolívars for foreign-facing work — inflation will erase the value before you spend it |
| Treat a remote foreign salary as a serious financial advantage worth defending | Undersell your rate just because the local cost of living looks low on paper |
| Keep documentation of your equipment and income for banking and travel purposes | Assume normal consumer banking abroad will treat a Venezuelan address the same as any other |

Kenya's remote-work story is one of infrastructure and ambition arriving faster than most predicted. The International Labour Organization's 2024 review of digital labour platforms in the country found a rapidly expanding pool of Kenyans doing freelance and platform-mediated work for clients abroad, and Nairobi has since rolled out a formal digital nomad visa aimed at capturing some of that traffic in the other direction. Local coverage now discusses the city, somewhat hopefully, as a candidate for "Africa's digital services hub." On Hofstede Insights' framework, Kenya scores 64 on power distance — moderately hierarchical, but with enough flexibility that a remote worker answering to a foreign manager isn't navigating totally foreign social math.

The friction shows up less in ambition and more in plumbing. PayPal still doesn't support receiving money in Kenya, which means every remote worker's first real project is assembling a payment stack — typically Payoneer, Wise, or a bank willing to hold dollars — before the first invoice even goes out. Power and connectivity are generally reliable in Nairobi's tech corridors but not guaranteed, so serious remote workers budget for backup batteries and a second SIM card the way an office in London budgets for nothing of the sort. None of this stops the trajectory: coworking spaces in Kilimani and Westlands are filling up, and the "remote work 2.0" narrative circulating in Kenyan business press is, for once, aimed at something the labour market can plausibly deliver.

Venezuela's version of remote work did not emerge from ambition; it emerged from necessity. An estimated 7.9 million Venezuelans have emigrated over the past decade, according to figures cited by regional outlets tracking the country's brain drain, and remote work has become the option of choice for the skilled workers who stayed, or who left and kept working for the same clients from somewhere else. Earning in USD from a foreign employer is, for many, the only realistic hedge against a domestic currency that inflation can hollow out within weeks. On the same Hofstede power-distance measure, Venezuela scores 81 — a considerably steeper hierarchy than Kenya's, which shapes how comfortable a Venezuelan employee is likely to be pushing back on a foreign manager's demands, at least at first.

The practical obstacles are more severe than Kenya's. Venezuela experienced significant nationwide blackouts as recently as 2024, and unreliable grid power is a standing planning variable, not an occasional inconvenience — remote workers build their schedules around outage windows rather than the reverse. Banking access from abroad is restricted enough that guides for foreign employers hiring in Venezuela now routinely recommend a three-pronged payment approach: cryptocurrency for speed, Payoneer for a multicurrency account and card, and PayPal, which — unlike in Kenya — has seen increasing local adoption. Tax obligations still apply at ordinary progressive rates regardless of where the paycheck originates, a detail more than one new remote worker has learned the hard way.

**The Reckoning:** Put side by side, the two countries reveal a distinction the "remote work is remote work everywhere" pitch tends to flatten: Kenya is optimizing an already-functioning system, while Venezuela is patching around a broken one. A Nairobi remote worker's biggest headache is a payment platform gap; a Caracas remote worker's biggest headache is the electrical grid. Both will tell you, correctly, that they work from home for a foreign company — but one is opting into a lifestyle and the other is opting out of an economy.

The irony cuts the other way too. Kenya, chasing digital nomads with a shiny new visa, is trying to attract the kind of remote worker that Venezuela's own economy is actively expelling. Both governments have, in their own way, concluded that people working from laptops for money that doesn't originate locally is good for the country — they've just arrived at that conclusion from opposite directions, one through policy design and the other through fifteen years of economic attrition.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A young professional in Kenya asking how to land an international remote job described the process as far less about talent than about payment logistics: since PayPal doesn't support receiving funds locally, the first real hurdle wasn't the interview, it was figuring out how to get paid at all before applying anywhere.*

> *Expat.com (Nairobi forum) — One long-term expat cautioned that Kenya's famously reasonable 8-to-5 workweek is a local norm, not a guarantee: several contributors described foreign-facing or higher-paid roles quietly expecting evening availability and occasional weekend work, with one account describing a colleague dismissed over a missed Saturday shift despite no such expectation being written down anywhere.*

> *Nexton.dev (a Caracas-based developer studio's blog, standing in for the harder-to-find live Venezuela expat forum threads) — Their guidance to Venezuelan remote workers treats a diversified payment setup as non-negotiable: a crypto wallet for speed, a Payoneer account for a usable card, and increasingly PayPal, since no single rail can be trusted to keep working uninterrupted for long.*

> *LinkedIn (professional perspective piece on Kenya's remote work scene) — A Nairobi-based writer described the shift as visible in real estate before it showed up in any statistic: coworking spaces in Kilimani filling with people on video calls at hours that don't match any local office schedule, which she took as the clearest sign the city's remote workforce was serving clients on someone else's clock.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing a move built around remote work, the honest question isn't "does this country allow remote work" — both clearly do — it's "what is remote work compensating for here." In Kenya, it's compensating for a domestic salary ceiling that international clients blow past easily, and the country is building infrastructure to keep that advantage growing. In Venezuela, it's compensating for a currency and a grid that can't be relied on to hold value or stay on, which makes the same laptop lifestyle look identical on a video call and completely different in practice.

Take the Kenya path if you want a well-supported, increasingly formalized remote-work ecosystem with a real community behind it. Take the Venezuela lesson to heart regardless of where you land: build redundancy into your payment method before you need it, not after the first delayed paycheck teaches you why everyone else already had.
