# Panama Pays You to Have the Baby. America Pays You to Stop Talking About It.

🇵🇦 Panama · 🇺🇸 United States

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Panama's labor code guarantees a new mother fourteen weeks of paid maternity leave and, since Law 238, extends formal job protection to new fathers as well. The United States guarantees, at the federal level, nothing paid at all — just twelve weeks unpaid, and only for the minority of workers whose employer is large enough and whose tenure is long enough to qualify. One of these countries is a developing Central American economy. The other is the largest economy on earth and, per the OECD's own comparative data, stands alone among its peers in having no national paid parental leave policy whatsoever.

## Do's & Don'ts: Panama

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm you're enrolled correctly in the Caja de Seguro Social (CSS) well before the birth — it's the system that actually pays maternity leave | Don't assume a private-sector job automatically includes benefits beyond the statutory minimum; ask specifically |
| Expect — and use — the fuero de maternidad/paternidad, the legal protection against dismissal around pregnancy and new fatherhood | Don't expect universal, subsidized childcare once leave ends; quality daycare in Panama City runs a real monthly cost and availability varies sharply by neighborhood |
| Lean on extended family or domestic help, which remains culturally normal and widely used by both local and expat parents | Don't expect paternity leave to mean an extended paid absence in practice; its strength to date is largely in protection against firing, not weeks off |
| Budget for private healthcare or supplemental insurance if you want a wider choice of hospitals for delivery | Don't assume rural and capital-city experiences of "having a baby in Panama" are interchangeable — services concentrate heavily in Panama City |
| Ask Panamanian colleagues what actually happened when they had kids, not just what the law says should happen | Don't bank entirely on the law's letter without checking your specific employer's track record of honoring it |

## Do's & Don'ts: United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether your employer and state offer anything beyond the federal floor — several states now mandate paid leave where the federal government does not | Don't assume FMLA means paid leave; it is unpaid, job-protected leave, and only for employers above a certain size |
| Negotiate parental leave explicitly during hiring if the role and leverage allow it; many professional offers are negotiable here | Don't expect subsidized daycare; costs frequently rival or exceed a mortgage payment in major metro areas |
| Research employer-specific policies before accepting an offer — variation between companies is enormous | Don't assume your job is protected if your employer has fewer than 50 employees; FMLA simply doesn't apply |
| Build a financial buffer for the gap between birth and any paid leave your specific employer offers, if any | Don't expect to discuss pregnancy or parental plans at work without first understanding your employer's actual, not stated, culture around it |
| Look into dependent-care FSAs and employer childcare subsidies as a partial offset to cost | Don't assume returning to work on a fixed, early timeline is a personal failing rather than a structural one |

## Panama: Legally Protected, Practically Uneven

On paper, Panama's framework is generous by the standards of many of its neighbors. The Labor Code sets maternity leave at fourteen weeks, paid through the Caja de Seguro Social, and Law 238 — fully in effect after emergency-era delays, per Mondaq's legal coverage — extends a formal fuero, or protection against dismissal, to new fathers as well as mothers. That protection is a meaningful structural safeguard: it is illegal to fire someone for becoming a parent, and that illegality has teeth in Panamanian labor courts.

What the law doesn't fully solve is what happens after leave ends. Childcare infrastructure in Panama concentrates heavily around Panama City, and families relocating from abroad routinely describe, across threads on Expat.com and Reddit's r/ExpatFIRE community, a reliance on domestic help and extended family networks rather than formal daycare systems — a pattern that is culturally unremarkable in Panama but can be logistically disorienting for a foreign family without that network already in place. The country's social safety net handles the legal mechanics of parenthood well; it leaves the practical mechanics — who watches the child at 8am on a Tuesday — largely to private arrangement.

Hofstede-style cultural research on Latin American workplaces consistently codes Panama as comparatively collectivist, and that shows up directly here: having a baby is treated as something the extended family absorbs together, not something an individual household is expected to solve alone through the market. For an expat without that family structure nearby, replicating it means paying for it — reliably, but not cheaply.

There is also a quieter, employer-side dimension worth noting. Panamanian labor courts have a track record of enforcing the fuero aggressively once a dismissal case is actually filed, which gives the protection real teeth on paper — but legal recourse still requires a parent to notice the violation, retain counsel, and pursue it, hurdles that fall disproportionately on workers without the resources or paperwork certainty that a well-connected local employee might have. For a foreign hire on a work visa tied to a specific employer, that dependency adds a layer of practical vulnerability the statute itself doesn't fully address.

## United States: No Federal Floor, Wildly Uneven Ceiling

The American system's defining feature is its absence. The Family and Medical Leave Act guarantees twelve weeks of unpaid, job-protected leave — but only to employees who have worked 1,250 hours in the prior year for an employer with 50 or more staff within 75 miles, a threshold that excludes a large share of the workforce outright. Per OECD comparative data and reporting from outlets including CNBC and The Conversation, the United States remains the only OECD member with no national paid parental leave program, a distinction that has held for years despite periodic legislative attempts to change it.

What exists instead is a patchwork: a handful of states now mandate paid leave, some employers — disproportionately large, white-collar ones — offer generous policies well beyond the federal floor, and everyone else negotiates individually or goes without. Childcare costs compound the problem; multiple analyses, including Chicago Fed Insights' comparative review of non-US childcare policy, note that American families routinely spend a share of household income on childcare that would be considered unusual, even alarming, in most peer economies. On forums like Mumsnet, the recurring American-parent question — "how do you actually manage with no leave?" — gets answered the same way each time: savings, family, luck, or some combination of the three.

The career impact tracks the policy gap closely. Where leave and childcare support are unreliable, research cited across this coverage consistently links new parenthood — and motherhood specifically — to measurable career disruption: reduced hours, slowed promotion, or exit from the workforce entirely, not because of any individual failing but because the system assumes someone else, usually unpaid, will absorb the gap.

Equitable Growth's analysis of declining U.S. labor force participation goes further, arguing that the absence of paid leave is itself a measurable drag on the broader economy, not just an individual hardship — workers, disproportionately women, simply leave the workforce rather than navigate an unpaid gap with no guaranteed return path. That a policy framed domestically as fiscal restraint shows up in national labor statistics as fiscal drag is the kind of irony that rarely makes it into the recruiting pitch.

## The Reckoning

Panama legislates generosity and lets logistics erode it at the edges; the United States offers no legislated floor at all and lets individual employers decide how much humanity to supply voluntarily. A Panamanian new parent is protected by law but may still be improvising childcare by Thursday. An American new parent may have excellent employer-provided leave or none whatsoever, with the only meaningful predictor being which company logo is on the paycheck.

Neither system optimizes for the parent who lacks family nearby and lacks a generous employer — which, not coincidentally, describes most relocating professionals in both countries. The difference is that Panama's gap is a known, named, visible one you can plan around; America's gap is often invisible until the offer letter actually arrives and you discover what, specifically, your employer is prepared to give you that the government will not.

## Conclusion

If you're planning a family around a relocation, treat these as two different categories of risk rather than two points on the same scale. Panama's risk is logistical — the law is on your side, but the infrastructure around it requires your own planning and, likely, your own money. America's risk is structural and employer-dependent — there is no floor beneath you at all, so the actual offer letter matters more than any national policy ever will.

Read the fine print in Panama. In America, read the fine print, the employee handbook, and your own HR department's actual track record, because the law isn't going to help you, and it was never going to.
