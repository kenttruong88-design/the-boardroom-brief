# South Korea vs. Canada: In One Country, Age Still Outranks Talent. In the Other, Nobody Will Admit They're the Boss.

🇰🇷 South Korea · 🇨🇦 Canada

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Seoul, a twenty-six-year-old analyst will still wait for the most senior person at the table to pick up their spoon before eating. In Toronto, a twenty-six-year-old analyst will call the CEO "Dave" in a Slack channel and ask him, cheerfully, to move a meeting. Both offices insist they are modernizing. Only one of them means it as a threat to the existing order, and it isn't the one you'd guess from the org chart.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wait for the most senior person to lift chopsticks or a glass before you do | Start eating or drinking before your elders at a group meal |
| Learn your company's actual rank structure fast — job title usually outranks age now | Assume more years lived automatically means more authority at work |
| Show up to at least part of a hoesik (team dinner), even briefly | Skip every after-hours gathering outright — it reads as disengagement |
| Raise burnout or overwork through HR or a trusted senior, not the group chat | Publicly contradict a superior's decision in front of the team |
| Track how your employer applies the 52-hour workweek cap in practice | Assume a legal cap means the overtime culture has already vanished |
| Use two hands when giving or receiving documents, cards, or drinks | Treat formal gestures as optional once you've "settled in" |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use first names with your manager, and often the CEO, unless told otherwise | Default to formal titles — it can read as oddly distant |
| Speak up in meetings; silence is read as disengagement, not respect | Wait to be invited before sharing an opinion |
| Make small talk before business — weather, weekend, commute | Launch straight into the agenda with no warm-up |
| Accept that a younger manager may supervise longer-tenured staff | Assume tenure or age entitles you to deference |
| Network relentlessly; most hiring runs through referrals | Expect a strong resume alone to open doors as a newcomer |
| Negotiate flexible or hybrid arrangements openly | Assume asking for accommodation signals weakness |

South Korea's workplace generation gap is not a vibe, it is a measured phenomenon with its own vocabulary. A 2020 survey of 13,000 office workers by the Korean Chamber of Commerce and Industry found that 63.9 percent had personally experienced it, and the culture has since produced "kkondae," shorthand for an older colleague who confuses seniority with correctness. Hofstede Insights scores South Korea at 60 on power distance and a mere 18 on individualism — a workplace built to defer upward and act collectively, which is precisely what its youngest workers are quietly opting out of. South Koreans still logged 1,833 average annual working hours in 2025, down from 1,865 the year before but still comfortably above the OECD average of 1,736 (OECD; Korea JoongAng Daily). The MZ generation's response has been less rebellion than exodus: research published in Human Resource Development International finds younger cohorts prioritizing meaningful work and horizontal communication over the loyalty their parents traded for a pension. Tellingly, the anonymous workplace app Blind, built specifically so Korean employees could vent without a superior reading over their shoulder, is now used by more than eight in ten white-collar professionals in the country — a staggering adoption rate for what is, functionally, a national complaints box.

Canada's version of the generation gap looks almost polite by comparison, which is very on brand. Statistics Canada confirmed millennials have overtaken baby boomers as the country's largest cohort, now roughly 23 percent of the population, ending a 65-year boomer reign. Hofstede pegs Canada's power distance at just 39 against a world average of 55, with individualism at 80 — a culture engineered for flat org charts and first-name emails. But flat does not mean frictionless: a Randstad Canada report found 32 percent of Gen Z workers planned to quit or change jobs in 2025, versus 20 percent of millennials, 18 percent of Gen X, and a mere 5 percent of boomers, who appear to have collectively decided that loyalty is still a virtue worth demonstrating on someone else's behalf. CBC News has covered the resulting scramble as employers try to manage four, soon five, generations under one roof, discovering that the main friction isn't hierarchy but scheduling — who wants to be in the office, and how often.

**The Reckoning.** Put plainly, South Korea has the more visible hierarchy and Canada has the more effective one. Seoul's org chart is explicit, ritualized, and increasingly resented by the people required to observe it; Toronto's org chart is nominally invisible, first-name-friendly, and still quietly determines who gets promoted, mentored, and heard in a meeting. The Korean generational conflict is a fight over the rules themselves — whether hierarchy should exist at all. The Canadian one is a fight over pace — how fast youth should be allowed to move within a system nobody will admit is a hierarchy in the first place.

The counterintuitive part is which generation is winning. In South Korea, younger workers are winning the culture war in public — hoesik attendance is optional almost everywhere now, and companies openly market themselves as kkondae-free — even as long hours and structural deference persist underneath the marketing. In Canada, younger workers are losing the culture war quietly: the flat structure they were promised turns out to reward the network-savvy and the well-connected just as reliably as any hierarchy did, only without a title to blame for it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora respondent, on adapting to South Korea — learning to read indirect feedback and seniority norms took real time, and getting it wrong quietly limited how much influence and trust they were given, long before anyone said so directly.*

> *A Samsung employee, via Blind and reported by Rest of World — reacted to a new company work-discipline memo by comparing it, only half-joking, to a Joseon Dynasty-era edict, capturing how much younger staff feel corporate rules are dressed-up feudalism.*

> *An immigrant blogger writing for Remitbee — was startled that colleagues, and eventually a senior executive, expected to be addressed by first name from day one, after coming from a workplace where you did not casually speak to anyone above you.*

> *A newcomer writing for CBC News First Person — took a demotion in title and pay after arriving in Canada with years of senior experience, and only regained footing once she stopped waiting for that experience to be recognized automatically and started re-explaining it in local terms.*

> *A contributor to The Newcomers Podcast — wished someone had told them earlier that Canadian small talk before meetings isn't filler, it's an informal audition, and skipping it quietly cost them a mentor relationship they didn't know was available.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between them on generational grounds alone, choose based on which discomfort you would rather negotiate: Seoul's is loud, named, and openly contested, which at least means you know what you are up against by lunchtime; Toronto's is unnamed and diffuse, which means you can spend a full year feeling vaguely excluded before anyone tells you why. Neither country has actually solved the problem of putting four different definitions of "respect" into one office. They have just made different choices about whether to argue about it in the open.
