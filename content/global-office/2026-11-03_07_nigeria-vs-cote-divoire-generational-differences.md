[HEADLINE] Lagos Wants a Mentor. Abidjan Wants an Uncle. Neither Wants Your Gen Z Slack Emoji.

🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Lagos, a twenty-six-year-old trainee at one of the continent's largest banks greeted the chairman by his first name and was treated, briefly, as a national security incident. In Abidjan, a job seeker will get further with one well-placed cousin than with a hundred online applications. Both facts describe the same underlying condition: age, in West Africa's two most consequential anglophone-francophone economies, is not a biographical detail but a management system, and the newest cohort of workers arrived carrying a different operating manual. The generational gap that Western HR consultants package into tidy slide decks about "digital natives" is, here, a live negotiation over who gets to speak first in a meeting, who gets called "sir," and who, eventually, gets to stop being called "small boy" at all.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the honorifics in your first week — "sir," "ma," "Oga" — and use them even with peers who outrank you by title alone | Address a senior executive by first name unless explicitly told to; ask what happened to the UBA trainee who called the chairman "Tony" |
| Treat a phone call or in-person stop-by as the senior generation's preferred channel, even if the company Slack looks thoroughly modern | Assume a flat org chart in the employee handbook means a flat org chart in practice |
| Follow the formal resignation and notice process, in writing, even if a colleague's exit went out as a one-line text | Expect instant validation for setting boundaries; "prioritizing mental health" over a deadline still reads as insubordination to most managers over forty |
| Push back on being handed personal errands outside your job description, but do it privately and after hours one | Escalate every disagreement on a public channel; seniority still absorbs criticism badly in front of an audience |
| Ask "why" when given a task — it is increasingly tolerated and even rewarded | Assume asking "why" works the same way with every manager; test the room first |

#### 🇨🇮 Côte d'Ivoire

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build your network before you build your CV — a personal introduction outperforms a cold application in most sectors | Rely on job boards alone; formal recruitment channels are thin outside banking, mining and a handful of multinationals |
| Default to "vous" and full titles with anyone senior to you, regardless of how relaxed the office otherwise feels | Mistake Abidjan's social warmth for workplace informality; the two run on separate rules |
| Budget realistically — formal salaries often sit well below what covers rent, power and transport in the capital | Assume a stated salary is the full picture; informal top-ups, side arrangements and family obligations shape real household income |
| Expect younger staff and older staff to occupy genuinely different economic tiers, not just different attitudes | Treat a 24-year-old's hustle (a side business, a second job) as a lack of commitment to the main one | 
| Use a staffing agency or chamber of commerce if you're arriving without local contacts | Expect quick formalization of a contract role; most new jobs since the mid-2010s have been informal, and that habit runs both directions |

Nigeria's generational story is, on paper, a demographic inevitability wearing a management crisis as a costume. Generation Z, born roughly between the mid-1990s and the early 2010s, already makes up close to a quarter of the country's workforce, according to Phillips Consulting's reading of CIA World Factbook data — and the people managing them are overwhelmingly Gen X and Boomers who came up believing that hard work, long hours and silent deference were the entire job description. Hofstede Insights' numbers, cited by BusinessDay Nigeria, give the structural reason this friction is louder in Lagos than in, say, Oslo: Nigeria scores 80 out of 100 on power distance (acceptance of hierarchical, unequal authority) against a strikingly low 13 on long-term orientation, meaning the culture is simultaneously deeply hierarchical and not particularly patient about preserving tradition for tradition's sake. That combination produces exactly the paradox on display in the "Good Morning, Tony" episode — a bank that markets itself on first-name informality until the first name belongs to the chairman, at which point hierarchy reasserts itself with startling speed.

Researchers at Lagos Business School, writing in BusinessDay, frame the day-to-day version of this as a communication mismatch rather than a values war: younger staff default to digital, asynchronous, informal channels; older staff expect face-to-face deference and formal escalation, and each side reads the other's preferred mode as a lack of respect. The viral case of the Gen Z employee who resigned by texting his manager "oya sope otilo" — roughly, "okay, that's that" — and walked off mid-ASUU-strike became, per BellaNaija's coverage of the backlash, a referendum on an entire generation's work ethic, even though the commentator covering it argued the reaction said more about Nigeria's economic anxiety than about Gen Z's character. Either way, the lesson for an arriving expatriate is the same: Lagos has not resolved this argument, and newcomers inherit it the moment they accept an offer letter.

Côte d'Ivoire's generational divide runs on a different fault line, because the country's defining fact is less about attitude than arithmetic. The World Bank's Africa Can blog noted the workforce stood near 14 million as of the mid-2010s and was projected to reach roughly 22 million within a decade — a wave of mostly young entrants hitting a labor market where, even amid 8–9% annual growth since 2012, four-fifths of workers remain in self-employment or family enterprises earning under 70,000 CFA francs a month. Formal business creation, the same report found, ran five to seven times lower than in more dynamic peers like Nigeria and Rwanda. The practical effect is that Ivorian workplaces sort less by generational attitude and more by access: who has the uncle in procurement, who went to the right lycée, who can afford the risk of waiting for a formal job rather than taking the informal one in front of them. Forum posts on expat.com and ExpatExchange.com, written by foreigners trying to hire or get hired locally, converge on the same practical advice — relationships beat applications — and the same complaint, that a monthly minimum wage near $120 doesn't stretch to cover basic urban costs, a gap every generation navigates together rather than separately.

**The Reckoning.** Put the two side by side and an uncomfortable irony surfaces: Nigeria's generational conflict is a fight over respect in a country rich enough to argue about etiquette, while Côte d'Ivoire's is a fight over access in a country too thin on formal jobs for etiquette to be the main event. A Nigerian Gen Z worker can plausibly quit on principle and find another informal hustle within the week; an Ivorian twenty-something more often cannot afford the gesture, because the formal rung they're standing on is one of relatively few. Nigeria's older generation clings to titles it increasingly can't enforce; Côte d'Ivoire's older generation controls something more durable — the actual networks that produce jobs — which makes deference there less performative and more transactional.

The other reversal: Nigeria, often caricatured as the brasher, noisier sibling, has the more codified, almost bureaucratic etiquette of address — honorifics, full names, correction when you get it wrong in public. Côte d'Ivoire, with its reputation for Francophone formality, runs more on who-you-know informality than any Nigerian HR manual would tolerate. The brochure version of both countries gets this backward.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *expat.com forum — a longtime Côte d'Ivoire poster tells newcomers that no official directory of foreign employers exists; you find work by asking everyone you know and following up relentlessly, because "recommendations are still the best way" into a job there.*

> *ExpatExchange.com — a foreign worker in Abidjan describes being asked for small payments by people entirely outside their employer, down to the security guard at their own bank, calling the gap between the minimum wage and actual living costs the thing that wears you down fastest.*

> *BellaNaija reader commentary — the much-discussed Nigerian office story of a junior staffer sent on personal errands by a senior colleague, then criticized for how badly they were done, with no acknowledgment either way.*

> *LinkedIn and Nigerian business press, on the "Good Morning, Tony" incident — commentators note the same chairman was filmed letting a Stanford audience call him "Tony" without correction months earlier, the cultural misread being less about the word than about which room you said it in.*

> *Fuzu pan-African careers forum — a thread for young managers handling older subordinates lands on one piece of advice above the rest: ask for their counsel before you issue instructions, because being consulted lands as respect in a way that being merely obeyed never does.*

### Conclusion

The single fact worth carrying onto the plane is this: in Nigeria, the generational gap is primarily cultural and increasingly negotiable — company by company, boss by boss — while in Côte d'Ivoire it is primarily structural, tangled up with who has access to the formal economy at all, and therefore slower to move no matter how progressive any individual manager wants to be. Manage a Nigerian team by reading the room on hierarchy and adjusting your register accordingly; manage an Ivorian team by understanding that your twenty-four-year-old analyst and your sixty-year-old department head may be separated less by worldview than by which side of the formal-informal line their families have ever stood on.

If you're moving for one of these jobs and want the version I'd actually tell you over a drink: in Lagos, figure out fast whether your boss wants to be your mentor or your monarch, because they will not announce which; in Abidjan, spend your first month making friends before you spend it making a plan, because the plan will go nowhere without them.
