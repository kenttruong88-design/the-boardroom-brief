**The 35-Hour Myth: Why France Logs More Brutal Workweeks Than "Overworked" Romania**

**🇷🇴 Romania · 🇫🇷 France**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

By one measure, 65 percent of Romanian employees work overtime, averaging over six extra hours a week. By the European Union's own stricter definition — 49 hours or more in a week — only 1.8 percent of Romanian workers actually qualify as overworked, among the lowest rates in the bloc, according to Eurostat's 2024 figures. France, the country that legislated the 35-hour week into a national identity, clears 9 percent on that same strict measure. The country famous for protecting its workers from overwork has more of its workers genuinely overworked than the country with no such law at all.

#### 🇷🇴 Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|

| Build in time for socializing at work — 87% of employees say they're fine with it, and it's part of how the day actually runs | Treat all workplace chat as wasted time that needs to be made up later |
| Ask directly whether extra hours are paid, tracked, or simply expected informally | Assume overtime pay is automatic just because labor law exists on the books |
| Recognize that budgetary-sector jobs come with real legal limits on hours — private sector varies much more | Assume every Romanian job has the same overtime protections as a public-sector one |
| Take vacation time fully — labor organizers still describe older-generation pressure to skip it as a real historical wound | Assume the pressure to skip vacation described by older workers has fully disappeared |
| Use the time during light periods to build skills or review upcoming work, a common local habit during slower stretches | Assume slower-looking days mean no real work is happening |

Romania's overtime culture is a genuine paradox once you look past the headline numbers. A 2017 Epson Europe study of 100 Bucharest-area employees found 65 percent working overtime, averaging 6.2 extra hours a week — driven, per the study, by unfinished tasks (39 percent) and meetings or reporting eating into core work time (42 percent). That sounds like classic overwork. But Eurostat's own 2024 figures, using the EU's formal definition of long hours (49 or more per week), put Romania at just 1.8 percent — among the lowest rates in the entire European Union, against an EU average of 6.6 percent. Analysts attribute the gap to a large share of Romanian workers sitting in the public sector, where legal hour restrictions genuinely hold, plus widespread informal arrangements and manufacturing wages pinned close to the minimum, which limits how much unpaid extra time employers can realistically extract. The overtime that does happen tends to be real but moderate — a few extra hours here and there, not a culture of marathon weeks. That said, the scars of an older overtime culture are still being actively discussed: Marin Florian, a retired union leader from the Daewoo Mangalia shipyard, described an earlier era bluntly to Radio Romania International — employers pressuring staff to skip vacations entirely, a pattern he said "even broke marriages," with some children, when asked to draw their family, drawing only their mothers because their fathers were simply never home.

France's relationship with overtime is almost the inverse: a country with one of the most famous work-hour protections in the world that still manages real, measurable long-hours exposure. The 35-hour week, introduced in 2000, is defended as what The Local's reporting calls "a social totem, a milestone in the fight for worker's rights" — durable enough that no French government since has managed to repeal it, even as a 2005 reform quietly allowed up to 13 hours of negotiated, paid overtime on top of it, bringing the legal ceiling to the EU's own 48-hour cap. The actual numbers tell a more complicated story than either the law's defenders or its critics usually admit: French workers log around 1,494 hours a year per worker as of OECD 2023 data — more than Germany's 1,340, though less than the EU average of 1,570 — while Eurostat's stricter long-hours measure puts over 9 percent of French workers above the 49-hour threshold, well above Romania's 1.8 percent. Public accounts minister Amélie de Montchalin has leaned into the comparative framing anyway, noting France works "100 hours less per person than in Germany" per year — a true statistic that, read at the per-worker rather than per-capita level, tells a notably less flattering story.

### The Reckoning

Here's the real inversion worth sitting with: Romania has no equivalent of France's 35-hour law, no international reputation for protecting work-life balance, and a documented overtime problem by its own looser definition — and yet, by the strict EU long-hours standard, it is one of the least overworked countries in Europe. France has the law, the reputation, and the political mythology, and still posts a meaningfully higher share of workers logging genuinely excessive weeks. The 35-hour week was never really a ceiling on total hours worked; it was a floor under negotiating power, which is a different thing entirely, and one that coexists just fine with a meaningful minority of French workers putting in 49-plus-hour weeks anyway. Romania and France also land close together on Hofstede's uncertainty-avoidance dimension, 90 and 86 respectively — a shared discomfort with ambiguity that, in both countries, has translated into a strong attachment to formal rules about work hours, even when the lived reality diverges from the rule.

If you're choosing a country based on which one will actually protect your time, the honest answer complicates the easy story both nations tell about themselves. Romania's protection is accidental — a function of public-sector size and limited extraction room rather than deliberate policy. France's protection is deliberate, symbolically enormous, and still routinely exceeded by a meaningful share of its own workforce.

### Conclusion

If raw legal protection is what you're optimizing for, France's 35-hour framework is real, hard-won, and worth having on paper, even if a meaningful share of French workers quietly exceed it anyway. If you're optimizing for the actual odds of landing in a genuinely brutal weekly schedule, the data says to look past the reputation entirely — Romania's looser laws and heavier informal overtime culture still land most of its workers in a more moderate weekly range than France's famously protected one.

Ask either country's hiring manager not "what does the law say about my hours," but "what did your worst week actually look like last month." The law tells you what a country wants to believe about itself. The worst week tells you what's actually true.
