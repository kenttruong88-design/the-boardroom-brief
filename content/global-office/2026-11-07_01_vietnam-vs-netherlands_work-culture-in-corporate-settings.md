**The 48-Hour Week and the 29-Hour Week: What Vietnam and the Netherlands Actually Expect of You**

🇻🇳 Vietnam · 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Somewhere in Hanoi, an employee is clocking a 48-hour week because the law still permits it and the market still rewards it. Somewhere in Rotterdam, an employee is leaving the office at 4 p.m. on a Friday, having worked a contractually blessed four-day week, and will spend the weekend being extremely honest with you about your presentation. Both will tell you, with total sincerity, that their country has a healthy relationship with work. Neither is lying. They are simply answering different questions.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive five to ten minutes early to meetings; punctuality reads as respect | Don't schedule anything during the midday break — shops, restaurants, and some offices genuinely shut down |
| Address colleagues by title plus first name (Anh/Chị + name), not surname | Don't publicly correct or challenge a superior's idea in a group setting |
| Accept tea or coffee when offered before a meeting starts | Don't skip the small talk and jump straight into business — it reads as cold |
| Route disagreements through a trusted intermediary rather than confronting someone directly | Don't assume a nod or "yes" is agreement — it may just be acknowledgment |
| Budget real time for relationship-building before expecting deals to close | Don't be surprised by last-minute schedule changes handed down from above |
| Dress neatly and conservatively, even in warm weather | Don't expect your job title alone to carry weight without demonstrated loyalty and tenure |

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say what you mean plainly; vagueness is read as evasive, not polite | Don't take critical feedback personally — it's about the work, not you |
| Check the shared calendar before proposing a meeting time; agendas are sacred | Don't show up without an agenda item — meetings run on structure |
| Expect colleagues on 0.8 or 0.9 FTE contracts; part-time is normal, even for managers | Don't assume everyone is in the office five days a week |
| Speak to your manager with the same directness you'd use with a peer | Don't expect titles or seniority to shield anyone from blunt questioning |
| Bring your own lunch or expect a modest communal one — lavish client lunches are rare | Don't schedule anything past 5 p.m. on a Friday; the office will be empty |
| Use first names from day one, regardless of rank | Don't over-apologize or soften requests excessively — it reads as insecure |

Vietnam's labour statistics tell a story of a workforce still converting hours into growth rather than growth into hours back. Workers there log roughly 2,320 hours a year, among the highest totals in Southeast Asia, under a Labour Code ceiling of 48 hours a week that the government has been trying, with limited success, to nudge down toward 44 (Hanoi Times, citing the Ministry of Labour and trade unions). The state's own Occupational Safety Department data ranks the country near the top of the region for hours worked and near the bottom for public holidays. This is not an accident of culture so much as an accident of arithmetic: labour productivity, despite roughly tripling since 2011, still sits at a fraction of regional peers — about 11% of Singapore's and a quarter of South Korea's — so hours have historically substituted for output per hour. Officials now openly debate whether a shorter week would help or hurt that catch-up, which is a very on-brand Vietnamese compromise: ambition expressed through scheduling.

What the hours data doesn't capture is the social architecture underneath it. Hofstede Insights scores Vietnam at 70 on power distance and just 20 on individualism, meaning authority is expected to be respected rather than questioned, and belonging to the group matters more than standing out in it. In a corporate setting this shows up as decisions that move up a hierarchy rather than across a table, feedback delivered sideways through a trusted go-between rather than head-on, and a genuine discomfort with public disagreement that outsiders often mistake for passivity. It isn't; it's a different theory of how consensus gets built. New arrivals who treat Vietnamese colleagues' agreeableness as weakness tend to discover, eventually and expensively, that it wasn't.

Multinationals operating in Vietnam have adjusted their own playbooks accordingly. Advisory work from firms like Deloitte and McKinsey on Southeast Asian workforces routinely flags Vietnam as a market where engagement scores hinge less on compensation benchmarking and more on manager trust and job security — a workforce that rewards stability-signalling employers disproportionately well, and punishes the opposite just as disproportionately. For a foreign manager used to flattening hierarchy as a show of goodwill, the lesson tends to land the hard way: flattening it too fast can look less like humility and more like abdicating the job you were hired to do.

The Netherlands runs the opposite experiment. According to OECD figures on annual hours worked, Dutch employees have for years logged the fewest average hours of any OECD economy, and Statistics Netherlands (CBS) puts the part-time employment rate at roughly 48% of the workforce — nearly half of all Dutch workers, including a majority of women and a meaningful share of managers, work contracted hours below full time. This is not slacking; it's policy. The Netherlands has one of the world's most developed legal frameworks for part-time work, with equal rights per hour regardless of contract size, and the culture has simply absorbed what the law permits.

The other defining export is directness, and Hofstede's individualism score of 80 against Vietnam's 20 explains a good deal of why. Dutch workplaces tend to be flat, informal, and allergic to softened language; a manager's criticism arrives as a clear sentence rather than a diplomatic parable. The 2021 InterNations Expat Insider survey found 78% of expats in the Netherlands rated their work-life balance favourably and 79% rated their hours positively, which tracks — but it is worth noting the same directness that produces efficient meetings also produces, by several accounts, genuinely bruised feelings among newcomers who weren't warned it would apply to them too.

Mercer and Korn Ferry benchmarking work on European mobility consistently cites the Netherlands as a low-friction relocation market precisely because so much is written down and agreed in advance — working hours, holiday entitlement, remote-work rights are all contractually explicit rather than left to managerial discretion. That same instinct for codifying things extends to the meeting itself: an agenda circulated beforehand is treated less as courtesy than as a prerequisite for anyone taking the discussion seriously, and arriving without one is the fastest way to be quietly written off as disorganised, regardless of seniority.

Here is the part the two countries' own data politely leaves out: both systems believe they have solved the problem of work consuming life, and both are at least partly right, but they solved it from opposite directions. Vietnam compresses social friction by avoiding direct confrontation and lets the hours run long; the Netherlands compresses the hours and lets the social friction run direct. An employee moving from Hanoi to Rotterdam will find the calendar lighter and the feedback heavier. An employee moving the other way will find the opposite trade, and will likely misread the quiet Vietnamese nod as consensus when it was, in fact, a request for someone else to disagree on their behalf.

The irony is that the country working the longer hours reports comparable optimism about its work culture to the one working the fewest. Vietnam's own growth narrative — fast, improvising, relationship-first — generates its own form of satisfaction that isn't captured by an hours-per-week line item, just as Dutch satisfaction isn't fully captured by the 29-hour average quoted for part-timers. Neither metric lies, but both are answering a narrower question than "is this a good place to build a career," which is really what anyone relocating is asking.

Promotion also works in reverse in the two systems, which trips up almost everyone who switches. In a Vietnamese corporate hierarchy, climbing usually means demonstrating loyalty over years and letting a sponsor advocate for you upward; in a Dutch one, climbing more often means arguing your own case, loudly and with evidence, in a meeting where your manager is sitting two feet away and will argue back just as loudly if they disagree. An employee who waits patiently for recognition in Amsterdam will wait considerably longer than one who waits patiently in Hanoi — because in Amsterdam, patience reads as having nothing to say.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit — a Rotterdam-based commenter of seven years described the local bluntness as "directness on steroids," noting colleagues who dished out criticism freely grew defensive and dug in when the same bluntness was turned back on them; another commenter in the same thread offered a gentler template that still counted as direct — a manager's actual phrasing, "I appreciate your effort but I don't think you quite got there yet" — as proof the bluntness doesn't have to be harsh to land clearly.*

> *Reddit — an Indian traveler recounted a Hanoi restaurant owner who refused to serve customers during staff rest hours, explaining he'd have to pay triple wages to break the break — and offered to cook only at a triple markup, which the group declined, calling it a refreshing level of respect for workers' time.*

> *Expat.com forum — a longtime foreign resident of Vietnam advised newcomers to research a colleague's family and personal connections before assuming merit alone explains their position, and to route any workplace conflict through a respected intermediary rather than raising it directly, since direct confrontation is taken personally.*

> *InterNations Expat Insider survey respondents in the Netherlands — roughly a third cited flexible hours and the option to work from home as the single biggest quality-of-life upgrade versus their home country, more than any salary-related factor.*

### Conclusion

If the choice comes down to one variable, make it this one: in the Netherlands, the system protects your time and expects you to defend your opinions; in Vietnam, the system protects the group's harmony and expects you to be patient with its clock. Neither is more "pro-worker" in the abstract — they're pro-worker according to two different definitions of what a worker is owed. Someone who wants their evenings back and can handle blunt feedback without flinching will thrive in Rotterdam. Someone who wants to build the kind of relationships that outlast a single job and doesn't mind a longer week will do better in Hanoi. The honest version Priya would give a friend over a drink: pick the Netherlands if you want your calendar to be kind to you, and pick Vietnam if you want your colleagues to be — because apparently, by both countries' own account, you rarely get both from the same postcode.
