# Two Ways to Call Yourself a Startup While Filing Corporate Paperwork

**🇵🇱 Poland · 🇵🇹 Portugal**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Poland logged the European Union's third-longest average working week in 2024, at 38.9 hours against an EU average of 36.0 — behind only Greece and Bulgaria (Eurostat, 2025). Portugal, meanwhile, sent 178 startups to Web Summit in Lisbon this November, a city that has spent a decade rebranding itself as Europe's answer to Austin (Jornal de Negócios, 2025). One country works like a corporation and calls it a startup scene; the other hosts a startup festival and calls it an economy. Neither is lying, exactly. Both are doing something more interesting, which is optimizing for a definition of "startup" that has very little to do with how long anyone sits at their desk — and everything to do with what the tax authority does once that person logs off.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate a B2B contract where possible — the flat lump-sum tax regime often beats a standard employment contract on take-home pay | Assume "startup" means flat hierarchy; many Polish startups run tighter KPI ladders than their corporate neighbours |
| Expect blunt, specific feedback delivered without a compliment sandwich | Mistake directness for hostility — it is usually just efficiency |
| Treat punctuality as a competence signal, not a formality | Expect a Warsaw shared-services centre to move at founder speed — process outlives ambition there |
| Job-hop without social cost; tenure loyalty is low by regional standards | Expect generous VC-funded perks — capital is comparatively scarce, so most teams bootstrap further than the pitch deck admits |
| Bring demonstrable technical depth — it is the actual currency of status | Skip documentation; audit trails matter even in year-one companies |
| Ask directly about relocation and Blue Card lock-in terms before signing | Assume a Big Tech badge in Warsaw pays Western-Europe money — it frequently doesn't |

#### 🇵🇹 Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build the relationship before the pitch; rapport precedes the deal, not the reverse | Open a meeting with KPIs and quarterly targets — it reads as crude before trust exists |
| Pad every bureaucratic timeline generously; patience is the actual productivity hack | Assume long office hours mean long output — presence and performance are tracked separately |
| Expect consensus-seeking before a decision ships | Push for a unilateral, founder-decides-today call in a first or second meeting |
| Enjoy the quality-of-life framing — it is sincerely meant, not a perk-page slogan | Expect every "startup" to chase the hockey-stick; the tax structure rewards staying small |
| Lean on Lisbon's genuine digital-nomad infrastructure and English-fluency | Expect Polish-style B2B contracting norms — the employment and tax culture runs differently |
| Show up to the Web Summit energy, then ask what happens the other 51 weeks | Mistake festival enthusiasm for ecosystem depth without checking the funding numbers |

Poland's paradox is that its startup sector behaves like a well-run corporation, while its corporations behave like understaffed startups. Dealroom's 2025 ecosystem report puts the country's tech value at roughly €56.5 billion, with fifteen unicorns and billion-dollar exits — more than any other Central or Eastern European market, ahead of Estonia's twelve — yet only 37% of that value sits in venture-backed companies, against 78% or more in the UK, Germany, and France. Translation: most of the money is in services, outsourcing, and corporate shared-services centres, the kind of operation that calls itself "tech" on a careers page while running on SAP tickets and a quarterly headcount review. Polish founders, for their part, have learned to do more with less: the same report finds Poland generating 19.8x enterprise value per euro of venture capital raised since 2019 — the best ratio among the European markets Dealroom tracks — while ranking ninth out of ten on VC funding as a share of GDP. It is a capital-efficient culture because it has had no choice; when the funding rounds are thin, discipline becomes the house style rather than a slide in the pitch deck.

That efficiency has a human cost, or at least a human texture. Hofstede's 6D framework scores Poland at 64 on masculinity, a measure of competitive, achievement-oriented norms, against Portugal's 31, and at 68 on individualism, against Portugal's 27 (Olsztyn Economic Journal, 2022) — meaning Polish professional culture rewards personal advancement over group loyalty, and switching jobs carries little stigma. Power distance sits high too, at 68, which helps explain why so many of those "startups" still run on approval chains that would be recognisable to any Warsaw bank. Combine all that with the EU's third-longest working week and a corporate-services sector that dwarfs its venture-backed one, and the practical result is a labour market where even the glossiest startup job interview still smells faintly of a performance review. Workers on forums describe the trade-off in financial terms rather than cultural ones: a Reddit thread on Google's Warsaw office spends most of its length comparing stock-vested employment contracts unfavourably against independent B2B contracting under Poland's flat lump-sum tax rate, with one commenter noting that a senior engineer's take-home as a contractor can run well ahead of the equivalent salaried offer — a distinctly Polish solution, turning everyone into a one-person corporation in order to escape the corporation.

Portugal's paradox runs the other way: a startup marketing operation sitting on top of a culture that, by its own cultural-dimension scores, does not particularly want to be one. Hofstede's low masculinity and low individualism scores point toward consensus-seeking, quality-of-life-first management and longer, steadier employer relationships — the opposite of the churn-and-burn instinct that venture capital tends to reward. Power distance, notably, sits only slightly below Poland's at 63, so hierarchy has not gone away; it has simply gone quiet, expressed through deference and waiting rather than org charts. Eurostat figures back the lived complaint about hours: 9.2% of Portuguese workers logged 49 or more hours a week in 2024, the fourth-highest share in the EU, well above the bloc's 6.6% average, even as the country's actual venture output remains modest next to the Web Summit spectacle staged in its capital every November. Long hours, in other words, have not obviously translated into the kind of growth-at-all-costs culture the festival programme implies, and uncertainty avoidance — Portugal's highest score of all, at 99 — suggests a workforce that would rather log the extra hour than take the risk that might make it unnecessary.

The sharpest account of that gap comes not from an economist but from a long-resident digital nomad: writing on his own blog, Pieter Levels argues that Portugal's progressive tax brackets actively discourage businesses from growing past roughly €150,000 in annual revenue, since crossing that line brings materially heavier taxation and bookkeeping — a policy, he contends, that keeps small businesses deliberately small rather than ambitious. He describes shop staff visibly unhappy to see a new customer walk in, which he reads not as rudeness but as a rational response to a system that charges more for success than it rewards for it. Whether or not one accepts his Singapore-style prescription, the underlying observation tracks with the Hofstede data: a culture scoring low on both competitiveness and individualism is not, structurally, built to produce aggressive founders, no matter how many unicorn-shaped stickers appear on laptops at Web Summit, and no matter how fluent the English on the pitch stage has become.

**The Reckoning.** Line the two cultures up and the labels swap. Poland's "startup ecosystem," per Dealroom, is efficient almost by necessity — short on capital, long on output, staffed by individualists who job-hop toward whoever pays best and tolerate a 38.9-hour week because the alternative, a B2B contract, is simply the corporate job wearing a founder's hat. Portugal's festival-grade startup branding, meanwhile, floats over a workforce whose own cultural scores — consensus-first, group-loyal, quality-of-life-oriented — describe something closer to a well-mannered family business than a Silicon Valley sprint, even while logging some of the EU's longest hours for comparatively modest venture returns.

Put differently: Poland has the corporate infrastructure and the startup grind; Portugal has the startup marketing and the corporate patience. Neither government press release will say so, but the Reddit and Blind threads on both countries agree, in their blunter way, on roughly the same thing — that whichever flag is on the office door, the actual work culture is determined less by the sign than by the tax code underneath it. A Polish "startup" job and a Portuguese "corporate" job may, in practice, ask less of each other's workers than their labels suggest, and a relocating professional who reads only the careers page will be mildly, consistently surprised on both counts.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit, r/cscareerquestionsEU — a prospective hire comparing a Google Warsaw offer against local B2B contracting discovers that a senior employment-contract salary can land under half of what an independent contractor nets after Poland's flat lump-sum tax, turning "join Big Tech" into a weaker financial move than "incorporate yourself."*

> *Blind — a foreign employee at Google's Warsaw office, a few months into the job, learns that Poland's work-permit rules require roughly two years of tenure before an internal transfer abroad gets easier, and that leaving the country sooner means restarting the visa process from scratch elsewhere.*

> *Reddit, r/AskEurope — a commenter who has managed teams across several countries describes projects in Portugal succeeding only when a foreign manager stays closely involved throughout, attributing repeated slippage to disengagement rather than any lack of skill.*

> *DigitalNomadExchange forum — a long-settled expat in Tavira advises newcomers that Portuguese bureaucracy is genuinely exhausting at first, but that patience grows once the rest of one's life slows down to match it — a piece of advice several threads wish they'd been given before, not after, their residency paperwork.*

> *Levels.io — a tech entrepreneur who has lived in Portugal for five years is struck less by rudeness than by apparent disinterest, describing shopkeepers who visibly react to new customers as an inconvenience rather than an opportunity, and tracing it back to a tax system that punishes exactly the growth a startup culture is supposed to chase.*

### Conclusion

Neither country is wrong about what it is; both are slightly wrong about what to call it. Poland runs a startup economy with a corporate soul — hierarchical shared-services centres bankrolling, almost by accident, some of the most capital-efficient venture returns in the region, staffed by people who have made peace with long hours by refusing long loyalty. Portugal runs a corporate economy with a startup costume — Web Summit's banners over a tax structure and a cultural temperament that both quietly prefer things to stay the size they already are. A mover chasing "startup energy" should ask less about the pitch deck and more about the tax bracket; it will tell you more about the culture than the office furniture does.

The honest version of the brochure would read: pick Poland if you want the grind without the capital, and Portugal if you want the capital's absence without the grind — either way, bring your own definition of ambition, because the one on the recruiting page was written by marketing, not by Eurostat.
