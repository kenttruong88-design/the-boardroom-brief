# The UAE Pays You Tax-Free and Invoices You for Everything Else; India Taxes You and Sends the Aunt

🇦🇪 United Arab Emirates · 🇮🇳 India

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Reddit post reported by Business Today in August 2025 modelled a 25-year-old software engineer in Dubai and in India and found that, after rent, the monthly savings came out almost identical: about 19,300 rupees a month in India and about 19,125 rupees a month in Dubai, despite the Gulf salary being tax-free. The result is a useful reminder that a salary is not a standard of living, and that the cost of a place is mostly composed of the things the offer letter does not mention.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In the UAE

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate a package that includes family health insurance and a school allowance | Accept a headline salary without reading what it covers |
| Ask what your employer's insurance covers, since cover is mandatory and usually employer-paid | Assume basic cover is adequate for a family |
| Compare rent and school fees against the tax saving | Treat "tax-free" as "savings" |
| Opt in to, or personally fund, a pension | Assume there is a state safety net or a path to residency |
| Pay school fees a year upfront if it earns a discount | Ignore the annual fee increase |
| Plan the stay as a sprint, with a savings target | Assume you will be able to stay for ever |

### In India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check what a private hospital stay really costs | Assume insurance covers the whole bill |
| Budget for daycare or domestic help on top of rent | Assume family will fill the gap in every case |
| Expect school fees to rise by 10 to 12 percent a year | Assume education costs are fixed |
| Compare your take-home after tax with the Gulf offer | Treat the Gulf offer as a guaranteed win |
| Build your own savings and insurance in a country with a patchy state safety net | Rely on the state for everything |
| Use family networks for help, and accept the opinions that come with them | Expect help without commentary |

## United Arab Emirates

The UAE's pitch is straightforward, and the invoice arrives in instalments. According to Aetna International's guide, annual rent in Dubai as of July 2025 ran from about AED 40,439 for a studio to AED 61,885 for a one-bedroom, AED 85,461 for a two-bedroom and AED 141,653 for a three-bedroom. The same guide, which is an insurer's publication and should be read as indicative, puts annual health insurance at AED 500 to 1,500 for basic cover, AED 3,000 to 7,000 for mid-range and AED 8,000 to 20,000 or more for premium cover, with family plans starting from around AED 17,000.

Schools are the larger line. Khaleej Times reported that Dubai approved a 2.35 percent Education Cost Index for 2025-26, with hikes ranging from about Dh200 to nearly Dh5,000 per child, and parents quoted by the paper said fees ranged from about Dh30,000 to Dh87,000 or Dh92,000 a year. One moved a daughter from a Dh55,000-plus school to one at about Dh30,000, and another said paying a year upfront cut a family's increase by about two-thirds.

The welfare system is built for a temporary population. The ILO reported that from 1 November 2023 the UAE offers an optional pension and savings scheme to replace end-of-service indemnities, where employers opt in with monthly contributions and migrant workers may contribute voluntarily. A financial adviser quoted by Business Today in April 2026 put the average Indian expat income at about AED 15,000 a month, said many remit close to 70 percent home, and described the Gulf as a sprint and not a marathon, since it offers no residency route or long-term social security. The UAE is a good place to earn and a poor place to be vague about when you plan to leave.

## India

India's cost structure is the reverse: cheaper at the front, more expensive in the exceptions. The National Sample Survey on health, reported by Business Today in April 2026, found that health insurance coverage was about 47 percent in rural areas and 44 percent in urban areas, up from 14 and 19 percent in 2017-18, largely because of government schemes. Out-of-pocket spending remains high: the average per hospitalization was Rs 34,064, and Rs 38,688 in urban areas, with a median of Rs 11,285. A public-hospital stay averaged Rs 6,631, and childbirth Rs 2,299 in the public sector against Rs 14,775 across all hospitals.

For children, the numbers are more anecdotal. A Bengaluru founder's LinkedIn post, reported by Business Today, estimated Rs 38 to 45 lakh to raise a child in urban India, with an extra Rs 20,000 a month for daycare or domestic help for working parents, and education inflation of 10 to 12 percent a year. This is an individual's estimate and not an official figure, but it matches the general shape of what Indian middle-class families describe.

The Indian system also includes something that does not appear in the cost tables: family. The grandmother, the aunt and the neighbor perform some of the work that Dubai's expatriate parents buy in. This is not free, since it comes with opinions and obligations, but it explains why a Bengaluru salary of Rs 60,000 can support what looks, on a spreadsheet, like less.

Two further details from the forums are worth setting beside the tables. The Aetna figures for Dubai describe an expatriate market in which health insurance is mandatory and usually provided by the employer, so the real question for a family is the quality of the cover and not its existence, and the gap between basic cover at AED 500 to 1,500 and family plans from about AED 17,000 is large enough to deserve a negotiation. In India, by contrast, the rise in coverage from 14 and 19 percent to 47 and 44 percent in rural and urban areas over seven years is substantial and is mostly government-led, so the question is what the scheme covers when you need it. In both countries the sticker price is the beginning of the sum.

## The Reckoning

Hofstede's older scores, reproduced by Expert Program Management, put the UAE (using the generic Arab-countries value) at a power distance of about 80 and India at about 77, which is the profile of two countries in which hierarchy and family structure the living arrangements. The difference lies in who carries the risk. In the UAE the employer carries part of it, through insurance and housing allowances if you negotiate them, and you carry the rest. In India the family carries part of it, and you carry the remainder, and the state carries what it can.

The headline comparison is that tax-free income and cheap rent are real, and so are school fees and the absence of a safety net. The Reddit model reached a draw. The honest conclusion is that the Gulf rewards a short and well-planned stay with a specific goal, and India rewards a long stay with a network.

The comparison is further complicated by the way each country treats the family unit. Dubai's school-fee increases, which Khaleej Times says ranged from about Dh200 to nearly Dh5,000 per child in a single year, are paid by the household, and the expatriate family has few other sources of support. In India, a family's costs are spread across more people and more years, which is why a nominally smaller salary can stretch further and why the figures in a salary comparison tend to say less than the living arrangements behind them.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit, via Business Today — A poster modelled a 25-year-old software engineer in both places: rent of about Rs 20,000 in India against AED 4,000 (roughly Rs 90,000) in Dubai, take-home of Rs 60,000 against AED 10,000, and monthly savings that came out almost identical despite the tax-free pay. The thread itself was not read; this is the news report of it.*

> *Khaleej Times parents — Dubai parents reported fees ranging from about Dh30,000 to Dh87,000 or Dh92,000 a year. One moved a daughter from a Dh55,000-plus school to one at about Dh30,000, another felt the pinch from a Dh2,000 rise per child and planned to cut extracurriculars, and one family cut its increase by about two-thirds by paying a year upfront.*

> *LinkedIn, via Business Today — A Bengaluru founder estimated that raising a child in urban India costs Rs 38 to 45 lakh, flagged education inflation of 10 to 12 percent a year, and added about Rs 20,000 a month for daycare or domestic help for working parents.*

> *Business Today adviser — A financial adviser said Indian expats in the UAE average about AED 15,000 a month, that rent can take a large share and that many send close to 70 percent home. He advised saving 50 percent or more, since the Gulf offers no residency path or long-term social security.*

> *Mumsnet — A few UK parents on a Dubai page said they were happy raising children there and chose their schools partly to avoid traffic. The page's cost commentary comes from its author and not from forum users.*

## Conclusion

Choose the UAE if you want a defined period, a savings goal and a package that covers the things the salary does not. Choose India if you want a lower cost of ordinary life, a family network and a long horizon, and are prepared to pay privately for health care when needed.

In either case, read the package before the salary: insurance, schooling, housing and an end-of-service plan are the real compensation. Ask what happens to your income if you leave, what happens to your family if you fall ill, and who looks after the children at six on a Thursday. The tax-free figure will be on the first page. The answers will be on the fourth.
