### Yes, Boss: A Field Guide to Taking Orders in Czechia and Austria

🇨🇿 Czechia · 🇦🇹 Austria

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Vienna, your boss will not know your first name informally until roughly the third ice age, but may hand you a six-figure budget before you've learned where the good lunch spot is. In Prague, your boss may invite you for a beer by week two, then quietly let a colleague know you've been "difficult" rather than ever say so to your face. Hofstede's power-distance research scores Austria among the flattest hierarchies measured anywhere in the world, while Czechia sits roughly midway up the European ladder — a gap that, on paper, suggests Austria is the easy posting and Czechia the stiff one. Anyone who has actually worked in either office knows it's nearly the opposite of what the paperwork implies.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use "dobrý den" with your boss and new colleagues until someone offers first names | Open with "ahoj" on day one — it reads as presumptuous, not friendly |
| Show up on time, ideally a few minutes early | Be even five to ten minutes late more than once — it gets noted, quietly |
| Raise concerns in writing or ask for a scheduled conversation | Challenge your manager's decision out loud in a group meeting |
| Join the lunch or after-work beer, even if briefly | Announce you hate beer, or eat a sandwich alone at your desk |
| Expect decisions to be deliberated on and arrive later, often after the meeting ends | Demand an instant yes or no in the room |
| Leave around 5pm like everyone else | Stay late to signal dedication — it reads as poor planning, not commitment |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn and use your counterpart's title (Magister, Doktor, Ingenieur) | Default to first names or informal "du" with anyone senior |
| Bring data, sources, and a clear agenda to any meeting | Oversell a proposal or respond to pushback with exaggeration |
| Expect goals to be set clearly, then be trusted to pick your own method | Assume formal hierarchy means your boss wants to approve every step |
| Respect the 38.5-hour working week and its actual boundaries | Call or email your manager expecting a weekend reply |
| Let the senior person enter the room first in formal settings | Try to fast-track a personal relationship with your boss before results do the talking |
| Negotiate your own perks and benefits explicitly | Assume standard benefits exist without asking — they vary sharply by employer |

### Czechia: Flat Vocabulary, Steep Reality

Czech workplaces present as unusually relaxed. Beer at lunch is normal, office slippers are a genuine institution, and the switch from formal "vy" to informal "ty" with a boss can happen within months, according to reporting from Prague-based outlet Expats.cz. But the 2026 Employee Barometer from benefits provider Up Benefity, cited by Expats.cz, found that nearly half of Czech employees avoid giving their boss direct critical feedback, routing concerns instead to colleagues, anonymous channels, or nowhere at all. Roughly three in ten of those who do speak up say honesty has backfired on them before. Trust runs thin in the other direction too: only about one in ten employees say they trust management almost completely, and roughly 40 percent believe managers filter or withhold information to suit their own interests.

Diversity trainer Dinah Spritzer, quoted in the same reporting, traces this to the country's communist-era management legacy — a system she describes as "shame-oriented," built around avoiding blame rather than encouraging open collaboration. The language agrees: Radio Prague International notes that Czech retains a rich vocabulary for abused rank, from "papaláši" (greedy bosses, a Communist-era coinage) to "podržtaška," someone who exists to carry a superior's bags. A study in the Journal of World Business found a "developed nation advantage" in how multinational teams rate leaders: Czech, Polish, and Albanian managers were judged less favorably than equally qualified German or Norwegian ones, a bias the authors say sidelines domestic talent in its own offices.

Ask actual Czech workers, though, and the picture splits. On a Reddit thread discussing work culture in the Czech Republic, one commenter flatly rejected the hierarchy framing — "we do not follow strict hierarchies" — describing most managers as approachable and toxic bosses as rare, concentrated mostly in smaller firms. Another, describing a Brno internship, said the opposite: a real pecking order where juniors don't push back and staying late unprompted is simply expected. The honest summary is that Czechia's hierarchy is informal in form and occasionally rigid in substance — which is harder to read as a newcomer than a country that is consistently one or the other.

### Austria: Strict Choreography, Surprising Trust

Austria looks, on the surface, like the more hierarchical of the two. The Cultural Atlas describes a "well-defined, strictly observed hierarchy" where the highest-ranking person enters the room first, titles are used religiously, and business relationships stay formal — "Sie," not "du," until explicitly invited otherwise. Management guide Commisceo Global calls Austrian business culture "conservative and hierarchical," with deference to rank expected as a matter of course. None of that is wrong. It is also not the whole story.

Hofstede's power-distance index, built from decades of workplace survey research, places Austria among the lowest-scoring countries in the world — a result that looks contradictory next to all that formality until you see how Austrian managers actually delegate. Commisceo's guide notes Austrian firms tend to be decentralized, with managers setting direction and trusting employees to pick their own methods. A first-person account on Welcometothejungle.com describes a French professional handed a six-figure budget within months of arriving, invited to call the department head by first name despite the formal titles elsewhere, and credited for ideas regardless of seniority. Separately, the Monster/IMAS Job Changer Study found 47 percent of Austrian employees rate their boss highly and only 9 percent are dissatisfied — among the more boss-friendly numbers in European workplace surveys.

The formality, in other words, is the choreography; the trust is the actual operating system. Confuse the two and you'll either annoy your Austrian manager by being too casual too soon, or underuse the autonomy you've quietly already been given.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the irony sharpens: Czechia reads as the casual office and governs like the formal one, while Austria reads as the formal office and governs like the casual one. A Czech boss will drink with you and still let feedback travel sideways through the office grapevine rather than upward, honestly, to your face. An Austrian boss will insist on your title and the correct seating order, then leave you alone with a serious budget and zero daily check-ins, because results — not deference — are what's actually being measured.

The practical consequence for a transplant is that Czechia requires you to read between informal lines for real hierarchy, while Austria requires you to perform formal lines that mostly aren't load-bearing. Get this backwards — treating your Czech boss's friendliness as license to challenge decisions openly, or treating your Austrian boss's titles as license to wait for instructions — and you will misjudge both relationships in the exact opposite direction from where the risk actually lives.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/Prague — described a boss who called on weekends and treated any pushback as a personal betrayal; said direct feedback culture basically doesn't exist there, and disagreements get aired behind people's backs instead.*

> *r/Prague — pushed back on the hierarchy narrative entirely, saying managers are often genuinely friendly with subordinates and that toxic bosses are the exception, mostly confined to smaller employers, though foreigners sometimes get treated worse than locals in the same office.*

> *Hacker News — in an old thread on European workplace flatness, one commenter noted that if you wanted to see what strict, old-school hierarchy actually looked like, "you might want to look at Austria" — a throwaway line that undersells how much has not changed in the formal structure since.*

> *Welcometothejungle.com — a French transplant to Austria described being handed a six-figure project budget within months of arriving and being surprised she could address a senior executive by first name, despite a workplace that otherwise ran on formal titles.*

> *Expat.com forum — a contributor working in Austria advised that benefits are not handed out by default and have to be negotiated directly with the employer, pointing out that legal support, for instance, is something most employees can already get through the Arbeiterkammer rather than expecting it from the company.*

### Conclusion

If you need one variable to plan around, make it this: in Czechia, judge your boss by what gets said in private, not in the room; in Austria, judge your boss by what you're actually allowed to decide, not by the title on the door. Czechia's risk is mistaking a beer with your manager for an open door on disagreement. Austria's risk is mistaking a stiff "Sie" and a seating chart for a boss who needs to approve your every move. Neither mistake is fatal, but both will cost you a few confused months figuring out why the office that felt relaxed was actually rigid, and the one that felt rigid was actually handing you the keys.

If a friend asked me over a drink which to pick, I'd say: go to Austria if you want to be trusted quietly and judged on results, and go to Czechia if you're fine never quite knowing what's being said about you until it's already decided.
