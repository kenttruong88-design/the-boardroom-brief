# The Meeting Already Happened Before You Walked In

**🇺🇸 USA · 🇯🇵 Japan**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

An American manager walks into a conference room believing the meeting is where a decision gets made. A Japanese colleague walks into the same room already knowing what the decision is, because the meeting was never where decisions happened — it was where they get ratified in front of witnesses. According to the OECD, the average American worked 1,779 hours in 2019 against Japan's 1,644, so it is not, strictly, a matter of who works harder. It is a matter of what everyone in the room is quietly pretending the room is for.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Come with a clear ask and a recommendation, not just a problem | Ramble through context before stating your point — say the headline first |
| Speak up in the meeting if you disagree | Wait for a "better time" to raise an objection — there usually isn't one |
| Follow up with a written summary of decisions and owners | Assume silence in the room means agreement — someone will ask afterward |
| Treat the meeting as the decision-making venue | Treat meetings as a formality once the real decision is already made |
| Push back on your boss's idea directly, with data | Read hierarchy into every disagreement — pushback is often just pushback |

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Do the rounds beforehand — visit stakeholders individually before the meeting | Introduce a new proposal cold, in the room, expecting a live debate |
| Treat silence as thinking time, not a cue to fill the gap | Interpret a lack of objections as enthusiasm — it may just be politeness |
| Confirm who actually has authority to approve before you push for a yes | Assume the most senior person in the room is the one who decided |
| Circulate materials well in advance so people can prepare | Spring a slide deck on the room and expect instant reactions |
| Address disagreement privately, one-on-one, ahead of time | Publicly contradict a colleague in front of the group |

### USA

American meeting culture treats the room as the arena. Decisions are meant to happen there, out loud, through argument — a system built for individuals who are rewarded for having a position and defending it. Hofstede Insights scores the US at 91 on individualism against Japan's 46, and it shows up exactly here: an American who stays quiet in a meeting is read as having nothing to contribute, not as being thoughtful. Meetings tend to be shorter and more selective than their Japanese counterparts, built around the assumption that whoever wasn't essential to the decision wasn't invited in the first place.

The tradeoff is speed that can look, from the outside, like recklessness. A Stanford Graduate School of Business study on cross-cultural communication found that American decision-making favors fast, individually-owned commitments — good for iteration, bad for anyone who needed to be consulted first and wasn't. American workers over-index on "let's just try it and adjust," which plays badly in a culture where getting it wrong in public carries a heavier cost than getting it slow.

### Japan

Japanese meeting culture runs on a mechanism called nemawashi — literally "preparing the roots" — a process of informal, one-on-one consensus-building that happens entirely before the meeting most outsiders think is where the work gets done. By the time a proposal is formally presented, according to GLOBIS Insights, the real negotiation is over; the meeting exists to confirm agreement that already exists, not to build it live. Skip this step, as visiting Americans reliably do, and a room that looks unified will quietly reject the idea for the crime of having been sprung on it.

Hofstede's uncertainty avoidance score puts Japan at 92 against the US's 46, which explains a meeting style built to minimize the risk of public error: silence is deliberation, not disengagement, and Japanese negotiators have been documented tolerating silences nearly twice as long as their American counterparts before feeling the need to speak. Authority is also more diffuse than the org chart suggests — Japan Intercultural Consulting notes that in most Japanese organizations, no single person, however senior, can unilaterally commit the group. The most senior person in the room is frequently not the person who decided anything.

### The Reckoning

The two systems are, in effect, solving for opposite failure modes. American meeting culture is optimized to avoid the failure of nothing getting decided; Japanese meeting culture is optimized to avoid the failure of someone being blindsided or humiliated. Neither is irrational — they're just answering different questions about what a meeting is for, which is exactly why an American who "wins the room" in Tokyo often finds the decision quietly reversed a week later, and why a Japanese employee who did all the correct groundwork in New York finds their idea died in the room because nobody there knew the groundwork had happened.

The irony is that both cultures believe the other's meetings are theater. Americans see nemawashi as an elaborate way of avoiding a direct conversation; Japanese colleagues see American open debate as a strange willingness to negotiate in public before anyone has actually agreed on anything. Both are right, and both are describing the same thing from opposite ends.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/japanlife — A transferee from a US software firm described spending his first quarter in Tokyo "winning" every meeting with a strong pitch, only to learn weeks later that none of his proposals had moved forward — nobody had told him the actual approval happened in the pre-meetings he hadn't been invited to.*

> *r/expats — One American engineer recalled sitting through a two-hour planning meeting where nobody objected to his plan, taking the silence as buy-in, and then watching the plan quietly not get implemented — he said it took him nearly a year to understand that disagreement in Japan rarely sounds like disagreement.*

> *Quora — Someone who relocated to Tokyo for a tech role wrote that the single biggest surprise wasn't the language, it was realizing that by the time a meeting happened, the decision was usually already made — the meeting was a formality to save face for everyone involved, not a debate.*

> *HackerNews — A commenter on a software engineer's account of working in Japan noted spending roughly a third of the week in meetings, many of them "information sharing" sessions where someone read slides aloud that the room could have read themselves — a ritual, he said, not a waste, just a different kind of work.*

> *Internations Tokyo — A British project manager described the professional cost of skipping informal pre-meeting check-ins with stakeholders: her proposal, though well-argued on the day, was met with polite silence and never resurfaced, while a colleague who'd done the individual rounds first sailed through with barely a comment.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving from the US to Japan, the operating principle to internalize fast is that the real meeting happens before the meeting — invest in the one-on-one groundwork or watch your best ideas die quietly in a room full of nodding heads. If you're moving the other way, understand that Americans expect the room itself to be the place where minds change, and staying silent will be read as having nothing to say, not as diplomacy. Neither approach is more honest than the other; they've just moved the honesty to a different part of the process. My advice, for what it's worth over a drink: learn to ask, in your first week, "where does this actually get decided" — because in one country the answer is "right here," and in the other it's "already happened, you just weren't in the room."
