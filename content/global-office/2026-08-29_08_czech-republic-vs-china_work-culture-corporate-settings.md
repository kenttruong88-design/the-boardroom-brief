# One Country Clocks Out at 39 Hours a Week. The Other Invented a Hashtag for Dying at Your Desk.

## 🇨🇿 Czech Republic · 🇨🇳 China

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Prague, the average employee works about 39 hours a week and takes a lunch break that starts, with near-liturgical precision, at 11:30 a.m. In Shenzhen, until a 2021 Supreme Court ruling declared it illegal, the average ambitious employee worked 9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week, and called it "996" — a schedule so normalized it needed a shorthand. One of these countries has a labor culture optimized around the sanctity of the after-work beer. The other built an entire tech industry on the premise that beer is for people who have given up. Both, per Hofstede Insights, score as hierarchical societies uncomfortable with uncertainty. Only one of them turned that discomfort into a national holiday schedule; the other turned it into a productivity cult with a body count of burned-out twenty-somethings. Here is what a corporate transfer actually looks like on each end.

#### 🇨🇿 Czech Republic

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use formal titles and the formal "vy" until invited to switch to first names — it may take years | Assume Czech reserve is coldness; it's a communication default, not a verdict on you |
| Show up on time for the 11:30 lunch window if you want company | Schedule a working lunch and expect anyone to treat it as optional |
| Bring data and precedent to disagreements — direct, fact-based pushback is respected | Expect praise for initiative that skips the chain of approval |
| Accept that August and the December holidays are functionally dead zones for decision-making | Plan a product launch around a Czech colleague's calendar in late December |
| Join the after-work beer if invited — it's where actual trust gets built | Mistake the invitation as purely social; unresolved work friction often gets aired there |
| Ask HR directly about the onboarding plan — 76% of Czech firms have no structured integration process for foreign hires | Assume your manager will proactively explain unwritten norms |

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Let your most senior colleague speak first and sit closest to the door-facing seat in meetings | Correct or contradict a superior in front of the team, even if they're wrong |
| Build guanxi before you need it — accept the dinner invitations, remember names and favors | Treat a business dinner as optional networking rather than the actual negotiation venue |
| Frame critical feedback as a suggestion or deliver it privately | Give direct criticism in an open meeting; it causes a loss of face that's hard to undo |
| Keep WeChat notifications on outside office hours if your team does | Assume evenings and weekends are truly yours until you've confirmed local norms |
| Ask what "flexible hours" actually means at this specific company before signing | Assume the legal 40-hour week bears much resemblance to actual practice |
| Praise the team's result, not your individual contribution, when things go well | Take public credit in front of your manager's manager |

### Czech Republic

The Czech Republic runs on a work culture that would strike a Shenzhen product manager as almost suspiciously restrained. Employees on the main job clocked roughly 39.08 hours per week in 2023, per Statista's tracking of national labor data, and the OECD's broader hours-worked figures put full-time employment across member countries at an average of 1,736 annual hours — a number Czech workers hover near rather than exceed. CZSO, the Czech Statistical Office, has spent decades tracking a labor market that is stable, unionized in patches, and allergic to spectacle.

What the brochures leave out is the formality underneath the calm. Hofstede Insights scores the Czech Republic at 57 on power distance and a striking 74 on uncertainty avoidance — a society that wants rules, precision, and a clear chain of command, even while it clocks out at a civilized hour. Reporting from expats.cz on integration in Czech workplaces found that management styles still carry a top-down inheritance, with feedback culture underdeveloped and soft skills valued less than international arrivals typically expect. Formality persists too: colleagues may use the formal "vy" address for years, a linguistic distance that Anglo-Saxon transplants often misread as unfriendliness rather than default professional courtesy.

The tension is between hierarchy and hedonism, and the Czechs have made peace with it by scheduling the release valve. The lunch break is sacred and synchronized; the after-work beer is where actual disagreements get resolved, once the vy has softened into something more collegial. It's a corporate culture that respects structure enough to build a stable career inside it, and respects personal time enough not to expect you to sacrifice it for the structure.

### China

China's corporate culture, particularly in tech, spent the 2010s optimizing for the opposite trade-off. The 996 schedule — 9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week — became so entrenched at firms like Alibaba and ByteDance that Jack Ma once described it publicly as "a huge blessing." Research reporting cited by the South China Morning Post and other outlets found more than 80% of Chinese white-collar workers regularly worked overtime, and Statista figures put average urban working weeks above 48.7 hours — well past the 40-hour cap written into Chinese labor law. China's Supreme Court ruled the practice illegal in August 2021. Enforcement, by most accounts, remains selective, concentrated hardest on workers with the least leverage to say no.

Underneath the hours sits a cultural operating system that has nothing to do with clock-punching and everything to do with relationships. Guanxi — the network of reciprocal obligation — and mianzi, or face, govern nearly every interaction that matters, from who speaks first in a meeting to how a disagreement gets raised at all. Hofstede scores China at 80 on power distance and just 20 on individualism, among the most collectivist, hierarchical profiles in the dataset, and the practical result is a workplace where public criticism of a colleague is a genuine transgression, not just bad manners. Gallup's State of the Global Workplace data shows East Asia posting the highest daily-sadness readings of any world region even as engagement metrics elsewhere sag — a reminder that long hours and cultural cohesion don't automatically add up to job satisfaction. Meanwhile a countermovement has emerged among younger Chinese workers: tang ping ("lying flat") and bai lan ("let it rot"), a quiet refusal to keep feeding the machine that 996 built.

### The Reckoning

Set side by side, the irony is that the more hierarchical, uncertainty-averse culture on paper — the Czech Republic, per Hofstede's own numbers — is the one that actually clocks out. China, despite scoring lower on power distance's Western cousin (individualism) and higher on collectivism, is the one that turned collective sacrifice into a competitive sport. Rules-loving Czechs go home at a reasonable hour and settle disputes over pilsner; relationship-driven Chinese colleagues stay until 9 p.m. and settle disputes by never raising them directly at all. Structure, it turns out, is not the enemy of work-life balance. Ambiguity about what "team spirit" actually costs you is.

The other divergence worth sitting with: Europe overall reports the lowest employee engagement of any world region in Gallup's tracking, at just 12%, despite — or because of — its comparatively humane hours. China's tech sector, by contrast, built global champions on a schedule that regulators themselves now call illegal. Neither outcome should be read as an endorsement. One trades ambition for equilibrium; the other trades equilibrium for scale, and increasingly, for a generation of workers quietly opting out.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — a longtime resident described Czech offices as far less formal than expected, with 11:30 lunch treated as a fixed, almost sacred appointment that the whole floor observes at once, and after-work beers functioning as the real venue for resolving team friction.*

> *Blind — a software engineer suggested a simple diagnostic for a Chinese office's actual culture: whether local colleagues sit down and eat lunch with you in English every week. If they don't, expect isolation regardless of what the offer letter promised about "international team culture."*

> *Quora — a foreign employee at a Chinese firm recounted being invited to lavish welcome banquets and given generous relocation perks, only to realize months later that "foreigner" status meant permanent exclusion from the WeChat groups where real decisions got made.*

> *Hacker News — a commenter pushed back on the idea that 996 produces better output at all, arguing that raw hours logged say little about actual productivity, which depends far more on flat structure and continuous process improvement than on time at a desk.*

> *expats.cz reporting — recruiters and HR consultants noted that most Czech companies employing foreign staff have no structured onboarding process, leaving new arrivals to absorb unwritten hierarchy and communication norms by trial and error.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two, choose based on what kind of tired you're willing to be. The Czech Republic will ask you to master formality, patience with bureaucracy, and the social choreography of a beer you didn't necessarily want but should probably drink. China will ask you to master relationship debt, the art of the indirect no, and a definition of "flexible hours" that flexes in exactly one direction. Neither country is lying to you in its recruiting materials, exactly — they're just leaving out the part where culture and calendar are the same document.

The honest version, the one I'd give a friend two drinks in: go to Prague if you want a career that fits inside a life; go to Shenzhen if you want a life that, for a few years, becomes the career — and know in advance which trade you're actually making, because the offer letter will not spell it out for you.
