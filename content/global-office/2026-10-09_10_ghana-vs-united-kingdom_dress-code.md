**The Government That Mandated National Pride and the Country That Can't Agree on Shorts**

**🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In 2004, Ghana's president asked the entire country to wear local fabric to work every Friday, as a matter of policy. Twenty-plus years later, a fabric seller in Accra reports selling no more than three cloths on a busy day, and a dressmaker with a full order book has exactly one customer asking for Ghanaian print. Meanwhile in Britain, nobody mandated anything, and the suit simply died on its own: only one in ten UK workers still wears one to work. Two countries, two completely different relationships between government, identity, and what you're allowed to put on your body before 9 a.m. Neither outcome is what either country's planners expected.

### Ghana

The modern story of Ghanaian office dress starts with a specific policy: in November 2004, then-president John Agyekum Kufuor launched "National Friday Wear," urging civil servants and office workers to swap Western business attire for local fabrics one day a week, explicitly to celebrate African culture and revive a textile industry that imports had gutted (csmonitor.com). The scale of what the policy was fighting is stark — Ghana imports roughly $43 million a year in used clothing, more than any other African nation, while its own clothing exports (mostly socks) totaled just $4 million, and textile-sector employment collapsed from about 25,000 jobs to roughly 3,000 (csmonitor.com).

More than two decades on, the campaign's actual grip on daily life is modest and contested. In Accra's Osu district, shop owner Denis Boateng says business on local print is slow — "people come and ask, but they turn away after you tell them the price" — while fabric seller Priscilla Adom reports selling no more than three cloths on a busy day (dandc.eu). In the north, trader Rabi Tijani stocked Ghanaian prints two years ago, found no buyers, and was left in debt; student Regina Enyonam put the generational verdict on the fabric plainly: "African style is not fashionable" (dandc.eu). Against that, Akosombo Textile's Joseph Annan still calls the policy "a good call," arguing identity outranks convenience: "As a Ghanaian you need to wear our garments; it shows your identity" (dandc.eu). What survives the ambivalence is the fabric itself in everyday tailoring — Ankara and wax-print cloth remain cheap, colorful, and fast to have custom-made, even among people who'd rather buy imported ready-to-wear for their main wardrobe (talesmag.com).

### United Kingdom

Britain's dress-code story has no government campaign behind it at all — it's a slower, bottom-up collapse of formality that accelerated hard after the pandemic. A 2018 Travelodge survey of 2,000 British workers already found only one in ten still wore a suit to work, with 43% saying the suit no longer belonged in the office and roughly three-quarters citing comfort as the main driver of dressing down. Indeed's job-posting data shows what happened next: mentions of "casual dress" in UK job listings sat at just 0.2% in early 2019, climbed to over ten times that level by 2025, and have not dropped below 3% since May 2021 — with Indeed economists Callam Pickering and Jack Kennedy linking the shift directly to how the pandemic reshaped expectations around both work and appearance.

The relaxation hasn't resolved the question so much as relocated the argument. A Banana Moon survey found 53% of UK workers no longer have a formal uniform at all, yet 36% still feel judged by colleagues over what they wear, and 29% say they genuinely struggle to identify what counts as suitable office clothing without one. The geography tells its own story: London posts the lowest casual-dress mentions nationally, at 2.3%, which analysts attribute to its concentration of financial-services jobs, where formality has held on hardest. And the unwritten rules can be stubborn in ways a policy memo never addresses — an engineering-forum thread on summer dress found men debating whether shorts are ever acceptable at all, with one contributor, Arran Cameron, tracing the stigma to mid-20th-century British schoolboys who wore shorts only until secondary school, making adult men in shorts read, to some, as refusing to "grow up." Another poster, Julie, put the underlying unfairness bluntly: men get a far narrower range of acceptable office clothing than women do, and nobody's really addressed why.

### The Reckoning

Hofstede's individualism index draws the clearest line through this whole comparison: the West Africa regional score (which includes Ghana) sits at just 20, against the UK's 89 — one of the largest individualism gaps measured anywhere in this series. That gap explains why the two dress-code stories run in such different directions. Ghana's Friday Wear was a top-down, collective project — a president asking a whole nation to dress a certain way as an act of shared identity — and its central tension is that a profoundly individualist, market-driven generation keeps quietly opting out in favor of personal preference and import prices. The UK's relaxation ran the opposite way: no government ever weighed in, change arrived through millions of individual employers and employees separately deciding comfort mattered more than conformity, and the Banana Moon survey's finding that over a fifth of workers specifically value casual dress for letting them "express their personality" is about as individualist a stated motive as a culture can offer.

The irony each country should sit with: Ghana's collectivist instinct produced a policy that individuals are quietly resisting, while Britain's individualist instinct produced a free-for-all that a third of its own workers now say leaves them anxious and judged. Dressing for the group turned out to need more buy-in than a decree can manufacture. Dressing for yourself turned out to need more shared agreement than "wear whatever's comfortable" can provide.

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Accra, the practical move is to get a few things tailored in local print — not because the policy succeeded, but because the fabric is genuinely good, cheap, and fast, regardless of what the Friday-adoption statistics say about everyone else's choices. If you're moving to London, the practical move is to ask, directly and early, what your specific office actually means by "smart casual," because the national data says there's no consensus left to lean on. The thing both postings quietly teach: a dress code imposed from above and a dress code abandoned from above fail for the same reason. Nobody told enough individual people, convincingly, why the choice should matter to them personally.
