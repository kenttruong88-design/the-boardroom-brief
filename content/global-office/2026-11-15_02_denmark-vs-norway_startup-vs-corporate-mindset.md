# Denmark Funds Its Startups, While Norway Admires Them From a Well-Padded Chair

🇩🇰 Denmark · 🇳🇴 Norway
*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two neighbours, one fondness for flat hierarchies and a 3 p.m. departure, and a very different attitude to risk. According to Dealroom, Copenhagen attracted $1.6 billion in venture capital in the twelve months to the third quarter of 2026 and is home to 22 unicorns. Oslo attracted $333 million and has 12. In the third quarter alone the gap was $327 million to $20 million. If you are weighing a startup against a corporate employer in Scandinavia, the first question is which of the two countries is in the mood to fund your employer.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask a startup about runway, vesting and strike price before accepting equity | Treat equity as rent money; assume it is a nice bonus until proven otherwise |
| Use first names with everyone, including the chief executive | Wait to be invited before disagreeing with your manager in a meeting |
| Expect several rounds of interviews with the future team | Be surprised when colleagues want a say in who joins |
| Check whether the employer imposes a summer closure on your holidays | Plan a long trip in July without confirming your holiday is yours |
| Leave early when you have arrived early; the hours are counted as a whole | Assume a salaried contract means unlimited overtime |

### Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive early; offices often open between 7 and 9 and empty by 4 | Schedule a meeting for late afternoon and expect a full room |
| Build a network; startup founders often recruit through contacts before advertising | Rely on a public job posting as your only route to a startup role |
| Check that a startup's salary meets the threshold for your work permit | Assume a funded-sounding startup can sponsor a non-EEA hire |
| Bring a simple packed lunch and eat at about 11:30 | Expect a long, late lunch or an after-work drink with the whole team |
| Offer your view in review conversations, which are about your voice | Treat the review as a verdict delivered from above |

## Denmark

Denmark has built a reputation for flat structures, and it appears to have earned it. According to the World Economic Forum's 2018 Global Competitiveness Report, as cited by Entrepreneur, Denmark ranked first in the world for the flattest work hierarchy. Hofstede Insights scores Denmark at 18 on power distance versus Norway at 31, which is the kind of gap only a sociologist, or a Dane, would find dramatic. What it means in practice is that a graduate may disagree with the department head and be thanked for the contribution.

The corporate side is correspondingly civilised. The OECD reports that Danes work 1,563 hours a year against an OECD average of 1,739, and a developer who moved to Copenhagen describes a 37 hour week, flexible starts, cake on a schedule and a Friday beer. Fortune's 2025 list of the best workplaces in Europe includes Novo Nordisk, where employees reportedly use the chief executive's first name and feel no pressure to work late. The same developer warns that some employers close for the summer and require staff to take their holiday then, which is the sort of friction a corporate job likes to introduce quietly.

Flat does not mean frictionless. Entrepreneur's profile of Airtame, a Danish startup with more than 90 employees and over 40 hires in 2018, shows its chief executive preferring the word transparent to flat, running anonymous employee surveys every two weeks, and conceding that rapid growth forces a rethink of structure. The same piece warns that flat organisations can turn pseudo-flat, with informal power that breeds fear rather than openness. The evidence is self-reported by the company, but the warning is credible to anyone who has attended a meeting where nobody is in charge and everybody is waiting for someone to be.

The startup option is real, but it pays like one. Jobrise's 2026 Copenhagen salary guide puts junior software engineers at 52,000 to 58,000 euros in startups and 60,000 to 68,000 at larger companies, with startup equity described as "possible, but often not life-changing." A 2015 Copenhagen Post column from a startup employee describes the trade as late nights, extra duties outside the job description and a business model still being discovered. What Denmark offers in return is capital: startups that survive are likelier to be funded in a deep local market.

## Norway

Norway makes the case for corporate comfort with fewer apologies. Life in Norway describes a typical office day that starts between 7 and 9 and ends between 3 and 4, a lunch of about 30 minutes, a flat management structure and about five weeks of paid holiday. Fortune notes that Norway, like Denmark, has three companies in the top 100 of its Best Companies to Work For in Europe list, and ranks seventh in the World Happiness Report. The Local, reporting official wage data, puts gross monthly pay for developers at 72,320 Norwegian kroner (about 6,059 euros, 2023) against 47,748 Danish kroner (about 6,399 euros, 2022) in Denmark, though estimated take-home was about 4,040 euros in Norway and 3,550 in Denmark.

The startup scene is where Norway wobbles. According to Oslo Business Region, citing a Menon report, Oslo ranks last among the Nordic capitals for both capital invested and capital per head, and the public funds of Finland, Denmark and Sweden each received four to seven times more funding over five years than Norway's Investinor. The article calls Norway's public capital sources "the weakest in the Nordics." Life in Norway adds that startup pay is modest, workloads can be heavier than at established firms, and job security is lower because a promising company can fail quickly.

Dealroom's figures put the enterprise value of the Oslo ecosystem at $53.0 billion against $148.9 billion for Copenhagen, and Bergen, Norway's other contender, drew $40 million in the last twelve months with one unicorn. The same index still places Oslo among the top 50 of 288 startup ecosystems, so the capital is thin rather than absent. Life in Norway also warns that a startup with limited funds may not offer enough salary to support a work permit for a non-EEA applicant, which is the sort of detail that tends to appear after the removal van has been booked. Consensus is deeply embedded: employee reviews, called medarbeidersamtale, are about the employee's own views, and people outside a project can weigh in late with equal weight, which means extra revisions.

## The Reckoning

On the evidence, Denmark is the better place to bet on a startup and Norway is the better place to bet on a salary. Copenhagen has roughly five times Oslo's recent venture capital, a larger group of unicorns and a deeper investor base, which means a startup there has a better chance of surviving its second year. Norway has higher take-home pay for developers, a quieter office rhythm and a public capital base that Oslo's own investors regard as thin.

For the corporate mindset, the two are nearly interchangeable and both are excellent: short days, flat hierarchies, five weeks of holiday and an expectation that you will go home. The difference is the startup's tolerance for ambition. Denmark can afford to let an employee gamble on a company, while Norway gently suggests that the oil fund has already made the gamble on your behalf, and that you might prefer a nice permanent contract.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/devcareerseurope — paraphrased experience: A commenter describing the Norwegian tech scene said it is concentrated in a few cities, with accelerators such as StartupLab and VIS hosting small firms, and that many companies have international teams. Some recruit from abroad with relocation packages. No salary figures were offered, only a link to a Norwegian-language pay report.*

> *Blind — paraphrased experience: In a thread about working in Denmark on a 100,000 dollar salary, engineers at large US firms said Danish pay follows the local market, that a jump to American levels is unlikely, and that a rate of 50 to 55 percent tax is the price of services Americans buy privately. One said it was a decent salary, roughly a tech lead's.*

> *Hacker News — paraphrased experience: In an Ask HN thread on the European country with the highest developer salary, commenters said Denmark ranks high on wages but is expensive, with one warning about a Copenhagen housing bubble. Norway was described as average on pay, with high living costs and high taxes.*

> *Copenhagen Post — paraphrased experience: A Danish startup employee wrote that startup staff are expected to give at least 1,000 percent, which means late nights and busy weekends, and that responsibility is a double-edged sword. People who find some chaos exciting will like it, he suggested, and those who do not should consider corporate life.*

> *Mat Duggan blog — paraphrased experience: A developer who moved to Denmark said colleagues expect to help choose new hires, tech salaries are noticeably lower than in the United States before taxes, and unions publish pay benchmarks that help in negotiation. He advised finding an employer that does not enforce a summer closure.*

## Conclusion

If your temperament is entrepreneurial and your passport is flexible, Denmark has the deeper pool of capital and the more energetic startup scene, but check the equity terms and budget for a salary below the corporate rate. If you want a secure, well-paid and tidy working life, Norway rewards the corporate route, provided your startup of choice can meet the salary threshold for your permit.

In either country, the work-life balance is real. The only difference is whether you will have the capital to enjoy it.
