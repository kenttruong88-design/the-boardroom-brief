# The Eight-Week Trolleybus and the Endless Sobremesa: Making Friends in Vilnius vs. Santiago

**🇱🇹 Lithuania · 🇨🇱 Chile**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Vilnius, a stranger will not make eye contact with you on the trolleybus for roughly eight weeks. In Santiago, a stranger may invite you to Sunday asado before you have learned their surname. Neither country is being rude, and neither is being effusive for your benefit — they are simply operating on two entirely different theories of what a relationship is for, and any new arrival optimizing for "make friends quickly" will need to unlearn whichever theory they brought with them.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇱🇹 Lithuania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join a recurring activity — a padel club, a language exchange, a sauna evening — and show up weekly | Expect a single coffee to produce a friendship; consistency matters more than charm |
| Accept the initial formality (vardenis, pavardenis — first name, surname) as respect, not distance | Read reserve as unfriendliness; Lithuanians rarely perform warmth they don't feel yet |
| Let colleagues invite you into their circle on their timeline | Push for personal plans with a coworker in week one |
| Use English confidently in Vilnius tech and finance circles | Assume the same fluency holds in smaller cities or older generations |
| Bring something specific (a dish, a bottle) to a home invitation | Show up to someone's home empty-handed — it reads as careless |

#### 🇨🇱 Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say yes to the asado, la once, or the after-work drink — this is where confianza starts | Decline social invitations repeatedly and expect the relationship to develop through work alone |
| Let sobremesa run long; leaving right after the meal reads as a snub | Rush the small talk before getting to business, even when you're pressed for time |
| Learn a few names and family details — Chileans build trust personally, not just professionally | Assume friendliness in a first meeting equals a deep bond; that takes months |
| Reciprocate invitations — hosting someone back matters | Treat home invitations as purely social; being invited in is a marker of real trust |
| Expect Sunday to be genuinely off-limits for family and asado | Schedule work social events on Sunday without checking first |

---

Lithuania's reputation for reserve is not a myth so much as a design choice. Consultancy Work in Lithuania, which advises companies relocating staff to Vilnius, describes local colleagues as people who "don't perform friendliness they don't yet feel" — the initial formality is respect, not coldness. This tracks with the country's Hofstede Insights profile, which shows a relatively high score on Individualism (60) even as strong Embeddedness — the sense of being interwoven into a wider collective — persists underneath it, a combination that produces exactly the pattern new arrivals report: slow to open, durable once open. The mechanism that actually builds friendship in Vilnius is not charisma but repetition. A relocation guide for expats notes that a stranger who ignores you on public transport becomes reliably warm after roughly eight weeks of showing up to the same padel club, language table, or Saturday café. Lithuania's small, walkable capital makes this kind of repeated low-stakes contact unusually efficient once you commit to a routine — the friendship infrastructure exists, but it runs on consistency, not introductions.

Chile inverts the sequence almost completely. Business and cultural guides converge on a single concept — confianza, loosely "trust," though the English word undersells it — as the operating currency of Chilean relationships, both social and professional. Confianza is built through visible, repeated personal investment: shared meals, the ritual of sobremesa (conversation that runs well past the end of a meal, sometimes for hours), and la once, the late-afternoon light-meal-that-is-really-a-social-institution. Expat.cl's etiquette guide is blunt that an invitation into someone's home is a genuine signal of friendship, not a courtesy extended broadly. Crucially, in Chile this isn't sequestered outside the office — Commisceo Global's management guide notes that Chilean business culture explicitly expects personal connection before substantive work discussion, so the "getting to know you" phase that a Lithuanian colleague might defer to after-hours activities, a Chilean colleague may expect inside the Tuesday 10 a.m. meeting.

### The Reckoning

The counterintuitive part is that both cultures ultimately produce durable, high-trust friendships — they just gate access to them through opposite filters. Lithuania gates on time: prove you're going to keep showing up, and the relationship deepens on a predictable, almost mechanical schedule. Chile gates on disclosure: prove you're willing to be personally known — your family, your Sunday, your table — and the relationship deepens in bursts around specific rituals. A newcomer who treats Santiago the way they'd treat Vilnius (professional, patient, low-key) will read as cold and be quietly filed as someone who "isn't really trying." A newcomer who treats Vilnius the way they'd treat Santiago (immediately warm, personal, inviting) will likely make people uncomfortable rather than charmed — the OECD's cross-country data on social connection suggests Northern and Baltic European populations report meaningful daily interactions at notably lower stated frequency than Latin American populations, without this implying weaker underlying bonds, just a different bar for what counts as "meaningful contact" worth mentioning to a pollster.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A response to "How easy is it to make friends with Lithuanians" noted that Lithuanians can be critical or slow to warm to outsiders at first contact, but once trust is earned, the friendship tends to be unusually steady and low-drama compared to what the poster was used to.*

> *Quora — Someone weighing a move to Santiago wrote that Chileans can be judgmental of foreigners initially, particularly non-Europeans, but that once "you earn their love," the friendship that follows is often more loyal and less transactional than friendships they'd had back home.*

> *Expat.cl (Chile expat community) — A contributor described skipping la once with new colleagues twice in a row, assuming it was optional small talk, and then noticing a distinctly cooler, more purely transactional tone in meetings for weeks afterward — nobody said anything directly, but the invitations stopped coming.*

> *Work in Lithuania relocation guide — One expat account described the first two months in Vilnius as "socially silent," with no invitations at all, followed by an abrupt shift once they'd become a regular at a weekly sauna evening — after that point, invitations to birthdays and weekend trips started arriving unprompted.*

> *InterNations country guide (secondary reference, aggregated member commentary) — A recurring theme in InterNations' Lithuania materials is that expats who joined a hobby-based club within their first month reported feeling socially settled by month three, versus those who waited for coworkers to initiate, who often reported feeling isolated well into their second year.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving for work and building a life is part of the calculation, the practical difference is about where you spend your early social effort. In Lithuania, front-load your calendar with recurring, low-pressure activities and be patient — the payoff arrives on a schedule, roughly two months out, and it's durable once it lands. In Chile, front-load your willingness to be personally known — say yes to the asado, let the sobremesa run long, host someone back — because declining those rituals doesn't just cost you a friendship, it can quietly cost you standing at work. Neither approach is warmer than the other in the ways that matter long-term; they're just warm on different clocks. The honest thing I'd tell a friend over a drink: in Vilnius, show up enough times that you stop being a stranger; in Santiago, show up once and let them actually see you — either way, the invitation you're waiting for is already scheduled, just not on the calendar you're used to reading.
