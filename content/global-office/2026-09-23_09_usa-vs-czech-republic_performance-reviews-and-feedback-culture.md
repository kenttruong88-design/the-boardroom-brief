# The Feedback Sandwich and the Straight Face: Reviews in the USA and Czech Republic

**🇺🇸 USA · 🇨🇿 Czech Republic**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

![USA vs Czech Republic — Performance reviews and feedback culture](https://res.cloudinary.com/dnpcbx89m/image/upload/c_fill,w_1200,h_630/boardroom-brief/global-office/2026-09-23-09-usa-vs-czech-republic-performance-reviews-and-feedback-cu-hero)
*Photo: cottonbro studio via Pexels*

An American manager will tell you three good things before the one bad thing, and everyone in the room understands the ritual well enough to skip straight to listening for the "but." A Czech manager will tell you the bad thing directly, without preamble, and then quietly not follow up on whether you did anything about it — not from cruelty, but from a workplace culture where nearly half of employees don't trust that raising an issue changes anything, in either direction. Both countries have real feedback cultures. Neither one's produces employees who feel especially well-served by the process — the mechanisms just fail in opposite ways.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask for feedback proactively between formal reviews — 80% of employees say they prefer immediate feedback over annual cycles, and managers respond well to the ask | Assume the annual review captures the real picture — 71% of companies still run them, but only 8% of companies believe the process is highly effective |
| Learn to hear the "compliment sandwich" for what it is — genuine praise plus a real, actionable critique, not just politeness | Take positive framing at face value without probing for the specific, sometimes uncomfortable point underneath it |
| Push for concrete, written documentation of feedback if a review feels vague — it protects you and clarifies expectations | Expect universal practice — American companies vary hugely, from continuous-feedback tech cultures to old-school annual paperwork |
| Use 360-degree feedback processes actively if your company has them — genuinely useful information often comes from peers, not just your manager | Assume every manager wants unfiltered feedback upward — only 24% of US employees report formally rating their own manager's performance |
| Advocate directly for your own accomplishments in review cycles — self-advocacy is expected and rewarded, not seen as immodest | Wait passively for recognition to surface on its own — American review culture generally rewards those who narrate their own impact clearly |

#### 🇨🇿 Czech Republic

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect direct, fact-focused feedback without much cushioning — it's not meant as harsh, it's simply the local register | Mistake directness for disrespect, and don't respond to critical feedback with visible defensiveness — respond to the facts, not the delivery |
| Respect hierarchy when receiving or giving feedback — a calm, competent, dependable leader earns more real trust than a charismatic one | Assume raising a concern with a boss will change much — nearly half of Czech employees avoid giving critical feedback upward, partly from historical mistrust of whether it matters |
| Ask for written or smaller-group feedback settings if you want more candor — Czech employees reportedly open up more in those formats than in large or high-visibility settings | Expect enthusiastic engagement with formal review processes — a striking 36% of employees say their company never seeks their feedback at all |
| Focus your own feedback, when giving it, on specific evidence and practical improvements rather than tone or personality | Assume the legacy of hierarchical, mistake-averse communist-era management has fully disappeared — its residue still shapes how comfortable people are challenging authority |
| Recognize that most employees genuinely want more systematic feedback (95% say it matters) even amid low trust that it'll be acted on — asking sincerely can still land well | Confuse Czech reserve about upward feedback with disengagement from the work itself — the two are frequently unrelated |

### USA

American performance review culture is in visible, well-documented crisis, at least by its own internal metrics. Despite 71 percent of companies still running formal annual reviews, satisfaction with the process is remarkably low: just 5 percent of HR leaders report being satisfied with their current performance management system, and only 8 percent of companies believe the process is highly effective at driving business value. The gap between stated preference and practice is stark — 80 percent of employees say they'd prefer immediate, ongoing feedback over annual cycles, and firms with genuinely strong feedback cultures report being 39 percent more effective at attracting talent and 44 percent better at retention, according to industry benchmarking. Yet the annual cycle persists at most companies, partly from institutional inertia and partly because managers already spend a documented 210 hours a year on performance-related activities — a real time cost that discourages more frequent formal touchpoints even as informal, continuous feedback is what employees actually want.

The historical extreme version of American review culture — stack ranking, popularized by GE's Jack Welch in the 1980s and adopted by roughly 42 percent of large US companies at its 2000s peak — has fallen sharply out of favor, with Amazon publicly dropping it in 2016 in favor of a "radically simplified" strengths-focused process. What remains distinctive about American feedback culture, even post-stack-ranking, is its emphasis on self-advocacy: employees are broadly expected to narrate their own accomplishments actively during review cycles rather than assume visibility will happen organically, a norm that surprises hires from more reticence-favoring cultures.

### Czech Republic

Czech feedback culture presents an instructive contradiction: genuinely direct in content, genuinely reluctant in delivery upward. Business etiquette guides describe Czech feedback as typically blunt and fact-focused, without the softening phrases common in American communication — when a Czech manager tells you something is wrong, expect it stated plainly. Yet that same directness doesn't flow symmetrically: reporting from Prague-based expat publication Expats.cz found that nearly half of Czech employees avoid giving their own bosses critical feedback directly, driven by both fear of backlash and a documented belief that feedback simply doesn't change anything — a dynamic explicitly traced to the legacy of communist-era management, where hierarchical, top-down authority and mistake-avoidance took precedence over open collaboration.

Hofstede data helps explain part of this: the Czech Republic scores notably high on uncertainty avoidance (74), consistent with a culture that adapts to change readily in practice but remains institutionally wary of the unknown — including, plausibly, the unpredictable consequences of challenging a superior. The engagement numbers are sobering regardless of cause: 36 percent of Czech employees report their company never solicits feedback from them at all, even though a striking 95 percent say they believe systematic feedback collection matters in the current economic climate. The desire for a functioning feedback culture is clearly present; the trust that the existing mechanisms will honor it is not.

### The Reckoning

The core irony: American feedback culture is stated to be continuous and is functionally still mostly annual and unsatisfying by its own participants' account; Czech feedback culture is genuinely direct and fact-based when it happens, but structurally suppressed from flowing upward at all. Americans get feedback wrapped in performance and positivity that can obscure the real point; Czechs get feedback stated plainly but withhold their own honest input from the same exchange, out of a reasonable, historically grounded skepticism that it will matter.

The practical implication for a newcomer moving between them: in the US, learn to decode softened language for its real substance and be prepared to advocate loudly for your own visibility, because the system rewards self-narration. In Czechia, learn to receive direct criticism at face value without defensiveness, and understand that your own silence about upward concerns will likely be read as normal rather than as disengagement — the culture doesn't expect you to speak up any more than it expects your Czech colleagues to.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering a question about whether performance reviews are effective, one respondent estimated that roughly 70% of American reviews "never got done or were filed" in any meaningful way at their past employers, describing the ritual as more procedural than substantive at several companies they'd worked for.*

> *Blind — A post titled "stack ranking is not performance management" argued that even years after most major US tech firms formally dropped forced-ranking systems, informal peer-comparison pressure persisted at several companies, with promotion decisions still quietly influenced by relative rather than absolute performance.*

> *Expats.cz (Prague-based expat news outlet) — Reporting on Czech workplace confrontation found that employees frequently cited a specific, concrete belief — that raising an issue with a manager simply wouldn't change the outcome — as a bigger deterrent to upward feedback than fear of retaliation itself.*

> *Quora — Someone asking how feedback culture shapes broader workplace culture noted that feedback landing well depended less on the review format and more on whether the person delivering it was someone the recipient already respected — a dynamic they felt mattered more in hierarchical cultures than flat ones.*

> *Culture Amp's Czech Republic workplace insights report (January 2026) found a disconnect between how much Czech employees say they value systematic feedback (95%) and how often they report actually receiving it — a gap the report attributed partly to inherited management habits still working their way out of the system.*

### Conclusion

If you're navigating either system, the practical move is to actively compensate for what each one structurally under-delivers. In the US, seek out informal, frequent feedback yourself rather than waiting for the annual cycle to tell you something useful — the data says most employees want this anyway, so asking rarely reads as unusual. In Czechia, take direct criticism as useful information rather than personal affront, and don't assume your Czech colleagues' silence about their own concerns means they have none. Priya's line for a friend navigating either system: in America, ask for the feedback nobody's volunteering; in Czechia, believe the feedback you're actually given, because it's rarely softened for your comfort.
