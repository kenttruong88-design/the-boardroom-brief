# Albania Hands You a Mentor. The Netherlands Hands You a Calendar Invite and Wishes You Luck.

🇦🇱 Albania · 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Tirana, a new hire's first week is typically spent trailing a senior colleague like a well-dressed shadow, absorbing the unwritten rules of the office by osmosis and the occasional direct order. In Amsterdam, a new hire's first week is typically spent locating the office kitchen unassisted, because nobody assumed they would need help finding it, or much else. Both approaches produce functioning employees eventually. Only one of them produces an employee who has cried quietly in a stairwell by Wednesday.

## 🇦🇱 Albania: Do's & Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat your first manager as a mentor by default, not an optional extra | Expect a written onboarding plan, a buddy system, or a checklist — ask for one and you will get a kind, confused look |
| Learn the informal hierarchy fast: who actually makes decisions, versus whose title says they do | Correct a senior colleague's work in front of others, however gently, however correct you are |
| Build a personal relationship with your boss — trust here is relational, not procedural | Assume competence will be judged purely on output; loyalty and deference count for a great deal |
| Ask questions privately, after observing for a while | Interrupt a superior mid-explanation to clarify, even if you are lost |
| Accept that "training" often means watching, not a structured course | Expect formal mentorship programs; where they exist, they are usually imported by a multinational employer, not local practice |
| Show up early and stay visibly present | Leave the moment your contracted hours end, especially in your first months |

## 🇳🇱 Netherlands: Do's & Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask direct, specific questions the moment you are confused — silence is read as disinterest, not politeness | Wait to be told what to do; initiative is assumed, not rewarded as a bonus trait |
| Expect blunt, immediate feedback on your work, often within days of starting | Take direct criticism personally; it is considered a professional courtesy, not an attack |
| Push back on a manager's idea in a meeting if you disagree — it is expected, even from the newest person in the room | Confuse the lack of hand-holding for a lack of care; structure exists, it's just self-service |
| Use your onboarding buddy (if assigned) for logistics, not emotional reassurance | Expect a hierarchy to lean on; flat structures mean your manager is a coordinator, not a protector |
| Read the handbook, the Slack channels, and the wiki before asking what's already documented | Assume someone will check in on you if you go quiet; in a low-context culture, no news is just no news |
| Treat the coffee corner as a genuine, if brief, networking opportunity | Skip it reflexively; informal cohesion still happens, just in short, efficient bursts |

## Albania: Mentorship as a Personal Relationship, Not a Program

Formal onboarding infrastructure in Albania is, by most honest accounts, thin. The OECD, in a report summarized by Euronews Albania, found workforce skills levels running well below EU standards, a gap the report ties partly to how little structured on-the-job training actually happens outside multinational subsidiaries and the public sector's better-funded corners. The European Training Foundation's statistical profile of Albania tells a similar story in dryer language: participation in continuing training remains low relative to EU peers, and employers report mismatches between what new hires know and what the job requires — a gap that, in practice, gets closed informally, by a manager deciding to teach someone personally, or not.

What fills the vacuum where a training department would otherwise sit is relationship. Albanian workplaces run on what Hofstede Insights' country comparison tool would categorize as a higher-power-distance, more collectivist culture than most of Western Europe — deference to seniority is default, and learning the job is inseparable from learning your place within a social structure, not a syllabus. A new hire's progress depends enormously on whether a senior colleague takes a personal liking to them, in which case mentorship can be extraordinarily generous — years of patient correction, networking introductions, even personal favors — and if they don't, in which case the new hire is mostly on their own, reading the room for clues nobody will spell out. This is, depending on the employee, either the warmest onboarding experience of their career or the most opaque.

This also means mentorship in Albania rarely ends when the formal training period does. It is common for a junior employee to stay attached to the same senior colleague for years, consulting them on decisions well outside the original job description, long after any Western HR department would have declared the relationship "onboarded" and moved on. The upside is a depth of institutional knowledge transfer that no checklist can replicate; the downside, as the ETF's employer surveys repeatedly note, is that when that one relationship is the entire training infrastructure, losing the mentor — to emigration, retirement, or simply a bad month — can leave a junior employee stranded with no backup system to fall back on.

## The Netherlands: Onboarding by Spreadsheet, Mentorship by Appointment

The Dutch version is better documented and considerably less personal. NL Times, reporting on national labor-market data, notes that roughly half of Dutch employees receive additional training during their careers — a figure consistent with the Cedefop/Eurostat Adult Education Survey, which places the Netherlands among the EU's higher performers for adult participation in job-related learning within any given year. Onboarding here tends to arrive as a structured, time-boxed process: a buddy, a checklist, a 30-60-90-day plan, a performance conversation scheduled almost before the new hire has found the printer.

What makes it feel cold to outsiders isn't the absence of structure — it's the absence of hovering. Dutch workplace culture runs on direct, low-context communication and famously flat hierarchy; a new hire is expected to ask for what they need, say when something doesn't make sense, and disagree with a manager in a meeting without anyone treating it as insubordination. Mentorship, where it's offered formally, functions more like a scheduled service than a relationship: useful, time-limited, and not meant to replace the new hire's own initiative. For someone arriving from a higher-context culture, this reads as abandonment. For a Dutch colleague, it reads as respect.

The documentation-over-relationship approach scales well, which is presumably why so many multinationals run their European onboarding playbooks out of Amsterdam or Rotterdam offices in the first place. A new hire who reads the wiki gets the same quality of answer as one who corners a senior manager at the coffee machine, which is precisely the point: knowledge is meant to live in systems, not in any one person's head, mentors included. The trade-off, consistently reported by newcomers regardless of seniority, is that the social integration Dutch companies formally delegate to a "buddy" rarely compensates for the informal mentorship a hierarchical culture provides for free. Competence transfers efficiently. Belonging is left as an exercise for the new hire.

## The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the real divide isn't about how much either country invests in new employees — it's about who is expected to do the emotional labor of figuring things out. Albania outsources the burden to personal relationships and hopes they're good ones; the Netherlands outsources it to the individual and assumes they're capable adults who will ask. Neither produces better-trained employees in any measurable sense documented by the OECD or Eurostat. They produce differently anxious ones.

The honest advice for anyone relocating between the two: coming from Albania to the Netherlands, over-communicate, because your new colleagues will not read your silence as modesty. Coming from the Netherlands to Albania, under-correct, because your new colleagues will not read your directness as helpfulness. Everyone involved will eventually adjust. Nobody will tell you that directly, in either country, which is itself rather the point.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum, Netherlands thread — paraphrased experience: a newcomer describing their first month at a Dutch employer noted that nobody walked them through internal processes step by step; the expectation, made explicit by a manager, was that questions get asked out loud rather than puzzled over quietly, and that this was intended as a kindness, not a test.*

> *Reddit, r/Netherlands (archived discussion) — paraphrased experience: a thread on starting a new job described the first week as largely self-directed, with documentation available but rarely proactively explained, and feedback on early mistakes arriving fast, specific, and without any cushioning small talk.*

> *Expat.com forum, Albania thread — paraphrased experience: a foreign business owner described learning, after some friction, that correcting staff errors publicly — even factually, even kindly — caused far more damage to working relationships than simply raising it privately days later, because visible correction read as a loss of face rather than feedback.*

> *Citizens.al human-interest profile — paraphrased experience: a young professional who returned to Albania after studying abroad described re-adjusting to a workplace where her new role's actual responsibilities were taught almost entirely through watching a more senior colleague handle it first, with no equivalent to the structured training she'd had access to overseas.*

> *Glassdoor review, Netherlands-based technology employer — paraphrased experience: a new hire praised a well-run onboarding process with an assigned buddy and a documented first-90-days plan, while noting that beyond the official schedule, nobody was going to seek them out socially — that part was left entirely up to the new employee.*

## The Bottom Line

Neither system is broken, exactly; they're just optimized for different failure modes. Albania's informal, relationship-driven mentorship produces employees who are deeply loyal to whoever trained them and occasionally undertrained in anything that colleague didn't personally know. The Netherlands' documented, low-touch onboarding produces employees who are technically well-resourced and occasionally lonely for the first several months, because competence was never in question, only companionship.

If you're moving between the two, pack accordingly: patience and a thicker skin for Tirana, a louder voice and lower expectations of small talk for Amsterdam. Mentorship, in both places, turns out to be real — it's just unclear in advance who's offering it, and whether you're supposed to ask.
