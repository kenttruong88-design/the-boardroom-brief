# Seventeen Meetings and a Long Lunch: Mexico and the USA Disagree About What a Meeting Is For

🇲🇽 Mexico · 🇺🇸 USA

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In a survey analysed by organisational scientist Steven Rogelberg, American white-collar workers averaged more than 17 meetings a week, about 3.5 a day, and judged roughly 30 percent of them unnecessary. In Mexico, according to the US government's own commercial guide, a business discussion may not begin until family and recent events have been properly covered, and lunch is a serious instrument of commerce. The two countries share a border, a trade agreement and a belief that the other one wastes time. Both are correct, in different units.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Request meetings in writing two to four weeks ahead and reconfirm a week before and on the day | Assume a calendar invitation sent that morning will produce a quorum |
| Spend the opening minutes on family, recent events and general conversation | Open with the agenda and a request for a decision |
| Address senior people by title and surname until they invite you to use their first name | Switch to the informal tú before a counterpart does |
| Get agreements in writing and follow up, since a polite yes can be reversed later | Treat a courteous maybe as a commitment |
| Keep email formal and use WhatsApp for quick, informal exchanges | Send a curt email and wonder why the reply is cool |
| Be on time yourself and be prepared to wait | Take a late start personally or comment on it aloud |

### USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive on time and state the purpose in the first minute | Expect the relationship to carry the meeting while the agenda waits |
| Say plainly what you think and what you need from the group | Rely on a hint, on the assumption that colleagues will decode it |
| Send an agenda and a follow-up note of decisions and owners | Leave a meeting without naming who does what |
| Decline meetings with no clear purpose, and propose an email instead | Accept every invitation as a sign of status |
| Keep small talk brief and let the agenda set the pace | Linger over lunch when a calendar block follows it |
| Expect decisions to arrive in the final minutes of the hour | Schedule the real decision for a later meeting without saying so |

## Mexico: Lunch Is the Agenda

The US International Trade Administration's guide to Mexico is unusually specific about time. Punctuality, it says, is the norm for business and government appointments, though social events may start up to an hour late. Offices may open around ten and run until eight, and it advises against meetings between two and four in the afternoon unless they are lunch meetings, which may begin around two or three and last two hours or more. Mexico News Daily adds that meetings should be requested two to four weeks ahead, that lateness of up to 30 minutes is not unusual, and that the conversation after a meal even has its own name, sobremesa. It also notes that hosts often offer to pay the bill and that businesses usually close for part of Friday afternoon, which is useful to know before proposing a Friday afternoon call. MIT's guide adds that breakfast is the preferred setting for business, which gives the early riser a small advantage.

Communication is where the visitor is most exposed, and email follows the same logic: the trade guide advises mirroring the formality of Mexican correspondence and using WhatsApp for quick, informal messages. The trade guide states that Mexicans emphasise tactful and indirect phrasing and that, therefore, yes does not always mean yes. The MIT MISTI guide describes the same pattern from the management side: a colleague who disagrees may say maybe, agreements arrive in several small steps, and a practice called dar largas, the polite deferral, is a common way to stall. It advises getting agreements in writing, which is the closest the literature comes to a universal rule, and warns that pulling rank or criticising someone in the room can seriously damage a meeting.

The hierarchy beneath this is real. A Wharton article from 2005, so a dated source, quoted academics describing communication that flows from the top down and a cultural expectation of avoiding conflict with superiors. Mexico News Daily offers a modern echo: it advises including an executive on your own team for decision-making meetings, and notes that negotiations are often lengthy with flexible deadlines, while the trade guide calls overly aggressive negotiating rude. A visitor who arrives with a firm deadline and a junior colleague is bringing two liabilities. That picture is almost certainly softer today, but it explains why the senior person's presence in a meeting changes what anyone else says. ## USA: Meetings as a Way of Life

The American problem is volume. The Rogelberg analysis, produced with the meeting-transcription company Otter.ai from more than 600 white-collar respondents, reported nearly six hours a week spent in meetings the workers considered unnecessary. The sample leaned senior, with a median salary of $185,000, and the sponsor sells meeting software, so the numbers should be read as indicative rather than definitive. They are nonetheless consistent with what anyone who has worked in an American office will recall. Three and a half meetings a day leaves a limited window for the work the meetings are about, and the survey's own arithmetic implies that more than five of the 17 weekly meetings could have been an email, a document, or silence. The American instinct is to treat this as a scheduling defect. The Mexican instinct, one suspects, would be to treat it as a failure to have lunch.

The style inside the room is the opposite of the Mexican one. The expectation is that the purpose is stated early, the speaker is direct, and the hour is used. A Hacker News discussion of a proposal to begin meetings five minutes past the hour, which drew 227 comments, shows how seriously the problem is taken. Commenters observed that meetings tend to fill the available time, that final decisions often arrive in the last few minutes, and that senior managers with full calendars tend to leave on time while individual contributors stay to sort out details. One commenter described a manager who used a kitchen timer to hold meetings to 22 minutes and felt productivity improved, an experiment few Mexican hosts would be tempted to repeat over a two-hour lunch. The cultural assumption is that the meeting is a decision-making device, which is why so many people leave one feeling that it was not.

## The Reckoning

The clearest difference is what each country treats as the work. In the United States, the work is the decision, and the relationship is a by-product. In Mexico, the relationship is the work, and the decision is what the relationship eventually produces. Each system fails in a recognisable way. American meetings generate decisions that nobody supports, and Mexican meetings generate warmth that nobody can calendar. The hours data does not settle the argument either: the OECD figures reported by Visual Capitalist put average annual hours at 2,128 in Mexico and 1,791 in the United States for 2021, so the country with the longer lunches also works longer. Slow meetings, it seems, are not a sign of idleness, and fast ones are not a sign of efficiency; the American calendar is merely better at looking busy in advance.

For an American moving south, the adjustments are mostly about sequencing. Build the relationship first, treat the first conversation as an investment, read hedging as information, and put the real agreement in an email. For a Mexican moving north, the adjustments run the other way: say the point early, accept that bluntness is not hostility, and do not mistake a crowded calendar for a sign that anyone is in charge.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Business Insider, an American expat in Mexico said that blunt talk she was used to can come across as rude, that people rarely refuse outright, and that a yoga class organiser spent roughly 160 words establishing that the group needed to choose a breakfast spot.*

> *Business Insider, the same writer described a nonprofit director arriving about 30 minutes late to their meeting, with staff promising she would return ahorita, and concluded that lateness should not be taken personally.*

> *Christian Science Monitor, an American in Monterrey said she arrived 20 minutes late to a lunch meeting and found her contact nearly an hour behind her, after misreading ahorita as a short wait rather than a possibly long one.*

> *VegOut Magazine, an American who spent a year in Mexico said the writer began by eating a desk salad while colleagues took long midday meals, then joined them and found afternoons more productive and stress lower once he stopped fighting the looser schedule.*

> *Hacker News, commenters in a thread about starting meetings five minutes past the hour said the real fix is fewer meetings, with an agenda and a stated decision, and that a five-past policy tends to drift to ten-past once everyone knows about it.*

## Conclusion

Neither country has solved the meeting. The United States has the better instinct for stating a purpose and the worse instinct for holding too many gatherings to pursue it. Mexico has the better instinct for building trust and the worse reputation for turning that trust into a firm commitment on schedule. A newcomer should expect to unlearn one habit, adopt one, and discover that the other country has been wrong about the first one all along.

The practical rule is to match the local sequence: relationship first in Mexico, agenda first in the United States, and a written summary in both. If your calendar says seventeen meetings and your results say two, the problem is probably not cultural.
