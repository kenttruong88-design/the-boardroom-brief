# The Ten-Dollar Promise and the Irish Invoice: Raising Children in Canada or Ireland

🇨🇦 Canada · 🇮🇪 Ireland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Canada promised parents childcare at ten dollars a day, and in a handful of provinces something close to it arrived. Ireland's parents, according to the OECD, handed over 22% of average earnings to childcare in 2023, against an OECD average of 12%. Both countries are English-speaking, courteous and reliably damp, which makes the comparison tempting and the fine print expensive. This guide covers what a household pays to live, mind children and stay well, and who sends the invoice.

## Do's & Don'ts

### Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check the provincial fee for your exact city before accepting a job, because the ten-dollar figure varies enormously by province | Assume the national headline applies in Toronto or Vancouver |
| Join childcare waiting lists as early as you can, ideally before you arrive | Plan to find a space the week you land |
| Ask whether your employer plan covers prescriptions, dental and physiotherapy | Treat provincial health insurance as covering everything |
| Apply for the Canada Child Benefit as soon as you are eligible | Leave federal money unclaimed out of politeness |
| Budget for provincial sales tax and tipping at restaurants | Forget that quoted prices often exclude tax |

### Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with a GP and ask about fees before the first visit | Expect a free doctor without a medical card or GP visit card |
| Apply for the National Childcare Scheme subsidy and the free preschool hours for ages two to five | Assume the subsidy covers the fee |
| Compare private health insurers every year, since premiums keep rising | Buy the cheapest plan without reading what it excludes |
| Join creche waiting lists while you are still house hunting | Sign a lease before knowing where childcare is available |
| Budget rent as the main event, particularly in Dublin | Trust a salary figure without the postcode attached |

## Canada: Ten Dollars a Day, Where Available

Canada's headline policy is its child care programme, and the headline is partly true. The Canadian Centre for Policy Alternatives' 2026 survey of fees found median base fees of $9.65 a day in Quebec and $10 in several other jurisdictions, but $22 across Ontario cities and, for infants in British Columbia cities, between $28 and $52. Infant fees come in at $10 or less in only 11 of the 35 cities surveyed, and the report notes that wait lists abound. The ten-dollar promise is a regional specialty, like poutine, though harder to obtain. The same report estimates the programme saves families with an infant more than $1,800 a month in Toronto and $500 to $1,000 in most other cities, but only $300 to $500 in British Columbia, where providers still set their own market fees and about 12% of full-time spaces are at ten dollars. The saving is real. It is simply distributed like the weather.

Beyond the daycare door, Ottawa pays parents directly. The federal government says the Canada Child Benefit reaches up to $8,157 a year for each child under six from July 2026, income-tested and indexed. Doctor visits are funded through the provinces, which spares newcomers the GP bill, though not the pharmacy. Statistics Canada, using 2019 survey data (old, and flagged as such), found about 80% of Canadians held some drug insurance, mostly through employers, but only around 68% of recent immigrants and 62% of the self-employed. A newcomer on a short contract is therefore exactly the person the system forgets to mention.

On general costs, livingcost.org, a crowdsourced site that deserves appropriate scepticism, puts Canada's cost of living about 27% below Ireland's, with a single person's rent at 1,185 against 1,825 in the site's currency. Its figures average 153 Canadian cities against twelve Irish ones, so a Toronto or Vancouver renter should expect something closer to Dublin than the average implies.

## Ireland: The Subsidy Helps the Way an Umbrella Helps in Galway

Ireland does childcare the other way round. The OECD's country note finds provision almost entirely private, with 99.1% of under-threes in private settings in 2022, and a universal subsidy that rose from €0.50 an hour in 2019 to €2.14 in September 2024. TheJournal.ie reported in October 2025 that parents pay about €190 a week on average, over €200 in Dublin, while Pobal counts roughly 77,000 children on waiting lists. The subsidy, for up to 45 hours a week, is worth around €5,000 a year, which is useful, and not the same as affordable. The same reporting records that more than 50 providers withdrew from the Core Funding scheme, leaving some parents facing weekly increases often above €70. The OECD adds a mitigating note: Ireland has introduced fee control mechanisms and a free preschool offer of 15 hours a week for ages two to five, so the direction of travel is better than the destination.

Health is the second invoice. The Department of Health reports that in 2024 only 29% of the population held a medical card and 13.4% a GP visit card, so most adults pay at the surgery door; a French expat blogging from Dublin put a visit at €60 to €70 in 2022. Perhaps unsurprisingly, the Health Insurance Authority, as reported by the Irish Examiner and The Irish Times in June 2026, counts about 46% of the population on private insurance. Average premiums rose 10.6% in 2025, and over 70% of insured people were on plans with restricted orthopaedic cover. Irish consumers are paying more for less, a tradition older than the state. For an expatriate arriving from a country with free primary care, this is the line item the relocation spreadsheet most often forgets, usually because the spreadsheet was written by someone with a medical card.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, Canada offers the better design and Ireland the better pay packet. Livingcost.org estimates monthly after-tax salary at 3,931 in Ireland against 3,044 in Canada, but also monthly living costs of 2,744 against 2,005 for a single person, so the Irish advantage largely evaporates in the landlord's account. For a family, the gap tilts further towards Canada if the family lands in one of the provinces that actually delivers ten-dollar childcare, and towards Ireland if both parents are high earners in tech who can absorb the nursery bill.

Hofstede Insights offers a country comparison tool for readers who want the cultural dimensions, but its scores could not be retrieved for this piece, so none are quoted here. What can be said is that both countries prize politeness, and both deliver bad news about fees in the same apologetic tone. The real difference is where the state sits in the household budget. Canada intervenes at the point of the daycare fee and the monthly cheque, imperfectly and unevenly. Ireland intervenes at the point of the subsidy and then lets the market do the rest, with predictable consequences for anyone with a child under three. A 2022 Irish Times account by a Wicklow public sector parent shows the mechanism: her subsidy shrank as her pay rose past the income threshold, and her monthly bill climbed to about €1,600 for three children, which she said made working feel optional. Two years of provincial tinkering in Canada and subsidy tinkering in Ireland have produced the same lesson in two accents, which is that the sticker price is a negotiation with your postcode.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/askTO, paraphrased experience: a Dublin resident thinking about Toronto was told by the top reply not to move to save money, because Toronto is expensive too, though one commenter who moved in 2018 said they had never regretted it.*

> *Blind, paraphrased experience: a Microsoft employee in Dublin on a large package complained about Irish healthcare, rural public transport and rain, and replies mostly advised taking the Irish passport first, with one saying Canadian healthcare has problems similar to the NHS.*

> *The Irish Times, paraphrased experience: a Wicklow public sector mother of three said her childcare bill had risen to about 1,600 a month, and that after-school care was so costly it made more sense for her not to work.*

> *The Irish Times Abroad, paraphrased experience: an Irish engineer in Vancouver found rent roughly comparable to Dublin city and groceries manageable, said Ireland keeps a bit of an edge on pay, and felt the lifestyle outweighed it.*

> *Nina Out and About blog, paraphrased experience: a French expat in Dublin described rents of at least 1,600 for a decent one-bedroom, a GP visit of 60 to 70, and a public system with long specialist waits that pushes people to private insurance.*

## Conclusion

For a family with young children, the question is not which country is cheaper but which province or city you will actually live in. Canada's best childcare is excellent value and its worst is merely Canadian prices; Ireland's best is a good subsidy attached to an expensive market. Budget for rent, childcare and health insurance before you accept a salary, and get the employer plan in writing, including the prescription cover. If you have a child under three, ask the prospective employer whether it offers any childcare support, and ask a local colleague what they actually pay, since official averages hide the capital cities. Check the date on every figure you are shown, because several of the sources behind this guide are a few years old.

Neither country will mislead you outright. They will simply let you discover the cost of a nursery place at the speed of a polite email, which is the national communication style on both sides of the Atlantic.
