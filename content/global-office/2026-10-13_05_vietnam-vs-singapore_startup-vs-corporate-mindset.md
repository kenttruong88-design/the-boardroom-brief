**Six Unicorns, Ninety Minutes Late, and Nobody's Fault**

🇻🇳 Vietnam · 🇸🇬 Singapore

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Ho Chi Minh City, a seed-stage founder will ship a broken feature on purpose just to see what breaks, then fix it live over coffee. In Singapore, that same feature sits in a review queue behind three sign-offs — not because anyone's lazy, but because the economy runs on the promise that nothing here breaks. Two Southeast Asian hubs, decades of divergent bets on what "professional" means, and one very different answer to what happens when you miss a deadline.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect titles to mean less than output — a 24-year-old engineer can outrank a 40-year-old on a given project | Assume seniority by age or title the way you might elsewhere |
| Treat a missed deadline as a data point, not a crisis — iterate and move | Expect the kind of formal post-mortem a corporate employer would demand |
| Show up ready to learn the tooling fast — hackathon culture and self-taught skill-stacking are the norm | Expect long onboarding programs or structured training tracks |
| Build relationships with your local team lead — they're often the actual bridge across culture and holidays | Rely purely on Slack messages to get buy-in from a Vietnamese team |
| Budget for real volatility — funding booms and corrections happen fast here | Assume the runway or roadmap you signed up for is fixed |

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect "ASAP" to mean ASAP, even without a hard deadline behind it | Assume urgency implies an actual crisis — it's often just the house style |
| Wait to be asked before offering an unsolicited opinion in a meeting | Jump in and challenge a senior colleague's framing unprompted |
| Clarify who reviews what, and how long sign-off actually takes | Assume a decision is final until it's cleared every layer |
| Plan your socializing — coffee or dinner invites are usually scheduled, not spontaneous | Expect after-work drinks to materialize organically the way they might in Sydney or London |
| Research which parent company's culture you're actually inheriting — Chinese-owned, Southeast Asian, or Western MNC all run differently | Assume "Singapore office" tells you anything about hours or pace on its own |

Vietnam's startup economy is no longer a rounding error: Tracxn counts more than 5,500 startups founded in the country, over 4,100 still active, and $3.2 billion raised all-time — nearly three-quarters of it in just the last five years. Six companies have reached unicorn status (Tiki, MoMo, Sky Mavis, VNPAY, VNLIFE, and VNG), and more than 290 venture firms have put money in. The talent pipeline behind it is large: over a million IT professionals, more than 50,000 new IT graduates a year, and a population where more than half is under 35. That combination — young, technical, underpriced relative to Western salaries by 50 to 70 percent — is exactly why global companies keep opening engineering outposts in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City rather than just hiring remote contractors.

What that funding and talent produces, culturally, is a workplace where hierarchy exists on paper but bends constantly in practice. Hofstede Insights scores Vietnam at 70 on Power Distance — hierarchy is real and accepted — but only 30 on Uncertainty Avoidance, which tracks with the flexible, build-fast-and-fix-later instinct founders describe. Pair that with an Individualism score of just 20, deeply collectivist, and you get a specific flavor of startup energy: fast and informal, but still oriented around group loyalty and the local team lead rather than the lone-genius-founder myth Silicon Valley exports. A widely shared Reddit account from an Indian traveler in r/IndianWorkplace, describing everyday business culture rather than startups specifically, captured an adjacent trait that shows up inside companies too: a near-obsessive respect for posted hours. Shops open at 8 and close near 10, staff take their midday break on schedule no matter who's asking, and one restaurant owner declined to serve the group during his workers' nap window even for triple the price. That's not rigidity for its own sake — it's a culture that treats the schedule you agreed to as more sacred than the one a client wishes you'd kept.

Singapore runs on the opposite logic: get the structure right, and speed follows. The Gallup and Singapore Institute of Directors' 2026 Workplace Report puts a number on what that logic costs: only 14 percent of the workforce is actively engaged at work, a figure barely moved since 2019 and now estimated to cost the economy roughly US$73.6 billion a year in lost productivity. Workers under 35 are hit hardest — 10 percent engaged versus 16 percent among older colleagues, with 53 percent reporting daily stress versus 37 percent for the over-35 set. Gallup attributes 70 percent of the variance in team engagement to the quality of the direct manager, and the report notes Singaporean firms pick the right manager only 18 percent of the time. Hofstede's numbers add texture: Power Distance of 74 (higher than Vietnam's), Individualism at 20 (identical to Vietnam, despite the very different corporate feel), and Uncertainty Avoidance of just 8 — startlingly low on paper, though in practice it shows up less as comfort with ambiguity and more as a demand that *someone else* resolve it quickly, through process, before it reaches you.

**The Reckoning**

The honest version of this isn't "scrappy versus corporate" — both places are collectivist by Hofstede's own numbers, and the real difference is what the collective organizes around. Vietnam's group absorbs risk by moving together, fast, fixing a broken feature over coffee rather than escalating it. Singapore's group absorbs risk by routing it through layers that each take a small, defined piece of accountability — protecting everyone individually, while leaving nobody feeling authorized to just decide. A Vietnamese founder dropped into a Singaporean MNC will read every sign-off as fear of commitment. A Singaporean hire dropped into a Hanoi startup will read every snap decision as recklessness nobody will own when it fails. Both are describing the same thing: where trust gets placed — in the people doing the work, or in the process that checks it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/IndianWorkplace (Reddit) — u/Simply_Param, describing business hours in Vietnam more broadly rather than startups specifically, recounted a restaurant owner who refused to pull staff from their scheduled nap break even for triple pay, and summed up the pattern: "It's not 14 hours of work — it's 14 hours of business hours."*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — On a thread asking how software-engineering life compares across Singapore employers, one widely upvoted reply broke it down by ownership: Chinese-owned firms and Chinese-culture companies that present as international were described as the likeliest to run "996" hours — roughly 9am to 9pm, six days a week — while large local firms were called "a mixed bag" depending entirely on the team, and US or European firms were said to mirror their home country's norms rather than a distinctly Singaporean one.*

> *MustShareNews, reporting a TikTok post by Nim, a 26-year-old Australian lawyer working in Singapore's trading and shipping sector — Nim said junior staff are expected to "follow the status quo" and are rarely asked their opinion, adding pointedly of the rare moments they are: "And often you won't be." She stressed she wasn't criticizing the culture, just naming the adjustment.*

> *HackerNoon — A piece aimed at founders hiring in Vietnam (written with a visible commercial interest in outsourcing, so read as advocacy rather than neutral reporting) argued the country's real edge isn't just cost but genuine enthusiasm for learning: teams that invested in training and hackathons saw outsized engagement.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing on energy alone, Vietnam will feel like the place where your ideas ship faster than you can second-guess them, and Singapore will feel like the place where your ideas get checked by people who outrank you before they move at all. Neither is really more "corporate" than the stereotype suggests — Singapore's low Uncertainty Avoidance score claims comfort with ambiguity it doesn't quite practice, while Vietnam's startups are collectivist to a degree most Western founders would call cautious. Priya's actual advice: in Ho Chi Minh City, earn trust with your team lead before you expect speed to extend to you. In Singapore, ask early who actually signs off on what — the org chart, not the ambition, decides how fast your next good idea moves.
