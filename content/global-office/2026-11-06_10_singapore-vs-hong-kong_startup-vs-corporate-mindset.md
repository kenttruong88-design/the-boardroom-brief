**Singapore's Startups Are Backed by the Government. Hong Kong's Are Backed by Spite and Long Hours.**

🇸🇬 Singapore · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Singapore captured roughly 60% of ASEAN's entire venture capital deal volume in 2024, worth about US$4.8 billion, with the government adding S$440 million to its Startup SG Equity scheme the same year, per Enterprise Singapore figures released via the EDB. Hong Kong, by contrast, recorded the single biggest ranking jump of any ecosystem in the 2025 Global Startup Ecosystem Report — not because of a comparable state fund, but because, as one 2014 analysis of the territory's business culture put it, a results-obsessed, money-first culture happens to produce an unusually large pool of commercially sharp collaborators almost by accident.

The company-registration numbers tell a similar story from a different angle. Hong Kong recorded 132,246 new local company registrations in 2023, up roughly 27% on the year before, bringing the territory's total registered companies past 1.4 million; Singapore's own business registry logged roughly 70,800 new formations across the first ten months of 2026 alone, against a total of over 640,000 registered entities. Both markets are, by sheer volume, extraordinarily easy places to legally start something. The actual difficulty, in both cases, begins well after the registration paperwork is done.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect even "startups" to run on consensus-driven decision-making and real relationship-building, not Silicon Valley speed | Assume the word "startup" guarantees a flatter or faster culture — plenty of nominally entrepreneurial firms are just as hierarchical |
| Verify salary, expense, and HR practices directly before joining an early-stage company | Assume a slick pitch deck means a stable back-office — a documented 2026 dispute involved unpaid final salary and misrepresented staff nationality |
| Treat the state-scaffolded ecosystem (Block 71, Startup SG Equity, Temasek) as a genuine structural advantage, not a sign of immaturity | Expect Silicon-Valley-style risk tolerance by default, even though Hofstede's own Uncertainty Avoidance score for Singapore is a strikingly low 8 |
| At Chinese-headquartered firms, expect norms benchmarked to the parent company's home market rather than fully localized | Assume "international" branding means fully localized HR and language practices |
| Recognize visible long hours as a real, openly discussed loyalty signal in many corporates | Mistake a low 14% Gallup engagement figure, trailing the 25% Southeast Asia regional average, for low actual output |
| Ask directly about your manager's working style before joining | Assume a title or seniority will shield you from public criticism on a team call |

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect genuinely long hours (60–80 a week is reported routinely at major corporates and Big 4-style firms) as structural, not exceptional | Expect strong employee engagement even at well-paying firms — Hong Kong's 5% Gallup figure is among the lowest recorded anywhere |
| Recognize "grind now, enjoy harder" as an openly discussed, even romanticized, ethos among younger professionals | Assume results-orientation automatically creates startup-friendly experimentation — one analysis argues the opposite, since fast-payment expectations squeeze out the patience many startups need |
| Validate your business model early if founding a startup — described by one insider as resembling "New York in 2008" | Assume local investors think like venture capitalists by default — Hong Kong investors are historically described as favoring real estate over tech |
| Plan your year around a real "busy season / slack season" swing in professional services | Be too eager to please during a probation period — one commenter specifically warns this sets a hard-to-reverse precedent |
| Plan your career-gap narrative in advance if you take a break — HR in some finance and audit sectors reportedly views gaps unfavorably | Expect HR flexibility by default outside the acknowledged slack-season window |
| Recognize finance, law, and medicine's outsized social prestige as a structural reason the local tech talent pipeline differs from Silicon Valley's | Dismiss that prestige gap as a stereotype — it's cited as a real structural factor, not folklore |

Singapore's startup scene is, by its own government's framing, a deliberately engineered one. A Harvard Business Review Analytic Services white paper commissioned by the Singapore EDB quotes Merck's Monica Maestre calling the country "culturally forward-thinking," with "a fair number of early adopters" useful for test-marketing — while the same paper notes critics who argue the ecosystem remains government-dependent and less organically mature than Silicon Valley's model. Hofstede's figures for Singapore show Power Distance at 74 and Individualism at just 20, alongside that strikingly low Uncertainty Avoidance of 8 — numbers that sit oddly next to the country's reputation for rule-bound order, a genuine tension worth noting rather than resolving. Gallup's engagement figure (14%) trails the Southeast Asia regional average of 25%, even as StartupBlink ranks Singapore 4th globally for startup ecosystems, up from 5th the year before — a workforce that is, by the numbers, simultaneously thriving institutionally and unenthused personally. An older World Bank ease-of-doing-business comparison — the last of its kind before the report was discontinued in 2021 over data-integrity concerns — had ranked Singapore first globally overall, strongest specifically in enforcing contracts and cross-border trade, with Hong Kong close behind in third; that legacy ranking still shapes how both markets are pitched to incoming founders today, even though the underlying report no longer exists to update it.

Hong Kong's corporate culture is better documented as a pressure cooker than as an innovation hub. A Deacons law firm survey of over a thousand employees, conducted during a period of intense local unrest and reported by the South China Morning Post, found 65% naming long hours as their top workplace complaint, with domineering senior management close behind. Hong Kong government statistics, cited in the same SCMP coverage, put over 670,000 employees — roughly a fifth of the entire labour force — working more than 55 hours a week. Hofstede's figures (Power Distance 68, Individualism 25) track closely with Singapore's own profile, but Hong Kong's startup ecosystem, unlike Singapore's, climbed the 2025 Global Startup Ecosystem Report's rankings without a comparably visible state apparatus behind it — rising, per the report's own language, as the single most improved ecosystem in the entire Top 40.

The talent pipeline underneath both ecosystems also looks different on close inspection. Hong Kong's deepest structural disadvantage for startups, according to the 2014 analysis cited above, isn't capital or regulation — it's prestige allocation: finance, law, and medicine carry outsized social status relative to technology, which quietly steers the most ambitious graduates away from the sector a startup ecosystem most needs to recruit from. Singapore doesn't carry quite the same prestige skew, but its startup graduates are arguably recruited into an ecosystem so thoroughly state-scaffolded — Block 71's incubation infrastructure, the National Research Foundation's venture funding, Temasek's sovereign capital reach since 1974 — that founding something genuinely independent of government-adjacent support remains the exception rather than the rule.

### The Reckoning

The deepest irony is that Singapore's startup culture, despite the "startup" label, often runs on the same consensus-driven, relationship-first decision-making as its corporates — multiple Blind commenters describe large, slow, meeting-heavy processes even at nominally entrepreneurial firms — while Hong Kong's corporate culture, despite its buttoned-up reputation, produces the kind of commercially aggressive, fast-moving operators that startups elsewhere spend years trying to recruit. The two cities have, in effect, swapped reputations with their actual behavior: Singapore's "disruptors" negotiate like bankers, and Hong Kong's bankers behave like founders.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind, "WLB Singapore vs USA" thread — A commenter identifying as working at a telecom described roughly 60-hour weeks and modest total compensation in a culture they called "competitive" and "face-time-focused," saying having a boss who isn't purely results-obsessed is what actually makes the job bearable.*

> *Blind, "Working in Singapore is the nightmare" thread — An original poster seven months into a Singapore role described feeling bored outside of work given the intensity of local work culture, and separately flagged HDB flat costs as a major financial stressor, with some buyers reportedly carrying housing debt for roughly twenty years.*

> *The Online Citizen — Reporting on a Reddit-sourced dispute involving a self-described "AI startup," the outlet detailed allegations that Malaysian staff were issued Singapore phone numbers and told to present as local to clients, alongside an unpaid final salary later resolved after the post became public; the company's CEO disputed the characterization but declined detailed comment, and Singapore's Ministry of Manpower said it was reviewing the matter.*

> *Fishbowl, Chinese-company-in-Singapore thread — One commenter said culture at a Chinese-headquartered "international" employer was highly team-dependent but Chinese influence persisted regardless, including continued internal use of Chinese language and retention policies benchmarked to norms from the parent company's home market.*

> *Reddit r/HongKong, "How do you deal with the work stress" thread — A commenter who moved from Australia said friends at Big 4 firms routinely worked 60–80 hour weeks under a local ethos summarized as "work hard, enjoy harder," while another, raised in Toronto, noted a friend at a bank had far more manageable hours for lower pay — a reminder that even within Hong Kong, the trade-off is a choice, not a fixed national setting.*

### Conclusion

If "startup energy" is what you're chasing, know which city is actually selling it. Singapore will hand you genuine institutional support, real capital, and a culture that moves more like a well-run ministry than a garage — which, if you're honest with yourself, is often exactly what a startup needs and rarely admits to wanting. Hong Kong will hand you less state support and more raw commercial instinct, forged by a corporate culture that works people hard enough that founding your own thing starts to look, by comparison, like the restful option. Choose based on which kind of pressure you'd rather answer to: a five-year plan, or a landlord.

Whichever you pick, verify the basics before you sign anything, especially at an early-stage company in either city — the specific, documented dispute cited above involving unpaid salary and misrepresented staff location is a reminder that "startup" is a marketing term, not a regulatory category, and it carries exactly as much protection as the paperwork you insisted on reading first. The pitch deck will tell you about the mission. The contract will tell you about the actual job. Read both, in that order, but believe the second one. And whichever city you choose, don't be surprised when the "corporate" one turns out to work the longer hours, and the "startup" one turns out to run the longer meetings — the labels, in both cities, describe the funding structure far more reliably than they describe the actual daily grind.
