**9-9-6 vs. Home by Five: Two Countries, Two Very Different Definitions of "Reasonable"**

**🇨🇳 China · 🇨🇿 Czech Republic**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

According to the International Labour Organization's 2024 figures, urban employees in China average 46.4 hours a week — nearly ten hours more than the OECD average of 36.4. In the Czech Republic, just under 6% of employees work very long hours, below the 11% OECD average, and the workday tends to end sharply enough that colleagues genuinely don't expect to hear from each other after 5pm. Both countries have labor laws capping the workweek at roughly 40 hours. Only one of them treats that number as a ceiling.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly, before accepting an offer, what the real expected hours are — job postings understate this | Assume the legal 40-hour cap reflects actual practice at most private employers |
| Expect tech, finance, and startup roles to run longer than manufacturing or state-sector jobs | Assume every industry or company runs on 996 — many don't, and enforcement is tightening |
| Track your overtime and know your legal entitlement to premium pay or time off in lieu | Treat unpaid extra hours as simply part of the job without checking your contract |
| Watch how colleagues over 35 are treated — age-related pressure around long hours is a real dynamic | Assume seniority automatically protects you from overtime expectations |
| Build in recovery time on your one guaranteed rest day | Plan your only day off around more work-adjacent obligations |
| Ask about actual enforcement of China's 44-hour statutory cap at your specific employer | Rely on what the law says on paper rather than what your office actually does |

#### 🇨🇿 Czech Republic

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a genuine 8am–4/5pm rhythm at most standard employers | Schedule calls or send urgent requests expecting replies after hours |
| Use your full four weeks of statutory paid vacation — it's normal, not a red flag | Feel you need to justify or apologize for taking all your leave |
| Get any overtime agreement in writing — it's a legal requirement, not a formality | Work unrecorded overtime on a verbal request alone |
| Expect the 150-hour annual overtime cap to be taken seriously | Assume Czech colleagues will match an "always-on" pace from elsewhere |
| Enjoy the clear boundary between work and personal life as the cultural default | Interpret a colleague leaving at 4pm sharp as a lack of commitment |
| Learn some Czech — language is repeatedly cited as the main friction point for foreign hires | Expect English alone to carry you through every workplace interaction |

**China**

China's overtime culture is best understood as a gap between statute and practice. On paper, Chinese labor law caps the standard workweek at 40 hours across five days, limits overtime to three hours a day and 36 hours a month, and requires union-negotiated agreement plus premium pay for anything beyond that — rules that, per legal trackers like Harris Sliwoski, have "always been there," with the recent shift being stricter enforcement rather than new law. In practice, especially in tech, finance, and startup environments, the informal 996 schedule — 9am to 9pm, six days a week, roughly 72 hours — has functioned for over a decade as an unofficial norm, publicly defended in the past by prominent tech founders as, in one widely reported phrase, a "huge blessing" for young workers.

The ILO's 46.4-hour average for urban Chinese employees reflects a national baseline already elevated well above OECD norms, before accounting for sector-specific extremes. Hofstede Insights scores China high on Long-Term Orientation, a dimension tied to pragmatism and endurance toward future reward — culturally consistent with a workforce willing to normalize present sacrifice, including long hours, for later payoff. Reporting from outlets like Foreign Policy has pushed back on 996 as a monolithic national culture — the practice is concentrated in specific industries and company cultures rather than universal — but the pressure it describes is real enough that some workers, particularly those over 35, report losing roles specifically because they could no longer sustain the pace. The result is a labor market where the legally guaranteed boundary and the practical, socially enforced one can be dramatically different numbers, and figuring out which one applies to a specific employer is something you generally have to ask about directly, because it won't be in the job posting.

**Czech Republic**

The Czech Republic runs closer to what its labor code actually says. Standard full-time work is a genuine 40-hour week, typically 8am to 4 or 5pm, and per Eurofound and OECD figures, the boundary between professional and personal life holds up in practice: overtime, when it happens, is capped at roughly 150 hours a year, requires a written agreement, and comes with a minimum 25% pay premium or equivalent time off. Just under 6% of Czech employees report working very long hours — about half the OECD average of 11% — and that gap tracks with a workplace culture where a 4pm departure isn't read as a lack of ambition, it's simply the schedule.

Four weeks of statutory paid vacation is standard and, per multiple expat and business-culture guides, actually used rather than accumulated and forfeited. For newcomers, the friction points aren't really about hours — they're about language and industry variance: assembly-line and shift-based roles run on more rigid, rotating schedules than white-collar jobs, and adaptation is reported as smoother for Western hires than for those arriving from further afield, largely because Czech remains the practical language of daily office life even where English is the working language on paper. The overtime system exists and is used, but it functions as an occasional, documented exception rather than the informal baseline expectation it can become elsewhere.

**The Reckoning**

The sharpest contrast isn't the hours themselves, it's what happens when the written law and the lived norm diverge. In China, that gap is wide and well known — everyone from labor lawyers to tech CEOs discusses it openly, which paradoxically makes 996 both more infamous and, in its way, more negotiable: workers who ask directly about real expectations before accepting a role can often get a straight answer, because the ambiguity is a known feature of the market. In the Czech Republic, the gap between law and practice is narrow enough that it rarely comes up as a topic at all — which is its own kind of tell, since a culture doesn't need to constantly re-litigate its overtime norms when the norms already match the statute.

There's also an inverted risk profile worth naming. China's overtime culture is concentrated and visible — you can identify the risky sectors and companies in advance and route around them if you're selective. The Czech Republic's risk is quieter and more individual: because unrecorded overtime is culturally unusual, an employer who does push it tends to do so informally and off the books, precisely because the formal system offers so little cover for it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A contributor addressing whether China's 996 reputation is overstated in Western media argued it's concentrated in tech and finance firms with an "American" startup culture rather than a nationwide standard, but conceded that workers who can't sustain it — especially those over 35 — do sometimes lose their jobs over it.*

> *Quora — Someone who taught English in China described being asked to teach evenings, weekends, and occasionally through public holidays despite the country's formal 996 framing being aimed at office and tech workers — a reminder that overtime pressure in China isn't confined to the industries that get the most attention.*

> *Quora — A long response to "what is the work culture in the Czech Republic" broke down the answer by industry, noting that office roles run a clean, boundaried schedule while assembly-line and shift work follow their own rotating pattern — the poster's overall verdict was that overtime is genuinely uncommon rather than technically-forbidden-but-actually-expected.*

> *Quora — Someone assessing how easy it is for foreigners to adapt to Czech working life pointed to language, not hours, as the real dividing line — describing an easier adjustment for Western hires and a harder one for those arriving from Asia or Africa, largely because so much workplace culture still runs through Czech rather than English.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two, the honest calculus isn't "which country works less" — it's "which country's gap between what's promised and what's delivered is smaller." China can absolutely offer a reasonable 40-hour job; you just have to ask hard, specific questions before signing anything, because the national statistics and the individual employer can diverge enormously. The Czech Republic mostly delivers what it says it will, which is less exciting to write about but considerably easier to plan a life around. The line I'd give a friend: in China, negotiate your hours like you're buying a used car; in Czechia, you can mostly trust the sticker price.
