**Pakistan vs. the United Kingdom: One Office Runs on Rank, the Other Pretends It Doesn't**

**🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Lahore, you greet the most senior person in the room first, because not doing so is a small social injury everyone will remember. In London, you greet whoever happens to be nearest the coffee machine, because acknowledging rank out loud is the small social injury. Both offices have hierarchies. Only one of them will admit it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet and address the most senior person in the room first, every time | Address your boss by their first name until explicitly invited to |
| Let a meeting open with unhurried small talk about family and health | Walk in and start the agenda cold — it reads as rude, not efficient |
| Treat "we'll see" or "we'll try" from a senior colleague as a soft no | Take "we'll see" at face value and show up expecting a yes |
| Dress conservatively and formally, even in creative or tech roles | Assume casual Fridays translate — jackets stay on for client meetings |
| Expect approvals to climb several levels before anything is final | Escalate around your manager to speed things up — it will not speed anything up |
| Build the relationship before the ask; trust precedes business | Pitch a stranger your idea in the first five minutes of knowing them |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up on time for meetings, even ones nobody else seems to care about | Assume lateness reads as relaxed rather than as faintly disrespectful |
| Phrase disagreement as a question: "Would it not be simpler if...?" | Say "I disagree" in a meeting without three sentences of throat-clearing first |
| Take your full lunch break away from your desk; nobody will stop you, but nobody will join you either | Expect colleagues to invite you along — inclusion is passive, not active |
| Use self-deprecating humour to defuse tension or take credit quietly | Mistake British understatement for an actual absence of ambition |
| Respect the stated end of the working day; presenteeism is not admired here the way it is elsewhere | Stay late to "show commitment" — it reads as poor time management |
| Decode "with respect" as the opening of a real objection | Hear "that's an interesting idea" as agreement |

---

Pakistan's workplace runs on a hierarchy that nobody bothers to disguise. The most senior person present is greeted and addressed first, as a matter of course, and junior staff rarely contradict a boss in open meetings — not from fear, exactly, but because open disagreement with a superior reads as a breach of *izzat*, the mutual face both parties are expected to protect. Decisions tend to climb several rungs before they're final: Pakistan's own labour law caps the standard working week at 48 hours, per WageIndicator's overview of Pakistani labour law, with overtime capped at roughly two extra hours a day — but the real bottleneck for most white-collar staff isn't the clock, it's the chain of signatures above them. According to guidance compiled for expats doing business in the country (ExpatArrivals), unhurried small talk precedes any substantive discussion, direct eye contact with a senior colleague can read as confrontational rather than engaged, and a polite "we'll see" is routinely a no wearing a more comfortable coat.

The United Kingdom's workplace runs on a hierarchy that everyone agrees not to mention. Formal rank exists — someone signs off on your leave, someone else signs your review — but it is wrapped in enough hedging, understatement, and self-deprecating humour that a newcomer can spend months missing it entirely. UK full-time employees worked an average of 36.7 hours a week in the final quarter of 2025, per ONS data summarised by LeaveWizard — genuinely closer to the stated working day than Pakistan's figure, because presenteeism (staying visibly late to signal commitment) is quietly looked down on rather than rewarded. The cost is legibility: a Pakistani hierarchy announces itself, so you know where you stand within a week. A British one disguises itself as consensus, so you can spend two years mistaking "that's an interesting idea" for a yes.

**The Reckoning.** Hofstede Insights' comparative data puts Pakistan at roughly 55 on power distance against the UK's 35 — Pakistan accepts and expects unequal distribution of authority, while the UK officially prizes flatness even as real power concentrates just as surely, only quietly. The inversion that catches people out is individualism: the UK scores among the most individualist societies measured (around 89), Pakistan among the least (around 14), yet it is the "individualist" British office that runs on indirect, face-saving speech, while the "collectivist" Pakistani office is often more comfortable with blunt, hierarchical directness between people who already know exactly where they stand with each other.

Pakistani arrivals in Britain tend to over-correct for the formality they expect and are thrown by how much of the real decision-making happens in corridor conversations and raised eyebrows rather than memos. British arrivals in Pakistan tend to under-correct for the formality they're not expecting and are thrown by how literally seniority is observed in a room that otherwise feels warm and informal. Both groups are navigating a chain of command. Only one of them gets a map.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — On a thread about Pakistani and Indian colleagues navigating South Asian workplace dynamics in Western tech, several posters described keeping things carefully neutral at work — steering around politics, religion, and cricket — while the real warmth showed up only outside the office, over cricket banter and shared jokes once the professional guard came down.*

> *University of Liverpool careers case study — A Pakistani graduate working as an audit trainee at a UK accountancy firm described the hardest part of arriving as not the work itself but the visa process: passing several assessment centres only to get a rejection call a week later because the firm didn't sponsor visas. The job that did work out, he said, gave him time off for professional exams without treating it as a favour.*

> *NHS staff interview, South Asian Heritage Month — A Pakistani NHS staff member recalled landing in London right as COVID hit, and finding the city "polite and kind, but much more reserved" than home — a place with "a strong sense of privacy" where even casual socialising felt more structured than she expected.*

> *ACE Money Transfer expat advisory — A guide aimed at newly arrived Pakistani professionals in the UK advises treating the first few weeks as pure observation: watch how coworkers actually communicate before saying much yourself, since the nonverbal cues — who interrupts whom, who speaks first in a meeting — carry more of the real information than anything said aloud.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing the move, the practical question isn't which country is more hierarchical — it's which kind of hierarchy you'd rather navigate honestly. Pakistan will tell you exactly where you rank within your first meeting, and you can plan your career around that information with some confidence. The UK will insist, cheerfully, that no one here thinks about rank at all, while your invitation to the important meeting quietly fails to arrive. Neither is more honest than the other so much as differently dishonest.

Here's what I'd actually tell a friend: go to Pakistan if you want to know your boss's expectations on day one, and go to the UK if you'd rather guess at them for a year while everyone insists you're all equals. Pack accordingly, and learn to read a raised eyebrow either way.
