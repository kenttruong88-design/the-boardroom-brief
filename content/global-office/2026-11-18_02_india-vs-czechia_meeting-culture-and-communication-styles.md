# Everything Is Urgent in India and Nothing Is Said Aloud in Czechia: A Meeting Survival Guide

🇮🇳 India · 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two offices, two ways of making sure nothing ever gets decided in the meeting. In one, a colleague agrees enthusiastically, leaves the room, and does something else. In the other, a call is scheduled at midnight on a Sunday and labelled urgent, as is the next one. Both are described by people who work there as normal, and both are baffling to arrivals from countries where meetings are meant to produce decisions.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show visible respect to senior colleagues, as seniority carries real weight | Contradict a manager bluntly in front of the team |
| Greet colleagues warmly at the start of the day and keep the tone soft | Open with a long run of personal questions before you know someone well |
| Confirm time-zone arrangements in writing before accepting late or early calls | Assume a call labelled urgent actually is |
| Raise disagreement privately or frame it as a question | Treat silence in a meeting as agreement |
| Agree explicitly which hours you are reachable | Accept Sunday calls without noticing that you have set a precedent |
| Expect the day to run roughly 9 to 5 in corporate roles, and plan commutes accordingly | Schedule a meeting at peak traffic and expect punctuality |

### In Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Give clear, structured answers with concrete examples | Mistake a polite nod for commitment |
| Follow up a meeting in writing with who will do what | Assume everyone will say aloud what they think of the plan |
| Ask for private feedback from colleagues you trust | Expect candid criticism of a manager in an open meeting |
| Say plainly if you want the meeting to run in English | Take offence if the lunch table drifts into Czech |
| Respect the 40-hour week and leave on time | Treat late-evening messages as the local norm |
| Learn a few Czech phrases and mention that you are learning | Interpret reserve as hostility |

## India

India is a hierarchical workplace, and the meeting reflects it. The Culture Factor comparison tool, which publishes the Hofstede scores, gives India 77 on power distance and 40 on uncertainty avoidance, a combination that suggests deference to seniority paired with a relatively relaxed attitude to rules and plans. Expat.com's guide to working in New Delhi says seniority matters, that juniors answer to middle and senior management, and that showing respect to seniors is expected. It also recommends speaking softly with colleagues, greeting them warmly and avoiding an excess of personal questions, and notes that corporate roles typically run from 9 a.m. to 5 p.m.

The sharper details come from people describing the pressure points. A Business Today report in February 2025 covered an Indian technology worker who complained that staff on US-facing teams are routinely pulled into late-night and early-morning calls, sometimes pushed to 11 p.m. or midnight so that American colleagues never give up daytime hours. The complaint was that managers hoping for a US posting drive the habit and that it is passed from senior staff to newcomers until it seems normal. Commenters were not unanimous: some said the Indian managers were stricter than their American counterparts, while others said the friction was local, not foreign.

An HRKatha essay, which this desk treats as opinion and not data, went further. It described Indian teams inside a multinational where nearly everything was labelled urgent, Sunday calls arrived without notice, and disagreement with a boss was read as disrespect. The same manager, it argued, could be collaborative with colleagues in London and authoritarian with a team in Bengaluru, which suggests that local norms, not temperament, set the tone. The essay cited Gallup's State of the Global Workplace, which it reported as finding that 86 percent of Indian employees describe themselves as struggling at work. That is a second-hand figure and should be read as one, but it is consistent with the stories above.

One further observation in the essay deserves attention from anyone weighing a transfer. The author noted that Indian employees of Indian multinationals posted overseas often receive flexible hours and generous leave, while colleagues on the same projects in India work late, and concluded that location and local management culture, not the company, drive the difference. If true, the choice of office matters more than the choice of employer. It is worth asking, before accepting, which city the team actually sits in and who its manager reports to, since the answers will shape the calendar more than the corporate values page will.

## Czechia

Czechia is a different problem, because in Czechia the friction is mostly silent. The Hofstede scores put the Czech Republic at 57 on power distance, 70 on individualism and 74 on uncertainty avoidance, which describes a population that is moderately hierarchical, personally independent and fond of plans that stay planned. Expat.com's Prague guide calls communication usually direct and practical and says employers value clear, structured answers with concrete examples. It adds that the standard week is 40 hours, that excessive overtime is less common than elsewhere, and that hybrid arrangements are common in international companies. There are 13 public holidays a year. The guide also says colleagues often socialise outside work, that team-building events are expected through the year, and that Fridays tend to be more relaxed, so the meeting that fails to produce candour may be compensated for by the pub that follows it.

Direct, in this context, does not mean candid upward. According to the Up Benefity Employee Barometer 2026, reported by Expats.cz, nearly half of Czech employees do not give their bosses critical feedback directly, nearly half say they sometimes adjust the truth to avoid conflict, about 40 percent say management filters information, and only around one in ten trust management almost completely. Concerns travel sideways, among peers, while management remains unaware. Among employees who hold back, nearly three in ten have seen honesty backfire, and a similar share think feedback would change nothing, which is at least a rational response to the evidence. When a serious company mistake affects staff, about two-thirds learn of it through official channels, and more than one in ten would never find out at all. The same article describes a legacy of hierarchical management and cites Journal of World Business research in which leaders from the United States and Western Europe were viewed more favourably than equally qualified leaders from Czechia, Poland and Albania.

For foreigners, the consequence is practical. A Czech colleague who appears to agree with a manager may simply be declining to challenge the decision in public, and the meeting will record consensus that does not exist. A second Expats.cz feature adds a quieter problem: in offices where Czech is the default, internationals can feel excluded at lunch and coffee breaks, although a consultant quoted in the piece estimated that around 90 percent of cases stem from thoughtlessness and not intent. Younger, technology-oriented workplaces tend to use English as the main working language.

## The Reckoning

The two systems fail in opposite directions. India, on the evidence of these accounts, over-communicates and under-respects the calendar: too many calls, too many labelled urgent, and a power structure that makes it costly to decline. Czechia under-communicates and over-respects the calendar: the 40-hour week holds, the meeting ends on time, and the useful disagreement happens in the corridor afterwards. Anyone expecting the meeting to be the place where truth is exchanged will be disappointed in both, in different ways and for different reasons.

The adaptation is correspondingly different. In India, the skill is boundary-setting without insult: agreeing in writing which hours you are available, and treating urgency as a claim to be verified. In Czechia, the skill is extraction: asking directly and privately what colleagues think, and writing down what was decided before everyone leaves. A foreigner who learns both gets a rare professional education. A foreigner who learns neither will spend several years agreeing with people who disagree and disagreeing with people who have already agreed.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Business Today (reader reactions) — Commenters on an Indian engineer's complaint about midnight calls with US clients said Western colleagues tend to respect calendars and end meetings on time, while some Indian managers were described as stricter than their American peers. One reported that an overseas visitor turned the office into a celebration and that the whole team was ordered in, even on remote days.*

> *HRKatha (essay on a Bengaluru professional) — A veteran of a multinational described London colleagues as calm and respectful of boundaries and Bengaluru colleagues as louder, with Sunday calls and constant urgency, and cited a Reddit Bangalore discussion with similar complaints.*

> *Expats.cz (feature on avoiding confrontation) — Internationals in Prague report that a Czech colleague's visible agreement with a boss may hide disagreement, and Czech staff working for foreign managers can suddenly be expected to give direct feedback that traditional workplaces never asked of them.*

> *Expats.cz (feature on language exclusion) — Expats describe feeling left out when colleagues default to Czech at lunch and coffee breaks, and the suggested remedy is to say politely that you are learning and ask for English, one colleague at a time.*

> *Expats.cz (2005 feature on communication) — An older expat piece notes that Czechs rarely voice disagreement openly, so a manager may believe instructions were accepted for weeks before noticing that something else was done.*

## Conclusion

If you are choosing on communication alone, choose based on your tolerance. India offers warmth, hierarchy and a schedule that can expand without notice. Czechia offers fixed hours, clear working practice and a habit of keeping the hard conversation out of the meeting. Neither gives you the pleasant fiction that the meeting is where the decision is made.

Practically, negotiate your reachable hours before you sign, and find the one colleague who will tell you what was really meant. In both countries, this person exists, and in both countries it is the person who is not speaking in the meeting.
