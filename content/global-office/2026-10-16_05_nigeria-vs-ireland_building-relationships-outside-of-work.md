## Lagos Hugs Strangers. Dublin Keeps Them at Arm's Length for a Decade: A Relocation Guide

**🇳🇬 Nigeria · 🇮🇪 Ireland**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Lagos, a stranger at a bus stop will ask after your family before you've exchanged surnames. In Dublin, a colleague of three years may still not know which neighborhood you live in. The 2025 World Happiness Report, drawing on Gallup World Poll data, ranked Nigeria 7th out of 147 countries for willingness to help a stranger, even as the country sat 105th overall on life satisfaction. Ireland, meanwhile, was flagged in a 2022 survey as having the European Union's highest reported rate of loneliness. Neither number is the whole story — but if you are moving for a job and quietly wondering whether you'll have anyone to call on a bad Tuesday, both are worth sitting with before you sign the relocation contract.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept food or drink offered in someone's home, even a small amount | Decline hospitality outright — it reads as an insult, not a boundary |
| Join a church, mosque, or alumni ("old boys/old girls") network early | Assume your workplace will be the edge of your social world — it rarely is |
| Greet elders properly and use titles before getting to business | Skip the greeting ritual to save time — it reads as rude, not efficient |
| Expect new acquaintances to ask about marriage, family, and faith quickly | Be alarmed when someone calls you "aunty," "uncle," or "brother" within a week |
| Show up for weddings, funerals, and naming ceremonies you're invited to | Treat an invitation as optional — no-shows are noticed and remembered |
| Budget time and money to host people back | Expect friendship without reciprocity — mutual obligation is the social contract |

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the pub invitation — it is the default venue for socializing | Expect a pub pint to translate into a dinner invitation anytime soon |
| Join a GAA club, running group, or pub quiz for a recurring reason to show up | Wait to be invited into someone's home — that comes late, if ever |
| Learn to enjoy being "slagged" (teased) — it is a trust signal, not hostility | Take the teasing personally, or worse, retaliate with real criticism |
| Expect friendliness fast and depth slowly — sometimes over months | Overshare personal details early — it can read as intense |
| Volunteer or join a community group; roughly one in six adults already do | Assume colleagues double as your social circle — work and life stay separate |
| Keep showing up to the same spot on the same night, repeatedly | Mistake "we should get a pint sometime" for a scheduled commitment |

### Nigeria: The Network Finds You, Whether You Wanted One or Not

Nigeria's social fabric is built on obligation as much as affection, and that is not a complaint — it is the operating system. According to the 2025 World Happiness Report, compiled with Gallup World Poll data and Oxford's Wellbeing Research Centre, Nigerians rank 33rd globally on trusting a stranger to return a lost wallet, but only 126th on trusting the police to do the same. The report's authors suggest that where institutions are weak, informal trust between people fills the gap — which is a polite, academic way of saying that in Nigeria, your neighbors and your church group are doing the job your government is not. That gap also explains why Nigeria placed only 105th on overall life satisfaction in the same report despite ranking 7th on stranger-friendliness: people are managing, warmly, around institutions rather than through them, which is a more exhausting arrangement than it sounds from the outside.

That informal infrastructure is dense and fast-moving. Posters on Nigeria's Expat.com forum consistently advise newcomers to "go out more," frequent local (not upscale expatriate) spots, and lean on introductions rather than wait for organic encounters — advice that assumes, correctly, that initiative gets rewarded quickly. A Tales from a Small Planet "Real Post Report" on Lagos describes locals as welcoming and encouraging of outsiders joining in, provided the newcomer steps outside the diplomatic-compound bubble; staying inside it, the same report notes, tends to limit contact to colleagues rather than the wider city. Hofstede Insights has never published an individually measured score for Nigeria, instead folding it into a broad "West Africa" regional estimate — itself telling, since the firm's model assumes enough collectivism there that country-level nuance wasn't worth isolating. The regional figure (an individualism score around 20, against a global scale running to the 90s) points the same direction the forums do: belonging is assumed, not applied for. Practically, this means the professional and personal spheres blur fast — a colleague's cousin's wedding is not a networking obligation so much as a genuine extension of the office relationship, and declining it costs more socially than skipping a work dinner would.

### Ireland: Friendly on Arrival, Private for Years

Ireland's reputation for friendliness is accurate and also somewhat beside the point. According to the OECD's 2025 report on social connections and loneliness, people across its member countries — Ireland included — are meeting others in person less often than a decade ago, with the sharpest declines among men and young adults. Ireland's own numbers add nuance rather than contradiction: research from the ESRI, using Healthy Ireland survey data, found that the share of adults reporting acute loneliness fell from 5.9 percent in 2021 to 3.9 percent in 2023 as pandemic isolation eased — yet Ireland had separately been identified, in a 2022 EU-wide survey, as the bloc's loneliest member state. The CSO's own well-being hub tracks this directly, measuring both the population who report feeling lonely and the population who say they have someone to count on in a crisis — an unusually frank admission, for a national statistics office, that connection is now a policy variable. The ESRI's analysis also found loneliness was more common among people not born in Ireland, which is the one line in the entire literature that speaks directly to the person this guide is written for.

The social texture explains the gap between warmth and closeness. The Irish pub remains, per multiple expat accounts, the default venue for low-stakes friendliness — chatty, funny, and happy to include you in the conversation that evening. What it does not reliably produce is the follow-through. An Expat Exchange thread on Dublin describes locals as having a strong, almost insular sense of community that can take months of consistent contact to crack; CSO Labour Force Survey data for the second quarter of 2025 shows roughly one in six people volunteering, which is one of the more reliable shortcuts into that community, since shared structured activity moves faster than cold socializing. None of this is unfriendliness. It is closer to a long qualifying round before the relationship is allowed to count as real, and newcomers who mistake early banter for the finish line tend to be the ones who end up quietly isolated a year in.

### The Reckoning

Put side by side, these are not two different amounts of friendliness — they are two different delivery mechanisms. Nigeria front-loads inclusion: you are family-adjacent within weeks, obligated and obligating, and the cost is that the relationship comes with expectations (attendance, reciprocity, generosity) baked in from day one. Ireland back-loads inclusion: you are welcome at the bar immediately and in the inner circle eventually, and the cost is a slower, quieter stretch where you may be liked without yet being known.

For someone relocating for work, the practical consequence is about where you spend your energy in month one. In Nigeria, energy spent accepting invitations and showing up for others' milestones compounds quickly into an actual support network, and the social debt you accrue is a feature, not a bug — it is what makes the network reliable when you eventually need it. In Ireland, energy is better spent joining something with a recurring schedule — a club, a class, a volunteering shift — because showing up repeatedly, not showing enthusiasm once, is what eventually converts "grand craic" into an actual friendship. The OECD's broader point about declining in-person contact matters here too: Ireland's social default is no longer effortless even for the Irish, so an expat waiting to be swept up by it may simply be waiting for something that has gotten rarer for everyone.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit (r/socialireland) — a 28-year-old relocating from Australia posted that she wanted to build connections "virtually before arriving" in June, admitting she was nervous about isolation; several Irish and fellow-incoming commenters responded warmly, offering to meet up, which suggests the anxiety is common and the online workaround is now routine.*

> *Expat.com (Nigeria/Lagos forum) — a longtime poster's advice to newcomers was blunt: stop sticking to safe, upscale spots, frequent local "joints" instead, and don't isolate yourself, because the forum consensus is that the city rewards people who show up in person rather than network from behind a gate.*

> *Tales from a Small Planet (Lagos Real Post Report) — a contributor described locals as very welcoming of outsiders who make an effort to join their culture, but also noted real prejudice that people are at least open about, rather than the more deniable kind found elsewhere.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com, Dublin relocation thread) — a commenter weighing a move flagged Dublin's rent as "London-level" and taxes as high, a practical frustration with a social knock-on: less disposable time and money for the pub-and-club circuit that Irish friendship actually runs on.*

> *Expat Focus (interview, Dublin) — an expat recalled calling home in tears early on, convinced she had no friends, after discovering that breaking into Dubliners' long-established friend groups took real, repeated effort; her advice to newcomers was to join a club or Facebook group and keep showing up, because the friendships that followed turned out to be solid.*

### Conclusion

If you want a social life installed on arrival, Nigeria will give you one, with interest payments due in attendance and reciprocity. If you want a social life that takes a while to trust you but tends to last, Ireland will eventually deliver one, provided you treat the first six months as infrastructure-building rather than friendship. Either way, the brochures undersell the timeline: one country hands you a network you didn't apply for, the other makes you earn a smaller one slowly, and both will insist, entirely sincerely, that they're the friendly one.
