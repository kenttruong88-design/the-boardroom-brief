# Bangladesh vs. India: Who Tells You What You're Worth, and Who Makes You Guess

🇧🇩 Bangladesh · 🇮🇳 India

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Dhaka, asking a colleague their salary is roughly as socially acceptable as asking their blood type unprompted. In Bangalore, you will eventually be told your junior earns more than you, usually by the junior. Neither country publishes pay bands in job listings as a matter of course, both treat the number on your offer letter as a closely held secret right up until the moment you resign, and both will leave you negotiating in the dark with great confidence on one side of the table and none on the other. This guide is for the version of "do your research before you move" that assumes the research will actually be useful.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇩 Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat your first offer as the starting line, not the floor — most companies budget room to move | Mention a competing offer unless it is real and recent; HR networks in Dhaka are small and word travels |
| Ask HR directly whether a salary chart or band exists for your grade before countering | Assume "negotiable" in a job post means flexible; it often means undecided |
| Get allowances (transport, medical, festival bonus) itemized separately from base pay | Discuss your exact figure with coworkers in the open office; take it outside or skip it entirely |
| Build your case around measurable output, not tenure, since appraisals lean subjective | Expect a raise from a strong review alone; be ready to show you have other options |
| Keep a dated record of role changes and added responsibilities from day one | Resign as a negotiating tactic unless you are genuinely prepared to leave |

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask recruiters for the CTC band in writing before the first call, in fixed vs. variable terms | Confuse "CTC" with take-home pay; the gap between the two surprises most newcomers |
| Push back, politely, if asked to share payslips; it is not legally required | Assume declining to share salary history will tank the offer outright — it rarely does, though it may slow things |
| Benchmark against city and company tier separately; Bangalore tech pay and Lucknow retail pay are different universes | Discuss your package with your reporting line; raise market data with your manager instead |
| Time your ask to appraisal cycles (typically April and October) | Wait for a scheduled review if you suspect inequity; by then the budget is already set |
| Line up an external offer quietly before escalating pay conversations | Go to HR first with a grievance; line managers have more room to act than process allows |

### Bangladesh: the chart nobody shows you

Bangladesh's labor market runs on an informal consensus that talking about money is a little bit rude, which is convenient for employers and expensive for everyone else. According to the World Bank's 2022 gender pay data, cited in a January 2026 Financial Express piece on the country's pay-secrecy norms, men in Bangladesh earn 35.8% more per hour than women on average, a gap that widens to 57.2% in agriculture — a spread that persists precisely because so few workers know what their colleagues make. HR departments frequently work from an internal salary chart, calibrated so new hires don't out-earn incumbents and spark resentment, but that chart is rarely shared with the people it governs. The Daily Star's interviews with entry-level workers found many earning BDT 20,000–30,000 a month against a Dhaka living-cost estimate (via the Centre for Policy Dialogue) of roughly BDT 23,003 as of mid-2022 — a margin thin enough that negotiating from a position of leverage is, for most fresh graduates, theoretical.

The one reliable lever is the external offer. Reporting in The Business Standard on why raises in Bangladesh often arrive only after an employee says "I resign" describes a pattern that recurs across media, telecom, and software firms: performance reviews and polite requests get deferred, but a competing offer produces a budget within days. A companion op-ed in the Dhaka Tribune argues this isn't really negotiation at all — with hundreds of applicants per opening, power sits almost entirely with employers, and candidates from elite urban institutions, who arrive with market knowledge and networks, out-negotiate equally capable first-generation graduates who simply don't know what to ask for. The system rewards information, not performance, and information is exactly what it withholds.

### India: loud about hustle, quiet about numbers

India talks more about career advancement than Bangladesh does and says almost nothing more about actual pay. According to a LinkedIn Workforce Confidence survey reported by DT Next, 61% of Indian professionals are more comfortable discussing salary with family than with coworkers or friends, only 13% would tell a trusted colleague, and 45% say their workplace actively discourages pay conversations. That discretion coexists with genuinely strong wage momentum — Mercer's 2026 India compensation survey projects a 9% average salary increase across corporate India, among the highest in Asia — which means there is real money moving, just not transparently. The ILO's India Wage Report has separately flagged persistent wage stagnation at the bottom of the distribution even as top-end pay accelerates, a divergence that salary secrecy makes easy to ignore until someone compares notes.

The CTC (cost-to-company) structure does its own quiet damage: the number on the offer letter includes provident fund contributions, insurance, and other non-cash components, so two offers with similar headline figures can differ substantially in actual take-home pay — a distinction that catches newcomers, foreign and domestic, with some regularity. Culturally, India's Hofstede power-distance score of 77 (against individualism of just 48, per Hofstede Insights data) tracks what shows up in practice: hierarchy is respected enough that challenging a manager's pay decision directly is uncomfortable, but a growing, increasingly mobile workforce has learned to route around that discomfort by escalating externally rather than internally — gathering offers quietly, then presenting them as fact rather than threat.

### The Reckoning

Both countries score as collectivist and high-power-distance on Hofstede's framework — Bangladesh at 80 power distance and 20 individualism, India at 77 and 48 — which predicts, correctly, that open salary disagreement with superiors is rare in either place. But the resemblance stops at the tactics. Bangladesh's leverage mechanism is almost entirely binary: you either have a competing offer or you have nothing, and the threat of resignation does more work than any appraisal document. India's system is more granular and more bureaucratic — cycles, bands, CTC breakdowns, HR policy on payslip disclosure — which creates more surface area to negotiate but also more surface area to get quietly outmaneuvered on, since each component (base, variable pay, ESOPs, benefits) is a separate, semi-opaque negotiation in miniature.

The irony is that India's larger, more formalized corporate sector gives the impression of more transparency while Mercer and LinkedIn data show the opposite: pay conversations are actively discouraged at nearly half of Indian workplaces, a rate that Bangladesh, with its smaller formal sector and starker hierarchy, doesn't even bother measuring because the taboo is simply assumed. A newcomer arriving with a Western assumption that "formal" means "documented" will spend a frustrating first quarter discovering that India's paperwork is extensive precisely where it is least useful to you.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/IndianWorkplace — a senior analyst at an Indian IT services firm described discovering that two juniors reporting to him earned 30–40% more, because their previous employers had paid higher base salaries; too wary of being seen as a complainer to raise it with HR directly, he instead started quietly benchmarking the external market.*

> *Blind — an engineer interviewing with a major bank refused to share payslips as proof of current salary; HR stalled for two weeks and eventually called the refusal "hard to deal with," but released a fair offer anyway once it became clear the candidate wouldn't budge.*

> *Quora — a recurring pattern across salary-related threads is newcomers to Indian and Bangladeshi workplaces discovering, well after signing, that "negotiable" in a job posting meant nothing had actually been decided, and that asking a direct question about pay bands early would have saved months of guesswork.*

> *Dhaka Tribune reader accounts — fresh graduates in Dhaka repeatedly described accepting the first offer out of fear that negotiating, or even asking about the number before applying, would cost them the role entirely in a market with hundreds of applicants per opening.*

> *The Daily Star — an HR executive at a mid-sized Bangladeshi firm admitted her company keeps an internal salary chart specifically so new hires can't be told their actual position relative to it, to avoid dissatisfaction among existing staff.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between the two on this axis alone, the practical difference is where the leverage lives. In Bangladesh, your only real card is a credible external offer — cultivate one before you need it, because internal advocacy alone rarely moves the number. In India, the leverage is distributed across CTC components, appraisal cycles, and your right to simply decline to share payslips, which means there's more to negotiate but also more ways to leave money on the table out of politeness. Neither country will tell you what the person next to you makes, so you'll have to build your own market data the hard way, in both places, before you land.

What I'd tell a friend over a drink: in Bangladesh, keep your resignation letter drafted and never send it unless you mean it; in India, keep your CTC breakdown spreadsheet open and never hand over your payslip unless you absolutely have to.
