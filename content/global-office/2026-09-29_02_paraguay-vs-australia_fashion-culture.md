# Paraguay Dresses for a Royal Tea Party. Australia Dresses for the Beach That's Fifteen Minutes Away.

**🇵🇾 Paraguay · 🇦🇺 Australia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

One business etiquette guide advises newcomers to Paraguay to dress "as if attending a royal tea party" — for a Tuesday client meeting. Indeed's Australian Hiring Lab, meanwhile, has spent the past several years tracking the steady creep of "relaxed dress" language into job postings, driven by an insurance sector where more than one in ten listings now explicitly waves off the blazer. Between a courthouse that turns away men in shorts and an office culture increasingly comfortable with a hoodie at your desk, the two countries sit at opposite ends of the same spectrum — and the wrong outfit choice in either one reads as a real social signal, not a fashion footnote.

### Paraguay: Formality as a Love Language

Paraguay's business etiquette guides are strikingly consistent on one point: appearance is treated as a direct expression of respect, not a matter of personal taste. Per ClickUp's business-etiquette summary, "casual Fridays are uncommon," full stop — the formal standard holds essentially every working day, with dark suits and ties for men and conservative, muted-color tailoring for women. Commisceo Global's country guide reinforces the same picture: Paraguayan business culture reads appearance as a proxy for how seriously you take the relationship, and a rumpled or under-dressed visitor is quietly read as under-invested before a word of business gets discussed.

This isn't limited to the office. WorldAtlas notes the country's traditional Ninduti lacework and other formalwear remain reserved for genuinely important occasions — weddings, religious services, milestone celebrations — meaning the cultural instinct to dress up for significance runs well beyond the workplace. A courthouse denying entry to a man in shorts, as one long-term foreign resident's account describes, isn't an isolated overreaction; it's a data point in a broader pattern where formality functions as social infrastructure, not decoration.

There are signs of generational movement — younger Paraguayans are visibly pushing for more relaxed norms, per multiple firsthand accounts — but "pushing for" is doing real work in that sentence. The shift is directional, not yet arrived. Anyone relocating for work should plan their wardrobe for the Paraguay of today, not the Paraguay a decade of gradual change might eventually produce.

### Australia: The Slow Death of the Blazer

Australia's dress-code story is a genuinely quantifiable trend, not just vibes. Indeed's Hiring Lab tracked "relaxed dress" language across job postings and found it climbing steadily since the pandemic — 0.41% of all postings by mid-2025, concentrated heavily in specific sectors: insurance leads at 11.6% of postings, roughly eight points ahead of the next closest sector, followed by legal (3.6%), accounting (2.9%), and data and analytics (1.9%). The correlation with remote-friendliness is explicit in the data: casual-dress language appears 3.2 times more often in high-remote-work occupations than medium-remote ones, and stays "exceedingly rare" in low-remote roles — appearance norms, it turns out, track opportunity to be seen in person more than they track industry glamour.

The demographic story reinforces it. Millennials and Gen Z together make up close to 60% of Australia's population, and the Hiring Lab's analysis explicitly credits their growing workplace influence with sustaining looser norms well past the initial pandemic-era disruption — this isn't a temporary blip reverting to blazers, it's a structural shift. But regional and sector variation remains real: the Australian Capital Territory's casual-dress mentions run roughly double the Northern Territory's, and client-facing law and finance roles remain considerably more buttoned-up than the national reputation for relaxed dressing would suggest.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely interesting contrast isn't "formal vs. casual" — it's what each country's dress norms are actually signaling. In Paraguay, formality signals commitment and respect in a relationship-first business culture; underdressing reads as a relationship failure, not a fashion one. In Australia, casualness increasingly signals trust and autonomy in a culture moving toward outcomes-based, remote-friendly work; overdressing can read as a mismatch with how the role actually operates, even if no one says so directly. Hofstede's individualism scores capture part of this: Australia ranks among the most individualist societies measured, where personal comfort and self-expression carry real workplace legitimacy, while Paraguay's more collectivist, relationship-bound culture treats appearance as something owed to the group, not chosen for the self.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Substack, "The Politics of Pants" (snaleracing)* — A long-term American resident described her Paraguayan partner being turned away from a courthouse for wearing shorts, and his own read on the norm: "people who dress up do it because they think they're better than everyone else" was, in his view, simply the wrong lens — in Paraguay, he told her, dressing up is what you owe the occasion, not a status performance. She noted the country "holds onto its traditions more strongly" than the US does, while acknowledging younger Paraguayans are visibly pushing back.

> *Blind (teamblind.com), South America-to-Australia relocation thread* — One user who lived in Australia for most of a recent year gave a blunt practical assessment unrelated to clothing but central to the actual adjustment: "It's extremely expensive and jobs don't pay nearly as well as US," adding that "most of your money will go to rent, food, and gas" — a reminder that Australia's famously relaxed dress code coexists with a cost of living that can undercut the appeal of it.

> *Indeed Hiring Lab Australia (industry survey, treated as an institutional voice on the ground)* — The report's own framing captured the cultural mechanism directly: "The pandemic reshaped how Australians work, impacting expectations not only about where and how we work, but also about what we wear" — dress code, in other words, is downstream of a much larger renegotiation of where authority over the workday sits.

> *ClickUp business-etiquette guide, drawing on practitioner accounts of doing business in Paraguay* — Newcomers are told plainly that "your appearance reflects your professionalism and respect for local customs," with the guide's memorable framing that visiting professionals should dress "as if attending a royal tea party" for an ordinary client meeting — advice that reads as hyperbole until you've actually sat across from a Paraguayan counterpart in a suit while wearing chinos.

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Paraguay, the practical move is to buy one excellent formal outfit before you land and wear it more often than instinct would suggest — treat every early meeting as more important than it might actually be, because underdressing there reads as underinvesting. If you're moving to Australia, the practical move is the opposite: watch your specific office for a week before committing to either extreme, because the national reputation for hoodies-at-work is real but sector-dependent, and a law firm in Melbourne will not thank you for testing it. The honest version, the one I'd tell a friend over a drink: in Asunción, overdress and you're safe; in Sydney, ask before you assume — and either way, pack fewer novelty T-shirts than you think you'll need.
