# Kenya Starts the Meeting When Everyone Has Greeted Everyone; the Netherlands Has Already Adjourned

🇰🇪 Kenya · 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Nairobi, the most senior person in the room usually opens the meeting and speaks first, and the agenda is understood to include several minutes of asking after your family. In Amsterdam, the most junior person in the room is expected to say the director's plan has a hole in it, ideally before the director finishes the sentence. Both systems work. Both will also humble a newcomer who wanders in with the other one's habits, and the relocation brochures tend to leave this out in favor of photographs of canals and giraffes.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Open with a firm handshake, eye contact and a "Jambo" or an inquiry about the family, then spend a few minutes on small talk before business | Jump straight to item one on the agenda as though the pleasantries were an optional extra |
| Use titles and surnames (Mr., Dr., Professor) in first meetings and greet the eldest or most senior person first | Use a first name with a director you met four minutes ago |
| Arrive on time, even if the meeting will not start on time | Treat a late start as permission to be late yourself |
| Allow extra commute time, especially in the morning and evening rush | Schedule back-to-back meetings across Nairobi and expect a clean record |
| Expect decisions to involve several stakeholders and follow up politely | Demand an answer in the room, or read slowness as refusal |
| Accept the lunch or dinner invitation; relationships are built there | Eat at your desk and wonder why nobody tells you anything |

### In the Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive on time and expect the agenda to be followed and a conclusion written down | Open with twenty minutes of conversation about your weekend |
| Speak early; meetings are where decisions form, not where they are announced | Offer your objection after consensus has formed (the Dutch have a phrase for it: mustard after the meal) |
| Say what you mean plainly and attach a reason | Hedge until nobody can tell whether you agreed |
| Challenge a senior colleague's idea politely and with evidence | Assume rank will protect your idea from criticism, or theirs from yours |
| Ask outcome-focused questions when feedback stings, such as what would make the proposal stronger | Take bluntness as a personal verdict |
| Use first names quickly and expect to split the bill | Insist on "Meneer" or expect the host to pay |

## Kenya

Kenyan working life runs on a nominal 8am-to-5pm day with an hour for lunch, according to Expat.com's guide to working in the country, which also lists at least 21 days of annual leave and about 12 public holidays. The Kenya National Bureau of Statistics, in a 2016 household survey reported by Business Daily, put average working time at about 35 hours a week including part-timers, below the global 40, with the largest single group working 40 to 48. That figure is old and averages in a great many people who do not work in glass towers. Expat posters describe something closer to nine-hour days with long tea breaks, Friday half-days at some firms, and an expectation that foreign hires will stay later because they are paid more.

The meeting itself is organized around hierarchy and relationship. Expat.com notes that junior staff generally defer to senior management and elders and that titles and full names are the norm at introductions. Cyborlink's business etiquette guide adds that the most senior person present typically leads and speaks first, that foreign visitors are expected to be punctual even though meetings may not start on time, and that decisions frequently consult several stakeholders and take time. Its Hofstede figures for Kenya are East Africa regional estimates (the source says Kenya-specific data is limited), and they are not subtle: power distance 64, individualism 27.

What follows for the newcomer is a form of patience that cannot be hurried. Small talk is not a delay to the meeting; it is the meeting's credentials check. Disagreement tends to be routed through seniority and private conversation, not delivered across the table. In Nairobi's professional circles punctuality is gaining ground, but outside the capital Expat.com cautions that business moves more slowly and lateness is less penalized. Anyone scheduling a Kenyan counterpart for a meeting that must conclude with a decision should budget for the follow-up call, and for the possibility that the follow-up call is where the real decision happens. Expat posters add that the employer matters more than the nation: the same city contains offices run with Swiss precision and offices run on tea, and the interview is the cheapest time to find out which one you have joined.

## Netherlands

The Dutch spend fewer hours at work and expect more from each of them. The OECD's annual-hours series, as tabulated on Wikipedia, shows the Netherlands at 1,436 hours per worker in 2025. The consequence is a culture suspicious of anything that wastes the hours that remain, and a meeting is the most likely thing to be suspected.

According to Undutchables, a Dutch recruitment firm, decisions are typically reached by consensus even where a manager could decide alone, with input gathered from everyone affected whatever their rank. This is the polder model, named after the cooperative management of reclaimed land, and it prizes long-term buy-in over speed. Junior staff may openly challenge directors, the firm says, and the intention is to surface disagreement early. IamExpat's September 2026 guide for newcomers makes the same point from the other side: meetings are where decisions take shape, so contributions made early carry weight and comments made after consensus has formed come too late. The article also warns that Dutch managers give blunt feedback, often without first praising what works, and that the correct response is curiosity, not injury.

The tradeoff is time. Undutchables concedes that the discussion phase can feel slow, with disagreements aired across several sessions, though implementation tends to move faster because everyone has already been heard. Silence is not assumed to mean agreement. The newcomer who sits quietly, nodding in the manner of a polite guest, will be read as having no view or, worse, as having one that will arrive in an email on Friday. Written follow-ups after meetings are common and expected, and Undutchables notes that agreement in this system means everyone had a fair chance to contribute, not that everyone left satisfied. That is a lower bar than it sounds, and a good deal more exhausting to clear.

## The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the gap is mostly about who is permitted to speak and when. Hofstede's scores, as reproduced by Cyborlink, give the Netherlands a power distance of 38, individualism of 80 and a masculinity score of 14; Kenya's East Africa regional figures are 64, 27 and 41. In practice a Kenyan meeting protects the relationship and the hierarchy, and lets the decision emerge from consultation upward. A Dutch meeting protects the argument, and lets the relationship survive it. Each treats the other's habit as a defect: the Dutch read ritual greeting as inefficiency, and Kenyans can read a junior colleague's public objection as an insult.

The shared ground is more modest than either side would like. Both expect punctuality from visitors, both distrust a decision that skipped the right people, and both deliver better results to those who learn the local sequence of events. For a mover, the question is which cost you prefer: patience in the early stages (Kenya) or thick skin in them (Netherlands). Neither route is free, and the invoices are of different sizes.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — A group of Indian expats in Nairobi describe a nominal eight-to-five week with long tea breaks and Friday half-days, then add that foreign staff on higher packages are expected to put in overtime and sometimes weekends.*

> *Expat.com forum — A Swedish intern at a Kenyan charity found business practice closer to the United Kingdom's in systems and language, with a distinctly African relationship to the clock: foreign partners were expected to be punctual, latecomers were forgiven, and some senior people used lateness as a display of dominance over smaller firms.*

> *The Filipino Expat — A Filipina hired to run a Nairobi college discovered that eating lunch with the faculty made them uncomfortable, because superiors were not expected to mingle. Her open forums with students were halted by the owner, and a memo she wrote after a dispute came back torn up. She resigned within three months.*

> *Reddit — On r/Netherlands, commenters swapped views on burnout. One argued that Dutch workplaces have a reputation for being demanding while being fairly relaxed in practice. Another said a partner burned out at the Dutch arm of an American company and that their most relaxed manager had been American, not Dutch.*

> *Substack — An essayist presenting at the Dutch central bank was interrupted mid-slide by a colleague asking why a section was unclear. He braced for embarrassment and instead concluded that he had rarely felt so respected, since feedback arrived immediately and nobody needed to decode a vague email afterward.*

## Conclusion

If you are choosing between the two for your own working sanity, decide first whether you are better at waiting or at being corrected. Kenya rewards the person who builds relationships before asking for decisions, uses titles until invited otherwise, and remembers that a late start is not a late arrival. The Netherlands rewards the person who reads the agenda, speaks in the first third of the meeting, and treats criticism as a service to the work rather than a comment on the worker.

Whichever you pick, ask in the interview who chairs the meeting, who decides afterward, and how feedback is delivered; the answers will tell you more than the careers page. Then take the habits of the other country with you in small doses. A little Kenyan courtesy would improve several Dutch meetings, and a little Dutch candor has been known to shorten a few Kenyan ones. Neither country has yet figured out how to shorten the meeting itself, and the Dutch have at least scheduled the attempt.
