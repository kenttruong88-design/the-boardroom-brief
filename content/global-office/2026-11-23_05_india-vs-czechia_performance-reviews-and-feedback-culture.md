# India Rates Everyone and Czechia Tells No One, and Both Call It Professionalism

🇮🇳 India · 🇨🇿 Czechia
*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In India, the annual appraisal is an event with a calendar, a rating scale and, in many large companies, a distribution curve that has decided how many people may be excellent before anyone has been observed. In Czechia, according to a 2026 Employee Barometer reported by Expats.cz, nearly half of employees do not give their bosses critical feedback directly, and a good number of bosses return the courtesy. One country produces a number you cannot argue with. The other produces a silence you cannot interpret. Both are, in their own way, forms of management.

## Do's & Don'ts

### India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your manager at the start of the cycle what separates a 3 from a 4 | Wait for the year-end meeting to learn the criteria |
| Keep a written record of your achievements, with dates and outcomes | Assume the manager will remember your best quarter in March |
| Raise concerns about a rating in a calm, private conversation | Argue with the rating in front of colleagues |
| Ask what the budget and the distribution look like for your team | Assume a strong year guarantees a strong rating |
| Request feedback twice a month rather than twice a year | Read encouraging words as a promise of a raise |
| Treat the hike as a negotiation that begins before the cycle opens | Treat the cycle as a surprise that arrives on its own |

### Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask directly and regularly how your work is perceived | Assume that silence means approval |
| Present your results to your manager in writing, with figures | Expect effort to be noticed without being reported |
| Disagree in a measured, factual way, preferably in private | Perform enthusiasm you do not feel, which colleagues will notice |
| Notice who is told what, since information tends to travel sideways | Assume that official channels carry the whole story |
| Ask for feedback from an international manager in plain language | Expect indirect hints to be decoded for you |
| Look at what praise looks like in your team before reading it as absent | Read a lack of praise as personal dislike |

## India: the ritual and the curve

The Indian appraisal is usually a formal, scheduled process, and its central feature is differentiation. A February 2026 Business Today feature on bell curves in Indian companies quotes an HR director at Genesys India who argues that the method was built for cost control and not for development. An IT professional at a Bengaluru services firm told the magazine that managers are forced to fit employees into predefined curves even when whole teams are strong. That is the cost of ranking in public: a good team becomes a statistical inconvenience.

The stakes are monetary. Aon reported average pay growth in India of 8.9 percent in 2025, according to YourStory, and Mercer projects around 9 percent for 2026, with individual performance among the top factors shaping increases. Aon India also notes that organisations tying performance closely to rewards give top performers nearly 1.7 times higher fixed pay increases. In effect the rating is a price tag. This helps explain why the conversation about it is rarely relaxed.

The older evidence is less flattering. Gallup's study of Indian employees, based on 2010 data, found that among those who strongly doubted their appraisal system could tell superior performers from others, 51 percent were disengaged, against 2 percent of those who strongly believed it could. Of those who understood how their performance was judged, 66 percent were engaged, against 1 percent of those who did not. The data are old and should be treated as indicative, but the lesson has lasted: Indian employees tolerate a hard ranking more easily than a mysterious one. Hofstede Insights scores India at 77 for power distance, and Czechia at 57, which helps explain why the Indian manager is expected to decide and the Indian employee is expected to accept, at least until the offer from another company arrives. The same source puts individualism at 48 for India and 58 for Czechia, a modest gap, which suggests the more decisive difference lies in how each system handles the awkward conversation rather than in how much people value themselves.

The Business Today piece also cites Betterworks research that 64 percent of employees see performance reviews as a waste of time, which suggests that Indian employees and their Czech counterparts may be less far apart than the formalities imply. The difference is that in India the waste of time is scheduled, minuted and followed by a letter.

## Czechia: the feedback that does not arrive

Czechia has the opposite problem. The Up Benefity barometer found that nearly half of employees do not give critical feedback directly to their bosses. Among those who hold back, almost three in ten had seen honesty backfire, and a similar share doubted it would change anything. Nearly half admitted to shading the truth at work to avoid conflict, and about 40 percent said management sometimes filters or obscures information. Only around one in ten trusted management almost completely. The article describes information moving sideways among colleagues rather than upward, and links the habit partly to a hierarchical management culture with communist-era roots, while noting that it is not unique to Czechia.

Downward feedback is not always more generous. A Czech article from Welcome to the Jungle lists why some managers do not praise: pressure to deliver, a habit of taking work for granted, and the fact that managers are rarely praised themselves. It offers no statistics, only the observation that employees who want recognition should ask for it and, failing that, consider another employer. The Czech Leaders blog, writing for Czech and Slovak managers working with Indian colleagues, makes a symmetrical point from the other side: Indian colleagues are indirect, prefer calm one-to-one conversations, and may offer an agreeable yes that does not always lead to action.

The Czech Leaders blog adds that the ideal Indian boss is a paternal or maternal figure who is competent and also cares about the staff, a role that Indian employees in Czech and Slovak companies often find missing. Where the Indian manager is expected to take an interest in your life, the Czech manager is more likely to take an interest in your deliverable and leave your life to you. Both can be described as respect, though only one involves asking about your family.

The result is a quiet system with few ratings and fewer explanations. The Barometer notes that Czech staff working under international managers may be expected to give direct feedback, which was not always encouraged in more traditional workplaces. That sounds like progress, and probably is, but it also means that the first person to say the unsayable at a Czech meeting is likely to be the foreigner.

## The Reckoning

In India, you will know where you stand, because a number will tell you, and you will probably also know that the number was influenced by a budget. The weakness is that the number often arrives without a conversation, and the conversation, when it arrives, can be a negotiation about money in the guise of development. In Czechia, you may not know where you stand at all. The weakness is that the absence of a rating is not the absence of an opinion, and a Czech manager who says nothing may still be building a view that you will discover only at the next restructuring.

The Indian system rewards visibility, and the Czech system rewards patience. A professional used to the Indian cycle will find Czech reviews vague and slightly alarming. A professional used to Czech reticence will find the Indian cycle theatrical and, depending on the curve, unfair. Neither country has solved the problem of honest feedback; they have simply chosen different ways of avoiding it. The practical consequence is that each newcomer misreads the other system in a predictable direction. The Indian arrival in Prague reads silence as indifference and starts looking for a new job. The Czech arrival in Bangalore reads a rating as an insult and starts looking for the person responsible. Both are acting on reasonable inferences from the wrong rulebook, which is how most workplace misunderstandings begin and, with luck, how a few end.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/developersIndia — A developer with about a year and a half of experience asked how to argue for a raise after a 3 out of 5 and 6 percent last year. A veteran with thirteen years in the field replied that ratings are largely pre-set by a bell curve regardless of effort, and a tech lead said you can usually tell your rating from how your manager treats you through the year. The poster ended up with the same 3 and the same 6 percent.*

> *Blind — An Indian software employee said a delayed appraisal ended with no raise, on the grounds that their salary was already above market for the role, even as they were asked to take on extra work. Commenters suggested requesting specific feedback at least twice a month.*

> *Blind — In a thread weighing a Prague offer against a salary in India, replies called the offer low, warned that savings would be much lower and career options fewer, and said locals can be unfriendly to non-EU newcomers. Others pointed to lifestyle as Prague's main appeal.*

> *Blind — In a discussion of Indian managers, one commenter said some of them were full of encouraging words and short on follow-through, while another, who is Indian, said good and bad managers exist in every background.*

## Conclusion

If you move from India to Czechia, bring a habit of asking questions you would normally consider unnecessary. Ask what good looks like, how you will be told if it is not, and who decides. Expect fewer ratings, less ceremony and a manager who treats absence of complaint as a form of respect. If you move the other way, bring a record of your achievements and a tolerance for the annual spectacle, and learn the shape of the curve before you accept the rating as a verdict on your character.

In both countries, the review will tell you less than the weeks around it. Whatever the formal system, the honest signal is in the small conversations, the speed of replies and the tone in the corridor. One system will give you a grade, the other a shrug, and in either case you will still be working out what your manager actually thinks.
