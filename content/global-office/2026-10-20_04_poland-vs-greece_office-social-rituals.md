# Poland Will See You at Your Desk; Greece Will See You at Lunch for Three Hours

🇵🇱 Poland · 🇬🇷 Greece

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Warsaw, 53% of office workers eat lunch alone at their desks and one in five skips lunch entirely, according to a 2023 Antal and Sodexo survey. In Athens, lunch is a scheduled, unhurried, two-hour social event that a colleague would find it genuinely rude of you to decline. Both countries call themselves relationship-oriented at work. Only one of them means it over an entire plate of moussaka.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept an invitation to "piwo" (a beer) after a shipped project or a win | Assume one round of drinks makes you close friends — trust builds slowly here |
| Bring something sweet on your name day (imieniny); it's the office's default excuse for cake | Expect birthdays to carry the same weight — imieniny matters more |
| Check whether your office treats desk-lunching as normal or frowned upon — it varies by employer | Assume all Polish offices eat alone; some multinational employers actively discourage it |
| Go on the "wyjazd integracyjny" (company integration trip) if invited | Skip it outright — attendance is technically optional but noticeably noted |
| Use formal titles (Pan/Pani) until a colleague invites first names | Overshare personal details early; let colleagues set the pace |
| Keep after-work chat light and work-adjacent at first | Bring up salary, family, or politics before real trust is established |

#### 🇬🇷 Greece

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Block out 2pm–4pm if a colleague proposes lunch — it is not a quick bite | Rush your coffee order or drink it standing at your desk |
| Accept a senior colleague's invitation for coffee or lunch | Decline without a very good reason — it reads as a social snub, not a scheduling choice |
| Expect dinners to run two to three hours and to be mostly about relationship-building | Expect business to be settled at the table; a handshake and a second meeting is standard |
| Join after-work socializing regularly — it's how you become known at the office | Stay visibly reserved or quiet; it reads as standoffish rather than professional |
| Answer personal questions honestly; colleagues ask them to build trust | Treat the questions as prying — refusing to engage closes doors, not just small talk |
| Greet each colleague individually with "kaliméra" or "kalispéra" | Give one group greeting and consider the ritual done |

### 🇵🇱 Poland

Polish office sociability runs on a narrow but real rail: colleagues are warm once trust is earned, and trust is earned slowly, with lunch rarely the place it happens. The 2023 Antal and Sodexo survey of over 1,000 employees found 53% of Polish office workers eat alone at their desks — rising to roughly 60% among managers and directors — and one in five skips a lunch break altogether. Despite averaging 41.1 hours a week, per the Eurostat figure the same study cites, the shared midday ritual anchoring Greek office life barely registers here; eating is something to get through, not a reason to leave your screen.

The real socializing happens after hours, and it is more structured — and more optional — than it looks. Mid-sized and larger companies often run "integration trips" (wyjazd integracyjny): weekend retreats, wellness getaways, or simple post-launch dinners, with attendance encouraged but technically voluntary. Informal invitations — "idziemy na piwo?", shall we get a beer — follow milestones like a shipped project, a birthday, or a promotion. On Hofstede Insights' individualism dimension, Poland scores 60 against Greece's 35, and the texture shows: Polish colleagues protect personal time and build friendships deliberately, one shared beer at a time, rather than assuming closeness by default. The same reserve shows up in how people address each other: formal titles, Pan and Pani, persist well past the point many Western European offices would have dropped to first names, and dropping them yourself is a decision colleagues notice.

None of this reads as coldness once you have been in the building a few months. The Antal and Sodexo survey also found that nearly 40% of employees snack at their desks through the day, more often among managers than junior staff, which suggests the appetite for company is there — it is simply rerouted away from a shared sit-down meal and into smaller, more frequent, less ceremonial breaks. A colleague who has never once suggested lunch together may still show up, unprompted, to help you move apartments.

What catches newcomers off guard is less the formality than its unevenness. A Goldman Sachs employee profile of its Warsaw office notes it is "definitely frowned upon to eat lunch at your desk" there — a norm flatly at odds with the Antal data, suggesting the eating-alone habit is a default at many domestic employers that some multinational offices are actively trying to engineer out. Meanwhile name days, not birthdays, are the standard excuse for office cake, and WhatsApp group chats hum with logistics far more than personal disclosure. The net effect: colleagues who are reliably decent, slow to open up, and only occasionally interested in spending their lunch hour with you.

### 🇬🇷 Greece

If Poland treats lunch as an inconvenience to clear, Greece treats it as a load-bearing institution. Business lunches commonly run from 2pm to 4pm and dinners from around 8pm onward, both stretching two to three hours and built explicitly around relationship-building before any business gets discussed, per the standard Greek meeting-etiquette guides. Skipping a colleague's invitation to coffee reads as impolite rather than efficient; accepting a superior's offer to talk over a freddo espresso functions closer to a professional obligation than a break.

This sociability sits on top of genuinely long hours. Eurostat data for Q2 2025 put 20.9% of Greek employees working more than 45 hours a week — nearly double the EU average of 10.8% — and Greece had the bloc's lowest share of part-time workers, at 6.1% against an EU average of 16.9%. Greeks are not socializing instead of working; they are doing both, for longer, in the same stretch of day. Hofstede Insights scores Greek individualism at 35, far more collectivist than Poland's 60, and the office mirrors it: eating alone at your desk is treated less as a personal preference than a small social failure, and quiet newcomers are actively nudged to open up.

The paradox is that all this closeness coexists with measurable strain. Gallup's 2025 State of the Global Workplace found 14% of Greek employees engaged at work — technically above Poland's 7%, though both trail the European average of 12% and 20% globally by wide margins — while 61% of Greek workers reported significant stress the previous day, nearly triple Poland's 22% and well above Europe's 39% average. Daily loneliness, notably, runs at 19% in Greece versus 6% in Poland and 13% across Europe, which is the one Gallup figure the long-lunch model would predict running the other way. Long lunches and loyal colleagues, in other words, do not add up to a relaxed office; they are simply where Greeks are stressed in company rather than alone, and apparently still lonely at a higher rate than their famously undemonstrative Polish counterparts.

### The Reckoning

Pick Poland if you want colleagues who respect your boundaries by default and would rather not hear about your divorce until month six: the eating-alone rate is high, the lunch hour is genuinely yours to spend as you like, and the "voluntary" integration trip is mostly that. Pick Greece if you want to be folded into the group whether or not you asked for it: lunch is communal by expectation, coffee invitations are near-mandatory, and solitude at your desk is read as a quiet cry for help.

Neither produces a visibly happier workforce by Gallup's numbers — Poland's calm looks closer to disengagement, Greece's warmth closer to exhaustion — but the social cost of newcomer awkwardness differs sharply between them. In Warsaw, failing to bond over lunch costs you nothing you'll notice for months. In Athens, it can cost you your office reputation by Thursday.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit — on a thread about Polish corporate work culture, commenters describe much of it as "toxic and top-down," define work-life balance as "how much personal business you can handle during office hours," and say colleagues are friendly on the surface but too self-focused for real friendships to form.*

> *Blind — on a discussion thread about Google's Kraków office, one commenter warns that "unless you are European, moving to Poland is rough socially," describing the city as striking to look at but not especially easy for an outsider building a social life from scratch.*

> *EuropeLanguageJobs.com — Marco, an Italian who relocated for work in Greece, in an interview series: "The workplace naturally becomes your first social circle," he says, noting that after a shift "you instantly have people to grab dinner or coffee with."*

> *Goldman Sachs careers blog — Carolina, describing her move to the firm's Warsaw office, notes it's "definitely frowned upon to eat lunch at your desk" there, a habit worth unlearning before day one even though deskbound lunches are the norm at many other Polish employers.*

> *Kraków Post — Effie Eleftheriadou, writing as part of Kraków's Greek expat community about relocating from Greece to Poland, says "we are respected at the workplace, have good relations with our colleagues and supervisors," a warmer reception than she expected from Poland's reputation for office reserve.*

### Conclusion

If it comes down to how you want to spend your lunch hour, it is really a question of what kind of attention you want from strangers who are about to become your colleagues. Poland will let you eat in peace and build friendships at the pace of a glacier inching toward a shared beer; Greece will seat you at the table whether or not you ordered anything, and expect a straight answer about your weekend by the second course. Either way you'll work long hours — Greece's are simply longer, and spent more often with company.

The brochure calls both "relationship-oriented cultures." One of them just means it more literally, and over a considerably longer lunch.
