**America Built a Personality Test Out of Birth Years. Malta Just Wants to Know How Old You Are.**

🇺🇸 United States · 🇲🇹 Malta

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In the United States, four generations now share the same open-plan floor, each one quietly convinced the other three have ruined the concept of a job: Boomers think everyone under forty-five is fragile, Gen Z thinks everyone over forty-five is surveilling them through a desk phone, and Gallup's own engagement numbers suggest both have a point, except about the Boomers, who since 2020 are the only cohort to have gotten more engaged at work, not less. Six thousand kilometres away, on an archipelago of roughly half a million people where your manager plausibly knows your mother, Malta has arrived at a tidier solution: nobody's generational grievance matters much until they have put in enough years to outrank it. Two countries, two very different ways of making junior staff feel unheard. This guide prepares you for both.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 United States

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask your manager directly whether they prefer Slack, email, or a call — assumptions break along generational lines | Assume a Gen X manager wants to run a mentorship programme; many expect you to figure it out and report back |
| Name your preferred feedback cadence in week one — it is now a normal ask, not a red flag | Book a "quick sync" call for something that fits in two sentences of chat — someone, somewhere, will resent it |
| Ask plainly about flexible hours and mental health days | Joke about "quiet quitting" without knowing your audience; Boomers may hear confirmation, Gen Z may hear solidarity |
| Expect to be asked your pronouns and to offer them in return | Treat a 9-to-5 as binding law without checking whether your team actually runs on outcomes instead of hours |
| Raise pay transparency questions early — younger colleagues increasingly do | Mistake a casual dress code for a casual chain of command |

#### 🇲🇹 Malta

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn your manager's first name and use it; informality is expected once you're "in" | Leave at 5pm sharp during probation without first checking whether unpaid overtime is the unwritten norm there |
| Accept being asked your age, your parents' jobs, and your relationship status as ordinary small talk | Mistake a loud, joke-heavy office for a flat hierarchy — job titles still carry real weight |
| Bring a small gift from home when visiting a Maltese business contact | Assume younger Maltese colleagues share your urgency about job-hopping; many still weigh stability highly |
| Use instant messaging for quick questions with younger colleagues, but default to a phone call or face-to-face with senior staff | Push back publicly on a senior figure's decision in a meeting; raise it privately instead |
| Ask early about the real promotion timeline, since senior roles are often held for decades | Expect open-plan offices to mean open-door management; seniority still decides who gets heard first |

### United States: Four Generations, One Spreadsheet, Zero Agreement

The American workforce is not actually that old, whatever LinkedIn implies. Bureau of Labor Statistics projections show workers aged 55 to 64 made up 16.3 percent of the labour force in 2023, a share expected to fall slightly to 15.2 percent by 2033, while the 16-to-54 band still accounts for roughly three in four workers. The problem was never the headcount. It was Gallup's 2024 engagement data, which found the share of actively engaged Gen Z workers sliding from 40 percent to 35 percent since the pandemic began, and older Millennials falling even further, from 39 percent to 32 percent. Baby Boomers, uniquely, became more engaged over the same period; six in ten now say they know exactly what's expected of them at work, compared with fewer than half of their younger colleagues, which flatters the Boomers rather less than it indicts a system still organised around them.

That system shows its seams in a Korn Ferry survey of 15,000 employees, reported by Fortune, in which 45 percent of Boomers said they have no trouble working across generations, versus just 17 percent of Gen Z. Nearly half of Gen Z wanted formal communication and teamwork training; barely a quarter of Boomers saw any problem worth training for. More than a third of Millennials described a genuine clash in workplace values with older colleagues, against roughly a quarter of Boomers who agreed. A shrinking layer of middle management, the report notes, has left senior leaders increasingly disconnected from the junior staff actually doing the work.

None of this is really a mystery to the junior staff. Deloitte's 2025 Global Gen Z and Millennial Survey found that roughly half of both generations want a manager who actively mentors them, and barely a third report getting one. Combine that with the finding that 89 percent of Gen Z and 92 percent of Millennials say a sense of purpose matters to their job satisfaction, and the American workplace starts to look less like a generational war and more like a long-running, mutually unacknowledged mismatch between what one side is asking for and what the other side assumed it was already providing.

### Malta: Hierarchy Doesn't Care What Year You Were Born

Malta's generational story starts with a quieter crisis than America's: there may simply not be enough of the next generation. GRS Recruitment, a Malta-based recruiter, points to fertility rates that no longer replace the workforce and migration that isn't bringing in younger professionals fast enough, concentrating the strain in engineering, healthcare, and finance, where senior staff are staying in post longer and leaving fewer promotions behind them. Hofstede Insights' cultural scoring helps explain why this lands so differently than it would in America: Malta sits markedly lower on individualism and higher on power distance than the United States, meaning deference to seniority is less a generational preference than a structural default everyone, eventually, inherits.

A joint study by the 3CL Foundation and Malta's National Skills Council, built from focus groups spanning ages 18 to 65, found that most friction had nothing to do with age at all — it came from unstated assumptions about which channel to use, who learns how, and what counts as professional conduct. Younger staff favoured instant messaging and flexible, self-paced online learning; older staff preferred a phone call and structured, in-person instruction, with disengagement among older participants linked more to fear of failure than any aversion to new tools. Values split along familiar lines, with older generations prizing loyalty and job security and younger ones prioritising fulfilment, autonomy, and financial wellbeing, a shift focus group participants described not as disloyalty but as a rational recalibration for a less secure economy, home ownership anxiety chief among their stated worries.

Deloitte's global survey — republished by its Malta office without a separate local breakdown — still frames the island's stakes accurately enough: the same desire for mentorship, the same gap between what's wanted and what's delivered, applies here too, layered onto an office culture where, as the accounts below suggest, hierarchy fills in wherever mentorship doesn't.

### The Reckoning

Here is the first irony: America's most engaged generation and Malta's most comfortable employees are, by different routes, the same type of person — someone who has simply outlasted everyone junior to them. Gallup shows Boomers thriving in a system built decades ago to their specifications; Malta's hierarchy rewards patience over initiative in much the same way, just without the engagement surveys to document it. Seniority, it turns out, is a universal cheat code; the countries just disagree on how loudly you're allowed to complain before you earn it.

Here is the second: American generational conflict is loud, surveyed, and consultant-fed because US workplace culture rewards naming your grievance out loud, in a town hall, with a slide deck. Malta's version is quieter and more structural, buried in who gets asked to stay late without pay and whose decision doesn't get questioned in the room — not because younger Maltese workers feel less friction, but because, per that same lower individualism score, airing it individually is less rewarded than simply changing employers, which Malta's own tight labour market appears to let them do anyway.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — A tech worker described their Gen X manager as responsive, big-picture, and refreshingly hands-off, while a Millennial manager meant well but was often pulled into personal matters during work hours and kept changing direction; both, maddeningly, defaulted to oversized synchronous meetings nobody had asked for.*

> *Blind — Several commenters on a thread comparing managers by generation pushed back on the entire premise, arguing that calling generation a "management style" is itself a form of ageism, and that a manager's effectiveness has far more to do with the individual than their birth year.*

> *Expat.com forum (Malta) — A long-term expat described the office as deeply hierarchical, with younger staff routinely on the receiving end of pranks, and the general atmosphere closer to "1950s Mad Men" than anything resembling a flat modern workplace.*

> *Expat.com forum (Malta) — One poster recalled a manager standing over their shoulder during a probation period, tapping a watch after they stayed ninety minutes late, and said resigning on the spot after the argument that followed was not an unusual outcome in that kind of environment.*

> *Expat.com forum (Malta) — A British arrival said colleagues seemed oddly fixated on exactly how old everyone was, that job titles carried outsized social weight, and that some employers treated unpaid overtime as an unwritten condition of the job.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing a country based on how it handles generational friction, be honest about what you're actually choosing between. American workplaces argue about it constantly, in the open, with Gallup numbers and Korn Ferry decks to back up every side — which means your grievance, whatever generation you belong to, will at least get named out loud, even if it doesn't get fixed. Malta absorbs the same friction quietly into its hierarchy, where it surfaces less as a survey finding and more as who gets handed the good project, who gets tapped on the shoulder for working late, and who simply leaves for a better offer instead of filing a complaint about it.

Pick America if you want your generational complaint taken seriously in a meeting that changes nothing. Pick Malta if you'd rather it stay unspoken but be reflected, with total consistency, in who gets the Christmas whiskey and who gets the closing shift. Either way: bring patience. Nobody under fifty, in either country, has been issued any.
