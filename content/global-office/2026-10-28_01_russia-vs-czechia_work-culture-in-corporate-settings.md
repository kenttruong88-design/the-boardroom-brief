**Nobody Here Disagrees With the Boss — They Just Quietly Do Something Else**

🇷🇺 Russia · 🇨🇿 Czechia

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Both of these offices will nod along in a meeting and then do whatever they were already planning to do. The difference is what happens next: in Moscow, that quiet redirection is a long-standing survival skill around a genuinely autocratic chain of command; in Prague, it is a mild, almost apologetic dodge around a boss nobody wants to embarrass. Average annual working hours tell their own story too — Russians clock roughly 2,087 hours a year against Czechs' 1,788, according to figures compiled from national statistical offices (Wikipedia's OECD-sourced labor-hours comparison, 2023) — which is to say: pick your hierarchy, but budget your evenings accordingly.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇷🇺 Russia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a personal relationship with your manager before expecting any flexibility | Challenge your manager's decision in front of the team |
| Address senior colleagues formally (first name + patronymic) until invited otherwise | Assume a lack of visible reaction means there's no objection |
| Expect decisions to move top-down and plan your week around waiting for them | Expect meaningful delegation in your first few months |
| Treat an invitation to dinner, a banya, or after-work drinks as real relationship-building, not optional extras | Skip the informal socializing — it's where the actual trust gets built |
| Raise disagreement with a superior privately, one-on-one | Push an idea through the "official" channel and ignore the unofficial one that actually moves things |
| Keep a paper trail — verbal agreements shift more often than written ones | Assume a handshake settles anything without a signed document behind it |

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn basic Czech greetings even if the office runs in English | Skip "dobrý den" and go straight to business — it reads as rude |
| Dress on the conservative, ironed side of business casual until you've read the room | Expect instant warmth — Czech colleagues often take months to loosen up |
| Raise criticism of a decision privately, not in the open meeting | Openly contradict a manager's call in front of colleagues |
| Expect a three-to-six-month probation period, longer for management roles | Assume probation is a formality — it isn't always |
| Plan around "cucumber season" (July to early September), when half the office is on holiday and auto-replies run the place | Schedule anything urgent for late July and expect a same-day answer |
| Accept that a beer at lunch is normal in many offices, not a red flag | Mistake quiet, formal colleagues for unfriendly ones |

### Russia

Russian corporate culture runs on a hierarchy that is less a management style than a load-bearing structure. Hofstede's widely cited cultural-dimensions index places Russia among the most hierarchical societies it measures, with a power-distance score in the low 90s — a number that shows up in practice as management guides describing bosses as "directive" rather than "consultative," expected to hold their authority rather than share it (Commisceo Global's Russia Management Guide). Subordinates generally don't challenge a manager's call in public, since doing so costs the manager face as much as the argument itself.

What foreign hires consistently underestimate is how much of the real decision-making happens off the agenda. Antal Russia's commercial director sums up the paradox bluntly: "in the West, everything works but nothing happens, whereas in Russia it is the exact opposite" — procedures look loose, but things still get done, often through relationships rather than process (The Moscow Times, via Antal Russia commentary). Corporate expats are frequently parachuted into senior roles specifically to install a Western company's house culture onto local staff before rotating out, which tells you something about how slowly that culture actually sticks (The Moscow Times, 2015).

On pure hours, the OECD-adjacent labor-hours data is unambiguous: Russian workers put in roughly 300 more hours a year than their Czech counterparts. Combine that with a hierarchy where initiative isn't rewarded the way deference is, and you get an office culture that runs long, not necessarily hard.

### Czechia

Czech corporate culture inherited its own hierarchy, but from a different source: four decades of top-down, mistake-averse, communist-era management, whose residue still shapes who feels safe disagreeing with whom. A 2026 Employee Barometer survey from Up Benefity found that nearly half of Czech employees don't give their bosses critical feedback directly — they raise it with colleagues, keep it private, or use anonymous channels instead, citing fear that honesty will cost them standing, bonuses, or the relationship itself.

That caution runs both directions. The same survey found roughly 40% of employees believe management filters information to suit itself, and only about one in ten trust management "almost completely." Research published in the *Journal of World Business* adds an uncomfortable wrinkle: leaders from the US and Western Europe are sometimes rated more favorably than equally qualified Czech, Polish, or Albanian leaders doing the same job — a bias that cuts against local managers trying to build the open-disagreement culture everyone claims to want.

Day to day, this shows up as formality with a long fuse: colleagues keep surnames and titles even after years of working together, lunch frequently includes a beer without anyone remarking on it, and many offices still observe "cucumber season" — the July-to-September stretch when half the building is on holiday and auto-reply has become the dominant form of communication (Expats.cz). None of it reads as hostile once you've been there six months. It reads that way in week one.

### The Reckoning

Here's the twist: both cultures score as hierarchical on paper, but the lived experience diverges sharply. Russian hierarchy is loud and personal — it runs on relationships, favors, and a boss whose authority you build around, not against. Czech hierarchy is quiet and procedural — it runs on formality and the avoidance of open conflict, a legacy of a system where being wrong in public once carried real professional risk. An American manager used to "flat" org charts will find both exhausting, but for opposite reasons: Russia demands you invest in the person at the top; Czechia demands you never put the person at the top in an awkward position.

The other irony is hours. The country with the louder, more overtly authoritarian management style (Russia) also logs the longer workweek, while the quieter, more conflict-avoidant one (Czechia) logs fewer annual hours than the OECD average for the region. Deference, it turns out, is not the same thing as overtime — it's just a different currency for the same transaction.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind (Yandex employee reviews) — One Software Engineer II described the experience bluntly as "Almost no WLB," adding that at review time you have to "always push yourself" to land a good rating — a frustration several reviewers echoed, though others on different teams reported the opposite extreme entirely.*

> *Blind (Yandex employee reviews) — Not every review was grim: one Mobile Applications Developer called it "one of the best" working cultures they'd found in Russia, and multiple reviewers singled out the office itself — unprompted — as a genuine highlight of the job, a detail that surprised more than one reviewer who'd expected the opposite from a Russian tech employer.*

> *Internations Moscow — The group's longtime Moscow ambassador, Peter Richter, described watching the local chapter grow from 389 members to more than 13,000, noting that events built around "mingling" quietly became the place where real business networking happened — business cards appearing only once the small talk had run its course, a sequence newcomers who skip the small talk tend to get backwards.*

> *Expats.cz (reader account, Prague) — An American research associate named Laura found her Czech colleagues standoffish for the first couple of months, only to have them start stopping by her desk to chat once they'd decided she was staying. She never felt singled out for being the only American in an all-Czech office — just slowly, eventually, let in.*

> *Expats.cz (reader account, Prague) — A former English instructor named Anna learned the formality rules the hard way: dressed conservatively to teach a business class, she was told by a director her style was "too independent" and was quietly removed from the assignment — a reminder that "formal" in a Czech office can mean something more specific than it sounds.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two postings on work culture alone, the honest framing isn't "which is friendlier" — it's "which currency do you want to pay in." Russia will ask you to invest in people, read a room that runs on unspoken rank, and work more hours while you do it. Czechia will ask you to be patient, formal, and comfortable with colleagues who disagree with you by saying nothing at all — and will hand back roughly 300 fewer hours a year for the trouble. Neither is the relaxed European office of the brochure. Pick the one whose silence you'd rather learn to read.
