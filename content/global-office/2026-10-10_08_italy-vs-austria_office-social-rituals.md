**Italy vs. Austria: One Office Bonds Over Espresso and Favors, the Other Over Table Football and a Hard Stop at 5pm**

**🇮🇹 Italy · 🇦🇹 Austria**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In an Italian office, skipping the post-lunch espresso with colleagues doesn't just mean you miss some gossip — it can read as outright coldness, the kind of social misstep that quietly costs you the relationships your career actually runs on. In an Austrian office, you can skip the coffee break, lose at table football, and call your boss by his first name, and none of it will matter nearly as much as being out the door by the time Friday afternoon officially ends.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇹 Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join colleagues for the morning espresso and the post-lunch caffè at the bar counter | Skip the coffee ritual repeatedly — it reads as coldness, not efficiency |
| Treat lunch as a real, sit-down meal away from your screen, ideally 60-90 minutes | Eat a packed salad at your desk while answering emails |
| Use the formal "Lei" until a senior colleague invites you to switch to "tu" | Switch to informal address before you've been invited to |
| Expect the office itself to function as a major social hub, central to career advancement | Assume all your networking should happen outside the building |
| Plan around the "chiusura estiva" — many companies fully close two to four weeks around August 15 | Schedule anything requiring Italian colleagues' availability in mid-to-late August |
| Address colleagues with a university degree as "Dottore" or "Dottoressa," even outside medicine | Assume professional titles are reserved only for doctors |

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to play table football; it's a genuine, respected way to socialise and build rapport | Dismiss the office table-football table as a toy rather than a social institution |
| Use "Sie" with superiors until real familiarity develops, even as peers use "Du" freely | Assume first-name informality with a boss signals weak hierarchy rather than trust |
| Expect colleagues to actually leave when the official workday ends, especially Friday afternoons | Expect anyone to answer work messages once Feierabend has started |
| Bring cake (and sometimes schnapps) on your birthday — it's a genuine workplace expectation | Let your birthday pass at the office without treating colleagues |
| Take advantage of flexible start times and part-time arrangements if your role allows it | Assume part-time work or a 90% contract signals lower commitment |
| Treat results, not visible hours at your desk, as what colleagues and bosses actually judge | Perform presenteeism by staying late when you're sick or simply out of work to do |

Italy's office rituals run on the idea that the workplace itself is a social institution, not just a place you clock into. One widely cited observation from The Local puts it plainly: for many Italians, the office functions as their most important social hub after family, and a meaningful share of a career gets built through networking and personal relationships rather than pure output — captured in the saying "le conoscenze contano" (knowing the right people counts). The daily rhythm reflects that: a morning espresso at the bar counter, a real sit-down lunch of 60 to 90 minutes away from the desk, and a post-lunch caffè, all genuinely social rather than merely caloric. Skipping them repeatedly doesn't read as productivity — it reads as standoffishness. Formality still structures all of it: colleagues use the formal "Lei" until a senior person invites "tu," and anyone with a university degree gets addressed as "Dottore" or "Dottoressa" in speech and in writing, a small but constant reminder that status is tracked even inside a famously warm office culture.

Austria's office rituals run on a near-opposite logic: socialising happens readily, but it stops hard at a line nobody's supposed to cross. The Local's own list of things to know before starting an Austrian office job highlights table football as a genuine social institution — many employees grew up playing it, tables are common in break rooms, and being decent at it earns real respect, alongside the all-purpose "Mahlzeit" greeting that's drifted well beyond actual mealtimes. Birthdays come with a mild social tax: the birthday person is expected to bring in cake, and sometimes schnapps, for colleagues. None of that extends the workday, though. Journalist Maddy French, reflecting on nearly a decade in Vienna for The Local, recalled trying to interview someone at 3pm on a summer afternoon only to be told flatly that they'd already gone home — a work-life boundary she contrasts with the long-hours culture she'd known in London.

**The Reckoning.** Austria holds the lowest Power Distance score of any country in Hofstede's original dataset, at 11 — and the office culture matches: flat, first-name, trust-based, and organised around results rather than visible hierarchy. Italy's Power Distance sits at a middling 50, with Individualism considerably higher at 76 versus Austria's 55 — producing a workplace that's simultaneously more personally networked (my relationships matter) and more formally ranked (your title matters) than Austria's at the same time. Axelle Gasne, a French writer who spent nearly eight years working in Vienna, described being sent to represent her 200-person R&D department at a meeting with Austria's Interior Minister within six months of joining, and watching a 24-year-old colleague get handed a €200,000 project with the explicit understanding that results mattered, not the method used to get there — a degree of flat, trust-first delegation that would be unusual inside Italy's more status-conscious, title-driven office hierarchy.

The practical cost shows up on opposite ends of each ritual. Italy's office-as-social-hub model has a dark side: InterNations' Expat Insider survey, reported by The Local, ranked Italy 52nd of 53 countries on its Working Abroad Index and dead last worldwide for job satisfaction, with respondents specifically citing a business culture that "does not support creativity, flexibility, or independent work" — the flip side of a system where who you know at the espresso bar can matter more than what you produce. Austria's hard-stop culture has its own cost: the rituals that do exist (table football, birthday cake, a shared beer after work) are genuinely optional add-ons to a system built around leaving on time, which is lovely for work-life balance and less lovely if you were hoping the office would function as your primary social circle the way it does for many Italians.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum, "The work culture in Austria" thread — A poster going by Vanessa2909 noted that in her sector it's common for employers to contribute up to €8 a day toward lunch, since the benefit is tax-free for the company, while another poster, Keloy, said their own employer offered no free lunch at all — both agreeing that such perks vary entirely by employer and are often negotiable rather than standard.*

> *Welcome to the Jungle — Axelle Gasne described calling her Austrian boss, Hans, by his first name as an early shock after a formal French upbringing, and recalled proposing an internal "Science and Technology for Dummies" seminar series — promoted by walking the office dressed as a giant yellow book — that her boss simply approved and let run successfully for six months.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A junior data scientist posting about Italy's tech sector said net salaries for software engineers and data scientists often land around €1,300-1,500 a month against roughly €700 rent in big cities, and described taking the internship despite it paying less per hour than his previous lifeguarding job; a commenter who'd spent six years in the UK before returning advised against accepting "standard Italian company deals" at all if a remote role at an international firm was on the table.*

> *The Local, "Seven things that surprised me about Austrians" — Journalist Maddy French, after years in Vienna, wrote that Austrians have a reputation for being grumpy with strangers but become genuinely warm and helpful once you've earned their trust — a slower social on-ramp than Italy's more immediately convivial office style, but one she found held up once it formed.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two office cultures, the honest question is what kind of social contract you want with your job. Italy offers a workplace that will genuinely absorb you — espresso rituals, long lunches, a real social world inside the building — in exchange for navigating real hierarchy and accepting that who you know will shape your career as much as what you do. Austria offers a workplace that will respect your time with almost monastic discipline — table football and birthday cake included, overtime emails excluded — in exchange for a social life that, however warm once earned, mostly waits for you outside the office door.

Here's what I'd tell a friend: if you want your job to double as your social life, go to Italy and learn to love a 90-minute lunch. If you want your job to leave you alone by 5pm so you can go build a social life somewhere else, go to Austria and get good at table football while you wait for Friday afternoon.
