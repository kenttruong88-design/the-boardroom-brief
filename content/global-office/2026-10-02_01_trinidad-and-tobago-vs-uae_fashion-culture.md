**Linen Diplomacy: Dressing for Work in Trinidad and the UAE**

**🇹🇹 Trinidad and Tobago · 🇦🇪 UAE**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Port of Spain, a government worker can now show up in torn jeans and slippers without being sent home — a 2024 policy change that one ministry described, un-ironically, as replacing "archaic and rigid" rules with a standard of mere "decency." In Dubai, the decency standard runs the opposite direction: a bank reportedly cancelled Casual Friday altogether because employees kept arriving in track pants. Between these two poles sits the entire question of what a newly arrived professional should actually put on in the morning, and nobody is handing out a syllabus.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇹 Trinidad and Tobago

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep a blazer at your desk for client meetings even if the office has gone casual | Assume the 2024 "decency" relaxation applies to your workplace — most private offices never adopted it |
| Invest in breathable fabrics; air conditioning is inconsistent outside major firms | Wear swimwear, sheer fabric, or anything bearing "obscene" prints — these remain explicitly banned even under the relaxed code |
| Dress up (not down) for first meetings with government ministries or regulators | Expect a uniform national standard — Trinidad's dress code is set office by office, not by law |
| Watch what senior colleagues wear on Fridays before copying the "casual" cue | Show up underdressed to a business lunch — hospitality and finance still run formal |
| Keep an umbrella and a backup shirt for sudden tropical downpours | Over-layer for "winter" — there isn't one; dress for humidity year-round |

#### 🇦🇪 UAE

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to a collared shirt and trousers for men, knee-length or longer hemlines for women | Wear shorts, sleeveless tops, or anything sheer to a client-facing role, even in 45°C heat |
| Choose natural fibers — cotton, linen, wool — to survive both the heat and the AC | Assume "startup casual" from home translates; even relaxed UAE offices expect tidy and conservative |
| Keep shoulders and upper arms covered in mixed-gender meetings | Get tattoos, piercings, or hair color that reads as unconventional in banking, consultancy, or luxury retail |
| Pack one formal suit regardless of role — government and client meetings can summon it without notice | Treat Ramadan as dress-code-neutral — modesty expectations tighten visibly during the holy month |
| Ask HR for the written dress code on day one — many contracts specify it in detail | Confuse mall or beach norms with office norms — the UAE runs by context, not by one blanket rule |

### Trinidad and Tobago: the ministry that un-clenched

Trinidad and Tobago's 2024 relaxation of its government dress code was less a fashion statement than an efficiency measure: ministries had been turning away citizens over sleeveless shirts and open-toed sandals, and officials eventually concluded that "archaic and rigid" enforcement was costing more in service delays than it was buying in decorum, according to reporting in Trinidad and Tobago's *Newsday*. Vests, torn jeans, and slippers are now technically permitted at a government counter; swimwear, bare torsos, and gang symbols are not, and the final word still sits with whichever official is standing at the door.

Private offices never got the memo, or rather, they wrote their own. Hospitality, banking, and energy — the twin-island republic's signature sector, home to its oil and gas majors — still run a formal dress code that would be recognizable in London or Houston, just adapted for humidity. The island's professional class dresses for air conditioning that frequently fails rather than air conditioning that works, which in practice means fewer wool suits and more breathable blends, and a general tolerance for loosening the tie the moment the client leaves the room. There is no single Trinidadian standard; there is a patchwork of office cultures, and the burden of reading the room falls entirely on the new arrival.

### UAE: the most codified wardrobe in the Gulf

The UAE's dress expectations are, by contrast, closer to law than suggestion, and the newcomer's safest assumption is that almost every employer has written them down somewhere. The Global Labour Market Migration programme's demographic data puts the UAE's resident population at roughly 90 percent foreign-born, and the federal workforce correspondingly runs on imported professionals who are, nonetheless, expected to dress as though they had been raised on local modesty norms. Recruiters quoted in the *Khaleej Times* describe companies building dress-code clauses directly into employment contracts specifically because a multicultural workforce cannot be trusted to converge on the same unwritten standard — tattoos, unconventional piercings, and neon hair all surface as named no-gos in banking, consultancy, and luxury retail.

What's distinctive is how much of the code is climate-adjacent rather than purely religious: cotton, wool, and linen are recommended not for modesty but because temperatures climbing past 45°C in summer make synthetic fabric actively dangerous. Hofstede Insights scores the UAE high on power distance and relatively low on individualism — a profile consistent with offices where the dress code is handed down rather than negotiated, and where deviating from it reads less as self-expression than as a breach of hierarchy.

### The Reckoning

The irony is that Trinidad, a nominally more relaxed and Western-aligned culture, now has the more *legally* liberal dress code on paper — slippers at a government counter are permitted in Port of Spain in a way they would never be in Abu Dhabi — while the UAE, frequently caricatured by outsiders as rigidly traditional, actually runs a more climate-rational wardrobe logic once you look past the modesty requirement. Both systems converge on the same practical truth: dress is a hierarchy signal first and a comfort decision second, and the newcomer who treats it as either purely cultural or purely climatic will misread the room in both places.

The other inversion: Trinidad's casualization was a bottom-up reform (citizens rebelling against being turned away at the counter), while the UAE's formality is largely top-down and contractual. One country loosened its dress code because enforcement was expensive; the other tightened its because ambiguity, in a workforce where nine in ten people were born elsewhere, was expensive. Neither country dresses for comfort. Both dress for control — just different kinds.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *FlyerTalk Middle East forum — A traveler preparing for a business trip to Dubai asked whether "khakis and tie" would suffice for a professional seminar, and was told that outside of government meetings requiring a full suit, most office days are spent in air-conditioned buildings where the heat barely registers — the real wardrobe planning happens for the ten minutes between the car and the lobby.*

> *ExpatExchange (Dubai culture-shock report) — An American relocating to Dubai described her early instinct to over-explain her clothing choices to local women, until she realized the stares went both ways — her advice to future arrivals was simply to dress conservatively by default and save swimwear-adjacent outfits for designated beach clubs, not errands.*

> *ExpatExchange (Port of Spain culture-shock report) — A long-term expat in Trinidad, 1.5 years in, noted she had still never been invited to a Trinidadian colleague's home for anything but a business function, and connected it to a wider pattern: professional life and family life stay in separate, dressed-differently compartments that outsiders rarely get asked to cross.*

> *TripAdvisor Trinidad and Tobago forum — A family relocating for a year asked practical questions about apartment costs and safety rather than office norms at all, a reminder from one respondent that dress code barely registers on most newcomers' worry list until the first client meeting is already on the calendar.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Trinidad, pack for your specific office, not for the island — the 2024 relaxation is a government-counter story, not a universal one, and finance and energy firms will still expect a blazer. If you're moving to the UAE, pack for the contract — read the dress-code clause before you read the salary clause, because it will govern more of your daily friction than almost anything else in the offer letter. The honest version of the advice, the one I'd give a friend over a drink: in Trinidad, dress for the client in front of you; in the UAE, dress for the law behind the client. Either way, bring more linen than you think you need.
