**On Paper, Nepal Gives New Mothers 14 Weeks; In Practice, 70% of Employers Give Them One or Two**

**🇳🇵 Nepal · 🇪🇨 Ecuador**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Nepal's maternity leave law reads reasonably generously until you learn that most private employers simply don't follow it. Ecuador's law is more generous still, and — by multiple independent accounts — is actually followed, backed by a culture that treats children as a shared social good rather than a private inconvenience. If you're relocating specifically because you're planning a family, the gap between these two countries' paper policies and lived reality is the whole story.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇵 Nepal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your employer's actual maternity leave practice in writing before you need it — statutory 98 days is not universally honored | Assume the Labour Act's 98-day, 60-day-paid provision reflects what you'll actually receive |
| Ask specifically whether a breastfeeding room exists — only about 19% of workplaces have one | Assume basic facilities are standard just because policy discussions treat them as such |
| Budget and plan around the absence of statutory national childcare for children under five | Expect public pre-primary childcare options comparable to what's common in Europe or Latin America |
| Confirm your specific employer's size and sector — the day-care mandate technically applies only to firms with 50+ female employees, and enforcement is inconsistent even there | Assume any employer above the threshold actually runs a day-care center; very few do |
| Take the full 15 days of paid paternity leave if eligible — it's a real, relatively recent legal right (since 2017) | Assume paternity leave is symbolic or rarely used just because maternity enforcement is weak |
| Talk to other working parents at your specific employer before accepting an offer | Rely solely on the written policy or recruiter's verbal assurance |

#### 🇪🇨 Ecuador

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect the 12-week paid maternity leave (75% social security, 25% employer) to be genuinely and reliably honored | Assume Latin American leave policies are uniformly weak — Ecuador is a regional leader here |
| Use the reduced six-hour workday available for up to nine months after return — it's a real, usable right | Rush back to full-time hours before you have to |
| Take the optional unpaid leave (up to nine additional months) if your finances allow — it's a genuine extension, not a formality | Assume unpaid leave requests will be viewed unfavorably; it's a codified entitlement |
| Expect a culturally warm, child-inclusive environment — Ecuador is described repeatedly as unusually family-friendly | Expect the same rigid work/family separation common in more individualist cultures |
| Take paternity leave seriously — 10 to 23 days depending on birth circumstances, and employer-funded | Assume paternity leave is a minor add-on; it's substantial and enforced |
| Investigate affordable private childcare and bilingual schooling options early — commonly cited as a genuine expat-family draw | Assume "affordable" means low-quality without checking specific providers |

---

Nepal's parental-leave framework looks reasonably progressive on paper: 98 days of maternity leave with a minimum of 60 days fully paid, and — since the Labour Act 2074 in 2017 — 15 days of fully paid paternity leave, a relatively recent and meaningful legal addition. But the gap between statute and practice is the real story, and it's a wide one. Reporting from the Kathmandu Post and ILO country briefs converge on the same finding: despite the legal provisions, pregnant employees routinely struggle to actually receive them, with an estimated 70% of organizations, mostly private ones, either providing no maternity leave at all or offering only a fraction of the statutory entitlement. Structural childcare support is essentially absent at the national level — Nepal has no statutory national childcare or pre-primary education system, with free public primary education only beginning at age five, and while the Labour Act nominally requires larger employers (50+ female workers) to run day-care centers, enforcement is close to nonexistent outside a handful of government institutions. Only 18.9% of workplaces have a designated breastfeeding room, though the data shows those that do see exclusive breastfeeding rates nearly 12 times higher — a clear illustration of how much the gap between policy and implementation actually costs working parents in practice.

Ecuador tells close to the opposite story: a genuinely robust legal framework that, per multiple independent accounts, gets meaningfully honored in practice. Twelve weeks of paid maternity leave splits the cost between social security (75%) and the employer (25%), paternity leave runs a substantial 10 to 23 days depending on birth circumstances, and returning mothers are entitled to reduced six-hour workdays for up to nine months — a concrete, usable accommodation rather than a theoretical one. Ecuador is one of just five Latin American countries (alongside Chile, Costa Rica, Cuba, and Mexico) that provide long-term care leave at all, per ILO/ECLAC regional analysis, putting it ahead of most regional peers. Beyond the legal architecture, expat-parent accounts consistently describe a cultural dimension that reinforces the formal policy: children are treated as a visible, welcomed part of public and professional life rather than something to be managed around, and affordable private childcare plus bilingual schooling options are repeatedly cited as genuine draws for relocating families, not just a legal safety net.

**The Reckoning:** The uncomfortable truth is that Nepal's stronger cultural narrative around family (extended-family childcare networks, strong intergenerational support) is quietly compensating for a workplace policy regime that doesn't reliably deliver, while Ecuador's workplace policy regime is strong enough that the cultural warmth functions as a genuine bonus layered on top of real enforcement, not a substitute for it. A parent moving from Ecuador to Nepal on the assumption that "family-oriented culture" implies comparable workplace protections will be in for a rude surprise the moment they need the leave their contract promises. A parent moving from Nepal to Ecuador, conversely, might underestimate just how much more reliably the system there actually works — and end up pleasantly surprised by how little advocacy is required to get what's owed.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Re:Nepal (Quora space, discussing Kathmandu Post reporting on maternity leave denial) — Contributors dissecting why employers deny statutory maternity leave despite clear legal provisions converged on enforcement, not law, as the real gap: labor inspection capacity is thin, especially outside Kathmandu, and workers without strong union or legal support rarely pursue formal complaints even when entitled to them.*

> *Expat.com (Ecuador forum, "Raising Kids in Ecuador" thread) — A relocated parent described being surprised at how normal it felt to bring a young child into ordinary professional and social settings — restaurants, some workplaces, extended family gatherings — in a way that would have drawn comment or logistical friction back home, framing it as a genuine quality-of-life upgrade rather than just a policy detail.*

> *Matador Network (feature, "The Reality of Parenting in Ecuador") — The piece's central argument, drawn from interviews with relocated parents, was that Ecuador's combination of affordable childcare, a culture that visibly welcomes children in public life, and genuinely usable leave provisions added up to something meaningfully different from parenting under a Western dual-income, minimal-support model — not utopian, but structurally different in ways that mattered day to day.*

> *ILO Country Brief (Care at Work in Nepal, drawing on field-level labor data) — The brief's own framing noted that even where day-care mandates technically apply, enforcement outside a small number of government institutions (the brief specifically names Singha Durbar as a rare functioning example) is effectively absent, meaning the legal requirement exists mostly as an unenforced aspiration for the private sector.*

> *Expat.com (Ecuador forum, "Raising Kids the Local Way" thread) — A separate poster compared notes with other expat parents on how Ecuadorian relatives and neighbors routinely folded their children into shared informal childcare arrangements — a level of casual, community-based support the poster hadn't anticipated and said meaningfully reduced the pressure that fell solely on a nuclear family.*

### Conclusion

If you're planning a family and weighing these two moves, the honest advice is to read Nepal's parental-leave law as an aspiration you'll need to actively enforce for yourself, and Ecuador's as something you can largely count on as written. That's an unusual thing to say about two countries at similar points on most development indices, but the data — and the lived accounts — back it up consistently. What I'd tell a friend: in Kathmandu, get your employer's real leave practice in writing before you sign; in Quito, you can mostly just trust the contract.
