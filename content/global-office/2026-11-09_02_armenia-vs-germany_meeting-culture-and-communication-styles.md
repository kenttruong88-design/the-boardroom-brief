# Armenia Will Discuss It Over Coffee. Germany Would Like the Agenda, Thank You.

🇦🇲 Armenia · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Yerevan, a one-hour meeting might open with twenty minutes of hospitality and close with a decision reached by consensus, warmth, and a faint sense that nobody wanted to be the one to say no. In Berlin, the same hour opens with an agenda emailed the night before, runs to the minute, and closes with a decision that was effectively made before anyone sat down. Both rooms think the other one is wasting time.

## 🇦🇲 Armenia: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Open with genuine small talk — relationship-building isn't a prelude to the meeting, it's part of it | Don't walk in and start with the agenda item; it reads as cold |
| Address seniority — defer visibly to the most senior person in the room | Don't contradict a senior colleague directly in front of the group |
| Expect decisions to firm up after the meeting, in follow-up conversations | Don't assume a nodded "yes" in the room is a final commitment |
| Bring a translator or clarify terms early if working across Armenian/Russian/English | Don't assume English fluency is universal outside Yerevan's tech and startup scene |
| Value in-person trust-building over email threads | Don't try to resolve a disagreement entirely by email — it will stall |
| Be patient with hierarchy-driven pacing | Don't push for an on-the-spot answer from a mid-level manager |

## 🇩🇪 Germany: Do's and Don'ts

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Send the agenda in advance, and stick to it | Don't show up without pre-reading the materials — it will be noticed |
| Arrive on time, or a few minutes early | Don't treat "informational" meetings as optional small talk sessions |
| Say what you mean, directly, even if it sounds blunt in English | Don't take direct criticism personally — it is about the work, not you |
| Come prepared to defend your numbers | Don't pitch a half-finished idea hoping to "workshop" it live |
| Expect meetings to end on schedule, sometimes mid-sentence | Don't schedule a meeting without a clear purpose — it will be questioned |
| Follow up with written minutes if a decision was made | Don't assume a friendly tone in the room means consensus has shifted |

## Armenia: The Room Decides, Eventually

Armenian workplace culture, still working out its post-Soviet inheritance alongside a fast-growing tech and startup sector, treats the meeting as a social instrument as much as a decision-making one. According to Rivermate's guide to Armenian cultural considerations, hierarchy is respected openly in professional settings, and open disagreement with a superior in a group setting is rare — dissent, when it happens, tends to surface afterward, in private, rather than in the room. A 2021 American University of Armenia thesis by Nshan Yegyan, applying Hofstede's framework to Armenian respondents (Armenia was never part of Hofstede's original surveyed set), found Armenians scoring notably high on collectivism and power distance relative to Western European baselines — which tracks with what every Yerevan-based manager already knows: the meeting is where relationships get maintained, and the actual call often gets made in smaller, quieter conversations around it.

This has practical effects on pacing. Trading Economics and ILOSTAT data put Armenia's labour force participation in long-term recovery mode after a decade of outward migration to Russia and the EU, and the resulting generational mix — older managers trained in Soviet-style administrative hierarchy, younger staff fluent in startup-speak from Yerevan's booming IT sector — means meetings can run on two clocks at once. The IT crowd wants a standup; the ministry-adjacent crowd wants a protocol. Both exist in the same building, often in the same meeting.

There is also a language layer that outsiders underestimate. Business in Yerevan routinely moves between Armenian, Russian, and English depending on who is in the room and which generation is speaking, and a meeting that looks informal — people switching languages mid-sentence, circling back to a point raised twenty minutes earlier — is often doing real cognitive work that a single-language transcript would miss entirely. A foreign manager who insists on English-only meetings "for clarity" frequently gets less clarity, not more, because half the room is now translating in their heads before they speak, and the Armenian instinct to avoid looking uncertain in front of a senior colleague means fewer people ask for clarification than actually need it.

## Germany: The Meeting Is a Contract

Germany's meeting culture is, by comparison, almost aggressively legible. Hofstede Insights' country profile puts Germany at a Power Distance score of 35 and an Uncertainty Avoidance score of 65 — low deference to hierarchy once the agenda is set, high discomfort with ambiguity. Translated into a conference room: everyone can speak, but everyone is expected to have done the reading first. Jobs.ac.uk's guide to work etiquette in Germany describes punctuality as close to a moral category rather than a courtesy, and Aetna International's breakdown of the German working environment notes that meetings called without a clear, stated purpose are liable to be quietly resented, if not openly questioned by a direct report asking why the meeting exists at all.

The directness is the part that unsettles outsiders most, and it's also the part Germans least understand as a cultural trait rather than simple honesty. IamExpat.de's piece on "embracing German directness" makes the point that criticism delivered flatly in a meeting ("this analysis is wrong") is not an attack but a statement of fact to be corrected — the emotional temperature stays level because, in German professional norms, it was never raised in the first place. The Reddit r/germany community's running discussion of workplace meetings tends to agree on one thing: once the agenda is exhausted, the meeting ends, even if that's four minutes in. Nobody manufactures small talk to fill the slot.

This extends to how decisions get recorded, not just discussed. Jobs.ac.uk notes that German teams tend to follow a meeting with written minutes distributed promptly afterward, functioning less as a courtesy and more as a lightweight contract: what was agreed, who owns which action, and by when. That paper trail is part of why German meetings can end mid-sentence without anyone panicking — the room trusts that the follow-up document will capture what mattered, so there's no instinct to linger and double-confirm verbally. For a newcomer used to oral consensus-building, watching a room empty out the second the clock runs out can feel like the meeting was interrupted. It wasn't. It was finished exactly on schedule.

## The Reckoning

Put an Armenian team and a German team in the same video call and the friction is almost mechanical. The German side wants the pre-read circulated, the agenda followed, and a clean yes-or-no by the end of the hour. The Armenian side wants to open with how everyone's families are doing, defer formally to whoever is most senior, and treat a firm answer delivered cold, in the room, as borderline rude. Neither side is wrong; they're optimizing for different things. Germany is optimizing for efficiency and unambiguous accountability. Armenia is optimizing for relationship durability and face-saving consensus — a slower build, but one that tends to survive disagreement better once it's actually reached.

The expat who struggles hardest in either direction is usually the one who assumes their home style is simply "professional" and the other country's is a deviation from it. The German transplant in Yerevan who keeps demanding agendas will get them, eventually, and be quietly seen as cold in the meantime. The Armenian professional moving to Berlin who waits for consensus to visibly form in the room will wait through several meetings that have already, functionally, ended.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/germany — paraphrased experience: a long-time contributor described German colleagues as unfailingly kind outside the meeting room and startlingly blunt inside it, and said the adjustment wasn't learning to tolerate criticism but learning to stop hearing it as personal.*

> *IamExpat.de — paraphrased experience: a British expat writer recalled being told, in their first week, that their project plan was "not good" with no softening clause attached — and later realizing the German colleague considered that a complete and useful sentence, not an opening insult.*

> *Expat.com forum (thread 1019744) — paraphrased experience: a contributor working in Germany noted that meetings they'd expect to run an hour in their home country routinely closed in fifteen minutes once the agenda items were addressed, and that lingering afterward to chat was read as a signal something was still unresolved.*

> *Brandon.am — paraphrased experience: an Armenian business blog described newer, foreign-trained managers in Yerevan trying to run "lean" stand-up meetings, only to find staff treating the shortened format as a sign the manager didn't value the team enough to sit with them properly.*

> *ExpatExchange — paraphrased experience, substituting for an unreachable Quora thread: a foreign resident in Armenia described sitting through a two-hour meeting that resolved nothing on the record, then getting a full decision by phone the next morning — concluding that the real meeting had happened in the follow-up call all along.*

## The Verdict

If you're moving from Yerevan to Berlin, over-prepare, say less, and don't be wounded when the room agrees with you by simply moving on. If you're moving the other way, budget real time for the room itself — the coffee, the small talk, the deference — because skipping it won't make the meeting faster, it will just make you harder to work with. Neither culture is being inefficient on its own terms; they have simply built two completely different machines for reaching the same destination, and handing either team the other's manual will not, in fact, speed anything up.

The honest summary for anyone relocating for work: Germany will tell you exactly what it thinks of your idea, immediately, and not think less of you for having proposed it. Armenia will tell you what it thinks eventually, warmly, and expect you to have already figured out the real answer somewhere in between.
