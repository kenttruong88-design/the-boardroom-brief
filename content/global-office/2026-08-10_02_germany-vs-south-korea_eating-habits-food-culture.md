# Bread Twice a Day vs. Dinner Three Times a Week: Germany vs. South Korea

**🇩🇪 Germany · 🇰🇷 South Korea**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A German colleague will eat the same cold bread supper four nights a week without complaint and consider one hot meal at midday a nutritional achievement; a South Korean colleague may be expected to eat a second dinner, at 9pm, with the same people he sat next to all day, while pouring drinks for the most senior person at the table with both hands. Neither will describe this as remarkable. Both are, by the standards of the other country, doing something slightly unhinged with their evenings.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say "Mahlzeit" when passing colleagues near lunchtime — it's a mealtime greeting, not small talk | Expect deep conversation over lunch; many Germans treat the canteen as fuel, not a social hour |
| Bring a dish from your home country to an office potluck — genuinely appreciated | Assume everyone eats out; many bring a packed Brotzeit of bread, cheese, and cold cuts |
| Get used to one hot meal a day, usually at noon, and cold bread-based meals for breakfast and dinner | Expect much spice — traditional German food leans hearty and mild, not fiery |
| Show up to the canteen right at 12:00 — the rush is real and predictable | Linger too long at lunch; the break is short and treated as functional, not leisurely |
| Learn a few regional specialties (Weisswurst, Currywurst) — food identity is often regional, not national | Assume "German food" is one cuisine; Bavaria and Hamburg eat quite differently |

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend hoesik (company dinners) when invited, especially in your first year — declining repeatedly is read as disengagement | Start eating before the most senior person at the table does |
| Pour drinks for seniors with two hands, and receive the same way | Turn your glass toward a senior person while drinking — turn slightly away as a sign of respect |
| Expect a fast, efficient lunch — 30 minutes is standard, and speed-focused restaurants cluster near offices | Expect quiet lunches; slurping noodles and audible eating are completely normal, unlike hushed German canteens |
| Ask about "dry hoesik" (non-alcohol) options if you don't drink — increasingly available, especially with younger teams | Assume hoesik has disappeared; it's declining in tech and among younger staff but still standard in banking, government, and traditional firms |
| Keep a packed dosirak (lunch box) as a backup — common for time-pressed days | Refuse food offered by a senior colleague outright; a small taste first is more diplomatic than a flat no |

### Germany

Germany's food culture at work is, consistent with its overall labor model, oriented around efficiency and clear boundaries. According to Clockify's 2026 working-hours data, Germany logs among the lowest annual working hours of any OECD economy, and that same restraint shows up at the table: one proper hot meal, usually lunch, bookended by cold bread-based Frühstück and Abendbrot. Deutschland.de notes that even large companies maintain staff canteens where "Mahlzeit" — a ritualized mealtime greeting — echoes through the halls at noon sharp, but the break itself is brief and not treated as a social occasion; per multiple expat guides, colleagues are not especially receptive to weekend chat while eating.

The deeper cultural signal is that food in Germany is functional first, communal second. A German employee who eats at their desk or brings the same modest Brotzeit — bread, cheese, cold cuts — every day is not signaling poverty or disinterest; they're following a well-worn national script in which lunch fuels the afternoon rather than builds the team. Socializing, when it happens, tends to move to after-work drinks or planned dinners rather than the workday meal itself.

### South Korea

South Korea inverts the equation almost entirely: the workday meal is efficient and rushed, but the after-hours meal is where the actual social and hierarchical work of the office happens. Labor law requires a 30-minute break for shifts over four hours, and in practice, per multiple workplace guides, Korean lunches are fast — dosirak lunch boxes and speed-focused restaurants near the office are built for a 30-minute turnaround, not leisure.

The real institution is hoesik — the after-hours company dinner, per Korea.net and the Korea Herald, that combines a shared meal, rounds of soju and beer, and sometimes a karaoke stop, all governed by an etiquette of who pours for whom and who eats first. It is framed as voluntary and increasingly is, especially among younger workers: a 2023 JobKorea survey found 64 percent of people in their twenties preferred alcohol-free formats, and South Korea's own working-hour reforms since 2018 (capping the workweek at 52 hours) reflect a broader generational pushback against exactly this kind of after-hours obligation. But in banking, government, and more traditional corporates, the old format persists, and repeated absence from hoesik still reads, to many managers, as a lack of commitment.

### The Reckoning

The counterintuitive twist is that Germany, often stereotyped as the more rigid, rule-bound culture, is actually the one that keeps food and work cleanly separated — eat, then get back to it. South Korea, often assumed to be the more hierarchical and formal of the two, is the one where a meal is explicitly used as an informal mechanism to flatten hierarchy, build "정" (jeong, emotional trust), and get things said that wouldn't be said in a meeting room. A German manager who skips team lunch is being normal; a Korean manager who skips hoesik repeatedly is making a statement.

The other divergence is where the calorie-dense obligation sits in the day. In Germany, the heaviest commitment is a light one, dropped into the middle of the workday and gone by 1pm. In South Korea, the heaviest commitment — dinner, drinks, sometimes karaoke — is dropped at the end of the day, when a German employee has already gone home. A new arrival who plans their evening commute around a fixed 6pm finish, German-style, will be caught off guard the first time a Korean team lead says "hoesik tonight" at 5:45.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone answering "what should South Koreans know about living in Germany" noted that Germans eat bread twice a day and reserve their only hot meal for noon, adding that Germans eat noticeably more meat and far less spice than Korean food typically involves.*

> *Quora — A respondent listing Korean eating customs for foreigners flagged that starting to eat before the oldest person at the table is a genuine faux pas, while things Westerners might consider rude — slurping noodles, audible chewing — are completely unremarkable in Korea.*

> *Medium — A former expat professional wrote candidly about Korean drinking business culture, warning that in some traditional workplaces, declining a drink at hoesik can be quietly read as a bad sign heading into the next performance review, even though no one will say so directly.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — An engineer weighing a relocation to Germany described the adjustment less in terms of food and more in terms of rhythm: work-life balance in Germany "is a way of life, not a myth," and colleagues genuinely leave lunch and the workday behind rather than letting either bleed into the evening.*

> *Expat.com forum — In a thread on German food culture, one long-term resident advised newcomers not to expect much small talk over the canteen meal, and to instead save real bonding for organized after-work events — a rough mirror image, they noted, of what they'd heard about Korean office dinners.*

### Conclusion

If you thrive on a clean separation between work and food — eat efficiently, go home, live your life — Germany's model will feel like a relief. If you build relationships through shared meals and can tolerate a late-finishing day every so often, South Korea's hoesik culture, while demanding, offers a faster route into the inner circle of a team than any number of polite lunches ever will. Either way, learn the local meal etiquette before your first week: in Germany it's a greeting word, in Korea it's a serving order, and getting it backwards is the fastest way to look like you didn't do your homework. The honest version I'd tell a friend: in Germany, lunch is fuel; in Korea, dinner is the actual meeting.
