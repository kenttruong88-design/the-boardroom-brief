# The Live-In Nanny vs. The Coal Stove: What "Affordable" Actually Buys You

**🇮🇳 India · 🇲🇳 Mongolia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Both India and Mongolia get filed under "cheap" in the average relocation spreadsheet, and both filings are technically true and practically misleading. In India, a mid-level expat salary buys a standard of living — a live-in cook, a driver, a full-time nanny — that would be unthinkable at the same income back home. In Mongolia, that same modest budget buys you a one-bedroom flat in Ulaanbaatar for around $550 a month, and then, every winter, a fight against an Air Quality Index that can spike past 300 from coal smoke alone. "Affordable" is doing very different work in each sentence.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget for private health insurance immediately — public welfare coverage excludes most expat income brackets | Assume low healthcare costs mean insurance is optional |
| Hire domestic help through a vetted agency (like Broomees) with clear written terms | Hire informally with no documented pay, hours, or notice terms |
| Expect insurance premiums to keep climbing — family plans rose roughly 27% year-on-year recently | Lock into a multi-year premium assumption without rechecking annually |
| Use domestic help norms as a genuine quality-of-life lever, not a guilt trip | Feel obligated to match Western staffing ratios or refuse help altogether out of discomfort |
| Get clear on your city's private hospital network before you need it | Wait for a medical emergency to learn where the good facilities are |

#### 🇲🇳 Mongolia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Install air purifiers before your first winter, especially in bedrooms | Wait until December to think about air quality |
| Verify double-glazed windows and insulation before signing a lease | Assume all "renovated" listings actually address winter heat loss |
| Check whether your building is on municipal central heating (mid-Oct to mid-April) | Assume you control your own heating schedule |
| Budget $100–250/month for private expat health insurance on top of rent | Rely on the public system's free-basic-care guarantee as an expat |
| Look into international clinics in Ulaanbaatar before an urgent need arises | Assume rural-level care access matches what the capital offers |

India's cost-of-living story is inseparable from its labor market structure. Expat Focus and multiple relocation guides converge on the same basic fact: a comfortable single life in a major Indian city runs roughly $800–1,500 a month, but the number that actually changes daily experience is what that budget buys in staffing. Domestic help — cooks, drivers, full-time nannies — is genuinely affordable and widely normalized, to the point that international insurance guides note expats "afford household staff, frequent restaurant dining, and regular overseas travel" on salaries that wouldn't stretch that far anywhere else. The catch is the welfare side of the ledger: India's public health coverage is explicitly means-tested toward very low incomes (guides cite an eligibility ceiling around €240/month), which puts nearly every expat outside the system by design. Private insurance is mandatory in practice, and it isn't static — one source flagged a roughly 27% year-on-year premium increase for family plans in early 2026, a number worth revisiting annually rather than assuming at hire time.

Mongolia's version of "affordable" is more literally about the physical building you live in. A single person can cover essentials for around $570 a month before rent, and Ulaanbaatar rents range from roughly $200 for a small flat to over $1,000 for something larger and better-insulated — the spread itself is a warning that price and quality diverge sharply here. The genuinely distinctive constraint is infrastructure: central heating runs on a fixed municipal calendar (mid-October to mid-April) regardless of actual outdoor temperature, and winter air quality, driven roughly 80% by coal-stove heating in the city's ger districts, can push the Air Quality Index past 300 — several multiples above what's considered hazardous. None of this is disclosed on a typical apartment listing; it has to be actively investigated, which is why every serious relocation guide for Ulaanbaatar leads with "verify insulation and windows" and "budget for an air purifier" as close to non-negotiable line items, not optional upgrades.

### The Reckoning

The two countries essentially trade which side of your life gets "affordable" applied to it. India makes the *human* side of daily life dramatically cheaper — help, service, convenience — while making the *safety net* side something you must privately construct from scratch, since the state's welfare system isn't designed with you in mind. Mongolia makes the *shelter and infrastructure* side of life cheap only at the entry price point, with the real cost showing up later as a health and comfort tax during roughly half the calendar year that a spreadsheet comparison would never surface. Someone moving from a high-welfare-state country to India will likely feel a genuine, if slightly uncomfortable, upgrade in daily convenience; someone moving to Mongolia should expect the opposite curve — a promising rent number in September that gets recontextualized entirely by January.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Responding to a question about the pros and cons of full-time domestic help, one Indian family living abroad and reflecting on India-based staffing described it as genuinely transformative for daily life, but stressed that clear, written terms on pay and hours mattered more than they'd expected, since informal arrangements led to repeated misunderstandings for friends who skipped that step.*

> *Quora — In a thread on hiring a nanny in Delhi, a respondent pointed newcomers toward vetted agency platforms rather than word-of-mouth hires, noting that agency vetting was the difference between a smooth first year and a stressful string of replacements.*

> *Expat.com Mongolia guide (aggregated contributor commentary) — A recurring theme among contributors relocating to Ulaanbaatar was underestimating how disruptive the fixed municipal heating calendar would be — several reported being cold in early October and overheated by late April, simply because the system runs on the calendar, not the thermometer.*

> *findawayabroad.com (expat guide, first-person account) — One newcomer to Ulaanbaatar described the first winter's air quality as the single hardest adjustment of the move, worse than the cold itself, and specifically recommended securing a good air purifier and checking a building's insulation before signing anything, rather than after moving in.*

> *Britcham Mongolia (relocation resource, aggregated member experience) — Members preparing to relocate were advised to treat Mongolia as "a permanent tightrope walk" between the appeal of the steppe and the harshness of Ulaanbaatar's winter infrastructure — advice that reads as poetic until you're the one pricing space heaters in November.*

### Conclusion

If you're comparing these two purely on a cost-of-living spreadsheet, you're comparing the wrong things. In India, the real question is whether you're comfortable privatizing your entire safety net — insurance, especially — in exchange for a standard of daily service that's hard to walk back from once you've had it. In Mongolia, the real question is whether a genuinely low baseline rent is worth the specific, seasonal, and non-negotiable cost of surviving a Ulaanbaatar winter well. Neither country's affordability is fake, but in both cases the actual bill arrives somewhere the initial number didn't mention — my honest advice over a drink: price the insurance in India and the air purifier in Mongolia before you price the apartment, because those are the numbers that actually run your year.
