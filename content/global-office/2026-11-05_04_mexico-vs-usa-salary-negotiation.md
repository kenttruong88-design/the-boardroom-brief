# Nobody Will Tell You the Number, and in One of These Two Countries, Nobody Expects You to Ask

🇲🇽 Mexico · 🇺🇸 USA

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

America's reputation as a nation of relentless negotiators is, on inspection, mostly a reputation. Nearly half of American workers accept a job offer without countering it at all. Mexico, meanwhile, is debating a law to ban the phrase "competitive salary" from job postings entirely, on the grounds that nobody has ever once been paid what that phrase implied. Both countries agree, broadly, that talking about money is uncomfortable. They disagree, specifically, on how expensive that discomfort is allowed to get.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research market rate independently before interviewing — postings routinely hide the number behind "sueldo competitivo" or "a convenir" | Trust a job posting's own description of the salary as "competitive" to mean anything specific |
| Negotiate indirectly, through relationship and rapport, especially with a more senior or hierarchical employer | Use blunt, confrontational US-style negotiation tactics — they can read as disrespectful, not assertive |
| Understand your full compensation package, including mandatory year-end and mid-year bonus payments (aguinaldo, prima) | Assume your base salary figure alone represents your total annual compensation |
| Expect a real wage gap versus an equivalent US-based role, even adjusted for cost of living | Assume a remote US salary is the norm for locally based employment in Mexico |
| Ask directly, early, whether your prospective employer uses one of the formal registered pay structures larger firms follow | Assume every employer, especially smaller ones, follows the same formal pay practices |

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether your state or city has a pay-transparency law — roughly a third of US states now do, and ranges are often posted when they apply | Assume salary ranges are posted everywhere in the US — most of the country still has no such requirement |
| Negotiate your initial offer — most employers expect it and have room built in | Assume the first number offered is final; a large share of applicants who ask for more get at least something |
| Treat your own salary as private by default, even with close colleagues | Assume coworkers will volunteer what they earn, even in a transparency-law state — most people still won't say |
| Research pay bands through sites built for exactly this (Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, PayScale) before an interview | Rely on an employer's framing of "competitive" without independently checking a real benchmark |
| Know that negotiation rates and outcomes differ meaningfully by gender and age — a documented, not anecdotal, pattern | Assume everyone negotiates equally confidently; many colleagues, especially older ones, report real discomfort with the topic |

The wage gap between the two countries is large enough to reframe the whole conversation before it starts: OECD data put average annual wages, adjusted for purchasing power, at roughly $82,900 in the United States against roughly $20,400 in Mexico — a difference on the order of four to one. That gap shapes everything downstream, including why Mexico's current legislative push matters so much. A bill moving through the Mexican Senate, sponsored by Senator Paloma Sánchez Ramos, would require employers to publish an exact salary or range in every job posting, explicitly banning vague phrasing like "sueldo competitivo" ("competitive salary") or "a convenir" ("to be negotiated"), with fines reaching into the hundreds of thousands of pesos for noncompliance. The bill's own cited rationale is stark: more than half of Mexico's formal workforce earns under 12,500 pesos a month, women earn roughly 15 percent less than men when their income is the household's primary one, and Mexico ranks 119th of 146 countries on the World Economic Forum's Global Gender Gap Index. Separately, Mexican business press has reported that opaque "competitive salary" postings are sometimes used as bait by organized-crime recruiters targeting vulnerable young job-seekers — a genuinely dark footnote to a phrase most job-seekers everywhere simply roll their eyes at.

The United States, despite its deep cultural attachment to the idea of the hard-nosed negotiator, turns out to be a surprisingly quiet place to talk about money. A CareerBuilder survey of thousands of workers and hiring managers found that 49 percent of US employees accept a first offer outright, even though 45 percent of employers expect and welcome a counter — a mismatch that amounts to leaving money on the table by default, split along predictable lines: older workers and men negotiate more often than younger workers and women. A separate Upgraded Points survey of a thousand employed Americans found 43 percent uncomfortable discussing salary even with coworkers, and only 41 percent claiming to actually know what a colleague earns — with comfort discussing pay splitting sharply by generation, from 45 percent of Gen Z workers down to 21 percent of boomers. The legislative response has been a genuine patchwork rather than a national policy: seventeen US states now require some form of pay-range disclosure in job postings, and New York City's law alone affects roughly four million workers, but outside those jurisdictions the "sueldo competitivo" problem has a direct American cousin in the simple absence of any number at all.

The generational split inside each country complicates the picture further. The Upgraded Points data breaks American comfort with the topic down by age in a way that undercuts any single national story: 45 percent of Gen Z workers and 41 percent of millennials say they're comfortable discussing pay, against just 22 percent of Gen X and 21 percent of boomers — meaning an American office today likely contains two genuinely different cultures of salary secrecy stacked on top of each other, with younger staff far more willing to compare notes than the managers evaluating them. Mexico's generational dynamic runs differently, filtered more through gender than age: the Bumeran-sourced commentary cited by IDC Online found that half of respondents blamed the country's gender pay gap mainly on cultural and workplace factors rather than on negotiation skill itself, with nearly a quarter saying women specifically negotiate worse outcomes because they prioritize flexible scheduling and family time over raw pay in the negotiation itself — a trade-off the same survey's respondents overwhelmingly agreed wouldn't meaningfully improve "until companies change their mindset," rather than until individual women simply asked more forcefully. Both patterns point to the same underlying truth: the loudest voices in each country's negotiation culture — American deal-making bravado, Mexican appeals to toughen up and ask — describe a minority of actual workplace behavior, not the norm.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast isn't that Americans negotiate and Mexicans don't — plenty of Mexican professionals negotiate fiercely, just rarely in the blunt, individualistic style American career advice tends to export. It's that both countries have built elaborate cultural cover for the same underlying discomfort, just in different directions. Mexico's cover is structural: hide the number behind vague language, and let hierarchy and relationship do the negotiating that an open market conversation would otherwise require. America's cover is cultural: maintain the myth of the fearless negotiator while nearly half of workers quietly never ask, and treat your own salary as a near-taboo subject even among people you see every day.

For anyone moving between the two systems, the practical trap is assuming the US side is more transparent just because it talks more about negotiation, or that the Mexican side is less negotiable just because the posting hides the number. Hofstede's own data offer a tidy explanation for why direct tactics travel badly: Mexico scores far higher on power distance and far lower on individualism than the US, meaning pay there is something handled through hierarchy and relationship far more than through an individual's confrontation with an HR department.

### Conclusion

If you're negotiating in Mexico, the number you need isn't on the posting — it's the one you bring in from independent research, deployed carefully through relationship rather than confrontation, with a clear understanding of what your bonus structure actually adds on top of base pay. If you're negotiating in the United States, the number you need is simply the one you ask for, because despite the national mythology, most of your future colleagues already quietly didn't.

I'd tell a friend taking either job the same thing: do the homework yourself, because in both countries, the posting — competitive or otherwise — was never going to do it for you.
