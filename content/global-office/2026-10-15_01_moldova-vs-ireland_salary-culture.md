**Nobody Will Tell You What They Earn: Moldova vs. Ireland on the Sacred Art of the Pay Packet**

**🇲🇩 Moldova · 🇮🇪 Ireland**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Chisinau, a software developer will quote you a figure in euros without blinking, because the number is the whole point of taking the job. In Dublin, a man with a mortgage and a company laptop will discuss his therapist, his situationship, and his opinion of the taoiseach before he discusses his salary, which by law he is now, grudgingly, going to have to start revealing anyway.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇲🇩 Moldova

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate your salary in euros or dollars if you're in IT or BPO — the sector runs on foreign-currency benchmarks, not lei | Assume the national average wage (about 15,987 lei, roughly $728 a month in early 2026) describes your sector; it's dragged down by budgetary-sector pay |
| Get everything in writing, including bonuses — informal "we'll sort it out" arrangements are common and common to go wrong | Expect a Western-style HR department to chase you down about a raise; you will need to ask |
| Budget for a widening gap between IT wages and everything else — rent in a decent Chisinau building has caught up with salaries faster than most other prices | Compare your offer to the official average and feel reassured; the real sector pays roughly 27% more than the budgetary one, and tech sits well above both |
| Ask returning diaspora workers what outsourcing clients actually pay — they know the going rate better than any published survey | Treat a verbal promise of "performance-based increases" as binding without a number attached |
| Learn basic Romanian or Russian workplace vocabulary — salary conversations with local accountants rarely happen smoothly in English | Assume every employer is used to paying a foreign hire market rate; some will test how little you'll accept |

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research your market rate before the interview — Ireland's new pay transparency rules are only just arriving, and old habits of silence persist | Ask a colleague what they earn, even as a joke; it remains one of the few truly reliable ways to end small talk permanently |
| Expect salary bands in job ads to start appearing through 2026 as the EU Pay Transparency Directive is transposed into Irish law | Assume a six-figure salary in Dublin behaves like a six-figure salary anywhere else — rent, income tax, and USC take a serious bite |
| Negotiate before you accept, politely and once — Irish hiring managers expect a counter but not a campaign | Bring up pay in a performance review as your main topic; aim it at your contribution first, number second |
| Budget as a household, not an individual — two incomes are the norm for anything resembling a comfortable life in Dublin | Expect transparency to arrive quickly or smoothly; employer groups have already signalled they are "cool" on the new disclosure rules |
| Use calculators (Irish Tax Hub, Revenue's own tools) to understand take-home pay before comparing offers | Confuse a high gross offer with a high net one; the gap between the two is where most negotiation regret lives |

**Moldova, Priced in Euros for a Reason**

Moldova's average gross monthly wage reached 15,987.1 lei — about $728 — in the first quarter of 2026, with the government projecting a yearly average near 17,400 lei, according to wage data reported by Logos Press. That headline number, though, obscures a country running two economies at once: the budgetary sector averaged 13,287.9 lei while the "real" (largely private) sector pulled in 16,909.6 lei, a gap of more than 27%. Real wages, adjusted for inflation, grew 4.3% year-on-year, which sounds respectable until you remember the base is one of the lowest in Europe.

The exception, and the reason this comparison exists at all, is IT. Moldova has spent the past decade marketing itself as an outsourcing hub — ICT.md and the national investment agency both tout tax-free IT-park status and a cost base a fraction of Western Europe's — and the sector pays accordingly, often quoted in euros or dollars rather than lei, insulated from the currency swings that make the rest of the labour market feel precarious. An expat living in Chisinau, cited anonymously on Expat Exchange, summed up the wider economy bluntly: "the economic situation in Moldova is not very stable," making it hard for anyone outside the favoured sectors to find well-paying work. Moldova doesn't yet have its own formal Hofstede Insights country profile — a small, telling fact in itself about how recently the country has become a subject of international management research rather than just regional politics.

**Ireland, Where Pay Is a Secret Everyone Is Legally About to Have to Tell**

Ireland's relationship with salary disclosure borders on the theological. An RTÉ documentary covered by the Irish Times opened with ordinary workers refusing, on camera, to say what they earn; multinational staff at firms like Accenture and Biomarin happily discussed wellness perks — yoga, mindfulness rooms — and went notably quiet the moment pay came up. For calibration, the programme noted that earning €25,000 puts a worker ahead of 30% of the national workforce, €50,000 sits near the middle, and €100,000 clears the top 5%. Separate workplace surveys regularly find that roughly two-thirds of Irish employees feel underpaid relative to their contribution, a statistic that coexists oddly comfortably with the refusal to discuss what "paid enough" would even look like.

That culture of silence is now colliding with EU law. The Pay Transparency Directive is being transposed into Irish legislation on a timeline running through 2026, requiring employers to disclose salary ranges in job postings, give candidates the expected pay band before interview, and report gender pay gaps more rigorously, according to coverage from the Law Society Gazette and multiple Irish HR outlets. Employer groups have been described, with admirable Irish understatement, as "cool" on the change. Hofstede Insights scores Ireland as highly individualist (70) with low power distance (28) — a profile that, on paper, should make direct salary talk easy. In practice, individualism here expresses itself as privacy, not openness: your pay is your business, and definitely not anyone else's.

There's a policy backdrop worth a sentence each. Moldova's EU candidate status, granted in 2022, is already reshaping wage expectations — Brussels-aligned labour reforms and freer movement toward Western Europe push employers to compete with emigration, not just with each other, which is part of why the "real sector" premium over budgetary pay keeps widening. Ireland's version of outside pressure is remote work: a Dublin-based multinational now competes for the same senior candidate against a fully remote role paying in dollars, which quietly does more to expose what salaries "should" be than any EU directive has managed so far — workers compare notes with people outside the country long before they'll compare notes with the person at the next desk.

**The Reckoning**

Here is the irony: Moldova, poorer and far less institutionally developed, is in some ways the more transparent market, because its IT sector was built from scratch on internationally benchmarked, openly quoted rates — there was no old pay culture to protect. Ireland, wealthy and thoroughly modern, is having to be legislated into transparency its workers have spent generations actively avoiding, documentary cameras included.

The two countries also diverge sharply on what a salary number actually buys. A Moldovan tech salary quoted in euros goes a long way locally precisely because the rest of the economy hasn't caught up; an Irish salary that sounds generous on a recruiter's email evaporates against Dublin rent, which routinely runs €1,500–€2,000 a month before a euro is spent on anything else. Both countries, from opposite directions, land on the same practical advice: ask what the number means after rent and tax, not what it says on the offer letter.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — A software engineer on a Critical Skills Employment Permit, discussing their €60,000 total compensation in Dublin, asked the forum whether that was competitive. Replies pegged the Irish national average near €50,000 and the median near €45,000, noting that, unlike in the US, a software engineer's pay in Ireland runs roughly double the average rather than triple it — and that progressive taxation means net pay never doubles along with it.*

> *Fishbowl — An accountant who had received a senior-level offer at a Big Four firm's Irish office for roughly €45,000 asked the platform's community whether Irish employers actually negotiate, and how the country was generally regarded as a place to build a career — a question that drew curiosity from other users eyeing the same move rather than a confident answer, which is itself a small data point about how little salary information crosses borders even inside the same firm.*

> *Expat Exchange — An expat living in Chisinau described the broader economy as "not very stable," with high unemployment and low pay outside a handful of sectors making it genuinely difficult to find well-compensated work or start a business locally, independent of how well any one foreign hire might be doing in tech.*

> *TalesMag Real Post Report — A US Embassy staffer's Chisinau posting report, while light on specific salary figures, advised that newcomers "will be able to save a great deal of money if you avoid western type goods and entertainment" — a reminder that in a wage economy this compressed, lifestyle choices move the needle on quality of life more than almost anywhere in Western Europe.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing Moldova against Ireland on pay alone, the honest framing is not "which pays more" but "which number can you trust." Moldova's is smaller but increasingly real-terms honest, at least in tech, where rates are quoted in hard currency and benchmarked against the same outsourcing market your employer sells you into. Ireland's is larger but was, until very recently, a cultural secret even from the colleague sitting next to you — and the legally mandated candour arriving through 2026 is being adopted with all the enthusiasm of a root canal.

Neither country will tell you, unprompted, what you should actually expect to walk away with after tax and rent — in Dublin because it's considered rude, in Chisinau because the market is still working out its own rules. Ask anyway. If a friend asked me which one to bet on: Moldova if you're chasing a specific, dollar-benchmarked tech role and don't mind a smaller city; Ireland if you want the number legislated into the open eventually, and don't mind waiting for everyone else in the room to stop looking at their shoes first.
