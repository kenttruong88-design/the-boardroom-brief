**Pakistan to Iran: The Hierarchy Gets Taller, the Bank Transfers Get Harder**

🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇮🇷 Iran

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Pakistan and Iran share a 900-kilometre border, a time zone, and roughly fifteen years of officially announcing a $10 billion trade target they have yet to clear a third of — the Federation of Pakistan Chambers of Commerce and Industry puts actual bilateral trade closer to $2.8 billion annually. For the Pakistani professional transferred to a Tehran office, the land crossing turns out to be the easy part of the move. What follows is a workplace where the org chart runs a layer or two taller, the bank transfer takes a week and a workaround, and the boss's opinion on a Tuesday-afternoon decision carries roughly the finality of a court ruling.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address seniors with proper titles ("Sir," "Madam," or "Sahib") and let the most senior person in the room speak first. | Expect a flat, first-name office — hierarchy is visible in seating order, speaking order, and who gets interrupted. |
| Build personal rapport before negotiating; tea and questions about family are expected before any business topic. | Jump straight to numbers in a first meeting — it reads as transactional and mildly rude. |
| Expect the workday to run past its posted closing time, especially near a deadline. | Assume a 6pm close means you can leave at 6pm if your manager is still at their desk. |
| Dress conservatively and formally — full suits for men, modest professional wear for women, in corporate settings. | Wear anything sleeveless or overly casual to a client-facing meeting, even in summer heat. |
| Factor Friday prayers into scheduling; many offices slow down or pause around midday Friday. | Schedule a hard deadline meeting for early Friday afternoon. |
| Expect salary conversations to happen in person, often through a trusted intermediary. | Send a one-line email demanding a raise; it stalls the conversation rather than speeding it up. |

#### 🇮🇷 Iran

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Open every meeting with genuine questions about health and family before the agenda. | Launch straight into the agenda item — it will be read as cold. |
| Use honorifics consistently ("Agha" for Mr., "Khanom" for Ms.), even with people you know well. | Default to first names once you're friendly — it rarely becomes the norm in a professional setting. |
| Expect decisions to move up a multi-layered approval chain; treat patience as a professional skill. | Press for a fast yes or no — "shayad" (maybe) means "give me more time," not a soft no. |
| Carry cash and know the unofficial exchange rate; sanctions make cards and transfers unreliable. | Assume your company card or an international transfer will simply work without a local workaround. |
| Dress modestly as standard business attire, consistently, inside and outside the building. | Treat the dress code as a formality that relaxes once you're past reception — it's enforced evenly. |
| Accept the offered tea and a seat before any paperwork begins. | Eat at your desk through a working lunch if colleagues are taking a proper midday break — it's conspicuous. |

### Pakistan

Pakistan's office hierarchy is tall and visible by design. Hofstede Insights scores the country at 55 on power distance and a striking 14 on individualism — one of the most collectivist, authority-respecting profiles the index records. In practice, decisions cascade down from a handful of senior figures, meetings open with a seating order that mirrors seniority, and a junior employee who corrects a senior one in front of others has made a bigger error than whatever was being corrected in the first place.

Officially, the workweek is capped at 48 hours under Pakistani labour law; unofficially, clerical and managerial staff are explicitly carved out of overtime pay under the factory rules, and the real cap tends to be whenever the client's deadline clears. A long-running thread on the Pakistani culture blog Pakistaniat described colleagues "sleeping on office desks" to finish projects at lean-staffed private and pharmaceutical firms — recounted not as a scandal, but as an ordinary Tuesday. Pakistan's official unemployment rate has climbed to 7.1 percent, and with a large, English-fluent graduate population chasing a narrower band of white-collar roles, switching employers for a modest raise carries little reputational cost. Employers know this too, which keeps raises modest and retention efforts correspondingly half-hearted.

Relationship-building is not a soft skill bolted onto the job; it is the job. Tea before terms is close to mandatory, salary conversations happen face to face or through an intermediary rather than by email, and a foreign manager who tries to run a negotiation purely on logic and numbers will find the room politely, immovably stalled.

### Iran

Iran's power-distance score sits even higher, at 58, with an individualism score in the low-to-mid 40s — enough to make Pakistan's own hierarchy look merely firm by comparison. Authority in an Iranian office is centralized and rarely delegated past the most senior tier; Rivermate's market guide describes multi-layered approval chains that can turn a routine sign-off into a multi-week process, and warns new arrivals that "shayad" is a request for more time, not a diplomatic no.

The statutory week is, on paper, shorter than Pakistan's: 44 hours over a Saturday-to-Thursday schedule, with Friday a mandatory day of rest and 26 days of paid annual leave guaranteed by law — genuinely generous terms by regional standards. What the law does not fix is the plumbing underneath it. International sanctions have made routine transfers, corporate cards, and even some productivity software unreliable enough that offices build informal cash and workaround systems into daily operations as a matter of course. Pakistan and Iran have spent years promising to lift bilateral trade toward $10 billion annually; the real figure, per the Federation of Pakistan Chambers of Commerce and Industry, sits closer to $2.8 billion, and the gap between target and reality is a fair proxy for the gap between what an Iranian org chart promises and what the office actually has to improvise around on a given Tuesday.

Dress code is enforced rather than suggested — modest, conservative attire is standard for everyone, and the expectation does not relax once the office door closes behind you. The World Bank puts Iran's female employment-to-population ratio at just 12.08 percent as of 2023, among the lowest such figures recorded anywhere, a number that shapes who a new arrival will typically be reporting to and sitting across from in most departments.

### The Reckoning

Set side by side, the two offices resemble each other more than either resembles, say, a Berlin startup — flat org charts are foreign to both, and a junior employee eager to move fast and break things will be gently, firmly re-educated in either country. The genuine divergence is in what slows the machine down. In Pakistan, it's an informal overtime culture and the sheer size of a labour pool competing for a narrower set of white-collar jobs, which keeps hours long and loyalty weak. In Iran, it's compliance plumbing: a workforce operating a genuinely shorter legal week, inside an economy where sanctions have turned basic financial transactions into a logistics project.

The irony is that Iran's labour law is, on its face, the more worker-friendly of the two — shorter hours, a mandatory full rest day, more guaranteed leave. Pakistan's statutes look reasonable too, right up until you notice how comprehensively managerial and clerical staff are written out of overtime protection. Neither country's law is the real predictor of how late you'll be at your desk on a given night; in both, that predictor is how senior the person waiting on your email happens to be.

Onboarding reflects the same split. A new hire in Lahore is typically handed a mentor on day one, mostly because the hierarchy makes it unthinkable to leave a junior person without someone senior to answer to; a new hire in Tehran gets the same informal mentor, plus a crash course in which bank, money-changer, or colleague's cousin handles which kind of payment, because no onboarding deck anywhere mentions that part out loud.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — A tech worker comparing notes on Pakistani versus Indian management styles found the thread mostly dissolved into stereotype, but one concrete story held up: a manager's personal politics had visibly shaped a performance review, and the poster's lasting takeaway was to separate a manager's online opinions from their professional conduct far more carefully before trusting either.*

> *Blind — A consulting employee who asked whether they could work from Pakistan while staying on a US-based client project got a flat answer from several colleagues at the same firm: against policy, full stop, with tax status, sanctions screening, and client-data residency rules all cited as settled reasons nobody had bothered to challenge.*

> *Pakistaniat.com — A commenter who had watched colleagues work until midnight at lean-staffed private firms, including a sibling at a pharmaceutical company who slept at his desk to finish projects, called the arrangement what it was: not dedication, but exploitation dressed up as a work ethic.*

> *Expat.com — A trader asking on the site's Iran forum about importing garments was told the market was better than its reputation suggested: a favourable exchange rate, genuine demand for international brands among younger buyers, and a government that still permits foreign goods in despite the sanctions regime surrounding the economy.*

### Conclusion

If the choice is between Lahore and Tehran, the variable that should weigh most isn't the org chart — both will ask you to respect a hierarchy you didn't build and to wait for an answer from someone more senior than you. It's the banking. A Pakistani professional moving to Iran needs a cash strategy before they need a seating chart; a shorter, legally generous workweek does not help much if getting paid, or paying for anything, requires a monthly workaround. Pakistan's trade-off runs the other way: the money moves fine, but the hours quietly never stop.

Pick your inconvenience, and budget for it honestly. In Tehran, that means literally carrying cash. In Karachi, it means accepting that 6pm on your contract is more of a suggestion than a clause.
