**Costa Rica to Canada: Pura Vida Opens the Meeting, Small Talk Closes the Friendship**

🇨🇷 Costa Rica · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Randstad Global Workmonitor survey found that roughly two-thirds of Canadian employees say they have close friendships with colleagues, and 53 percent spend time with coworkers outside working hours — numbers that sound, on paper, like a famously sociable workplace culture. Meanwhile, in Costa Rica, posters on an expat forum warn newcomers that launching straight into business before a proper round of greetings "is considered rude." Both countries, by their own telling, value connection at work. What differs sharply is how far that connection is actually expected to travel once the workday ends.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇷 Costa Rica

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect meetings to open with real, unhurried personal conversation — sometimes longer than the business portion itself. | Launch directly into the agenda; expat guides specifically flag this as rude by local standards. |
| Greet everyone individually when entering a room, and say goodbye to each person when leaving, even if you have to leave early. | Skip individual greetings or farewells to save time; several accounts describe this as a real social misstep. |
| Expect a light cheek-kiss greeting between men and women to be normal even in some business contexts. | Assume strictly handshake-only greetings are universal; local norms can be warmer than a newcomer expects. |
| Treat being a few minutes late to a meeting as genuinely unremarkable. | Arrive extremely early expecting punctual counterparts; you may simply end up waiting alone. |
| Expect "Pura Vida" and "Tico Hospitality" to be real, frequently invoked cultural touchstones, not just tourism marketing. | Dismiss the warmth as performative; employees across multiple accounts describe genuinely proactive, unprompted welcoming behaviour from colleagues. |
| Expect dress and formality expectations to flex with context — some sectors genuinely don't require a suit. | Assume universal informality; some deal types and sectors still expect fully formal business dress. |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect genuine, friendly colleague relationships — about two-thirds of workers report close workplace friendships. | Expect those friendships to automatically deepen into close personal ones; several accounts describe hitting a social "glass ceiling." |
| Take the initiative yourself to keep a work friendship going outside the office. | Wait for colleagues to reach out repeatedly; more than one account suggests Canadians can default to "cocooning" once initial contact is made. |
| Attend organised social events when offered — nearly half of workers who do credit them with stronger colleague relationships. | Assume these events happen often by default; only half of employers hold them even quarterly. |
| Recognise that a friendly, polite tone at work is close to a baseline cultural norm, not a special signal of personal closeness. | Read ordinary workplace friendliness as an invitation to a deeper friendship; multiple posters describe this exact mismatch. |
| Expect regional variation — some posters describe the Pacific coast specifically as reserved compared to its reputation for friendliness. | Assume "Canadian friendliness" is a single, uniform national trait; several long-term residents, including Canadian-born ones, report real difficulty building close ties regardless of region. |
| Expect holiday parties, team lunches, and seasonal events to be the most common bonding formats where they exist. | Expect frequent, elaborate team-building retreats as standard; simpler, lower-cost formats are far more typical. |

### Costa Rica

Costa Rican workplace bonding runs almost entirely through ritual courtesy and unhurried relationship-building before any actual business happens. Two expat forum posters, writing under the handles packagecr and samramon, both describe social conversation as the expected opener to any meeting, with packagecr noting plainly that it "is considered rude to launch right into business." That same courtesy extends to arrivals and departures — acknowledging everyone in a room when you enter, saying a proper goodbye to each person when you leave, even mid-meeting — and to physical warmth that would read as unusually familiar in many other business cultures, including a light cheek-kiss greeting between men and women that both posters describe as normal rather than exceptional. Punctuality bends accordingly: being a few minutes late carries little social cost, and arriving exactly on time might just mean waiting alone for everyone else.

Corporate accounts, while naturally more polished, describe the same underlying texture. At SentinelOne's Costa Rica office, Andrea Mesen, who joined roughly six months before being quoted, recalled that "people just introduced themselves without anyone asking them to," attributing it to a team "known for being warm people." VP Alex Hanlon described a culture with "no separation between functions, just a shared commitment to doing what's right," where people "step in without hesitation," while Johan Tijerino, who helped open the office, remembered its early spirit as built on "collaboration, friendship, and a willingness to jump in and make something great together." The company frames this explicitly through "Pura Vida" and "Tico Hospitality" — concepts genuinely rooted in Costa Rican national identity rather than invented for the careers page, even if a corporate blog is, naturally, going to present them in their best light. Hofstede's data scores Costa Rica at a notably low 15 on individualism against Canada's 80, alongside a strikingly high 86 on uncertainty avoidance — numbers consistent with a workplace culture where group warmth and established ritual matter more than individual achievement or improvisation.

### Canada

Canadian workplace bonding looks, by the numbers, remarkably healthy — and then turns out to have a ceiling that several long-term residents describe running into directly. Randstad's Global Workmonitor data puts close workplace friendships at roughly two-thirds of employees, with 53 percent socialising with colleagues outside work hours and only 21 percent believing those friendships interfere with work, below the 26 percent global average. Randstad Canada's Stacy Parker summed up the cultural logic plainly: "Canadians associate a good working environment with having good relationships with their colleagues." A more recent ADP Canada survey adds texture on format: only half of employers hold lighthearted social events even quarterly, most commonly simple team lunches (29 percent) or seasonal gatherings (22 percent), yet workers who do get them report real benefits — 48 percent cite stronger colleague relationships, 40 percent a greater sense of belonging, 37 percent higher morale. ADP Canada's Heather Haslam frames the stakes directly: "Creating opportunities for employees to connect in meaningful ways can have a lasting impact on the workplace."

The friction shows up once you look past the aggregate numbers and toward individual accounts of actually trying to turn a colleague into a friend. A Victoria-based poster on an expat forum described Canadians as "generally very nice, polite and friendly," but said that warmth is often tied tightly to a specific setting — work, school, a shared gym — and rarely travels beyond it. A newer Vancouver arrival found people "very friendly" yet struggled to move relationships past the acquaintance stage, while a Kelowna-based poster described being invited to a colleague's home for Thanksgiving but still feeling she'd hit a "glass ceiling" in building anything closer. Tellingly, a Canadian-born poster on the same thread said they'd found it hard to make close friends in British Columbia for decades, attributing a kind of passive, "cocooning" social default to the province's own history rather than to any newcomer-specific barrier. Hofstede's Canadian individualism score of 80 — one of the higher figures the index records globally — tracks with exactly this pattern: plenty of pleasant, voluntary interaction, but little cultural expectation that it owes anyone deeper obligation once the workday or the shared context ends.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast here isn't warm-versus-cold; both countries report genuinely friendly workplace cultures by their own data and their own residents' accounts. It's ritual-versus-optional. In Costa Rica, bonding behaviour — greetings, farewells, unhurried small talk before business — functions as close to a social obligation, woven into ordinary professional etiquette rather than treated as a discretionary extra. In Canada, bonding is real but structurally optional on both ends: only half of employers even bother organising it regularly, and multiple accounts suggest colleagues themselves won't necessarily push a relationship past pleasant proximity unless someone actively does the pushing.

For a newcomer, the practical implication cuts in opposite directions. In Costa Rica, skipping the ritual is the risk — launch into business too fast, skip a goodbye, and you've already done something that reads as mildly rude, however unintentionally. In Canada, the risk is closer to the reverse: assuming that broad, genuine friendliness is an invitation to real personal closeness, when several long-time residents, Canadian-born and newly arrived alike, report that the deeper tier simply requires more visible, sustained initiative than the easy early warmth suggests it should.

### Conclusion

If you're choosing where the office party actually means something, the honest answer is: it depends what you're hoping it means. In Costa Rica, the courtesy is the connection — show up, greet properly, and you're already most of the way toward belonging. In Canada, the courtesy is just the opening move, and the real friendship, if you want one, is a project you'll likely have to run yourself.
