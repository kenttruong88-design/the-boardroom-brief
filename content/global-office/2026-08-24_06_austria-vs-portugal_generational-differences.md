# Austria vs Portugal: In One Office the Boomers Won't Retire; in the Other, the Interns Already Left the Country

## 🇦🇹 Austria · 🇵🇹 Portugal

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Austria, the employment rate for 55- to 64-year-olds has more than doubled since 2005, to 60.9 percent, according to Statistics Austria — meaning the office boomer everyone keeps waiting to retire is, statistically, going nowhere. In Portugal, the generational tension resolves itself differently: more than half the country's emigrants are aged 20 to 34, and roughly 61,000 Portuguese citizens left for OECD countries in 2023 alone, per the OECD's International Migration Outlook. One country's generations are stuck in the same conference room, arguing about the thermostat. The other's younger generation has simply left the building.

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address senior colleagues by academic title (Dr., Mag., Ing.) until invited to drop it | Assume startup first-name culture extends automatically to a 60-year-old department head |
| Clock out at a fixed hour — the standard week is 38.5 hours | Schedule "quick" calls after 5pm, even if you're the one initiating |
| Take Bildungskarenz-style educational leave without apologizing for it | Treat part-time work (Teilzeit) as a résumé red flag — over a quarter of employees use it |
| Stay home when sick, no doctor's note needed for short absences | Show up sneezing to prove commitment; it reads as poor judgment, not dedication |
| Expect flat, consensus-driven management at most modern firms | Skip the formal "Sie" until a senior colleague explicitly offers "du" |
| Bring documentation and precedent to a disagreement with an older manager | Expect enthusiasm alone to substitute for process |

#### 🇵🇹 Portugal

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use "Senhor/Senhora" plus surname with anyone above your pay grade | Assume Lisbon's startup scene speaks for the national labour market |
| Treat long lunches as legitimate negotiation venues | Rush a relationship-building lunch to get back to your desk |
| Ask directly about contract type; fixed-term arrangements are common | Assume a full-time offer implies permanent status |
| Plan for a talented younger colleague possibly leaving within 2-3 years | Take it personally when a 27-year-old accepts an offer in Germany or the UK |
| Defer visibly to seniority in meetings, even mid-disagreement | Publicly challenge a senior figure's plan — raise concerns privately after |
| Expect older colleagues to prize job security over salary growth | Pitch "disruption" as a universal motivator; frame change around stability instead |

### Section: Austria

Austria's generational story isn't rebellion, it's arithmetic. Pension reform and stricter early-retirement rules have kept older workers on payroll far longer than a decade ago — the employment rate for 55- to 64-year-olds has more than doubled since 2005 (Statistics Austria). Meanwhile the OECD's 2026 Economic Survey of Austria names demographic ageing as the country's central fiscal challenge going forward. The practical result: younger employees aren't waiting for the boomers to retire and free up headcount, because the boomers, statistically, aren't retiring.

What has shifted is the culture around that fixed cast. Hofstede Insights gives Austria one of Europe's lowest power-distance scores, and firms have leaned into flat management as a lure for younger talent: first-name terms with a department head, real budget authority handed to new hires within months, autonomy standing in for oversight. Younger Austrian employees, per Deloitte's 2026 Gen Z and Millennial Survey and related workforce research, rank flexibility and intrinsic reward above formal advancement — helped along by Bildungskarenz (educational leave of two to twelve months every four years) and Teilzeit, used by roughly 28 percent of the workforce. The friction isn't over who's in charge, since Austrian firms ceded formal hierarchy to consensus culture years ago; it's over pace and boundaries. The 38.5-hour week and a customary Friday afternoon off are honored across generations, but younger workers police the edges harder, treating an unanswered after-hours message as a boundary rather than an oversight.

### Section: Portugal

Portugal's generational story isn't about who runs the meeting; it's about who's still in the country to attend it. Data compiled via CEDEFOP show 53.3 percent of workers aged 15-24 on fixed-term contracts — the fifth-highest share in the EU-27 — falling only to 24.3 percent among 25- to 34-year-olds, second-highest in the bloc. Insecurity that acute doesn't produce office rebellion so much as exit: more than half of Portuguese emigrants are aged 20 to 34, and Portugal Resident has taken to calling young professionals the country's "biggest export."

What remains is an older workforce operating inside a culture Hofstede scores as high on uncertainty avoidance, with a formality that outlasts the generation that built it. Titles matter, seniority is visibly deferred to, and — per Springer Nature's research on Portugal's multigenerational workforce — the generational split that does exist tends to run geographically rather than attitudinally: Lisbon's tech-adjacent roles pull in the young workers who haven't yet emigrated, while traditional sectors in smaller cities and rural regions run on an older, more security-minded bench almost by default.

### Section: The Reckoning

Here's the inversion worth sitting with. Austria's generational tension exists because the system succeeded, arguably too well, at retaining older workers, then flattened the culture around them anyway. Portugal's generational tension exists because the system failed to retain its younger workers at all. An Austrian 26-year-old grinds gently against a 58-year-old department head who, thanks to pension reform, isn't leaving for another decade. A Portuguese 26-year-old skips that argument entirely and takes a job in Munich or Amsterdam paying two to three times as much. One country solved the wrong half of the problem; the other never got to solve it.

For the arriving professional this cuts both ways. In Austria, expect genuine generational coexistence — negotiated, occasionally passive-aggressive about who answers the 6pm email, but coexistence nonetheless, inside a labour market that isn't shedding anyone. In Portugal, expect an office that skews older than the national population pyramid would suggest, because the 28-year-olds who'd normally balance it out are in Berlin. Neither is worse for a foreign hire, but each rewards a different instinct: in Vienna, patience with process; in Lisbon, patience with absence.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Welcome to the Jungle — A French professional recalled being on a first-name basis with her Austrian department head within weeks of starting, then being sent, before hitting six months on the job, to represent her 200-person employer at a ministerial working group; the flat hierarchy, she noted, extended well past the company slogans.*

> *Expat.com Portugal forum — A long-term resident, not employed locally, observed that construction and farm crews routinely work without basic protective equipment — gloves, goggles, harnesses — and read it less as an individual failing than as a broader institutional shrug toward workforce wellbeing that predates any single generation.*

> *Expat.com Portugal forum — A 71-year-old returning after decades in the United States recalled his mother starting factory work at age eleven, and his family later bribing officials to secure emigration papers in the late 1950s, framing his return as a bet that today's Portugal, fixed-term contracts and youth flight included, is still a considerable improvement on the one his parents fled.*

> *Quora — Respondents to a thread on generational workplace expectations largely agreed the sharpest divide isn't attitude but process: older colleagues learned to apply for jobs through in-person visits and phone calls and still expect deference earned the same way, while younger hires assume digital-first communication and flatter feedback loops are simply how work functions now.*

> *Quora — An expat with three decades in Austria warned that professional warmth there — first names, flat teams, shared jokes — doesn't reliably extend past 5pm; several respondents agreed that mistaking office informality for a fast track to friendship, at any age, is a common and slow-dawning misread for newcomers.*

### Conclusion

The practical upshot for anyone weighing a move: Austria offers a workplace where the age profile skews older but the culture skews flatter, where seniority is largely a formality and process is the real hierarchy — corporate friction is real but survivable, wrapped in a reliable 38.5-hour week and near-automatic time off when sick. Portugal offers warmer, more overtly hierarchical offices where deference to age and title is sincere rather than performative, but where the generational bench is thinner than the population pyramid implies, because a meaningful share of the peers you'd expect to find there took a better-paying job somewhere colder.

Neither country will hand you a generational culture war on the scale of an American open-plan office; Austria has mostly re-engineered its way around the conflict, and Portugal has mostly exported it. Choose Austria if you want to negotiate with your elders inside a flat structure that insists it doesn't have any. Choose Portugal if you'd rather skip the negotiation and simply notice, a year in, that you're the youngest person in most meetings by a margin that keeps quietly growing.
