# Nobody Here Is Staying: Hong Kong vs. Australia

**🇭🇰 Hong Kong · 🇦🇺 Australia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Hong Kong is, on paper, one of the most collectivist workplace cultures measured anywhere, and Australia is one of the most individualistic. By the logic of the org chart, Hong Kong should be a company town of lifers and Australia a revolving door. The data says the opposite happened, and nobody at either company seems especially surprised.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Line up your next role quietly before resigning, if you can manage it | Assume a "naked resignation" (quitting with nothing lined up) carries no risk — over a third do it anyway |
| Expect to be asked in interviews why you left each previous job, specifically | Treat a 10+ year tenure on a CV as an unambiguous plus — some hiring managers read it as stagnation |
| Negotiate hardest at the 2-4 year mark of any role — that's where pay jumps cluster | Expect loyalty to be rewarded with raises that outpace the market |
| Keep your LinkedIn update cadence slow and deliberate | Signal "actively looking" the moment you're mildly annoyed at your manager |
| Ask directly about culture and management style in interviews | Assume a prestigious name on the offer letter compensates for a toxic manager — it's the #1 reason people actually leave |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect roughly 1 in 10 colleagues to have changed jobs in the last year — it's unremarkable | Treat two years in a role as "too soon to leave" — many don't |
| Use a job change to negotiate pay rather than waiting for an internal review | Assume tenure alone earns you the next promotion over an external hire |
| Frame job-hopping on your résumé around what you learned, not what you escaped | Job-hop so frequently (every year or less) that it reads as an inability to commit |
| Expect younger colleagues (15-24) to move roles nearly 15% a year — it's a generational norm, not a red flag | Assume older colleagues (45+) share that appetite for movement — mobility drops sharply with age |
| Keep professional references current — reference checks are frequent given the mobility rate | Burn a bridge on the way out; the market is smaller than it feels |

### Hong Kong: Collectivist on Paper, Restless in Practice

Hofstede Insights scores Hong Kong at 25 on individualism, a profile that should, by theory, produce exactly the kind of company loyalty East Asian corporate culture is stereotyped for. The actual numbers tell a stranger story. A Towers Watson Global Workforce Study covering more than 32,000 employees across 26 markets found that only 38% of Hong Kong employees expected to stay with their current employer, against a global average of 46%, while 31% expected to leave within two years, above the 26% global norm — and a startling 61% described themselves as fully disengaged or detached from their employer, nearly 20 points above the worldwide figure, as reported by the South China Morning Post.

The jobsDB 2019 Job Seeker Salary Survey, covering over 3,000 Hong Kong job seekers, put numbers on how that plays out in practice: 36% of respondents had quit a job without having another lined up — a phenomenon local HR commentators call a "naked resignation" — and half of those cited simply hating the job enough to leave on principle. Younger workers were the most willing to jump with no net: half of those under 25 had done it. The same survey found a clear financial logic underneath the restlessness — workers who switched jobs roughly every three years saw average pay gains of 17% above their starting salary, compared to just 6% for those who never switched at all. Loyalty, in other words, is being actively priced out of the Hong Kong labor market by its own compensation structure, regardless of what the collectivism score suggests should happen.

Hong Kong hiring managers have grown candid about this trade-off rather than pretending it doesn't exist. Robert Half Hong Kong has published research noting that millennial job-hopping is no longer treated as a disqualifying red flag by most local employers, a shift from a decade earlier when a CV with four jobs in six years would have gone straight to the bottom of the pile. The practical upshot for an arriving expat employee: a résumé that would read as commitment-phobic in a conservative Western market can read as market-savvy in Hong Kong, provided each move has a legible reason attached to it in the interview.

### Australia: Individualist, and Oddly More Settled Than You'd Think

Hofstede puts Australia at 90 on individualism, near the top of the global scale, which should predict a workforce treating every job as a rung on a personal ladder with no particular attachment to any single employer. The Australian Bureau of Statistics' job mobility data for the year to February 2023 complicates that story: the overall job mobility rate was 9.5%, the highest in a decade, but still meant roughly 9 in 10 workers hadn't changed jobs in the past year. Tenure data from the same period showed 44% of the employed workforce had been in their current role for five years or more, and more than 1 in 10 had stayed two decades or longer.

The mobility that does happen is heavily concentrated by age and, specifically, by choice rather than desperation: the 15–24 bracket moved jobs at nearly 15% a year, more than seven times the rate of workers 45–64 (5.9%), and of the 2.3 million people who left or lost a job in that period, 32% did so specifically to pursue something better, not because they were pushed. Industry commentary, including from recruitment and accounting publications, has increasingly reframed job-hopping from résumé red flag to "selling point" — a reversal that would have been unthinkable in the same market two decades ago, and one that Hong Kong's HR circles have been slower, and more publicly anxious, to make.

The generational split is where the real story sits. Workers 65 and over moved jobs at just 2.1% a year — a figure close to statistical noise — while the youngest cohort moved at nearly seven times that rate, suggesting Australia doesn't actually have one job-mobility culture so much as two running side by side: a highly fluid early-career market layered on top of a conspicuously settled one for anyone over 45. An arriving manager used to reading tenure as a single, uniform signal will misjudge both halves of their new team if they don't separate the two.

### The Reckoning

The irony sits right at the center of both Hofstede scores: the collectivist culture produces the more disengaged, more mobile workforce, and the individualist one produces the steadier tenure numbers. Part of the explanation is structural rather than cultural — Hong Kong's compressed, hyper-competitive job market makes switching unusually lucrative in a way Australia's, with its larger domestic economy and more even pay growth across tenure, simply doesn't replicate as sharply. But part of it is a genuine mismatch between what each culture says about itself and how its workers actually behave: Hong Kong talks about relationships and face; Australia talks about individual hustle. On the evidence, it's the hustle culture that's quietly become the more loyal one.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *teamblind.com — One contributor opened a thread asking why they'd "seen many people staying in a same company from long time (10+ years)" and whether that made sense. Replies split sharply: one called the long-tenured path "loyalty and being invested in a company's future," while another, less charitably, called the same behavior "bootlicking" — with a third warning that a decade at one desk can make a colleague a liability if they've simply gotten comfortable rather than good.*

> *preplounge.com — A member of this consulting-career forum, three months into a financial-services role in Asia and openly unhappy, admitted "I am not enjoying it" and was weighing a return to North America. A coach answering in the same thread said taking a wrong-fit job is common enough that people "move on quickly and learn from them" — but added a specific caution that switching every year or two, repeatedly, starts to read to employers as an inability to commit.*

> *hrmasia.com — Citing the jobsDB survey, one HR commentator noted that Hong Kong's Gen Z workers increasingly value sabbaticals and flexible arrangements over permanent roles outright, and urged employers to stop treating a resignation gap as a character flaw and start asking what actually drove someone to leave without a backup plan in hand.*

> *Advice worth having before you land: in Hong Kong, line up the next role before you resign if you possibly can — a third of your future colleagues didn't, and about half of them regretted the job enough not to care. In Australia, don't let two years at a company make you feel like a flight risk on paper; the market has already decided that's normal, even if your manager hasn't quite caught up.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing which market rewards patience, the honest answer is: neither does, particularly, but Australia punishes impatience less visibly than Hong Kong punishes patience. A career built on staying put will cost you more in Hong Kong, in real percentage points of forgone salary growth, than the same career would in Australia, where tenure is simply unremarkable rather than quietly penalized. The thing I'd tell a friend over a drink: in Hong Kong, your resignation letter is a financial instrument; in Australia, it's just Tuesday.
