# India Will Tell You Everything About the Job Except the Number. Czechia Will Tell You the Number in 2027.

## 🇮🇳 India · 🇨🇿 Czechia

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Bengaluru, a candidate can learn a prospective employer's entire variable-pay structure, ESOP vesting schedule, and the CEO's leadership philosophy before anyone mentions a number that will actually appear in a bank account. In Prague, a candidate can go through two full interview rounds without salary coming up at all, because raising it early is widely read as a sign you care more about money than the work — a suspicion no amount of charm fully dispels. Both countries are, in their own ways, extremely forthcoming about pay. Neither is forthcoming about the actual figure, at least not on schedule, and the European one has an EU directive and a missed deadline to prove it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask peers or alumni WhatsApp groups for real band ranges before you interview | Assume the headline "LPA" figure on the offer is what lands in your account monthly |
| Get the full CTC breakup — basic, HRA, variable, ESOPs — in writing | Treat a single lump number as the real offer; the breakup changes everything |
| Bring an actual competing offer letter if you have one | Bluff about a rival offer — many HR teams will ask to see it |
| Negotiate hard before signing, not after joining | Expect a future "annual review" to quietly fix a lowball starting number |
| Ask directly what the ceiling of the next band is | Go quiet after a counter — silence is read as acceptance, not modesty |
| Keep two or three interview processes running at once | Volunteer your current CTC first if you can help it, even though many firms will still insist on it |

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask HR for a precise range at the first call, even if the ad is vague | Expect the job ad itself to list a number — that isn't mandatory here until 2027 |
| Wait until the second conversation to raise pay | Lead with salary in interview one; it reads as mercenary, not efficient |
| Bring market data (Platy.cz, levels.fyi) to justify your ask | Invent a rival offer — Czech counterparts quietly check, and the deal just goes cold |
| Ask for the net number, not just gross | Forget that roughly 15% tax plus 11.6% insurance disappears before you see it |
| Time a raise request right after finishing a project | Cite rent, loans, or personal costs as your reason for wanting more |
| Treat a second foreign language (German, Dutch, Nordic) as a real lever | Assume being a foreigner alone bumps your number — it only helps paired with the right language |

India's relationship with pay is a study in selective transparency. According to hiring data from Indeed reported via Storyboard18, the share of Indian job postings listing a salary figure climbed from 26% in March 2022 to over half by mid-2025 — real progress, concentrated almost entirely at the bottom. Junior and mid-level roles disclose pay 32% of the time, entry-level 28%, but senior leadership roles manage only 13%. The closer you get to the number that actually matters, the quieter everyone gets. Meanwhile the ILO's Global Wage Report, a 73-country study cited by The Swaddle, found India's gender wage gap at 34%, the single worst in the survey — a figure that predates most of this transparency push and, going by the postings data, has had little reason to improve since.

What fills the gap is a negotiation culture built almost entirely on leverage rather than disclosed benchmarks. Hofstede Insights data (via Cyborlink) puts India's Power Distance score at 77, among the highest measured anywhere, which tracks with how often the actual negotiating happens not at the table but around it — in competing offer letters, in CTC breakups demanded by HR before a number is even discussed, in multi-month interview sprints designed purely to generate counteroffers. One Blind thread recounts a candidate at four years' experience working his offer from roughly 18 lakh to 61 lakh per annum purely by stacking rival bids and using each to leverage the next; another describes a two-week standoff with Goldman Sachs HR over a candidate who simply refused to hand over payslips, which ended in an offer the candidate ultimately declined anyway, for an unrelated, better one. The system rewards stamina and nerve far more than it rewards information.

Czechia, by contrast, is building formal transparency from the top down and getting there slowly. The EU's Pay Transparency Directive required salary ranges in job ads and before first interviews by June 7, 2026; Czechia will miss that by roughly seven months, with the Ministry of Labour pursuing what the Union of Employers' Associations president Jiří Horecký calls a "minimalist" transposition aimed at January 2027. Until then, Expats.cz notes that Czech job ads remain "notoriously vague" on pay, and that discovering your real take-home — after roughly 15% tax and 11.6% insurance — often takes more arithmetic than anyone expects walking in. The country also carries the EU's second-highest gender pay gap at 18.5%, according to Expats.cz, widest in finance (35.6%) and narrowest in water utilities (1.3%), with the gap peaking at 21.6% for workers aged 35 to 44 — a glass ceiling with remarkably precise coordinates.

Czech negotiation style, meanwhile, is direct almost to the point of discomfort, just not direct about money, and not early. Recruiters quoted via Expats.cz warn that raising salary too soon in an interview reads as being "focused on the cash, and not the role," which is an odd standard to hold a job interview to, but there it is. Hofstede's Power Distance score for Czechia sits at a middling 57, versus India's 77 — flatter hierarchies, more room to simply ask a manager for a number — but the Randstad Workmonitor 2023 survey found 50% of Czechs want raises more than once a year, against a 39% global average, suggesting the appetite for better pay is high even where the willingness to ask directly and early is not.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the irony sharpens: India discloses almost everything about a role except the figure that defines it, then expects you to extract that figure through competitive combat; Czechia will eventually hand you a legally mandated number in writing but currently prefers you not mention money until it's already decided you're worth hiring. An Indian candidate arriving in Prague expecting the gloves-off, multi-offer leverage game they know from Bengaluru will find no partner for that dance — Czech HR won't bluff back, it will simply stop calling. A Czech candidate landing a role in Mumbai expecting the ad's listed range to mean anything fixed will find a CTC structure with more moving parts than a parliamentary coalition.

The genuinely strange twist is who ends up better served by each system's timeline. Czechia's coming transparency law exists specifically to stop employers lowballing women and newcomers by anchoring them to a previous, unequal salary — the exact problem India's own gender wage data suggests is already happening at scale, with no comparable law in sight. Czechia is legislating its way toward the fix roughly a year late. India, for now, is relying on individual nerve.

There is also a tenure effect worth planning around. The Randstad Workmonitor data showing half of Czechs expect a raise more than once a year is a signal about turnover risk as much as it is about pay appetite: a market where people ask that often is one where staying quiet about money for too long is read, eventually, as settling. Indian salary growth, by contrast, is heavily front-loaded into the job-change itself rather than the annual review — which is precisely why Blind and Reddit are full of engineers switching jobs every eighteen months purely to reset their baseline, rather than waiting for a raise cycle to catch up to the market on its own.

### Conclusion

If you take one thing from either country's playbook, take this: India will teach you to never let a number sit unquestioned, and Czechia will teach you to never let a number be the first thing you say out loud. The practical move, wherever you land, is the same — find out what the role is actually worth before you walk in, because neither country's current system is going to volunteer it on your behalf, not yet, and certainly not politely. As I'd tell a friend over a drink: negotiate like an Indian, but wait to raise it like a Czech, and you'll annoy almost no one on either continent.
