# Nine-to-Five, Give or Take a Continent: Australia vs Kenya on Overtime and the Sanctity of 5pm

## 🇦🇺 Australia · 🇰🇪 Kenya

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Australia recently passed a law giving employees the legal right to ignore their boss after hours, a concept so foreign to much of the world's workforce that it reads like satire even when reported straight. Kenya, meanwhile, has a statute mandating overtime be paid at 1.5 times the hourly rate, a rule so frequently honoured in the breach that Kenyan business media runs regular exposés about it. Between a country legislating the right to switch off and a country still litigating the right to be paid for staying on, there is a lesson for anyone weighing a relocation: read the labour code, then ignore it, then ask someone who actually works there.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clock out at your contracted hours — 38 is the statutory full-time week and colleagues will notice if you don't | Send emails after 6pm expecting a same-day reply; the Fair Work right to disconnect means they can legally ignore you |
| Use your full annual leave (four weeks minimum) — not using it reads as a personal failing, not dedication | Frame unpaid extra hours as "showing commitment" in a job interview; it reads as a red flag to Australian managers |
| Ask about rostered days off (RDOs) and time-in-lieu policies in your first week — they're common and underused by newcomers | Assume "flexible working" means "always available"; it usually means the opposite — compressed or adjustable hours |
| Expect Friday afternoons to wind down early, especially outside major cities | Schedule non-urgent meetings after 4pm on a Friday; you will be the only one who shows up mentally |
| Take sick leave when sick — it's separate from annual leave and using it is unremarkable | Try to out-hustle local colleagues to impress a foreign head office; it tends to backfire socially |

#### 🇰🇪 Kenya

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm your contract explicitly states overtime terms before signing — verbal assurances are not enforceable | Assume the 45-hour statutory week is what you'll actually work; many white-collar roles run well past it unpaid |
| Build relationship time into your day — the shared lunch hour is a genuine part of how business gets done | Skip lunch to "save time"; you'll lose more in goodwill than you gain in minutes |
| Learn who holds seniority in a meeting before you speak; hierarchy shapes who talks first | Publicly push back on a senior colleague's decision, even a bad one, in a group setting |
| Budget extra commuting time for Nairobi traffic — it routinely adds hours to an eight-hour day | Treat "office hours end at 5pm" as a reliable prediction of when you'll actually leave |
| Ask other expats which sectors (NGO, multinational, government) have realistic overtime norms before accepting an offer | Assume all employers are equally lax on hours — multinationals and NGOs are often closer to Western norms than local firms |

## Australia: The Legislated Nap

Australia's average annual hours worked sat at roughly 1,611 in the OECD's most recent tally, comfortably below the OECD average of around 1,700, and the country has now gone further than merely working less — it has legislated the right to stop thinking about work. Since August 2024, the Fair Work Ombudsman's right-to-disconnect provisions have given employees at businesses with 15 or more staff an enforceable right to refuse after-hours contact from employers or clients, unless refusing would be "unreasonable." That word is doing a lot of quiet legal work, and lawyers have already made careers out of arguing what counts as reasonable, but the symbolic shift is real: a Western economy decided that ignoring your inbox is a protected activity rather than a character flaw.

The Australian Bureau of Statistics shows monthly hours worked climbing gently through 2025, alongside a persistent pool of roughly 1.5 million underemployed workers who would prefer more hours, not fewer — a reminder that "balance" here is often a function of job security rather than universal virtue. Gallup's most recent Global Workplace data found engagement in Australia and the Pacific sitting at a modest 20%, below the global average, with only a third of workers describing themselves as genuinely thriving. The takeaway for an incoming professional is not that Australians are lazy or content — it's that the culture has built structural guardrails (award wages, RDOs, statutory leave, and now a disconnect law) around a workforce that is, by its own admission, not wildly enthusiastic about work in the first place.

## Kenya: The Overtime That Time Forgot

Kenya's Employment Act sets a standard week of 45 hours with overtime owed at 1.5 times the hourly rate on weekdays and double time on Sundays and public holidays — on paper, a more generous overtime regime than Australia's, which relies on award-by-award loading rather than a blanket multiplier. In practice, enforcement is where the story gets interesting. Kenyan business coverage, including reporting in the Daily Nation on a pan-African Willis Towers Watson employer study, has repeatedly found that a majority of workers feel underpaid relative to hours worked, and unpaid or under-compensated overtime remains a live grievance pursued through Kenya's Employment and Labour Relations Court rather than resolved by habit.

Gallup's 2025 findings complicate the picture further: Kenyan wellbeing metrics actually surged to 64%, well above the global average of 44%, and 57% of Kenyan employees said their managers genuinely cared about their mental wellbeing — the highest share in the Middle East and Africa region. The strain, per the same data, is overwhelmingly financial: 65% of respondents cited cost of living as their primary stressor, not hours themselves. Add Nairobi's notorious traffic, which routinely turns an eight-hour contracted day into a ten-hour commute-inclusive one, and you get a workforce that is, by several measures, more personally resilient than Australia's but operating inside a system with far weaker structural protection around when the workday actually ends.

## The Reckoning

Set side by side, the two countries invert the usual assumption that legal protection tracks with lived stress. Australia has the stronger overtime and disconnect legislation and the lower average hours worked, yet Gallup finds its workforce less engaged and less likely to describe itself as thriving. Kenya has the weaker enforcement and the longer, less predictable hours, yet its employees report higher overall wellbeing and stronger faith that their managers care. Hofstede's cultural-dimension data offers one partial explanation: Kenya scores meaningfully higher on collectivism and power distance than Australia, which tracks with the emphasis both expat sources and official guidance place on shared lunches and hierarchical deference — colleagues functioning as a support structure that a purely individualist, legislated system doesn't need to supply.

The honest read is that Australia has optimised the letter of work-life balance while Kenya has, somewhat by necessity, optimised the practice of tolerating its absence. Neither is obviously winning: an Australian on the legal right to disconnect can still feel disengaged doing nothing in particular, and a Kenyan professional putting in unpaid extra hours can still report thriving because the people around them make it bearable. Balance, it turns out, is not purely a policy output.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A software engineer who relocated to Australia reported that colleagues genuinely logged off at contracted hours, and that trying to signal dedication by staying late or answering emails at night was met with mild concern rather than admiration.*

> *TeamBlind — An Australian engineer weighing a move to a US tech company said the higher salary wasn't worth trading away a culture where 40-hour weeks were the norm rather than the exception, noting counterparts in American release cycles routinely hit 50-plus hours.*

> *British Expats forum — A long-term arrival pushed back on the "Australia has perfect work-life balance" narrative, describing a hospitality role where informal expectations to stay back unpaid persisted despite the legal framework, arguing the reputation depends heavily on industry and employer.*

> *Expat.com forum — A contributor working in Nairobi described the informal end of the workday as a moving target, with the official 5pm close routinely extended by client calls and traffic-driven scheduling, but noted the shared lunch hour functioned as real social infrastructure rather than a formality.*

> *Quora — A respondent who'd worked in both Australian and East African offices advised newcomers to Kenya to clarify overtime terms explicitly in writing before signing, since verbal assurances about compensation for extra hours were rarely honoured once the workload actually arrived.*

## Conclusion

The practical advice writes itself once the data and the forum grumbling are read together: in Australia, trust the system — the right to disconnect, the award rates, and the RDOs are real and enforceable, so use them without apology. In Kenya, trust the contract and the colleagues — get overtime terms in writing before you need them, and invest early in the relationships that will actually get you through a long commute and a longer day. Neither country will hand you balance by default; one legislates the attempt, the other socialises around its absence. Choose accordingly, and set your out-of-office reply either way — Australia will enforce it, Kenya will simply admire your optimism.
