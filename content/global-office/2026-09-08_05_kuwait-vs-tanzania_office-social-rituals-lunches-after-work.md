# The Diwaniya and the Long Lunch: How Kuwait and Tanzania Actually Bond at Work

**🇰🇼 Kuwait · 🇹🇿 Tanzania**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Kuwait, the most important business meeting of your week might not appear on your calendar at all — it happens after dark, on cushions, in someone's diwaniya, over coffee poured from a dallah into small finjan cups, and skipping it without a very good reason is a bigger professional misstep than missing an actual meeting. In Tanzania, the equivalent ritual is quieter and happens in daylight: a genuinely unhurried lunch where rushing back to your desk reads not as diligence but as a small act of disrespect to the people you're eating with. Neither country socializes at work the way a Western employee handbook describes "team bonding," and both will judge you, gently but definitely, for treating their version of it as optional.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇼 Kuwait

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept an invitation to a diwaniya if one is extended — it is a genuine mark of trust, not just a social nicety | Treat a diwaniya invite as optional networking; declining repeatedly can quietly close doors |
| Expect the workweek to run Sunday–Thursday, with Friday and Saturday as the weekend | Schedule client calls assuming a Western Monday–Friday week |
| During Ramadan, respect the shift to 36-hour weeks and avoid eating, drinking, or chewing gum in shared office spaces | Assume Ramadan hours mean lower output expectations — deadlines still exist, just around a different rhythm |
| Attend a corporate Iftar if invited — it's a significant relationship-building event, not just a meal | Skip Ramadan-season team events assuming they're purely religious and not for you as a non-Muslim colleague |
| Build relationships through informal, unhurried business lunches, ideally at a restaurant, hotel, or diwaniya setting | Rush a hosted meal — lingering is part of how trust gets built here |

#### 🇹🇿 Tanzania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet colleagues individually and respectfully before getting to business — greetings are not a formality to skip | Walk straight past colleagues to your desk without greeting them individually |
| Take the lunch break as a real, unhurried social occasion, not a working lunch at your desk | Eat at your desk by default — it can read as antisocial rather than efficient |
| Use formal titles and respectful language with managers and senior colleagues, even in casual settings | Challenge a manager's decision directly in front of others — raise concerns privately instead |
| Show deference to age and seniority in team settings, even outside strict reporting lines | Assume flat, Western-style informality applies just because a meeting feels relaxed |
| Learn a handful of respectful Swahili greetings — genuine effort here is noticed and valued | Assume English fluency in the office means Swahili greeting customs don't matter socially |

Kuwait's office social life runs through an institution older than any modern employer: the diwaniya, a dedicated gathering space — traditionally a room in a family home — where business, politics, and community life have always been discussed together rather than kept separate. What's notable for a new arrival is how directly this has migrated into corporate life: some Kuwaiti business associations and firms now run explicitly "workplace diwaniyas," scheduled recurring forums that blend the traditional hospitality format with modern professional development goals. Hofstede's broader "Arab World" cluster data (Kuwait is typically grouped with Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Egypt, and neighboring states in the absence of a Kuwait-specific study) shows high power distance and strong collectivism — hierarchy is respected, but relationships and trust, built through exactly these informal social forums, are what make that hierarchy navigable day to day. Ramadan adds a second, entirely different social layer: reduced hours (36 a week, down from the standard schedule), workplace accommodations for fasting colleagues, and corporate Iftar dinners that function as some of the year's most significant relationship-building events — attending as a non-Muslim colleague is generally expected and appreciated, not presumptuous.

Tanzania's office social rituals are less institutionalized but no less real. Workplaces are described consistently as hierarchical, with age and seniority carrying weight well beyond formal job titles, and direct challenges to a manager's authority — especially in front of others — are rare and can land badly. Where Kuwait channels bonding through a dedicated evening institution, Tanzania channels it through the ordinary rhythm of the working day: greeting each colleague individually rather than issuing a group "morning" on the way to your desk, and treating the lunch break as genuine social time rather than a boxed-in interruption to be minimized. Expats in Dar es Salaam consistently describe a warm, welcoming baseline culture — "people are generally welcoming" is a near-universal refrain — but one that rewards patience and individual courtesy over efficiency-minded shortcuts that might read as normal, even virtuous, in a faster-paced Western office.

The reckoning: both countries treat workplace socializing as infrastructure rather than decoration, but Kuwait's version is scheduled, evening-based, and institutionally named (you can literally be invited to "a diwaniya"), while Tanzania's is diffuse, daytime, and embedded in the texture of ordinary interactions rather than a distinct event you either attend or skip. That makes Kuwait's social culture easier to identify and easier to accidentally decline, and Tanzania's easier to overlook entirely if you're moving fast and don't realize that the greeting you skipped or the lunch you ate at your desk was actually a small, cumulative social cost.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering "how is expat life in Kuwait," one respondent who had worked there several years said plainly that anyone offered the opportunity "would go" again, citing the depth of relationships built through informal social settings as something that took real time to access but paid off far beyond what the compensation package alone suggested.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a detailed question about the Dar es Salaam expat experience noted that greeting people respectfully "will likely benefit you in both social and professional life," framing it less as etiquette advice and more as a practical rule for how quickly you'd be accepted into a team.*

> *InterNations — Kuwait City's active local chapter runs monthly events across sports, arts, and book-club interest groups, and members describe using the network for concretely professional purposes — one commonly cited example is finding trusted recommendations for something as specific as choosing a school for their children, illustrating how thoroughly professional and personal networking blend in Kuwait's expat social scene.*

> *Expat.com — The Dar es Salaam community network page highlights how much of the city's expat professional life still runs through informal, face-to-face social connection rather than digital-first coordination, with newcomers repeatedly advised to prioritize in-person meetups over relying solely on messaging groups to build a real foothold.*

### Conclusion

Coming from a culture where "team bonding" means a scheduled happy hour you can politely skip, both Kuwait and Tanzania will feel like the social stakes have quietly gone up. In Kuwait, say yes to the diwaniya invitation the first time it comes, because it usually doesn't come twice if you decline. In Tanzania, slow down at lunch and greet people by name, because the efficiency you think you're demonstrating is actually costing you the goodwill you'll need later. Neither is difficult once you know the rule — the trouble is that no orientation packet tells you the rule exists until you've already broken it.
