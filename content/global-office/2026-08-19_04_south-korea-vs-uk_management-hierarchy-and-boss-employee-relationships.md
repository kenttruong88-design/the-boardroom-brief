# The UK Will Let You Question Your Boss. Korea Will Let You Know Exactly Ten People Outrank You.

**🇰🇷 South Korea · 🇬🇧 UK**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A British employee at a Korean company will spend their first month trying to figure out where they actually rank, because nobody hands out an org chart — they hand out a business card, and your title is the org chart. A Korean employee at a British company will spend theirs waiting for a superior to tell them what to do, and slowly realizing that in this office, waiting is itself the mistake.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the title ladder — Sawon, Daeri, Gwajang, Chajang, Bujang, Isa, and up — and use it | Address a superior by first name, even in an otherwise informal team |
| Let your manager make the first move on informality — it's their call, not yours | Skip the chain of command to raise an issue with someone two levels above your boss |
| Show deference to age and tenure even outside a strict title hierarchy | Assume a young high performer outranks an older, longer-tenured colleague by default |
| Expect a paternalistic relationship — a good boss checks in on you beyond just deliverables | Read a manager's personal questions as overstepping; concern for your life outside work is normal here |
| Build trust through time and consistency before expecting real autonomy | Expect full independence in your first months, however capable you are |

#### 🇬🇧 UK

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Speak up if you disagree with your manager — it's expected, delivered diplomatically | Assume hierarchy is absent just because it's informal — it still shapes who gets heard first |
| Use first names with your boss unless told otherwise | Over-formalize your language with senior colleagues; it can read as distant |
| Take self-deprecating humor as a normal register, even from senior staff | Take self-deprecating comments from your boss as a sign of low competence — it usually isn't |
| Manage your own workload and flag blockers proactively | Wait to be told what to do next; initiative is expected and rewarded |
| Raise disagreement in the room, softened but clearly | Assume silence from your manager means approval — ask directly if unclear |

South Korea's Hofstede power-distance score of 60, layered onto a Confucian social structure that predates the corporation by centuries, produces one of the most explicit hierarchy systems in the developed business world. According to corporate title research from firms like Nowak & Partner, the Korean office ladder — Sawon (staff), Daeri (assistant manager), Gwajang (manager), Chajang (deputy team leader), Bujang (team leader), up through Isa (director) and beyond — isn't decorative. It determines seating order, speaking order, and who apologizes first when something goes wrong. Commisceo Global's management guide to South Korea describes the boss-employee relationship as often paternalistic: a good manager is expected to take a genuine interest in an employee's life outside the office, not just their output, a dynamic that reads as intrusive to outsiders and caring to insiders.

The UK sits closer to the individualist end of Hofstede's scale, with a power-distance score of 35 against South Korea's 60 and an individualism score of 89. British management style, shaped by that combination, tends toward what researchers describe as participative — subordinates are expected to challenge or offer suggestions to superiors, and the relationship is judged less by deference and more by whether the working relationship produces good results. First names, informal humor, and open pushback in meetings are all standard, even as an unspoken pecking order still very much exists beneath the casual surface — it's just rarely put into words, let alone into a title on a business card.

The reckoning is that Korean hierarchy is loud and British hierarchy is quiet, and both trip up the wrong kind of newcomer. A British employee joining a Korean firm may find the explicitness almost freeing once they learn the rules — you always know exactly where you stand, even if where you stand is "several ranks below the person who just corrected you." A Korean employee joining a British firm may find the opposite disorienting: the hierarchy hasn't disappeared, it's just gone underground, expressed through tone, seating near the boss, and who gets the benefit of the doubt in a debate — and reading it wrong, in either direction, costs you standing you didn't know you'd lost.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A respondent describing Korean management culture noted that a "good" boss checking in on your personal life, your family, even your weekend plans, wasn't nosiness but a genuine marker of the relationship — and that employees who kept their boss at arm's length were often read as harder to trust, not more professional.*

> *Quora — Someone comparing Korean and Western corporate structures wrote that titles function as a complete social operating manual in Korean offices — knowing someone's exact rank told you immediately how to address them, whether to bow, and who spoke first in any given room, which removed ambiguity but also removed flexibility.*

> *teamblind.com — A poster who had worked for a US company while living in South Korea described the odd experience of technically reporting into a flat, Western-style structure on paper while still being expected, socially, to defer to older Korean colleagues in the office — two hierarchies operating on top of each other.*

> *teamblind.com — Someone reflecting on a same-level manager appointed after a reorg noted that even in flatter, Western-leaning tech structures, unofficial pecking order reasserted itself almost immediately — a reminder that British and American offices aren't hierarchy-free, just hierarchy-quiet.*

> *Quora — A commenter working under Korea's ranking system described repeatedly having ideas shut down without discussion by a superior, and learning that the fix wasn't pushing harder in the room but building enough personal rapport outside it that the same idea, raised later and framed as the manager's own suggestion, actually got adopted.*

### Conclusion

If you're heading to Korea, the fastest way to lose standing is trying to flatten a hierarchy that isn't going anywhere — better to learn your rank and use the deference it buys you. If you're heading to the UK, the fastest way to lose standing is staying too deferential when initiative and pushback are what get rewarded. Figure out which country expects you to know your place, and which one expects you to speak past it — then do the opposite of what feels safe.
