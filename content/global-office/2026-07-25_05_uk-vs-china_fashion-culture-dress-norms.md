**Britain Quietly Retired the Suit; China's Gen Z Is Wearing Pajamas to Protest It**

**🇬🇧 UK · 🇨🇳 China**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Only 4.3% of UK employers still enforce a formal dress code, an 80% collapse in just a few years, according to industry compliance tracking — meanwhile in China, 60% of employees still believe what they wear directly affects whether they get promoted, even as a very online cohort of Gen Z workers stages a quiet rebellion by showing up in pajama tops. Britain abandoned formality by employer decree. China is abandoning it, unevenly, by generational insurrection.

### UK: The Great Quiet Abdication

British office fashion didn't so much evolve as collapse, and recently. The share of UK employers formally enforcing a dress code fell to just 4.3%, an 80% drop in a short span, according to [DavidsonMorris](https://www.davidsonmorris.com/office-dress-code/), with ties and suits now genuinely rare outside specific sectors. The shift tracks demographics and industry as much as attitude — Northern Ireland leads the country on casual-dress job postings while London sits at the bottom, just 2.3%, a gap explained almost entirely by London's concentration of financial-services employers who've held the line, per the same reporting.

Even historic holdouts have bent. Lloyd's of London, a byword for City formality, now tells staff to "dress for their day" with no blanket requirement for business dress, and major employers including Amazon actively discourage candidates from over-dressing for interviews, per [euronews](https://euronews.com/business/2025/11/08/casual-is-the-new-corporate-uk-offices-redefine-workwear-norms) reporting on the shift. Legally, the UK's Equality and Human Rights Commission requires dress codes to apply equivalent — not identical — standards across genders, formalizing flexibility that was already spreading informally through "smart casual" and what UK trend-watchers have taken to calling "corp-core."

### China: Conservative Center, Rebellious Edges

Chinese workplace dress remains, at the center of the distribution, considerably more formal than Britain's. Men in suits, women in modest business dresses or skirt suits, muted colors, minimal skin — the baseline holds especially firmly at state-owned enterprises and government-adjacent employers, where flashy branding or flamboyant styling is strongly discouraged even without a written rule, according to [HRone](https://hrone.com/blog/what-do-chinese-people-wear-to-work-in-china/). Sixty percent of Chinese employees report believing that dressing appropriately genuinely affects career advancement — a belief with real behavioral consequences in how seriously formality is taken.

But the edges are fraying visibly, and loudly. A wave of mostly younger, mostly female workers has taken to Chinese social media under hashtags translating roughly to "gross outfits at work," deliberately wearing pajama-adjacent, deeply unglamorous clothing to the office as a documented extension of the "lying flat" movement — an explicit, low-effort protest against grinding work conditions, according to [Fortune](https://fortune.com/2024/03/30/china-gen-z-gross-outfits-at-work-lying-flat). Running in the opposite cultural direction, the guochao ("national wave") trend has pulled traditional Chinese aesthetics — qipao-inspired tops, hanfu-style jackets — into ordinary daily wear, a Gen Z-driven movement expected to anchor a fashion market worth over three trillion yuan by 2028, per [Heuritech](https://heuritech.com/articles/china-fashion-heritage/).

### The Reckoning

The two countries are converging on informality from opposite institutional directions. In Britain, casualization came top-down — employers relaxed policy, and culture followed. In China, casualization is happening bottom-up and unevenly — the institution hasn't moved, so younger workers are pushing back through individual, semi-viral acts of sartorial protest that coexist, oddly, with a simultaneous embrace of traditional formalwear elsewhere in the same demographic. Hofstede Insights scores the UK notably higher on individualism than China, and dress culture reflects that gap precisely: British informality is sanctioned and uniform once an employer adopts it, while Chinese informality is personal, contested, and legible as a statement precisely because the institutional baseline hasn't budged.

For a newcomer, the practical trap is symmetrical. Arrive in London dressed for a 2015 finance floor and you'll look like you missed a memo. Arrive in Shanghai dressed for a 2026 Shoreditch startup and, outside a handful of hip tech firms, you'll look like you're not taking the job seriously — the exact opposite read your outfit was going for.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who relocated from London to Shanghai for a finance role wrote that they underestimated how sharply sector mattered — their friend at a Shenzhen startup dressed almost identically to a London tech worker, while they themselves needed a full formal wardrobe within the first week.*

> *TeamBlind — A professional comparing dress norms across offices noted that Chinese men's business attire skews more uniformly conservative than Western offices assume, with dark suits remaining the default even among younger managers who dress casually outside work hours.*

> *Quora — A traveler who spent time in both London and Beijing offices observed that London's casualness reads as a deliberate policy choice communicated clearly by HR, while Chinese casualness, where it exists, tends to be read as risk-taking by the individual rather than something sanctioned from above.*

> *TeamBlind — An employee in a UK banking team said their dress code remained close to traditional formal even as peer companies relaxed theirs, underscoring how much sector — not just country — still determines the actual rule in Britain despite the national trend toward informality.*

> *Quora — Someone advising a colleague moving to China for work suggested erring conservative for the first month regardless of company type, noting that dress norms are far easier to relax once observed in person than to walk back after arriving underdressed on day one.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to the UK, the safe move is to ask, not assume — the "British office" of stereotype barely exists anymore, replaced by a patchwork that ranges from full formal in the City to genuinely anything-goes in a Shoreditch startup. If you're moving to China, default conservative and watch before you adjust — the formality gradient is real, sector-dependent, and unforgiving of a wrong first guess in a way Britain, having mostly given up on enforcing a dress code at all, no longer is.
