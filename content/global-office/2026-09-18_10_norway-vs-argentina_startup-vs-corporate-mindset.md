## Flat by Default, Scrappy by Necessity: Startup Mindsets in Oslo and Buenos Aires

**🇳🇴 Norway · 🇦🇷 Argentina**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Oslo, a cleaner and a chief executive might genuinely stop and chat in the hallway, because flatness isn't a startup affectation there — it's how Norwegian institutions of every size, corporate or not, have run for decades. In Buenos Aires, 1 in 7 people is running some kind of entrepreneurial venture, less because flat structures are trendy and more because reinvention has historically been a survival skill. Norway's startups look like its corporations. Argentina's corporations increasingly wish they looked more like its startups.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect genuine flatness — even senior leaders are typically approachable without excessive formality, at startups and large firms alike | Assume title or seniority alone commands automatic deference; it doesn't function that way here |
| Contribute opinions and pushback in meetings — group input into decisions is expected, not an imposition | Wait to be asked for your view; silence can read as disengagement, not respect |
| Expect a real 37.5-hour standard week and genuinely flexible hours, even outside the startup world | Assume Norway's flatness and flexibility are startup-specific quirks that disappear at established companies |
| Treat managers as coaches and facilitators, not authority figures to be managed carefully | Perform excessive deference toward leadership; it can read as odd or even a little uncomfortable |
| Lean on Norway's strong labor unions and worker-rights framework as a genuine backstop, not just a formality | Assume startup energy means startup-level job insecurity — Norwegian labor protections generally still apply |

#### 🇦🇷 Argentina

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest real time in relationship-building with colleagues — it's described as the single biggest factor in making work life function well | Treat rapport-building at the start of a meeting as a delay before the "real" business begins |
| Expect executives specifically to work notably long hours, sometimes until 10pm, even where junior staff keep closer to 9-to-5 | Assume the demanding executive pace extends evenly to all levels — many employees genuinely disconnect after hours |
| Learn Spanish seriously if you want access to the full job market — it's the default working language outside a narrow multinational/tech slice | Expect an English-only career path outside multinational, tech, remote, or tourism-adjacent roles |
| Take Buenos Aires's startup scene seriously — it's a top-100 global ecosystem with hundreds of active ventures | Assume Argentina's broader economic volatility means the entrepreneurial energy is superficial; the scrappy reputation is earned |
| Expect fluid time culture — meetings starting later than scheduled is normal, not a sign of disorganization | Read a late start as disrespect for your time; it reflects a different, relationship-first cultural clock |

### Norway: The Whole Economy Runs Like a Startup

Norway's defining feature isn't that its startups are flat — it's that flatness was never really a startup innovation there to begin with. Flat structure, equality, and trust are described as core values across Norwegian working life broadly, with important decisions typically made through group consultation rather than top-down directive, regardless of company size or age (Magnus Legal, Working with Norwegians). Managers are expected to function as coaches and facilitators; even in large, established organizations, senior leaders are described as genuinely accessible, capable of stopping to chat with a cleaner with the same ease as with another executive (Working with Norwegians, Quora discussion on Norwegian job culture).

Where Oslo's tech and startup scene does stand out is in degree, not kind — flatness is "particularly evident" there, per relocation guides, precisely because the sector attracts founders already comfortable with collaborative, low-hierarchy decision-making as a competitive advantage (NLS Norway Relocation Group). But the underlying legal and cultural scaffolding — a 37.5-hour standard week, flexible hours as the norm rather than the exception, and strong union protections — applies just as much to a 100-year-old shipping company as to a two-year-old fintech (Quora, NLS Norway Relocation Group). A newcomer expecting Norwegian corporate life to feel meaningfully more rigid than its startups will be surprised by how little daylight there actually is between the two.

### Argentina: Scrappiness Is the National Operating System

Argentina's entrepreneurial rate is genuinely exceptional — the country ranks in the global top 20 for share of the population engaged in entrepreneurial activity, with roughly 1 in 7 Argentines involved in some kind of venture, and 60% of Argentine youth reportedly preferring the entrepreneurial path over a traditional job (Kunturlab, LatamList). Buenos Aires anchors this as the fifth-largest entrepreneurial hub in Latin America, with over 1,200 active startups as of recent counts, a 25% increase from 2020, and a top-100 global ranking from StartupBlink (Kunturlab, Expat.com).

What's distinct is where this scrappiness actually comes from: rather than a story of abundant capital chasing innovation, commentators point to Argentina's economic volatility itself as the generative force — a "figure it out and make it happen" spirit born of necessity rather than comfort, especially compared to wealthier regional peers (Kunturlab). Venture capital investment remains strikingly thin, just 0.025% of GDP in 2022, among the lowest in Latin America (Kunturlab) — meaning the entrepreneurial energy runs largely on resourcefulness rather than funding. Corporate Buenos Aires, meanwhile, still runs on a more traditional relational, hierarchical clock: fluid time culture, real relationship investment before business gets done, and a notable split between executives who reportedly work until 10pm and staff who cleanly disconnect at 5 (Expat.com, Howdy).

### The Reckoning

The instinctive framing — Norway's flat and Argentina's scrappy — undersells how differently each country arrived at its current posture. Norway's flatness is an institutional, decades-deep feature applied uniformly across company size and age; it's the default operating mode of the whole economy, startup or not, backed by strong formal labor protections. Argentina's scrappiness is a response to instability rather than a structural design choice, concentrated specifically in the entrepreneurial sector, and it coexists uneasily with a more traditionally hierarchical, unevenly-distributed-hours corporate culture running in parallel just a few blocks away.

The practical result: a Norwegian startup and a Norwegian corporation will likely feel far more similar to each other than a Buenos Aires startup and a Buenos Aires corporation, which can feel like genuinely different countries depending on which floor of which building you're on.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone asking how job culture works in Norway and whether a hierarchy system exists was told that formal titles technically exist but function almost decoratively — the person answering said they'd seen a company's top executive casually debate a decision with a first-year hire without it registering as unusual to anyone in the room.*

> *r/argentina — A contributor working at a Buenos Aires multinational described the split between executive and staff hours as the most jarring adjustment for foreign hires — watching senior leadership visibly present well into the evening while their own 9-to-5 contract was treated as a hard, respected boundary took time to trust was real.*

> *Quora — Someone comparing startup versus corporate work culture broadly noted that startups often win on autonomy and flat structure but lose on stability and benefits — a tradeoff a commenter said mapped almost perfectly onto Argentina's contrast between its scrappy startup scene and its more traditional established firms.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A poster who'd relocated to Oslo for a tech role wrote that the hardest cultural recalibration wasn't the flat hierarchy itself but realizing that offering an unsolicited opinion in a meeting, something they'd have hesitated to do at their previous job, was actively expected of them from day one.*

> *Expat.com (Buenos Aires guide) — A relocation resource advised that building genuine rapport with colleagues before diving into business wasn't just etiquette but the single biggest practical factor separating expats who found working life in Buenos Aires enjoyable from those who found it frustratingly slow.*

### Conclusion

If you're deciding between these two moves, ask yourself whether you want flatness as the whole economy's default, or scrappiness as a specific sector's survival trait sitting next to a more conventional corporate world. Norway will ask you to speak up, share the workload of deciding things, and expect that same treatment regardless of which employer signs your contract. Argentina will ask you to invest in the relationships first, figure out which floor of the building you're actually on, and accept that its startup energy and its corporate hierarchy are, for now, running two different playbooks. My honest advice, over a drink: in Oslo, use your voice; in Buenos Aires, use your patience — and your Spanish.
