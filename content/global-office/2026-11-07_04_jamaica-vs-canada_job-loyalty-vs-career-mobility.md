**Jamaica Wants You for Life; Canada Wants Your Resume Updated by Friday**

🇯🇲 Jamaica · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Begin with the premise that loyalty is a virtue employers love to praise and almost never reward, and you have the shared operating theory of two very differently sun-drenched labour markets. In Jamaica, workers leap from retail counters to call-centre headsets for salaries that can double overnight, according to the Jamaica Observer's reporting on the island's business-process-outsourcing sector — a rational response to an economy where unemployment just hit a record low near 3.5 percent, per STATIN-sourced figures. In Canada, Statistics Canada reports that more than a third of workers have logged a decade or more with one employer, yet separate workforce research finds 88 percent of them only got promoted by quitting first. Loyalty, it turns out, is not the same thing as stability, and a serious move abroad for work deserves to know which of the two you are actually signing up for before the moving truck arrives.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇯🇲 Jamaica

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn a few Patois greetings and workplace phrases — it signals respect, not performance. | Don't assume "likkle more" or "soon come" is an actual deadline. |
| Negotiate salary directly and firmly before accepting — bands are looser than they look. | Don't badmouth a former employer in an interview; professional Kingston is a small room. |
| Greet the most senior person in the room first; hierarchy is read, not just felt. | Don't skip small talk before business — going straight to the point reads as cold. |
| Build a genuinely personal relationship with your manager; trust travels person to person. | Don't expect a formal exit interview or reference pipeline — leaving well is relational, not procedural. |
| Keep your own network current, since colleagues treat migration and job-hopping as normal planning. | Don't take it personally when a trained colleague leaves for a BPO job paying double. |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep your resume current while employed — official data shows promotion rarely comes from inside. | Don't wait for a tap on the shoulder; ask directly for advancement or start looking. |
| Treat networking and visibility as part of the job, not an after-hours hobby. | Don't mistake office politeness for a commitment to keeping you long-term. |
| Negotiate again at every renewal; pay tracks the market, not years served. | Don't assume a decade at one firm reads as ambition — among younger staff it often reads as the opposite. |
| Ask about internal-mobility policy at the offer stage, not two frustrated years in. | Don't skip the exit interview — it's standard, polite, and occasionally negotiable. |
| Use interprovincial licensing and mobility rules to your advantage if your profession is regulated. | Don't burn bridges leaving; small-world sectors like finance, tech and health remember both ways. |

Jamaica's labour market is, by official measure, in rude health: STATIN put unemployment at a record low of roughly 3.5 percent in early 2025, a figure widely cited across the island's financial press. What those headline numbers understate is churn beneath the surface. The Jamaica Manufacturers and Exporters Association told the Jamaica Observer that staff loss to migration and job-hopping is now a "top-of-mind concern" for members, and companies including Lasco and the grocery chain Purity have had to raise wages mid-cycle — in one executive's words, taking "off the gloves" — simply to stop rivals poaching trained staff outright. Employees moving from retail counters to BPO call centres can double their salaries in the process, which rather undercuts any lecture about staying the course for institutional memory's sake. Purity's management, for instance, told the Observer it had to revise salaries and expand headcount specifically to slow the bleeding of truck drivers and sales staff to competitors offering more for the same job description.

The Jamaica Employers' Federation's Wayne Chen frames this as a market functioning rather than malfunctioning: younger workers now move for wages, conditions and work-life balance instead of staying until retirement the way earlier generations did. "Those days are virtually behind us," he told the Observer. The flip side is a brain-drain problem serious enough that Chen ranks Jamaica among the world's most affected countries, with chronic shortages in healthcare, education and STEM teaching — fields where the most mobile workers leave the country rather than merely the employer. Hofstede Insights' comparative data situates Jamaica as a relatively collectivist, higher-power-distance culture, which makes its workforce's growing willingness to simply walk all the more notable — one would expect loyalty to the group to extend to the employer, and increasingly it does not, because the group itself is migrating.

Statistics Canada's job-tenure figures tell a similarly split story. Overall, 36.1 percent of workers aged 25 and over have logged a decade or more with the same employer, up from 32.5 percent in 1976 — hardly the portrait of a nation incapable of commitment. But disaggregate the number and the loyalty is unevenly distributed: only 7.9 percent of workers aged 25 to 34 have hit the ten-year mark, recent immigrants are more than twice as likely as Canadian-born workers to have under a year's tenure, and Black Canadian workers show a similarly elevated churn rate. Whatever stability exists in Canada's labour market is concentrated among older, native-born, often public-sector or agricultural workers — not the newcomers the country spends considerable effort recruiting. Immigrants who landed within the past decade are roughly 24 percent likely to have under a year on the job, more than double the Canadian-born rate, a gap Statistics Canada's own researchers attribute partly to credential recognition delays and partly to simple churn while newcomers find their footing.

For nearly everyone else, mobility is simply how the ladder works. A widely cited Canadian workforce study found that 88 percent of workers who climbed into a more senior role did so by changing employers, that only about 11.5 percent of promotions happen internally, and that the average worker holds roughly fifteen jobs across a career, typically needing three or four different employers just to go from entry-level to supervisor. Canadian managers, the same research notes, tend to judge an employee they already know more harshly than a stranger in a first interview, and employers are more likely to fill a senior opening with an external candidate than to run a formal succession plan at all — which is as good an argument as any for treating one's current employer as a waiting room rather than a destination.

The irony is that these two labour markets, superficially opposite, converge on the same lesson from different directions. Jamaica's churn is wage-driven and almost entirely external to the firm: workers leave because a rival company, or an entire sector like BPO, is simply paying more for the same skill, and STATIN's tidy unemployment figures mask how much reshuffling is happening underneath. Canada's churn, by contrast, is structural and internal to how the corporate ladder itself is built: Statistics Canada's relatively high headline tenure numbers coexist with a promotion system that statistically requires an exit first. One country's workers leave because the market outside is hot; the other's leave because the org chart inside is cold. Put differently: a Jamaican employer can, in theory, out-compete the churn by simply paying more, and some are now doing exactly that; a Canadian employer would need to redesign how promotion itself works, which is a far less popular agenda item at the annual budget meeting.

What looks like a cultural gap — Hofstede's relatively collectivist, higher-power-distance Jamaica set against Canada's famously individualist, consensus-driven workplace — produces nearly identical personal advice once an actual offer letter is on the table. The Jamaican employer lamenting "poaching" and the Canadian manager quietly judging a familiar face more harshly than a new one are, functionally, running the same experiment: testing how long a good employee will stay without being pushed. In both countries, the honest answer the data gives is: not very long, and increasingly, not for lack of affection for the job itself. The brochure version of each country — Jamaica as warm and relational, Canada as orderly and meritocratic — survives the move intact. What it quietly omits is that warmth and order both turn out to be fairly weak substitutes for a counteroffer.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit, r/jobs — a commenter described turning down several better offers to stay loyal to one employer for over a decade, only to be laid off anyway.*

> *Reddit, r/jobs — another commenter described moving from twelve-fifty an hour to a six-figure salary within five years, entirely by changing employers rather than waiting on internal raises.*

> *Expat.com, Jamaica forum — a longtime visitor warned newcomers that local labour law requires employers to give hiring priority to Jamaican citizens over equally qualified foreign applicants, a rule that caught several hopeful movers off guard.*

> *Expat.com, Canada forum — a Malaysian immigrant settled in Halifax advised securing permanent residency before job-hunting, since employers took applications noticeably more seriously once status was no longer a question mark.*

> *Narcity (first-person newcomer feature) — a lawyer who arrived in Canada and started out as an administrative assistant described being promoted to partnerships manager within six months, crediting managers who looked past the resume gap rather than at it.*

### Conclusion

For someone actually weighing the move, the useful distinction is not which country is more loyal to its workers — neither, particularly — but which kind of churn you would rather manage. Jamaica will ask you to compete for your own salary against the BPO call centre down the road. Canada will ask you to functionally resign from your own company in order to get promoted within it. Both will describe this, with a straight face, as a healthy labour market, and both will be able to point to an official statistic that technically agrees with them.

Pack accordingly: in Jamaica, keep your network current, because the next raise is probably external; in Canada, keep your resume current, because the next promotion almost certainly is. If a friend asked me over a drink, I would tell her what I would tell anyone about a serious relationship with a paycheck attached: find out, before you sign anything, which direction the loyalty is actually supposed to flow.
