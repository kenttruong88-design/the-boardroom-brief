# In Hong Kong, "No" Is a Feeling You're Meant to Detect. In Australia, "No" Is a Sentence You're Meant to Hear.

**🇭🇰 Hong Kong · 🇦🇺 Australia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Hong Kong meeting can end with everyone smiling, nodding, and agreeing to nothing in particular, because a direct "no" would have been read as hostile. An Australian meeting can end with a colleague telling you, to your face, that your report's numbers in section three are wrong — and both parties walk away thinking the meeting went fine. Neither culture is being rude. They have simply decided, independently, that the other one's normal is unbearable.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet the most senior person in the room first, with a firm handshake | Address the most senior person last, or skip the greeting ritual |
| Exchange business cards with both hands, and read the one you receive | Pocket a business card without glancing at it first |
| Listen for what isn't said — a hesitant "maybe" often means "no" | Push a senior colleague for a hard yes/no in front of the group |
| Schedule meetings well ahead and avoid Lunar New Year entirely | Book anything important around the Lunar New Year or major holidays |
| Stay composed even when a negotiation gets tense | Raise your voice or visibly lose patience — it reads as unprofessional |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Speak up in meetings — silence reads as disengagement, not respect | Wait passively for instructions from someone more senior |
| Call your manager by their first name | Open an email with "Dear Sir/Madam" — it reads as oddly formal |
| Expect direct feedback on the work itself, not on you personally | Take criticism of a report as a personal attack |
| Make small talk about weekends and sport before getting to business | Skip straight to the agenda with no warm-up at all |
| Push back on an unreasonable workload professionally | Assume saying no to a manager will damage the relationship |

### Hong Kong

Hong Kong's meeting culture runs on protocol before it runs on content. Rivermate's cultural guide and Compass Offices both describe a fixed opening sequence: the most senior person in the room — sometimes the eldest associate rather than the highest title — is greeted first, business cards are exchanged with both hands, and only then does substance begin. Underneath the ritual sits a deeper rule about disagreement: Compass Offices notes that "'yes' and 'no' are communicated in non-direct ways, as a direct 'no' could be taken as too harsh," and pressuring a senior colleague for a clear answer "can be seen as aggressive and hot-headed." The goal throughout is composure — staying "calm and well-mannered in the heat of negotiation" is treated as a professional skill in its own right, not just good manners.

The result is a meeting style built for reading subtext. A hesitation, a shift in tone, a vague "we'll consider it" — these carry the actual information, and foreign managers who take them at face value routinely walk out of meetings believing they got a yes when the room understood they'd gotten a no.

### Australia

Australian meeting culture does the opposite on almost every axis, and it does so loudly. Performance Education's guide for migrant professionals describes feedback delivered plainly — "the report was good, but the numbers in section 3 need to be double-checked" — with no cushioning compliment attached, because Australians are taught to critique the work rather than the person. Migratio's guide for new arrivals goes further: managers will tell an employee outright, "this isn't ready, here's what's missing," and staff are expected to challenge ideas and raise problems directly with decision-makers rather than escalating quietly through layers of hierarchy. Waiting to be told what to do reads as disengagement, not deference.

None of this makes Australian meetings efficient, however — just honest about being inefficient. An Atlassian survey of Australian workers, reported by the ACS, found meetings ineffective 76 percent of the time, with nearly two-thirds of respondents sitting through meetings whose goal was never actually stated, and three-quarters saying meetings frequently produce nothing but a decision to schedule another meeting.

### The Reckoning

The paradox is that Hong Kong's indirectness is often more efficient at avoiding wasted meetings than Australia's directness — a vague "we'll consider it" ends a conversation cleanly, while an Australian team can spend 45 minutes directly debating an agenda item and still walk out having decided only to meet again next week. Hofstede Insights data frames part of why: Hong Kong's power-distance score of 68 makes hierarchy the organizing principle of every meeting, while Australia's score of 36 makes hierarchy something actively downplayed, even when a senior person is in the room.

The irony cuts deeper on what each culture considers polite. Hong Kong's indirectness exists specifically to protect feelings and status — softening a "no" is an act of consideration. Australia's directness exists for an almost identical reason, just inverted — Australians consider it more respectful to tell someone the truth about their work than to let them walk away misinformed. Both cultures believe they are being kind. Neither one recognizes the other's kindness as kindness at all.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Performance Education — The migrant-focused career guide flagged a specific feedback example that routinely blindsides newcomers: a manager saying plainly, "the report was good, but the numbers in section 3 need to be double-checked," with no personal compliment attached to soften it — intended as professional, often received as cold.*

> *Migratio — The newcomer-orientation guide noted that junior staff are expected to "challenge ideas they disagree with, and raise problems directly with decision-makers," rather than working through hierarchy — a practical misread risk for anyone arriving from a chain-of-command culture who assumes silence is the safe, respectful default.*

> *Compass Offices — The guide's warning about Hong Kong etiquette doubles as a practical heads-up: pressuring a senior colleague for a definitive answer "can be seen as aggressive and hot-headed, affecting your professional reputation" — a reputational cost that isn't obvious until after it's already been paid.*

> *IOR World — The cross-cultural training resource's business-etiquette notes for Hong Kong recommend scheduling meetings well in advance and explicitly avoiding major holiday periods, a logistical detail that sounds minor until a visiting executive discovers their counterpart has vanished for Lunar New Year with no warning.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving from Hong Kong to Australia, the single hardest adjustment isn't the accent — it's recalibrating what silence means. In Hong Kong, saying nothing in a meeting can be strategic; in Australia, it gets read as having nothing to contribute. If you're moving the other way, the hardest adjustment is learning that a hesitant "we'll see" is doing real work in the conversation, and that pushing for the direct answer you're used to can cost you more goodwill than the clarity is worth. Neither style is more honest than the other — they're just honest about different things. Learn which one you're in before you mistake a Hong Kong "maybe" for interest, or an Australian "no" for an insult.
