**Blazers in Bucharest, Boardshorts in Brisbane: The Corporate Dress Code, Decoded**

🇷🇴 Romania · 🇦🇺 Australia

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In one of these countries, a government department had to issue a formal memo asking employees to stop wearing onesies and sheepskin boots to the office. In the other, failing to shake a new business partner's hand is filed, informally but seriously, under "insult." The relocating professional who packs one wardrobe for both postings will be either grossly overdressed or quietly unemployable within the week, and the difference is not a matter of taste. It is a matter of org charts.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇷🇴 Romania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a blazer or structured jacket to first meetings, even in August | Show up in shorts, sandals, or a "casual Friday" T-shirt to a multinational's Bucharest office |
| Keep hemlines at or below the knee for client-facing roles | Remove your jacket in a meeting before the most senior person does |
| Use *Domnul*/*Doamnă* plus surname until invited to switch to first names | Interrupt a senior colleague's point, even to agree with it |
| Bring a small, tasteful gift (roughly 100 lei or less) to a first meeting with a new partner | Joke about the communist period or assume Romania is interchangeable with its neighbors |
| Shake hands with everyone in the room, every time | Skip the handshake — it reads as a deliberate slight, not an oversight |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask about the dress code during the interview itself; it varies enormously by employer | Wear thongs (flip-flops), a onesie, or ugg boots to the office — one federal department had to ban this in writing |
| Default to smart casual (collared shirt, trousers, closed shoes) until you've read the room | Over-dress for an internal pitch meeting; it can read as compensating for something |
| Expect to call your manager by their first name from day one | Mistake the relaxed dress code for relaxed deadlines |
| Take lunch away from your desk; stepping out for coffee isn't treated as slacking | Be thrown when a junior hire disagrees with you openly in a meeting |
| Keep one blazer at your desk for the occasional client visit | Comment on a colleague's appearance or clothing — it is taken seriously, and careers have ended over it |

---

Romania's workplace is still, structurally, a hierarchy with a dress code attached rather than the other way around. Hofstede's Power Distance Index — a measure of how much a society accepts unequal distribution of authority — puts Romania at 90, among the highest in Europe, a figure cited repeatedly in Romanian management literature analyzing why decisions flow down and are rarely challenged from below. ExpatArrivals and BusinessCulture.org, both widely used etiquette references for incoming professionals, describe near-identical first-meeting expectations: a conservative suit, a tailored jacket, discreet jewelry, skirts at or below the knee, titles used with surnames until explicitly waived. None of this is vanity. It is a visible signal of where you sit in a system that still runs on seniority.

The code is not monolithic, though outsiders often assume it is. ExpatArrivals notes that business casual — a jacket paired with jeans for men, or a blouse and knee-length skirt for women — becomes acceptable once you're past the first formal introduction, typically at business lunches or in less hierarchical settings. The real split runs by sector rather than by city: Bucharest's banking, legal, and government offices still dress for the hierarchy Hofstede's numbers describe, while the IT and outsourcing firms that increasingly anchor the capital's economy run closer to the global tech default of smart casual, because their clients and often their management sit elsewhere. A new arrival's safest first move is still the blazer; how quickly you get to retire it depends entirely on which Bucharest you've landed in.

Money sharpens the picture. Romania's National Institute of Statistics put the average net wage in Bucharest at RON 6,978 (about €1,372) in October 2025, 27% above the national average of RON 5,492, according to Economedia's reporting via Romania-Insider — a reminder that Bucharest's corporate towers and the rest of the country are, economically, closer to two different labor markets than one. The formality travels with the paycheck: the better the job, the stiffer the collar. A returning Romanian professional, writing in Business Magazin after years in a US office, described a culture where staff genuinely wait for a manager's signal before acting on their own judgment, and where admitting fault or saying thank you happens more slowly than she'd gotten used to abroad — a workplace style that outsiders sometimes misread as coldness rather than caution.

Australia inverts nearly all of it. Hofstede's Power Distance score for Australia sits at 36, less than half of Romania's, and the flatness shows up in practice long before it shows up in a textbook: insiderguides.com.au notes that Australian offices are "generally informal" with "a flatter hierarchy" where even senior staff are approachable by first name on day one. The dress code follows the hierarchy down. The Australian Bureau of Statistics' August 2025 Characteristics of Employment survey found 36% of employed Australians usually worked from home, with that figure climbing to 59% among managers and professionals specifically — the group, not coincidentally, most associated with suits in other countries. A lot of Australian "corporate attire" now exists for the handful of days a month someone is actually in a room with a client.

Here, too, the uniform (or lack of one) tracks the industry rather than the national mood. insiderguides.com.au, a resource aimed specifically at newly arrived professionals, notes that law, finance, and technical roles run noticeably more formal than the national stereotype suggests, especially in the capital cities, while creative industries default to smart casual and retail or hospitality workers simply wear whatever uniform the employer hands them. The practical advice given to newcomers is almost comically modest: dress a notch more formally than expected for the interview, then spend the first week quietly checking what everyone else is actually wearing before committing to a wardrobe. Nobody will spell out the dress code outright, because in a flat hierarchy nobody especially wants to be the one enforcing it.

But informal does not mean unregulated, and this is the trap that catches new arrivals who overcorrect. In 2015, Australia's Department of Immigration had to issue an explicit dress-code memo — reported at the time by HR Grapevine — after staff turned up in one-piece pajama suits, sheepskin boots, and flip-flops, prompting a senior official to tell a parliamentary committee, on the record, that "there are certain things that wouldn't constitute professional business dress." Smart casual in Australia has a floor. It is simply a much lower floor than Bucharest's, and it is enforced by social pressure and the occasional memo rather than by an unspoken sense of rank.

The Reckoning is this: Romania's dress code punishes you for underestimating the room, and Australia's punishes you for overestimating it. Arrive in Bucharest in a hoodie and you will be quietly recategorized as junior, foreign, or both, regardless of your actual title — the clothing is read as a statement about where you think you sit in the hierarchy, and getting it wrong costs you authority you will spend months earning back. Arrive in Sydney in a three-piece suit for an internal strategy meeting and you will not lose authority, but you will be treated, gently, as someone who hasn't figured out the room yet, which is its own kind of cost in a culture that signals trust through ease rather than polish.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — A senior American executive who relocated to an Australian company for a leadership role kept to the conservative business suits she'd always worn, only to find herself in a male-dominated boardroom where colleagues still commented on her appearance and clothing choices; she noted the informality of Australian offices does not always extend evenly to how women in senior roles are treated.*

> *WayUp Community — An American intern in Sydney, used to dressing conservatively for US internships, wore a sheer blouse to the office without a second look, while male colleagues worked in denim, shorts, and sneakers; she found the relaxed dress code came with a genuine loosening of supervision, including taking coffee and lunch breaks without asking permission first.*

> *InterNations Expat Insider survey — Expats in Romania rated the country a below-average 37th of 59 destinations on the Working Abroad Index overall, but a relatively strong 71% said they were satisfied with their work-life balance, suggesting the hierarchy is rigid about rank but not necessarily about your evening plans.*

> *Romania-Insider / Business Magazin — A Romanian professional returning home after years in a US office described the adjustment less in terms of clothing than conduct: colleagues who waited for explicit management direction rather than acting on their own judgment, and a slower, more cautious culture around apologizing or saying thank you to clients.*

> *HR Grapevine — Australia's own Department of Immigration had to put its casual-Friday culture in writing after staff showed up in onesies, sheepskin boots, and flip-flops, proof that "smart casual" is a real standard with real enforcement, not just a vibe employees are trusted to intuit correctly.*

### Conclusion

If you are choosing between these two postings on dress code alone, choose based on how much you want your clothing to do your talking for you. In Romania, a tailored jacket is a functional tool: it borrows you credibility in a system where credibility is otherwise earned slowly and from the top down, and skipping it costs you more than comfort. In Australia, the jacket mostly just makes you warm; credibility there is earned by not making anyone's job harder, dressing is read as social calibration rather than rank, and the real test is whether you can take a direct comment from a junior colleague without flinching.

Pack the blazer for Bucharest and wear it like you mean it. Pack it for Sydney too, but leave it on the back of your chair, because the person who actually needs it is the one who forgot their own read on the room.
