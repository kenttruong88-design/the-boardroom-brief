# In China, the Meeting Is Theater. In the Netherlands, It's the Whole Play.

🇨🇳 China · 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Beijing, a two-hour meeting can produce ninety minutes of respectful silence, a senior executive nodding at a slide deck, and exactly one real decision — reached afterward, over tea, by two people who weren't scheduled to be there. In Amsterdam, a twenty-minute meeting can produce four open disagreements, one unsolicited critique of your spreadsheet, and a binding decision made on the spot, out loud, by everyone in the room, including the intern. Both offices will tell you their system is efficient. Neither is being sarcastic. That, roughly, is the distance you are about to travel.

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Let the most senior person enter the room and speak first | Open with your point before rank has been acknowledged |
| Treat "we will consider it" as a polite no | Demand a straight yes or no in front of the group |
| Save real disagreement for a private follow-up conversation | Contradict a colleague or superior in front of others |
| Expect the actual decision to happen after the meeting, over a meal or on WeChat | Assume the meeting itself is where the outcome gets decided |
| Stay until the ranking attendee signals it's over | Check your phone or leave early, even discreetly |
| Invest in the relationship before you make the ask | Lead with business on a first meeting |

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Say plainly what you think, including that an idea is weak | Bury your objection in so much diplomacy that nobody can find it |
| Bring data; someone will ask for your source mid-sentence | Rely on confidence or seniority to carry an argument |
| Expect junior staff to challenge a manager's numbers out loud | Assume rank settles the disagreement |
| Start and end exactly on time | Treat the scheduled finish as a soft suggestion |
| Read blunt feedback as being about the work, not about you | Take a sharp comment on your report personally |
| Expect consensus-building even when one person could simply decide | Expect a fast, top-down call by default |

### China: The Meeting as Ceremony

According to Hofstede Insights' country comparison, China scores 80 on Power Distance against the Netherlands' 38 — a gap that shows up in a Chinese meeting room the moment you notice who walks in first, who sits where, and who is permitted to speak without being asked. Meetings in Chinese firms often function less as decision-making forums than as ritual confirmations of a decision already substantially made, a dynamic Harvard Business Review has documented in its research on authority and decision-making across cultures: hierarchical consultation happens beforehand, and the meeting itself exists to display alignment, not generate it. Junior staff speak selectively, if at all, and the senior-most voice in the room typically speaks first and last.

This ceremonial quality coexists with genuinely long hours. The National Bureau of Statistics of China put average weekly working hours for enterprise employees at roughly 48.6 in recent data, and the South China Morning Post has spent years documenting the "996" schedule — 9 a.m. to 9 p.m., six days a week — that persists in tech despite running afoul of national labor law. None of that overtime is evenly distributed across the meeting itself; a great deal of it is spent waiting for the meeting to matter. Direct refusal is rare by design: the concept of face, or miànzi, means a hard no in front of a group causes a loss of dignity that can outlast the business relationship that triggered it, so disagreement gets rerouted into euphemism, delay, and private conversation instead.

### Netherlands: The Meeting as Argument, Held on Time

The Netherlands sits near the opposite end of nearly every relevant Hofstede dimension, and the OECD's Better Life Index gives the abstraction some teeth: only about 0.4 percent of Dutch employees regularly work very long hours, versus an OECD average near 11 percent, and the Netherlands ranks first among OECD countries on work-life balance. CBS, the national statistics office, puts average weekly hours for a full-time employee at 39.2, with the overall workforce average pulled down to roughly 27.7 hours by the country's unusually high rate of part-time work. There is, in short, less time to waste in a meeting, and the culture treats wasting it as close to a moral failing.

What fills that time is argument, not performance. Dutch colleagues will tell a room a proposal is illogical, overcomplicated, or a waste of everyone's morning, and expect that to be received as useful information rather than an insult — directness here is framed as a form of respect for other people's time, not a breach of politeness. The paradox is that flat hierarchy does not mean fast decisions: because status doesn't settle an argument, agreement has to be built the hard way, through open debate, before anything closes. It is efficient in the sense that no one leaves confused about where they stand. It is not always efficient in the sense of leaving quickly.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the intuitive story — hierarchical China moves slow, egalitarian Netherlands moves fast — turns out backward in the room that matters most. A Chinese meeting can wrap in twenty disciplined minutes precisely because the outcome was pre-negotiated through private channels; the public performance is short because nothing is actually being decided live. A Dutch meeting can run long and heated precisely because nothing was pre-negotiated — flat structure means the fight has to happen where everyone can see it, and it doesn't end until the room actually agrees, not until the senior person says so.

The other inversion concerns risk. Foreign managers who import Dutch-style open debate into a Chinese team, or Chinese-style deference into a Dutch one, tend to get the failure mode backward. In China, unstructured debate without a clear final decision-maker leaves staff unwilling to act, because nobody knows whose call it actually was. In the Netherlands, importing top-down deference produces a room that nods and does nothing, because a decision that skipped the argument doesn't feel legitimate enough to execute. The dysfunction isn't the meeting style; it's exporting one country's meeting style into the other country's expectations about what a meeting is for.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A foreigner working at a Chinese firm described sitting through meetings where only the department head spoke for most of the hour, everyone else took notes in silence, and the actual project priorities got reshuffled afterward in a WeChat group they weren't part of.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A foreign manager at a China-based team tried running Western-style open debate in meetings, inviting everyone to weigh in freely; the result was paralysis, because staff wouldn't commit to action without knowing who had actually signed off, and "everyone's opinion counts" read as "no one is actually deciding."*

> *Quora — An expat new to a Dutch company had their first proposal picked apart line by line in front of the whole team and assumed they'd made an enemy; colleagues later explained, genuinely puzzled, that the criticism was about the spreadsheet and had nothing to do with how anyone felt about them personally.*

> *Quora — Someone who relocated to the Netherlands cited the directness itself as a reason for choosing the move, saying meetings there were the first in their career where they left knowing exactly where they stood, instead of decoding a manager's tone for a week afterward.*

> *Quora — A businessperson working with Chinese counterparts learned, after chasing an approval for months, that "we will consider it" had functionally meant no since the first conversation, and wished someone had told them to read silence and vague enthusiasm as a decline rather than a delay.*

### Conclusion

Neither system is being coy for its own sake. China's meeting choreography exists to protect relationships that are expected to outlast any single deal, which means the real information travels through channels a newcomer can't see until someone explains them. The Netherlands' bluntness exists to protect everyone's time and to make sure a decision, once made in the room, doesn't quietly unravel because someone never actually agreed to it. Learn to read the silence in one country and the argument in the other, and you'll stop mistaking either for dysfunction.

The honest summary for anyone packing a bag: in China, the meeting tells you what already happened; in the Netherlands, the meeting is where it happens; and in both places, the person who thinks they understand the room after one visit is the person who understood it least.
