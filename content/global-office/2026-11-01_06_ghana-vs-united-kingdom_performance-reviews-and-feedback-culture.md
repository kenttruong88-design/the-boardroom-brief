**Ghana to the United Kingdom: The Feedback Gets Quieter, Somehow Also Harder to Decode**

🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

More than 135,000 Ghana-born people now live in the United Kingdom, up from under 96,000 at the last census, and the two countries trade roughly £1.6 billion worth of goods and services a year, with British investment backing over 670 projects and some 44,000 jobs across Ghana. What the trade figures don't capture is what happens the first time a Ghanaian professional sits across from a British line manager at an annual review, or vice versa, and tries to work out whether "that's quite a strong piece of work, on the whole" is praise, a warning, or nothing at all. It is, depending on who you ask, one of the two.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect feedback to arrive wrapped in a compliment first, a correction second, and a reassurance third — the "sandwich" is the norm, not evasiveness. | Mistake the opening compliment for the whole message; the correction in the middle is usually the actual point. |
| Treat a raised voice or a sharp comment about a mistake as still within normal range for some managers, however uncomfortable it feels at first. | Assume every manager who criticises bluntly is being unusually harsh by local standards — some genuinely are, but it isn't automatically an outlier. |
| Prepare for your university, hometown, or "which school did you attend" to come up if you make a visible error — it's a real, if unfair, rhetorical move some bosses use. | Take a background-based jab as a formal part of your appraisal record; it's a put-down, not documentation. |
| Approach a formal appraisal expecting more ritual and rhetoric than hard metrics, per academic research on the civil service specifically. | Assume your written appraisal score reflects a rigorous, metric-driven process across every employer — practice varies enormously by sector. |
| Save written praise when you get it — colleagues and researchers both describe praise as the genuinely scarce resource, not criticism. | Expect frequent affirmation for solid, unremarkable work; "no news" is often the closest thing to a good review. |
| Watch how senior staff address a mistake in front of others before you push back visibly yourself. | Publicly contradict a superior's assessment in the room, even if you believe it's wrong — raise it privately afterward instead. |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Listen for qualifiers like "quite," "fairly," and "on the whole" — they routinely soften criticism to the point of near-invisibility. | Take a mild-sounding comment at face value; British understatement can bury a serious concern inside a gentle sentence. |
| Push for specifics if a review feels vague — only 44 percent of UK staff say they've actually been trained to give or receive feedback well. | Assume vagueness means there's nothing more to say; ask a direct follow-up question instead. |
| Expect to be encouraged, in principle, to ask for feedback at any time rather than wait for the annual cycle. | Assume that "ask anytime" culture means managers volunteer hard truths unprompted — 48 percent of UK staff report discomfort giving or receiving feedback at all. |
| Write your own self-assessment carefully — in many UK and multinational firms it anchors or caps your final rating. | Under-rate yourself as a show of humility; several professionals report a low self-score becomes a ceiling, not a starting point for discussion. |
| Ask your manager to sanity-check your self-review before it goes to a director or committee. | Assume your manager's rewrite of your self-assessment will capture the work the way you'd describe it yourself. |
| Treat being told you're "doing fine" as the likely ceiling of unprompted praise at most firms. | Expect enthusiastic, explicit affirmation as standard; British workplace culture tends to default to the unstated. |

### Ghana

Ghanaian feedback culture runs on a structure practically everyone who's lived inside it describes the same way: praise, then the actual criticism, then a softer close — the "sandwich," as a Jobberman Ghana piece on office communication puts it, titled, not subtly, "Office Buzz: When Feedback Feels Like Pepper." The metaphor holds up. Criticism is specific and direct about the thing that went wrong, which can read as blunt to an outsider, but it's typically bracketed by social cushioning on either side rather than delivered cold. The piece also makes a sharper point: praise, not criticism, is the genuinely scarce commodity in many Ghanaian offices, so a rare compliment tends to be remembered and repeated for months.

Formal appraisal is a different animal from day-to-day feedback, at least in the public sector. A University of Ghana study of performance appraisal in the civil service, pointedly titled "Performance Appraisal or Praising Performance?," found the process shot through with rhetoric and ritual rather than rigorous measurement — appraisals that exist as a procedural formality more than a genuine assessment tool, a pattern the researchers link to a wider culture of deference to hierarchy rather than candid evaluation. That deference has sharper edges elsewhere too. A Ghanaian professional who relocated abroad, in a widely shared comparison piece on a Ghanaian news site, described a former boss at home who'd respond to even minor mistakes with insults and, memorably, the pointed question "which school did you attend" — a jab at the employee's competence rather than feedback on the work itself. Commenters on the piece largely agreed that the behaviour was recognisable, not an outlier. Hofstede's data, which scores Ghana only as part of a broader "West Africa" regional cluster rather than individually, puts power distance in that cluster at a steep 77 against the UK's 35 — a number consistent with feedback flowing down a hierarchy more easily than it flows up or sideways.

### United Kingdom

British feedback culture has, by most accounts, been quietly abandoning the once-a-year appraisal in favour of something more continuous, at least in principle. A Randstad UK Workmonitor survey found 67 percent of employees say they're encouraged to give feedback at any time, not just during a formal review window — but only 44 percent say they've actually had any training in how to do it well, and a notable 48 percent admit to feeling uncomfortable either giving or receiving feedback at all. The gap between the stated culture and the lived one is, in other words, substantial: an "ask anytime" policy doesn't automatically produce managers who are good at saying anything specific when asked.

What gets said tends to be filtered through understatement, a trait so consistent it's become a minor genre of workplace translation guides. "Quite good," in a British review, frequently means "not good enough but I don't want the conversation this would require." Specificity, several professionals discussing the system on the workplace forum Blind noted, is often sacrificed in exactly the moments it matters most: one described a manager with little time to properly review a full year of work simply rewording an employee's own self-written summary before passing it up to a director, which left the employee with only a vague sense of how their actual contributions had been judged. The same thread describes self-assessment ratings acting as a soft ceiling rather than a starting point — rate yourself modestly, several posters warned, and that modesty becomes the final number, not merely an opening bid in a negotiation. An American who'd worked in England, writing on an expat forum, put a sharper point on the broader pattern: "communication is often poor compared to stateside," instructions can be unclear enough to cause real mistakes, and in at least one case a show of initiative was actively discouraged by a supervisor rather than rewarded — a style of management feedback that arrives, if it arrives at all, well after the fact.

### The Reckoning

Set side by side, the two systems fail in almost opposite directions. Ghana's feedback, at its most functional, is specific and clearly delivered, wrapped in enough social cushioning that it doesn't land as pure attack — the problem, when things go wrong, is less ambiguity than occasional cruelty, with background-based insults standing in for genuine critique. The UK's feedback, at its most functional, is delivered gently enough to avoid humiliating anyone — the problem is that "gently enough" tips, with real frequency, into "not actually said," leaving employees to reverse-engineer their standing from qualifiers and silences rather than from anything resembling a clear verdict.

Neither country's formal appraisal process comes off especially well under scrutiny. Ghana's civil-service research describes a system heavy on ritual and light on rigorous measurement; the UK's own data shows a policy of open, anytime feedback running well ahead of the training and comfort level needed to actually deliver it. A newcomer moving between these two systems in either direction should expect to recalibrate not just tone but the entire signal-to-noise ratio: in Ghana, assume the criticism buried in the compliment is real and act on it; in the UK, assume there's more criticism underneath the mild phrasing than was actually spoken aloud, and ask.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Jobberman Ghana — An office communication piece aimed at Ghanaian professionals described feedback there as typically arriving "sandwiched" between praise and reassurance, and noted that genuine, unprompted praise is rare enough in most offices that staff tend to remember and repeat it for months afterward.*

> *yen.com.gh — A Ghanaian man working abroad described his former boss back home responding to small mistakes with insults, including pointedly asking which school he'd attended, and contrasted it with a current boss abroad he called measured and tolerant even when correcting the same kind of error; several commenters agreed the behaviour was familiar rather than unusual.*

> *Blind — A commenter described managers with little time to properly review a full year's work simply rewording an employee's self-written summary before sending it up the chain, while others in the same thread warned that rating yourself modestly in a self-assessment tends to set a hard ceiling on the final score rather than open a discussion.*

> *expat.com (England forum) — An American who had worked in England described communication there as "often poor compared to stateside," said unclear instructions had led to real mistakes, and recalled a supervisor who actively discouraged initiative rather than welcoming it.*

### Conclusion

The honest advice for anyone moving between these two systems isn't "Ghana is harsher" or "Britain is kinder" — it's that both countries bury the real message somewhere you have to go looking for it, just in different places. Ghana hides it inside the sandwich; Britain hides it inside the understatement. Either way, the review that actually tells you where you stand is rarely the one you were handed — it's the one you have to ask a second, more direct question to get.
