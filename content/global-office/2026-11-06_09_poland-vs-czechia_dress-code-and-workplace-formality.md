**Poland Irons Its Shirts. Czechia Keeps Slippers Under the Desk. Both Call This "Business Casual."**

🇵🇱 Poland · 🇨🇿 Czechia

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Poland works 40.4 hours a week on average, per Eurostat data reported by Notes From Poland — second only to Greece in the entire EU. Czechia isn't far behind at roughly 40 hours including part-timers, per Eurostat figures via Expats.cz. Both countries will insist their office dress code is "smart casual." Only one of them keeps a spare pair of slippers under the desk for when the formal shoes come off.

Both countries also happen to be doing unusually well by the metrics that typically get cited alongside workplace culture — Eurostat's 2022 figures put Czechia's unemployment rate at just 2.2%, the lowest in the entire EU, against Poland's own still-enviable 2.9%, and more recent OECD-adjacent reporting groups both countries, alongside Slovakia, as the bloc's clear "top performers" on employment. None of that economic strength appears to have loosened the dress code one bit — if anything, low unemployment seems to have hardened everyone's conviction that their own formality standard is the one correctly calibrated to a labour market this competitive.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇱 Poland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress business or smart-casual by default; err formal until you've read the room, especially in BPOs and traditional corporates | Assume "business casual" means jeans and sneakers everywhere — ties and dark suits remain the baseline in many offices |
| Keep clothing neutral-colored, clean, and genuinely well-ironed — multiple sources flag this specifically | Skip ironing — a visibly rumpled shirt is noticed and remembered |
| Expect Casual Fridays in many offices, but revert to formal for client meetings even on a Friday | Wear revealing clothing — low necklines and short skirts are explicitly flagged as inappropriate across etiquette sources |
| Use formal titles and surnames with colleagues, especially seniors, until invited to switch to first names | Mistake the country's low 7% Gallup engagement figure for low effort — hours worked are genuinely high; engagement and hours are decoupled |
| Budget for longer in-office hours than the EU average | Expect flexibility on start-time or dress-code conditions as a new or young hire — this is a documented generational flashpoint, not settled practice |
| Learn Polish statutory overtime and rest-period rules, even though enforcement varies by employer | Assume a Polish startup dresses like startups elsewhere — informality there is the exception against an otherwise formal backdrop |

#### 🇨🇿 Czechia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to business casual, and keep everything "ironed and neat" — specifically and repeatedly cited as the norm | Blast the office air conditioning — a genuine, recurring point of friction |
| Keep indoor slippers under your desk — a widely reported real practice, not a stereotype | Expect quick personal bonding at work — Czechs reportedly keep work and friendship separate, socializing mostly after hours |
| Copy senior colleagues' dress level when unsure | Be surprised by rigid hierarchy and unpaid-overtime pressure specifically in corporate retail, even though the overall culture reads as laid-back |
| Expect early starts and early, often short lunches — a beer with lunch is culturally acceptable | Skip acknowledging birthdays or name days — offices mark these with cake and well wishes, and not participating can read as standoffish |
| Use formal address with older or senior colleagues even after years of working together | Assume the shorter average workweek (39.9 hours, down from 41.3) means genuinely low hours — Czechia still runs above the EU average |
| Plan around a real summer slowdown ("cucumber season," July–early September) | Lowball your own pay negotiation out of politeness — workers who visibly stand out reportedly see better outcomes |

Poland's formality is backed by an unusually strong statistic: Hofstede records a Uncertainty Avoidance score of 93, one of the highest measured for any country globally, which tracks with a culture that follows rules carefully and dresses, by default, to signal seriousness. And yet a Pracuj.pl survey of Polish employees found a real gap between institutional formality and personal preference: 69% want dress freedom "within certain limits," only around one in five actually prefer a strict code, and 26% say strict dress standards would negatively influence whether they accept a job at all — rising to 36% among Gen Y specifically. The same survey found a genuine generational split on the trajectory: 57% of respondents believe Poles have, on balance, dressed better for work over the past dozen-plus years, against only 9% who disagree — a population that broadly approves of its own slow drift toward formality even while a sizeable minority quietly resents having to participate in it personally. Gallup's engagement figure for Poland sits at just 7%, among the lowest recorded in Europe, a number entirely decoupled from the country's genuinely high weekly hours — Poles are working a lot; they are not, per Gallup's own measure, especially engaged while doing it.

Czechia runs slightly less uncertainty-avoidant (74 on Hofstede's scale, still high by global standards) but noticeably warmer on long-term orientation (70, against Poland's 38) — a culture that plans for the long run rather than guarding against the immediate unknown. The practical result is a workplace that polices neatness ("always ironed," per multiple independent sources) while being genuinely relaxed about structure: early lunches, a beer at midday, and a real institutional slowdown during "cucumber season" that email auto-replies openly acknowledge rather than quietly tolerate. Gallup's engagement figure for Czechia (16%) sits meaningfully above Poland's, consistent with a culture that treats formality and personal ease as two separate, non-competing questions rather than a single trade-off.

The comfort gap compounds across the workweek rather than resetting each morning. Czech working hours have genuinely shortened over time — from 42.2 hours in 2011 to 41.8 in 2015 to 39.9 (including part-timers) more recently, per Eurostat data via Expats.cz — while the formality of dress has, by multiple independent accounts, stayed essentially fixed across the same period. Poland's hours haven't moved nearly as much, dropping only modestly from a 2008 peak of 41.3, which means a Polish employee today is working almost exactly as hard as one fifteen years ago, dressed almost exactly as formally, for a Gallup engagement score that has, if anything, gotten worse rather than better over the same stretch.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely counterintuitive finding is that Poland — the more uncertainty-avoidant, more rule-bound, more formally-dressed country on paper — is also where employees say, in actual survey data, that they most want to loosen the dress code, while Czechia — the country that actually keeps slippers under the desk — polices neatness just as strictly, just through a different lens. Formality and comfort turn out to be independent variables in both cultures, not opposite ends of a single dial, which is exactly the kind of distinction a packing list never tells you about.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/cscareerquestionsEU — A Polish backend developer working in the UK, considering a move home, worried openly about work-life balance; a commenter working in international HR replied that Poland's formal labour-hour protections look stronger on paper than in practice, with enforcement varying widely — multinationals and remote-first firms tend to be meaningfully more flexible than traditional local employers.*

> *r/czech — One commenter with Western work-culture experience called Czech corporate life comparatively relaxed, citing solid leave entitlements and perks like meal vouchers, while flagging that Prague wages (citing an average gross wage around 49,215 CZK) barely cover the city's rising cost of living; a separate commenter in the same thread flagged unpaid overtime, pressure to stay late, and rigid hierarchy as recurring complaints specific to corporate retail roles — a clear exception to the broader "laid-back" reputation the rest of the thread describes.*

> *PolishForums — An HR professional interviewed under the name "Iwona" described a young job candidate who set conditions on start time and openly pushed back on the dress code — "even at school it doesn't apply anymore" — a moment she said visibly shocked the older staff present, a concrete generational flashpoint on formality rather than an abstract one.*

> *PolishForums — A separate interviewee, an editor identified as "Agata," said young hires at her company often quit within months because an eight-hour day simply feels excessive to them — framed by the publication as a genuine cultural shift rather than a one-off complaint.*

### Conclusion

Practically speaking, if you're packing right now for either posting: bring the iron regardless of which country you land in, because "neat" is non-negotiable in both. What differs is where the formality actually lives — in Poland, it lives in the org chart and the client meeting; in Czechia, it lives in the shirt collar while the lunch hour, the slippers, and the midday beer stay genuinely informal. Dress sharp either way. Just don't assume sharp means the same thing once you're actually sitting down at the desk.

If you're arriving as a younger hire specifically, expect to be the test case for a conversation your new employer hasn't fully had yet. Both the PolishForums interviews and the Reddit threads above describe genuine, ongoing friction between what younger staff expect and what older colleagues still consider standard — not a settled culture you're adapting to, but an active negotiation you're walking straight into on day one. Dress a notch more formally than you think you need to for the first month, watch carefully, and adjust once you've actually seen where your specific office currently sits in that ongoing argument. Both countries are, by every employment metric that matters, doing well enough to afford the occasional debate over ties — treat the dress code as a genuine point of pride, not a minor inconvenience standing between you and a comfortable pair of shoes. You can keep the slippers for after hours in Prague, and the sneakers for the commute in Warsaw — just don't let either colleague catch you wearing them at your desk before you've earned the right to, and until you have, iron absolutely everything twice, even the one shirt you're fairly confident nobody in the entire office will ever actually notice or remark upon.
