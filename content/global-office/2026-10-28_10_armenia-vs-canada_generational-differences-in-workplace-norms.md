**One Workforce Is Rebuilding Itself After Losing a Generation. The Other Is Watching Half of One Generation Threaten to Walk Out.**

🇦🇲 Armenia · 🇨🇦 Canada

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Fifty-five percent of Canadian Gen Z workers say they plan to look for a new job in 2026, compared with 39% of Millennials, 30% of Gen X, and just 6% of Baby Boomers (survey reported via Benefits and Pensions Monitor) — a generational gap so wide it reads less like a career-stage pattern and more like two entirely different labor markets sharing an office. Armenia's generational story runs on a completely different axis: the country's tech sector isn't negotiating between generations so much as rebuilding one, after the Soviet collapse and the First Nagorno-Karabakh War triggered what one economist called the "hard stage" when mathematicians and engineers left en masse, hollowing out the very workforce the current IT boom is now trying to replace almost from scratch (Eurasianet).

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇲 Armenia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a genuine mix of Soviet-era formality and newer, foreign-influenced "working culture" imported by international tech firms | Assume the whole workforce operates on one single generational script |
| Treat the arrival of major international employers as a real training opportunity for local staff to gain new skills | Assume local talent lacks ambition just because the ecosystem is young |
| Respect the formal-but-family-like balance many workplaces strike across hierarchy | Mistake warmth for informality — the hierarchy is usually still there underneath |
| Adjust your own schedule expectations — many offices run later starts than a rigid old-school "7-to-4" structure | Assume a 9 or 10 AM start means a relaxed pace once you're in |
| Recognize that global competition for Armenian tech talent is real and growing | Assume local salaries or expectations are static — this market moves fast |
| Factor in that many skilled workers (and some capital) left after 1991 and the First Nagorno-Karabakh War, shaping today's talent pipeline | Assume today's workforce size and depth reflects decades of uninterrupted growth |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take Gen Z's dissatisfaction seriously — 46% say they're not working in their preferred field, the highest of any generation | Dismiss younger staff's job-search intent as generic restlessness |
| Expect an expectation-reality gap on raises — 82% of Gen Z feel they deserve one in 2026, but only 53% expect one | Assume stated ambition from younger staff equals naive entitlement |
| Offer real flexibility if you want to keep Millennials specifically — 53% say they'd leave if hybrid work disappeared | Assume one blanket return-to-office policy will land the same way across every generation |
| Recognize Baby Boomers as your most retention-stable cohort right now — only 6% plan to job-search in 2026 | Assume older workers are disengaged just because they're staying put |
| Pair any full-time office mandate with real compensation conversations — 95% of Gen Z expect a raise to accept one | Spring a return-to-office mandate on younger staff without addressing pay |
| Treat underemployment as a real, measurable issue — 32% of Gen Z say their job falls short of their education level | Assume a degree automatically converting to a matching role is still a safe bet for new graduates |

### Armenia

Armenia's generational workplace story is inseparable from its economic history. The country was once a genuine center of Soviet computing — the Yerevan Scientific Research Institute of Mathematical Machines employed more than seven thousand people at its peak — before the USSR's collapse and the First Nagorno-Karabakh War triggered an outflow of skilled workers and capital that, in economist Artak Manukyan's framing, began with "the 'hard stage' when mathematicians and engineers left the country" (Eurasianet). The generation that came of age during that outflow and the generation now entering a booming IT sector are, in a real sense, bridging a gap left by a missing middle rather than negotiating ordinary generational friction.

What's filling that gap now is a wave of foreign firms and returning talent actively reshaping norms on the ground. Krisp co-founder Artavazd Minasyan credits the arrival of major international employers with introducing practices that didn't previously exist locally: "There weren't this kind of major companies in Armenia before, and this will lead to a new working culture," he said, pointing specifically to the skills transfer available to local staff who "can go and gain new skills and experience from successful companies." One repatriate, Nareg, who now works at Digitain, described the simplest generational-adjacent culture shock of returning: trading a rigid old "7-4 schedule" for a 9-or-10-AM start that initially left him feeling like he "was running behind all the time."

### Canada

Canada's generational divide is less about historical rupture and more about radically different expectations converging in the same offices at the same time. The headline numbers are stark: just 6% of Baby Boomers plan to job-search in 2026, compared with 55% of Gen Z — and the gap isn't just about restlessness. Gen Z report real structural mismatches: 32% say their current role falls short of their education level (versus 16% of Gen X), and 46% aren't working in their preferred field at all (versus 25% of Gen X). The expectation-versus-reality gap compounds it: 82% of Gen Z believe they deserve a raise in 2026, but only 53% actually expect one (Benefits and Pensions Monitor, citing recent workforce survey data).

Hybrid work has become the sharpest generational fault line specifically. A Mortar Research survey commissioned by IWG Canada found 95% of Gen Z workers would expect a salary increase to accept a full five-day office return, compared with 84% of Millennials, 71% of Gen X, and 61% of Baby Boomers — and 53% of Millennials specifically said they'd look for another job if hybrid work disappeared entirely, versus just 32% of Baby Boomers. Fifty-five percent of Gen Z also say hybrid work has directly benefited their career growth, nearly double the 36% of Baby Boomers who say the same — suggesting the generations aren't just disagreeing about preference, but about whether flexibility helps or hurts a career at all.

### The Reckoning

The real contrast is what's driving each generational story. Armenia's generational dynamic is downstream of geopolitical rupture — a missing cohort of mathematicians and engineers who left during a specific historical window, now being compensated for by foreign investment and a young, fast-growing tech sector building something closer to new rather than renegotiating something old. Canada's generational dynamic is downstream of pure economic mismatch within a stable, mature labor market — a well-educated younger cohort that feels underemployed and under-rewarded relative to older colleagues who entered the workforce under completely different conditions and are, understandably, in no hurry to leave.

For an incoming manager, that changes the actual intervention. In Armenia, building a durable multigenerational workplace means actively investing in skills transfer and training, because the "older generation" pipeline was genuinely interrupted — there's less institutional memory to lean on than headcount alone would suggest. In Canada, it means taking a specific, measurable expectation gap seriously: Gen Z isn't vaguely dissatisfied, they can tell you precisely which raise and which promotion they expected and didn't get.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Repatriate account via RepatArmenia.org — Nareg, now working at Digitain after returning to Armenia, described the disorienting shift from a rigid early-start schedule to a later, more flexible one: moving from a "7-4 schedule" to starting at 9 or 10 AM "felt like I was running behind all the time!" — a culture-shock detail about pace rather than hierarchy.*

> *Eurasianet (named interview, tech-sector figure) — Areg Gevorgyan, head of the ISTC incubator, framed what Armenia's workforce actually needs to retain talent across generations in concrete terms, not just salary: "What we need are secure pensions, education and healthcare to create a more positive and appealing work environment."*

> *Armenpress (named interview, Krisp co-founder Artavazd Minasyan) — Describing the effect of major international firms newly operating in Armenia, he was direct about the generational and cultural shift underway: "There weren't this kind of major companies in Armenia before, and this will lead to a new working culture," adding that local staff "can go and gain new skills and experience from successful companies."*

> *Benefits and Pensions Monitor (2026 Canadian workforce survey reporting) — The starkest generational data point wasn't about preference but about expectation: 82% of Gen Z respondents said they deserve a raise in 2026, while only 53% actually expect to get one — a 29-point gap the report frames as a core driver of Gen Z's elevated job-search intent relative to every older cohort surveyed.*

### Conclusion

If you're building a team in Armenia, your generational challenge is really a talent-pipeline challenge wearing a generational costume — invest in training and skills transfer, because the historical gap is real and institutional knowledge didn't pass down the way it would have without the disruptions of the 1990s. If you're managing a Canadian team, your challenge is more immediate and more measurable: your Gen Z staff can already tell you the exact raise and promotion they think they're owed, and whether they get an honest answer about it will likely decide whether more than half of them are still there next year.
