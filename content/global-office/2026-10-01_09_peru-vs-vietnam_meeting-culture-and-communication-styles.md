# Peru Once Held a Presidential Ceremony to Fix Lateness. Vietnam's Meetings Run Late on Purpose, Every Time.

**🇵🇪 Peru · 🇻🇳 Vietnam**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In 2007, Peru's president stood in Lima's main square, synchronized a clock with the Navy's official time, and launched a national campaign against tardiness — complete with a mock funeral procession mocking his notoriously late predecessor. Vietnam has never needed such a ceremony, because its meetings aren't late by accident. The real decision was never going to happen at the scheduled time anyway; the meeting is where the group ratifies what long private consultation has already settled.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive on time for business meetings and dinner invitations — formal punctuality is expected and valued. | Expect the same promptness at a casual social gathering; 30-60 minutes late is standard there. |
| Invest time in relationship-building before and around formal meetings; it genuinely precedes the transaction. | Rush straight to business talk; indirect, polite, relationship-first communication is the norm. |
| Accept closer physical proximity and light touch during conversation as normal, not an overstep. | Back away from a conversational touch on the arm — it can read as cold or unfriendly. |
| Note the seating arrangement; it reflects real hierarchy, with senior figures and decision-makers placed deliberately. | Assume where you sit is random — a junior staffer and project manager seated near the senior executive signals something. |

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat the meeting as a ratification step, not the actual decision-making moment. | Expect a clear yes/no in the room; most real authority sits with a few people at the top, consulted beforehand. |
| Build trust through repeated in-person interaction and socializing — it isn't optional groundwork. | Expect virtual meetings alone to build the trust a deal needs; Vietnamese business culture leans heavily on face-to-face contact. |
| Learn to hear "that will be difficult" as a polite no, not a solvable logistics problem. | Push for direct contradiction or an explicit "no" — it's rarely how disagreement gets communicated. |
| Build real slack into your timeline for long discussions and last-minute changes. | Treat a late schedule change as an exception; it's treated as routine, not a special circumstance. |

**Peru**

Peruvian meeting culture runs on a split personality around time that catches a lot of newcomers off guard: formal punctuality is a genuine value, loudly so, while social punctuality is almost its opposite. The contrast got a literal national stage in 2007, when President Alan García launched "La Hora Sin Demora" ("Time Without Delay"), synchronizing a ceremonial clock in Lima's Plaza de Armas with Peruvian Navy time and declaring that "tardiness shows a lack of respect" for both relationships and economic productivity. The ceremony reportedly included a mock coffin mocking his predecessor's reputation for keeping foreign dignitaries waiting for hours — a reputation specific enough to have earned its own nickname, "Hora Cabana." Business meetings and dinner invitations are squarely covered by the formal standard; casual social gatherings are not, where arriving 30 minutes to an hour late remains genuinely normal.

Underneath the punctuality question sits a communication style that's indirect and relationship-coded: verbal communication tends toward politeness over bluntness, personal space runs closer than North American or European defaults (light touch on the arm during conversation is common and backing away can register as cold), and formal titles matter, especially early in a relationship. Seating itself carries real information — seniority, gender, and positional importance all shape where people sit around a table, with proximity to the senior executive functioning as a visible marker of standing, not an accident of room layout.

**Vietnam**

Vietnamese meetings are structured around a premise that inverts the Western assumption that a meeting is where decisions get made. Management guides describing Vietnamese business culture are blunt about it: "most decisions are made at the top and then given to the employees to implement," with real influence concentrated among "a few key people," and rank-and-file staff generally reluctant to act independently given a culturally embedded aversion to individual risk-taking. The meeting itself functions more as the forum where a decision, already substantially settled through prior consultation, gets formally presented and accepted by the group — and getting a new idea to "take hold" requires it to be perceived as genuinely good for the group, not just approved from above, which is why guides consistently warn managers to expect "lengthy discussions with all relevant stakeholders" before anything moves forward.

A personal account from a foreign businesswoman working in Vietnam captures the texture well: she described learning that "that will be difficult" functions as a polite rejection requiring the listener to read between the lines, rather than an invitation to problem-solve the stated obstacle; she also described genuinely struggling, by her own admission, to adapt to last-minute schedule changes that Vietnamese colleagues treated as entirely routine rather than exceptional. Relationship-building runs in parallel with and often ahead of the formal agenda — the same account noted a recurring, almost institutional sight of groups of businessmen still lingering at lunch venues well into the afternoon, "usually a little the worse for the amount of Heineken consumed," relationship capital accumulating well outside the conference room.

**The Reckoning**

Hofstede's uncertainty avoidance scores run in genuinely opposite directions here — Peru scores high (87), Vietnam notably low (30) — and that gap maps cleanly onto how each culture handles the unexpected inside a meeting. Peru's high uncertainty avoidance shows up as rigid formal punctuality and clearly signaled hierarchy (the seating chart as information system): ambiguity gets minimized through visible structure. Vietnam's low score shows up as comfort with fluid scheduling and indirect, deferred decision-making: the ambiguity of "that will be difficult" or a meeting that reschedules itself mid-stream isn't a system failure, it's simply how business there tolerates uncertainty without needing to formalize every variable upfront.

Where the two genuinely converge is in what a visiting professional needs to do before either country's meetings make sense: build the relationship first. Peru frames this through close physical and conversational warmth layered onto formal structure. Vietnam frames it through sustained, patient, face-to-face trust-building that precedes and substitutes for the Western instinct to settle things over video calls. In neither country does the calendar invite tell you what's really going to happen.

### Conclusion

If you're walking into a Peruvian meeting, show up on time, read the seating chart, and don't rush the relationship-building that happens around the edges of the agenda. If you're walking into a Vietnamese one, assume the real decision already happened somewhere you weren't invited, treat the meeting as the group's ratification of it, and build enough slack into your week that a rescheduled afternoon doesn't throw you.

The honest version: in Peru, the clock is a value people genuinely fought a symbolic battle over. In Vietnam, the clock was never really the point — the relationship was, and it's still being built over the second round of Heineken, long after your meeting was supposed to end.
