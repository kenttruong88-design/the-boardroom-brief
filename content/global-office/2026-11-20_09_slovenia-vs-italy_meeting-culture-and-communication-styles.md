# Slovenia Starts the Meeting on Time and Ends It with Minutes; Italy Holds the Meeting After the Real Meeting

🇸🇮 Slovenia · 🇮🇹 Italy

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

According to BusinessCulture.org, being late to a meeting in Slovenia is considered extremely rude, and hosts are expected to send minutes with action items within a few days. According to Expat Arrivals, in Italy the decision has often been made before the meeting, which exists mainly to confirm and inform. The two countries share a border and a fondness for coffee, and almost nothing else about the conference room. A professional moving from one to the other should be prepared to discover that the other one is on a different schedule, a different agenda and, sometimes, a different language.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Slovenia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Arrive on time, and phone ahead if you will be late | Treat the start time as approximate |
| Send documents beforehand and follow up with minutes | Arrive with the paper still to be printed |
| Use titles and family names until invited to use first names | Open with a first name and a joke |
| Expect several meetings before agreement | Press for a decision at the first one |
| Follow a verbal agreement with a written contract | Treat a handshake as final |
| Avoid July and August when scheduling | Plan a launch for the peak of the holidays |

### In Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in the relationship, and expect decisions to form outside the room | Assume the meeting is where the decision happens |
| Bring data, but hold it until the conversation has warmed up | Open with a spreadsheet |
| Arrive on time anyway | Take a late start as permission to be late yourself |
| Expect interruptions and overlapping talk | Wait for silence before speaking |
| Be prepared for the language to drift into Italian | Assume English will hold for the whole meeting |
| Accept a long lunch as part of the work | Schedule a hard stop at one o'clock |

## Slovenia

Slovenian meetings are punctual, hierarchical and written down. BusinessCulture.org says that hierarchy is an essential part of the Slovenian business community, that punctuality is expected, that visitors should send materials in advance and that the host sends minutes with action items within a few days. It adds that several meetings are expected before agreement is reached, that a verbal agreement is followed by a written contract, and that titles and family names are used until invited otherwise. The best time for meetings is the morning, it says, and July and August are best avoided.

The tone is reserved. A lower-authority guide from the Ling language app, whose hours figures contradict one another and are not used here, describes the style as polite, reserved and indirect, avoiding confrontation and warming up after business is done. BusinessCulture.org adds practical cautions: avoid the Second World War and comparisons with other former Yugoslav states, and describe Slovenia as Central European. Those are not meeting tips so much as survival advice.

The point to take from this is that politeness is not agreement. A Slovenian counterpart who says nothing against your proposal may merely be waiting for the paperwork. The way to reduce the risk is to send the proposal early, expect questions in writing and confirm decisions in minutes. The forums are less tidy than the guides: on ExpatExchange, two expats described the pace in Slovenia as more leisurely and timekeeping less strict than they had expected, which is the opposite of what the guidebooks say. The honest reading is that the formal expectation is punctuality and the practice is variable.

## Italy

Italy's meetings are expressive, flexible and often pre-decided. Expat Arrivals describes flexible agendas, frequent interruptions and early time spent getting acquainted, with an expressive, theatrical style and a preference for face-to-face communication over written, and decisions made top-down by an owner or a family core group, as is common in family-owned small businesses. HousingAnywhere describes standard office hours of 9:30 to 18:30, lunch that can last up to two hours, the best meeting slots at 10:00 to 11:00 and after 15:00, and a business lunch or dinner that can last up to three hours, with the host paying. Punctuality is described as a sign of respect but not a priority, meetings run over and decisions are rarely made on the spot.

The Local Italy published a first-person account by a foreign journalist who said that although most colleagues spoke English, meetings drifted into fast Italian that she could not follow, requiring an English-speaking colleague to summarize afterwards. She also described meetings starting late, one colleague arriving 20 minutes after the start and asking whether she had eaten, and plans changing at the last minute. Expat.com's guide to Rome adds that punctuality is not a primary principle there, that lateness is usually not disrespect, that people like to talk things out over coffee and that honorifics such as Dottore matter.

For a newcomer the lesson is that the meeting is the minutes of a conversation that has already happened. The effective professional goes to the coffee, speaks to the owner and arrives at the meeting already knowing the outcome.

A word on language is also in order, because it shapes how meetings feel. The Local Italy's account suggests that the transition from English to Italian mid-meeting is not unusual, and the Expat.com guide describes honorifics such as Dottore as significant. In Slovenia, the ExpatExchange posters named the language barrier as the main difficulty, and BusinessCulture.org's advice to use titles and family names until invited otherwise implies that formality in address is part of the code. Eurostat's figure for the EU average working week, 36.0 hours in 2024, down from 37.0 in 2014, provides a baseline against which both countries operate, although the country-level values for Slovenia and Italy were not readable in the source. What can be said is that in both, the length and shape of the meeting are matters of custom as much as of the clock.

## The Reckoning

Hofstede's scores, recalled from the published dataset and worth checking before print, put Slovenia at a power distance of about 71 and Italy at about 50, with masculinity at about 19 against 70. That is a considerable gap. Slovenia is the more hierarchical and more modest; Italy is the less hierarchical and more assertive, which fits the pattern in which a Slovenian meeting defers to rank and procedure while an Italian one defers to the person who speaks best.

The contrast is therefore not only about time. In Slovenia the meeting is a formal step in a documented process, and the record matters. In Italy the meeting is a social occasion in which relationships are confirmed and the document follows. A Slovenian professional in Italy will find the process opaque, and an Italian in Slovenia will find it slow. The shared ground is that both prefer to know who is in the room before deciding what to say.

The comparison should not be pushed too far. Both countries are small enough, and their business communities close enough, that the formal and informal codes overlap: a Slovenian who has worked with Italian clients will have learned to allow for a longer lunch, and an Italian who has worked with Slovenian partners will have learned to send the document first. The newcomer who watches the first three meetings, takes note of who speaks and when, and asks a colleague afterwards what actually happened will usually have the code by the end of the month.

## Conclusion

If you like a meeting with an agenda, a start time and minutes, choose Slovenia, and bring titles and a printed contract. If you like a meeting that is as much a conversation as a decision, choose Italy, and bring patience, some Italian and an appetite.

Whichever you choose, ask your future colleagues who decides, when and where, and how you will find out. In Slovenia the answer will be in the minutes. In Italy it will be at lunch. In both, the surest way to be remembered is to be punctual, and in only one will you be thanked for it.
