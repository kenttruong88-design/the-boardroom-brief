**India Is Fighting Its Way Back to the Office. Tanzania Never Really Left.**

🇮🇳 India · 🇹🇿 Tanzania

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Eighty-two percent of Indian employees are currently under some form of return-to-office mandate, per JLL's 2025 Workplace Preference Barometer — a striking number for a country whose tech sector spent 2021 publicly promising the opposite. In Tanzania, the debate barely exists, for the straightforward reason that roughly 94% of employment there is informal, per ILO figures, which makes "remote work policy" less a contested office perk than a category that mostly doesn't apply yet.

The reversal is sharp enough to be worth sitting with. India's formal sector spent the pandemic years building an entire vocabulary around flexibility — hybrid schedules, async work, output-based management — and then largely abandoned it once leadership decided it preferred the old vocabulary better. Tanzania's informal sector never had the formal vocabulary to abandon in the first place, which means a worker there isn't negotiating a rollback of a promised flexibility; they're negotiating whether a written employment relationship exists at all.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Plan for in-office days even at employers that market themselves as flexible | Assume "hybrid" in a job posting will still mean hybrid in eighteen months — TCS, Wipro, Infosys, and HCL have all walked back earlier remote commitments |
| Frame any remote request around specific deliverables, not general trust | Expect automatic buy-in from managers used to visible, in-person presence — skepticism about remote output is well documented |
| Ask explicitly whether your employer subsidizes mobile, broadband, or IT peripherals | Assume these costs are automatically covered — ask, since practice varies by employer |
| Build a deliberate off-work social routine if you do land remote work — isolation is a real, commonly reported issue | Expect remote work in India to replicate the same social rhythm it has elsewhere — the infrastructure around it is still catching up |
| Communicate up the hierarchy properly even over chat — don't skip a management layer just because it's virtual | Push back on a return-to-office mandate in a way that reads as adversarial — quiet job-switching is the more common response |
| Dress and present modestly even on video calls | Assume casual dress is a given just because you're working from home |

#### 🇹🇿 Tanzania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Get your contract and payslip arrangement in writing before you start | Accept a cash-only or partially informal pay arrangement on a verbal promise |
| Get a dedicated mobile data plan as backup — 4G coverage is reported as reliable even outside major cities | Rely solely on fixed-line internet, which is less consistently available |
| Learn basic Swahili for everyday negotiating, deliveries, and local services | Assume English alone will get you the same prices or service quality as a local speaker |
| Confirm your visa and work-permit status matches your actual work arrangement before relying on an employer's informal promise | Arrive on a visitor or business visa assuming a work permit will simply follow |
| Build a small circle of trusted local contacts gradually | Trust unsolicited new contacts who approach you first — multiple long-term expats specifically warn against this |
| Research tax treatment of foreign-employer remote income before assuming anything is settled | Assume favorable tax treatment is guaranteed — this is genuinely unresolved territory and needs direct expert confirmation |

India's remote-work story is really a story about reversal. The Microsoft India Work Trend Index found 74% of Indian employees wanted more flexible or remote options, with a near-identical 73% simultaneously wanting more in-person team time — a "hybrid paradox" that employers resolved, in practice, by picking the in-person half. Attrition tells the rest of the story: major Indian IT firms saw voluntary turnover spike to 17–20% in 2021 as return-to-office pushes accelerated, according to figures drawn from company filings and reported by restofworld.org, and the Microsoft data found 62% of Indian employees intended to switch jobs that year, rising to 51% for Gen Z specifically. Hofstede's India figures put Power Distance at a striking 77 — the highest of any country researched for this series — which helps explain why remote-work friction in India plays out less as open confrontation and more as quiet, hierarchy-respecting exit. By contrast, 2025 JLL data shows 83% of Indian employees now describe their work environment as "close to ideal," well above the regional APAC average of 64%, even as 60% cite flexibility as their top factor in choosing a next employer — a workforce that has, somewhat improbably, made peace with the office it didn't choose.

A separate Deloitte 2022 survey of Gen Z and Millennial employees in India adds a useful wrinkle: 66% of Gen Z and 67% of Millennials say they favor hybrid work personally, yet only 19% and 23% respectively say they'd actually prioritize offering flexible hours if they were the ones setting company policy. That gap between personal preference and institutional instinct suggests India's current return-to-office posture isn't purely a top-down imposition — it has real buy-in even from the generation most often assumed to be fighting it.

Tanzania's picture is structurally different rather than culturally opposed. With internet penetration at 85.3% and 58.6 million subscriptions as of late 2025, per the Tanzania Communications Regulatory Authority, connectivity is not the bottleneck — formality is. Hofstede has never profiled Tanzania individually; the best available figures group it into a regional "East Africa" cluster alongside Kenya, Ethiopia, and Zambia, with a Power Distance of 64 and Individualism of 27 — numbers that should be read as an approximation, not a verified national score.

Internet infrastructure, notably, isn't the gating factor it might be assumed to be. The Tanzania Communications Regulatory Authority's own Q4 2025 figures show 85.3% internet penetration and 58.6 million subscriptions nationally, with mobile broadband making up more than half of all connections — numbers that would support a genuine remote-work economy if the surrounding formal employment infrastructure existed to make use of them. The bottleneck in Tanzania isn't bandwidth. It's the absence of the written contracts, HR departments, and enforceable labour protections that would let "remote" mean anything more specific than "nobody is watching." What expat accounts consistently describe is not a flexible-work debate but a more basic question of whether a given job has any written structure at all: one long-serving expat on Expat.com's Tanzania forum explicitly warns incoming workers to expect 12–15 hour days, including Sundays and holidays, with duties that expand indefinitely once you're inside the arrangement.

### The Reckoning

The sharpest irony is that India's remote-work argument is, fundamentally, an argument about trust between two parties who already have a formal employment relationship, a written contract, and a shared understanding of what "flexible" is supposed to mean — they are negotiating the terms of an established system. Tanzania's situation, for a large share of its workforce, predates that negotiation entirely: with employers in some reported cases withholding pay for months or holding onto an employee's passport, the relevant protection isn't a flexible-work policy, it's a functioning written contract in the first place. One country is debating how much freedom remote work should grant; the other is still establishing the baseline the debate assumes.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/developersIndia (via mirror) — A remote developer described falling into a repetitive work-eat-scroll-sleep loop within weeks of going remote; several commenters said a fixed daily routine — gym or a walk plus set hours — took months to establish but was the only thing that actually fixed it.*

> *r/developersIndia (via mirror) — Multiple commenters in the same thread said remote work in India makes meeting new people genuinely difficult, with some choosing to go into the office once or twice a week purely to counteract the isolation, even when fully remote was an option.*

> *Expat.com forum, Tanzania — A long-time poster explicitly warned incoming workers, in capital letters, to expect unregulated 12-to-15-hour days including Sundays and holidays, with job duties that expand indefinitely once you've accepted the role.*

> *Expat.com forum, Tanzania — Separate posters recounted employers withholding pay for months and, in one case, holding an employee's passport for roughly six months with no clear retrieval plan — a serious practical warning for anyone assuming "informal" simply means "relaxed."*

> *TalesMag Real Post Report, Dar es Salaam — An NGO employee reported internet became genuinely reliable only after the SEACOM undersea cable arrived, though it remains billed by data volume in both directions and stays expensive — with a 3G modem still functioning as a workable primary home connection even outside the city.*

### Conclusion

If flexibility is what you're relocating for, be honest about which kind you mean. India will give you a workplace that has mostly decided, after a brief and expensive experiment, that it prefers you in the building — plan your negotiating leverage accordingly, and don't expect the posting to stay hybrid just because it says so today. Tanzania will give you genuine geographic and schedule freedom, provided you first establish, in writing, that you have a job with boundaries at all. One market needs you to argue for flexibility within a system; the other needs you to build the system before flexibility is even a sentence worth having.

Whichever you're walking into, do the unglamorous paperwork first. In India, that means confirming in writing exactly which days you're expected in the building, since the posting's own language has proven unreliable within this decade alone. In Tanzania, it means confirming you'll actually be paid, on a schedule, with your passport remaining in your own possession throughout. Neither question should need asking. Ask it anyway. The word "flexible" is doing very different structural work in each market, and the gap between what it promises and what it delivers is exactly where a careless new arrival loses the most time, money, and patience.
