# "Gone Are the Days Young People Only Obeyed Their Elders": Generations Colliding in India and the USA

**🇮🇳 India · 🇺🇸 USA**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

An Indian conglomerate chairman recently told the press, without apparent irony, that "gone are the days when young people only obeyed their elders" — a sentence that would sound unremarkable in an American HR memo but registers as a genuine tectonic shift in a culture built on deference to seniority. Meanwhile, American offices are dealing with their own generational reckoning, just further down the same road: Gen Z now outnumbers Baby Boomers in the workforce, and the fight isn't over whether to obey elders but over what a boss is even for.

### India: A Hierarchy Renegotiating Itself in Real Time

India's workforce composition still skews traditional in structure even as its cultural expectations shift fast: SHRM India and springworks.in data put the workforce at roughly 54 percent millennial, 26 percent Gen X, 15 percent Gen Z, and 5 percent Baby Boomer as of 2026. What makes India's generational story distinct from the West's isn't the age breakdown but the cultural starting point — a historically strong deference to seniority, sometimes summarized as "yes boss" culture, that's now being challenged openly rather than quietly.

Business press coverage of Indian CEOs shows genuine range in reaction, from open enthusiasm — younger staff described as bringing fresh perspective and meritocratic instincts — to visible frustration, including one widely circulated comment dismissing Gen Z as lacking "batamiz" (manners) toward elders. Deloitte's India country report on the 2025 Gen Z and Millennial Survey found manager engagement in South Asia dropped eight points in a single year, a concrete signal that the traditional hierarchical relationship — directive, seniority-based authority — is under real strain as younger employees increasingly expect mentoring-style leadership and engagement to be earned rather than assumed.

### USA: The Fight Has Moved Past Hierarchy Itself

America's generational story starts from a different baseline, since hierarchy was never as central to begin with — Hofstede Insights' relatively low power-distance score for the US reflects that. The current friction is less about whether to defer to a boss and more about what work is supposed to deliver. Deloitte's 2025 Gen Z and Millennial Survey, spanning over 23,000 respondents across 44 countries, found Gen Z and millennials pursuing what the report calls a "trifecta" of money, meaning, and well-being, with fair pay, flexible hybrid arrangements, and real mental health support ranking as top priorities — alongside a notable statistic that only 52 percent of Gen Z respondents rated their own mental well-being as good or very good.

The communication gap is measurable and stark: 65 percent of Baby Boomers prefer face-to-face interaction against just 34 percent of Gen Z, while 55 percent of Gen Z favour instant messaging versus 28 percent of Boomers. Counterintuitively, though, several surveys find Gen Z reports more friction with fellow Gen Z coworkers than with Boomers — the loudest headline conflict isn't necessarily the one employees actually feel day to day.

### The Reckoning

India and the US are running the same generational transition on different clocks and starting points. India is negotiating the more foundational question — should hierarchy itself still command automatic deference — in real time and publicly, with CEOs weighing in on both sides. The US settled that particular question generations ago and has moved on to negotiating something subtler: what exactly a job is supposed to provide beyond a paycheck, and how much emotional infrastructure an employer owes its youngest workers.

The counterintuitive overlap is that both countries' Gen Z cohorts want strikingly similar things — boundaries, authenticity, mental health support, respect earned rather than assumed — but the Indian version of that demand reads as a much sharper cultural break from the preceding generation than the American version does, because the preceding generation's baseline expectations were so much further apart to begin with.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone reflecting on managing a mixed-generation Indian team said the hardest skill to learn wasn't handling Gen Z pushback itself but managing the older team members' reactions to it — senior staff often read a younger employee's boundary-setting as personal disrespect rather than a generational shift, and defusing that misread took more effort than the original disagreement.*

> *teamblind.com — A millennial startup manager posted anonymously asking whether he was losing his mind managing Gen Z engineers, sparking a large thread; one Boomer commenter's reply became the widely cited takeaway — that every generation looks chaotic to the one managing it, and the managers who adapted fastest stopped waiting for the new hires to change and started rebuilding the workplace itself.*

> *Reddit — An American Gen Z employee described the most common misunderstanding with older colleagues as being mistaken for disengaged when actually just direct — declining to stay late without explanation felt, to them, like a reasonable boundary, but registered to a Gen X manager as a lack of commitment.*

> *Reddit — Someone working in an Indian multinational's Bangalore office noted that the generational shift was visibly uneven even within one building — one floor's leadership actively solicited junior pushback in meetings, while an adjacent team still operated on a strict "don't question the senior" norm, with no company-wide standard reconciling the two.*

### Conclusion

If generational friction is a live concern for your next move, the practical question isn't "which country's Gen Z is harder to manage" but how far along each culture is in openly renegotiating its own baseline assumptions. India is having a louder, more public argument about whether deference to seniority still applies at all; the US had that argument decades ago and is now quietly arguing about what employers owe employees beyond a paycheck. Neither fight is close to settled. If a friend asked me which environment to walk into with more patience prepared: bring extra patience to India's hierarchy in flux, and bring extra structure — mentorship, clear paths, explicit boundaries — to the American version, because informal norms alone won't hold either workplace together much longer.
