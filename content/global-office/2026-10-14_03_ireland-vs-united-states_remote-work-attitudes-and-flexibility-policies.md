**Ireland vs. United States: Who's Actually Allowed to Log Off?**

**🇮🇪 Ireland · 🇺🇸 United States**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Ireland, the share of employees working from home at least some of the time nearly doubled between 2019 and 2022, from 19.9 percent to 36.2 percent — the single biggest jump recorded anywhere in the EU, according to Eurostat figures cited by the American Chamber of Commerce Ireland. In the United States, a tidier 22.9 percent of the workforce teleworked for pay as of the first quarter of 2024, per the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Two democracies, two broadly similar headline numbers, and almost no overlap in what "flexible" is actually permitted to mean once you read past the percentage sign.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇪 Ireland
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate your in-office days before signing — 42 percent of Irish employers are already tightening them | Assume a Dublin-based HQ means a remote-friendly job if your actual reporting line sits in Austin or Boston |
| Confirm your employer is Irish-registered if you're not an EU citizen trying to relocate | Try to freelance into Ireland on a foreign remote salary — there is no digital-nomad visa lane |
| Invoke the EU Working Time Directive's 11-hour rest rule as a legitimate reason to skip a 9pm call | Take "the office is optional" as permanent once a landlord notices Dublin leasing volumes creeping back up |
| Expect two office days a week as the real, mainstream baseline, not a favour | Burn your annual leave on guilt alone — the entitlement is statutory, not a gift |
| Ask directly whether "unlimited PTO" gets used or just advertised | Mistake Ireland's low disengagement scores for an easy workload — people are checked out, not under-worked |

#### 🇺🇸 United States
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask what share of people at your specific level and function actually get remote or hybrid status | Mistake a laptop and a Slack badge for a protected right to log off |
| Get any remote arrangement in writing — there is no statutory floor underneath it | Assume state law gives you anything beyond what your employer chooses to grant |
| Expect return-to-office mandates to arrive fast and with real enforcement | Plan a quiet Friday afternoon — American teams are famously ramping up new requests exactly then |
| Clarify whether PTO is capped, "unlimited," or quietly tracked against you | Expect a legal ceiling on your working week — there isn't one |
| Treat flexibility as a negotiated, individual perk, because that's what it is | Compare your offer to a colleague's and assume consistency across the company |

On paper, Ireland looks like the remote-work success story of Europe. Eurofound's 2024 European Working Conditions Survey puts 29 percent of Irish employees working from home at least sometimes, well above the EU average of 22 percent, trailing only Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Sweden. Morgan McKinley's Global Workplace Guide, reported in The Irish Times, found 92 percent of Irish respondents strongly prefer a hybrid or remote model, and that only 42 percent of Irish employers are pushing staff back into the office — gentler than the 56 percent doing so globally. Dublin, in other words, is being squeezed less hard than most places, which is a genuinely low bar to clear and Ireland clears it.

The complication is who signs the paycheque. Ireland hosts roughly 245,000 workers employed directly by US multinationals, a number that has grown 16 percent in two years, and AmCham's own members — 98 percent of whom offer remote work to non-manufacturing staff — mostly route that flexibility through a US head office. The Irish Times's reporting on life inside these arrangements describes staff splitting their day across US time zones, meetings scheduled as late as 9pm, and one manager for whom working until 11pm had become "part of the norm." Unlimited PTO policies exist; using them without apology does not always follow. Ireland's labour law — the EU Working Time Directive's 48-hour weekly cap, four weeks' paid leave, 11-hour daily rest — is real and enforceable. It is also no match for a Slack notification from a time zone that hasn't gone to bed yet.

Across the Atlantic, American flexibility is less a national mood than a credential. BLS data show telework concentrated almost entirely among the management and professional class — 37.9 percent of them teleworked in Q1 2024 — against 5.4 percent in service occupations and 3.2 percent in production and transportation. There is no US equivalent of the Working Time Directive: no statutory cap on hours, no mandated rest period, no floor under paid leave at all beyond whatever an employer decides to offer. Hofstede Insights scores the United States at 91 on individualism against Ireland's 70, and the gap shows up exactly here — American flexibility tends to be framed as something you personally negotiated and could lose, rather than something the state guarantees everyone.

That framing has teeth. Return-to-office mandates in large US employers have been both faster and firmer than their Irish counterparts at the same companies; one Blind thread on Amazon's Dublin office describes a three-day-a-week requirement rolled out "globally," with Dublin staff grumbling less about the policy itself than about the parking, traffic and rents around the campus — a three-day mandate that, by domestic American standards at firms with five-day pushes, reads almost generous. Morgan McKinley's global data found 56 percent of fully onsite workers actively job-hunting within six months, against 41 percent of hybrid and 44 percent of fully remote staff — a retention risk employers keep accepting anyway.

Here is the part that should unsettle anyone drafting a relocation spreadsheet: Ireland, with its statutory rest periods, its right-to-disconnect provisions and its government actively subsidising rural remote hubs, posts one of the worst employee engagement scores Gallup has measured anywhere — just 9 percent of Irish workers engaged in 2025, below both the European average of 12 percent and the global average of 20 percent, with 82 percent "not engaged" outright. The country built the legal scaffolding for a healthy relationship with work and the workforce simply isn't showing up for it emotionally, flexible hours or not.

The United States, with none of that scaffolding and a culture that treats being reachable as a virtue, has long tracked meaningfully higher on the same Gallup measure. The lesson is not that American workers are happier because the system is harsher — it's that legal protection and lived flexibility are two separate currencies, and a country can be generous with one while being bankrupt in the other. The question worth asking before a move is not "does this country allow remote work," but "whose expectations is my actual job answering to."

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/ireland — one thread arguing Ireland is obsessed with companies forcing return-to-office while ignoring the fully remote employers quietly hiring; a commenter countered that the commercial property and banking sectors have every incentive to keep office mandates alive, regardless of what's actually best for output.*

> *r/MoveToIreland — a recurring piece of advice in this subreddit's relocation guide: remote work is not, on its own, a route into the country. Non-EU citizens need an Irish-registered employer to sponsor their visa; you cannot simply keep your foreign job and move your laptop to a Dublin kitchen table.*

> *Quora — a US citizen with a fully remote job for a US company asked what permit, if any, they'd need to spend a few months working from Ireland, a question that exposes how many Americans assume "remote" is a passport rather than a paperwork problem.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — Amazon employees discussing the Dublin AWS office's three-day in-person requirement noted it was rolled out as a global policy rather than a local one, with the sharpest complaints reserved not for the mandate but for the lack of parking and the traffic getting there.*

> *r/ireland — amid the cynicism, one commenter made the unfashionable point that removing the commute and office noise had genuinely improved their output, cutting against the thread's assumption that remote work mostly produces excuses rather than results.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two on the strength of remote-work policy alone, choose based on who your manager actually reports to, not which flag is on your contract. Ireland gives you the legal floor — the rest periods, the leave, the right to eventually disconnect — but if your HQ is in California, that floor gets quietly waived every time a 9pm invite lands. The United States gives you none of that floor, but concentrates real flexibility in exactly the roles that are already well paid and well protected by market leverage, and somehow still gets more out of its workforce emotionally than Ireland's better-regulated, worse-engaged one.

The honest version for a friend over a drink: Ireland will protect your evenings on paper and interrupt them anyway if the client's in Denver; America will never promise your evenings at all, but at least it isn't pretending otherwise.
