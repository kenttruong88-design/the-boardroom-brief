# Germany Will Sue You Over Your Vacation Days. Denmark Will Just Judge You.

🇩🇪 Germany · 🇩🇰 Denmark

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Germany, an employer who fails to grant your full statutory leave can end up in court over it. In Denmark, an employer who even seems surprised you're taking five weeks off in July will end up gossiped about at the next skat (tax) office barbecue. Both countries will get you your vacation. One does it through the Bundesurlaubsgesetz. The other does it through sheer, unbearable social pressure. For the relocating professional, the difference between a legal floor and a cultural norm is the whole ballgame, and nobody tells you that until you're the only person left in the Copenhagen office in week three of August, answering emails into a void.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check your contract or Tarifvertrag (collective agreement) for your real entitlement — most employers grant 25–30 days, well above the 20-day statutory floor | Assume the 20-day legal minimum is what you'll actually get; it's a floor, not a norm |
| Submit an "Urlaubsantrag" (leave request) in writing and get it confirmed before booking anything | Expect your Krankenschein (sick note) to count against Urlaub — German law treats certified sick days and vacation as entirely separate pots |
| Plan around Christmas and Himmelfahrt (Ascension) bridge days — colleagues block these out months in advance | Schedule anything urgent in the two weeks around Christmas; most of white-collar Germany quietly shuts down |
| Learn your Bundesland's public holidays — Bavaria and Baden-Württemberg have far more than Hamburg or Berlin | Assume national holidays are uniform across the country; Germany's calendar is federal, not centralized |
| Carry over unused days by informal agreement, but confirm the deadline — many contracts forfeit leave after March 31 of the following year | Let vacation days silently expire; ask HR about carryover rules the week you're hired, not the week you need them |

#### 🇩🇰 Denmark

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect to accrue 2.08 days per month under the "concurrent holiday" system and use them as you earn them | Wait until a traditional "leave year" to use your days — the 2020 reform lets you take leave almost as soon as you've earned it |
| Block out three consecutive weeks for your main summer holiday between May and September, as the Holiday Act expects | Try to split your main summer leave into odd long weekends; the law and your colleagues both expect one real block |
| Apply for your holiday allowance (feriepenge) through Borger.dk or FerieKonto well before you travel | Forget that holiday pay is often a separate 12.5% disbursement, not simply your normal salary rolling on |
| Learn the word "sommerferielukket" — many shops, clinics, and offices post it and simply close for weeks | Show up expecting your dentist, your gym, or half of Copenhagen's bureaucracy to be open in July | 
| Say "god sommerferie" (happy summer holiday) to colleagues before the office empties out — it's the done thing | Email a Danish colleague "just checking in" during their summerhouse weeks; it reads as faintly alarming, not diligent |

### Germany: The Floor Is High, the Ceiling Is Negotiated

Germany's statutory minimum is deceptively modest: the Bundesurlaubsgesetz guarantees just 20 working days on a five-day week, which sits exactly at the European Union's legal floor. What the brochure leaves out is that almost nobody in white-collar Germany actually lives on 20 days. Collective bargaining agreements (Tarifverträge) and standard employment contracts routinely push the real number to 25, 28, or 30 days, and the Expedia Vacation Deprivation Report, cited by Fortune, puts the typical German professional's annual leave at around 31 days when public holidays are folded in. The paradox is that despite this abundance, 84 percent of German workers told Expedia's surveyors they still feel deprived of vacation — a statistic that says less about German leave law and more about German relationships with unfinished to-do lists.

According to the OECD's "Average Annual Hours Actually Worked per Worker" series, Germans log roughly 1,340 to 1,370 hours a year, among the lowest annual totals of any major economy, achieved not through short days but through long, well-defended absences. The system is federal and uneven: public holidays range from 9 national days to as many as 13 depending on the Bundesland, so a transfer from Hamburg to Bavaria can hand you several extra paid days off purely by geography. Hofstede Insights scores Germany at 65 on Uncertainty Avoidance, and nowhere is that more visible than in how precisely Germans document, request, and defend their Urlaub — a culture that treats a vacation day less as a vibe and more as a contractual asset to be tracked.

That same Uncertainty Avoidance score explains why the paperwork matters so much to the people enforcing it. A German Urlaubsantrag is not a Slack message to your manager; it is a formal request that goes into a system, gets a formal confirmation, and becomes something HR can point to later. The upside of this bureaucratic thoroughness is real: once your leave is confirmed, it is treated as close to sacred, and the convention of not contacting colleagues during Urlaub is enforced socially as much as legally. The downside is that the system rewards planning far in advance and punishes the improviser — book your Himmelfahrt bridge-day trip in February, not May, because by spring the good hotels, and your preferred days, will already be gone to colleagues who filed first.

### Denmark: The Law Sets the Floor, the Calendar Enforces It

Denmark doesn't bother with Europe's four-week minimum; the Ferieloven, or Holiday Act, guarantees five full weeks — 25 days — to nearly every salaried employee, and a 2020 reform replaced the old "earn this year, spend next year" system with a "concurrent holiday" model that lets staff use leave almost as fast as they accrue it, at roughly 2.08 days per month. Fifteen of those days are expected to be taken as one continuous block during the May-to-September "main holiday period," and this is not a polite suggestion — per The Local Denmark, Danish law and custom both lean on employers to grant three consecutive weeks, and businesses from hair salons to municipal offices simply post "sommerferielukket" and vanish for a stretch of July.

The infrastructure behind this is practically national: Statistics Denmark data, reported by the Copenhagen Post, shows a notable share of summerhouse owners live within a short drive of their own holiday property, often inherited through generations, with DST's Erik Slentø noting that "the accessibility of the summerhouse plays a huge part in its popularity." Hofstede Insights scores Denmark at a strikingly low 16 on Masculinity and just 18 on Power Distance — numbers that map neatly onto a culture where taking your full leave isn't a status flex reserved for senior staff, it's simply what everyone, including your manager, is expected to do at the same time as everyone else.

### The Reckoning

Here is the trap for the new arrival: Germany's leave looks bureaucratic and Denmark's looks relaxed, but the lived experience inverts that. German leave requires paperwork, formal requests, and a working knowledge of your Bundesland's holiday calendar — yet once approved, it is fiercely respected; German managers genuinely will not contact you on Urlaub, and OECD data confirms the system delivers some of the shortest working-hour totals in the OECD. Danish leave requires almost no paperwork at all, yet it comes with an unwritten social contract that is far less negotiable than any German HR policy: miss your three consecutive summer weeks, and you're not breaking a rule, you're breaking an expectation, which in a Power-Distance-18 culture is considerably worse.

The other inversion is timing. Germany's system is built around flexibility and personal scheduling discretion within a generous but individually managed allotment. Denmark's is built around synchronized national absence — the country doesn't stagger vacation, it collectively exhales in July. An American transplant optimizing for "maximum control over my own calendar" will find Germany's system more legible; one optimizing for "maximum certainty that nobody will ping me" should move to Denmark and simply not be in the office in week 29.

There's a third wrinkle worth knowing before you sign anything: Germans rent Danish summerhouses more than any other nationality, which means the two cultures' leave habits quietly overlap every July, with one country's workforce decamping into the other's holiday infrastructure. It is, in its way, the most honest cultural-fit test either country offers — watch where the Germans go on their 30 days, and you'll find exactly where the Danes already were on their 25.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com forum — a Germany-based intern described being told by her employer she had zero entitlement to paid leave, only to learn later the issue wasn't nationality but contract type: mandatory academic internships can fall outside standard vacation law entirely, while voluntary ones of the same length typically earn around two days per month.*

> *Hacker News — a commenter flagged the number that trips up newcomers doing the math from job postings: 25 vacation days isn't a generous perk in Germany, it's close to the unremarkable baseline once collective agreements are factored in.*

> *NBC News interview, American expat in Germany — she described finding leave requests "extremely easy" to get approved, and said the bigger adjustment was cultural: discussing vacation plans openly with colleagues, rather than treating time off as a private matter the way she had in the US, and discovering her manager genuinely would not raise work matters with her while she was away.*

> *The Local Denmark — a recurring pattern in reader coverage of Denmark's summer shutdown season: newcomers unfamiliar with "sommerferielukket" routinely arrive at a shuttered pharmacy, dentist, or city office in July, having assumed at least one branch somewhere would stay open.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two on leave culture alone, the real question isn't "how many days do I get" — both countries will give you more than almost anywhere else your American or Asian peers are working. The real question is what kind of system you want enforcing your rest. Germany hands you a generous, individually tracked entitlement and trusts you, and your paperwork, to use it; Denmark hands you a slightly smaller legal number but backs it with a collective calendar so synchronized that opting out feels less like a personal choice and more like a diagnosis. Choose Germany if you want control. Choose Denmark if you want permission you'll never have to ask for twice.

Either way: block out next July before you've even signed the lease, because in one country your colleagues will silently judge you for not taking your Urlaub, and in the other, the office will simply be closed whether you judged it correctly or not.
