# Dress for the Job You Have, Not the Country You Think You're In: Pakistan vs. Japan

🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇯🇵 Japan

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Lahore, a new hire can get away with jeans so long as the blazer thrown over them looks expensive enough to compensate. In Tokyo, a new hire can show up in a jacket that cost more than their first month's rent and still be quietly informed, via a colleague's raised eyebrow, that the navy was two shades too adventurous. Both countries will tell you, with total sincerity, that they have a "dress code." Neither means remotely the same thing by it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Pack at least one well-tailored shalwar kameez and one Western suit — both read as equally professional | Wear shorts or sleeveless tops outside a private compound or home |
| Ask HR for the written dress code on day one; it's often stricter than what you see on the floor | Assume "casual Friday" exists as a national norm — it generally doesn't |
| Keep a shawl or dupatta on hand regardless of your own gender norms back home | Mistake one relaxed, founder-led startup office for the industry standard |
| Overdress slightly for client meetings and let a colleague tell you to relax | Show up in tight-fitting clothing to any government-linked institution — some now ban it outright |
| Iron everything; visible wrinkles read as unprofessional no matter how good the cut | Offer a handshake to a Pakistani woman colleague unless she extends her hand first |
| Treat conservative dress as a sign of respect, not an imposition | Confuse Islamabad's embassy-circuit norms with Karachi's or Lahore's — they diverge |

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Default to a dark suit (charcoal, navy, black), white shirt, subdued tie for your first 90 days | Assume a Tokyo job ad's "business casual" means what it does in Austin or Berlin |
| Watch what your most senior colleague wears, and quietly match it | Wear sneakers unless your specific employer has explicitly cleared them |
| Buy the suit locally if you can — fit and cut read as effort | Get a visible tattoo uncovered, even in 2026 — most firms still ask staff to hide them |
| Follow Cool Biz cues (no jacket) only when your office visibly signals it | Mistake a summer energy-saving dress policy for permission to dress off-duty |
| Keep nails, hair, and jewelry deliberately unremarkable at traditional firms | Assume one retailer's relaxed hair policy means the whole country has moved on |
| Save bold personal style for employers that have explicitly gone casual | Leave before your boss in your first weeks without a genuinely good reason |

### Pakistan: A Conservative Floor, a Negotiated Ceiling

Pakistan's workplace dress code runs on a sliding scale that has less to do with law than with institution, and anyone expecting a single national standard gets corrected within the first week. Hofstede Insights' country-comparison data puts Pakistan at a strikingly low 14 on individualism — one of the lowest readings recorded anywhere — against a middling 55 on power distance. Translated out of consultant-speak: offices run on group identity and rank, and how you present yourself signals which group, and which tier, you're claiming. In client-facing roles that typically means a Western-style suit for men or a well-cut shalwar kameez, both treated as equally professional, while women are expected to favor loose-fitting clothing covering arms and legs — a norm new arrivals learn less from an orientation packet than from a swift, polite social correction.

The formality is not static, and lately it has tightened in at least one visible sector. In July 2026, Karachi's Directorate of College Education formally banned T-shirts, jeans, tight-fitting clothes, and "fancy sunglasses" for staff at government colleges, citing casual attire as "inconsistent with professional standards" (ProPakistani). It's a narrow, regional rule, but it's indicative: formality in Pakistan frequently arrives top-down, in writing, after the fact, rather than developing as an ambient office norm. That sits oddly alongside the roughly 80.8% of the national workforce the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics' 2024-25 Labour Force Survey classifies as informally employed — a sector no circular has ever reached, where "professional attire" is whatever keeps the shop running that day.

The gap between multinational offices and domestically run ones compounds the confusion. Decades of diplomatic "Real Post Report" entries describe everything from strict suit-and-tie expectations to "fairly casual, no heels," often for the same city a few years apart, depending seemingly on the office, the year, and the ambassador's own tolerance for air-conditioning. The practical upshot: the national culture sets a conservative floor, but the ceiling on how buttoned-up to actually be is negotiated office by office — and the safest opening move is to overdress and wait to be told otherwise.

### Japan: Uniform on Paper, Uniform by Design

Japan's formality is better documented and, on paper, more consistent: a dark suit, white shirt, and subdued tie for men, similarly muted blazers and tied-back hair for women, with bright colors or visibly standing out actively discouraged at traditional firms (Coto Academy). Hofstede's cross-country figures help explain why the uniformity holds: Japan scores extremely high on masculinity and uncertainty avoidance, two dimensions that, in plain terms, reward visible achievement-signaling and punish ambiguity. A dress code, among other things, is a tool for removing ambiguity about who is serious. The result is a workplace where rank is read through small variations within sameness, rather than through the individual expression many Western offices treat as a given.

That uniformity is cracking, but unevenly, and mostly where demographics force it to. Japan's working-age population has fallen 16% since its 1995 peak, per OECD data cited in Reuters reporting carried by Malay Mail, and roughly two-thirds of Japanese firms now say labor shortages are seriously hurting their business. Retailers and drugstore chains — Don Quijote, Fuji Yakuhin, Tokyu Store — have dropped rules on hair color, nail art, and piercings to compete for scarce staff; at Don Quijote, 55% of employees now have non-black hair. None of that has reached the banks. Sumitomo Mitsui Banking has no formal hair or nail policy and doesn't need one: staff understand, unwritten, that appearance should not stand out, which is arguably a stricter form of control than any circular Karachi has issued.

The stakes of all that formality compound with hours worked, or at least with hours logged. OECD figures put Japan's average annual hours per worker at 1,598 in 2025 — genuinely fewer than Pakistan's comparable figure of roughly 2,471 hours, per cross-country labour-hours data — yet overwork remains a formally tracked government category: Japan's labor ministry recognized 1,304 overwork cases in fiscal 2024, including 89 involving suicide or suicide attempts, per figures cited by Coto Academy. The suit, in other words, is rarely the hardest part of the job. It's worn on top of a culture that still measures devotion by visible, prolonged presence, long after the actual working hours stopped requiring it.

### The Reckoning

The country that looks more conservative from an airplane window is, perversely, the more improvisational one in practice. Pakistan's formality is openly contested and locally renegotiated — a circular in one government college banning jeans, a looser office two streets over, a Lahore startup letting its product team show up in trainers — producing wide day-to-day variance under a conservative national headline. Japan's formality looks, from outside, like the newly flexible one: Cool Biz, sneakers for some airline staff, dyed hair at the discount chain. But underneath those headlines sits one of the most uniformity-enforcing cultural profiles anywhere; the deviations are permitted precisely because they're centrally sanctioned — a government campaign, a company memo — not because ambiguity itself has become acceptable.

The real trap for a new arrival is reading either country's exceptions as the rule. A foreign hire in Karachi who spots a casually dressed junior colleague and assumes the whole office has relaxed will be the only person in a tight-fitting shirt when the client meeting that closes the deal gets booked. A foreign hire in Tokyo who reads about Cool Biz and shows up jacket-less to a first client pitch in April has confused an energy-saving campaign with a personal style license. In both countries, the skill actually being tested isn't taste. It's whether you can read a room that was never going to state its rules out loud.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *TalesMag Real Post Report, Islamabad — one diplomatic spouse recalled the specific frustration of packing her usual work wardrobe for the posting, only to discover that the conservative dress expectations on the ground made most of it unwearable, a recalibration that took longer than the move itself.*

> *Expat.com forum, Lahore thread — a long-time contributor's blunt reassurance to a nervous newcomer: in Lahore specifically, you can dress about as you please, since the city draws people "from all walks of life" — though the same poster warned the identical outfit in Kashmir would draw stares.*

> *Teamblind — a commenter who had worked both Tokyo and New York tech offices pushed back on the idea that tech dress codes are universally casual, noting the Tokyo office carried a persistent "degree of formality" the New York one never did, regardless of what either job posting's "business casual" implied.*

> *Japan Reference (JREF) forum — a visiting postdoctoral researcher asking what to pack got the fastest diagnostic on offer: check the faculty's staff photo page online, since whatever the professors are wearing in their own headshots is, with near-total reliability, the actual dress code nobody was going to otherwise volunteer.*

> *Quora threads on relocating for work to Japan return less a single viral story than a recurring pattern: new arrivals repeatedly describe misjudging what a Japanese employer means by "business casual," discovering only once in the office that the term sits several notches more formal than its usual Western meaning.*

### Conclusion

If you're deciding where to pack the extra suit, pack it for Japan and plan to actually wear it; pack it for Pakistan and plan to negotiate it down. Japan's formality is the more codified of the two, underwritten by high masculinity and uncertainty-avoidance scores that reward conformity and specifically punish the kind of ambiguity a newcomer is most likely to generate — your job is to read the room, or the faculty photo page, and match it, not to look for a loophole. Pakistan's formality is the more contested and locally renegotiated, settled daily between a conservative cultural floor and a workplace that varies sharply by employer, client, and generation — your job there is less to match a single code than to triangulate one from whoever is actually in the room with you.

Either way, overdress for the first week and quietly downgrade once you've watched who gets away with what; it's cheaper than the alternative, and it happens to be the one piece of advice that works identically in Karachi and Tokyo. If you're asking me over a drink: buy the Japan suit before you land, and buy the Pakistan wardrobe after you arrive. One country forgives you for guessing wrong on day one. The other doesn't, and no amount of ironing changes which is which.
