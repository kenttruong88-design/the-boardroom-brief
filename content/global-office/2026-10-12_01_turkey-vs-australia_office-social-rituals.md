**Turkey Will Feed You Three Meals a Day at the Office. Australia Will Just Hand You a Beer and Call It Team Building.**

**🇹🇷 Turkey · 🇦🇺 Australia**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Istanbul, a job offer comes with an unspoken clause: you will be fed, repeatedly, by people who will take mild offense if you don't eat. In Sydney, the equivalent clause is that you will, at some point on a Friday, be handed a schooner you didn't ask for and expected to enjoy it standing up. Nearly half of Turkish employees work more than 50 hours a week, according to the OECD's wellbeing data — the highest share among the organisation's 35 member countries — while the average Australian clocks a comparatively restrained week and still finds energy to debate whether "knock-off drinks" are compulsory. Both countries insist their office rituals are optional. Neither country means it.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇷 Turkey

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the first glass of çay (tea) offered to you, even at 11am on your first day | Decline food from a colleague's desk — it reads as a rejection of the relationship, not the snack |
| Let your host order and pay at a business meal; reciprocate on your next visit | Offer to split the bill — it embarrasses the person who invited you |
| Expect your boss's instructions to come as instructions, not suggestions | Assume a Turkish general manager's directive is a conversation starter |
| Confirm meeting times a day in advance, and again that morning | Treat a scheduled time as fixed the moment it's said out loud |
| Join the Turkish coffee at the end of a meal, even briefly | Rush out immediately after eating — the coffee is part of the meeting |

#### 🇦🇺 Australia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Show up to Friday drinks at least once in your first few weeks, even for 15 minutes | Skip every single social event and expect to be read as "just busy" |
| Speak up in meetings, including to disagree with someone senior | Wait to be invited to share your opinion — nobody's going to ask twice |
| Use first names with managers and executives, no exceptions | Default to "Mr." or "Sir" — it will get a confused laugh, not respect |
| Take your full four weeks of annual leave, every year | Let leave accrue as a badge of honour — nobody's impressed, least of all HR |
| Bring a soft drink to the pub and leave after one if that's your pace | Make your non-drinking a topic of discussion — it's a non-event here |

### Turkey: Hierarchy With a Full Stomach

Turkish companies run on two overlapping operating systems: the formal org chart, and the much older rule that relationships get built over food, not Slack messages. World Business Culture describes business lunches and dinners as "an integral part of the all-important relationship-building process" — the meal isn't a reward for closing the deal, it's the mechanism by which the deal becomes possible at all. The person who extends the invitation pays, full stop; offering to split the bill doesn't read as thoughtful, it reads as a small insult to the relationship being built. Meals typically end with a cup of thick, sweet Turkish coffee, and lingering for it is treated as part of the meeting rather than an optional dessert course.

That warmth coexists with a management style foreign executives have spent decades politely calling "directive." DHL's Turkey country manager once told local business press he still saw "traces of family company culture" in how decisions moved through the ranks — top down, with staff expected to execute rather than co-author. HP's regional head went further, noting that in traditionally structured domestic firms, Turkish general managers hold outsized authority and "employees are more respectful and distant" as a result. None of this is coded as dysfunction locally; it's simply where the hierarchy sits. By law, standard hours cap at 45 a week, with overtime paid at 150% — yet the OECD still finds nearly half the workforce logging 50-plus hours regularly, the highest rate in the OECD. Punctuality runs on its own elastic clock, too: one expat.com regular described Turkish colleagues as reliably helpful and warm but noted that keeping agreed times "might sometimes be an issue" — their fix was nailing down a specific day and hour, then confirming it again the morning of.

### Australia: Flat Structure, Loud Opinions, Mandatory Fun

Australian offices run flatter than almost anywhere in the English-speaking world, and the flatness is load-bearing, not decorative. Hofstede Insights scores Australia at roughly 36 on power distance against Turkey's 66 — among the widest hierarchy gaps you'll find between two G20-adjacent economies — and it shows up in the small stuff before the big stuff: first names for everyone, managers who eat lunch at the same tables as their reports, junior staff who interrupt without waiting for permission. A guide for new migrants from SettleAu notes that disagreeing with a manager in a meeting is read as engagement, not insubordination, and that Australians tend to downplay their own achievements, which makes confident self-promotion land as faintly embarrassing rather than impressive.

The social calendar, meanwhile, runs almost entirely on beverages. Morning tea, Friday "knock-off" drinks, office birthday cards passed around for signatures, a weekly footy-tipping competition, and a company sweep on the Melbourne Cup each November — the rituals are described by employers as purely social, but a 2022 Roy Morgan study found 67.9% of Australians had a drink in the past month, and The Ethics Centre has documented how alcohol-centred socialising quietly sorts colleagues into those who build relationships after hours and those who, for religious, health, or simply personal reasons, don't. The trend lines are shifting — the share of 20-somethings who don't drink at all nearly tripled between 2001 and 2019 — but workplace culture, by the Centre's own account, has been slower to adjust than the drinks aisle. Annual leave sits at a legally guaranteed four weeks, plus ten days of paid personal leave, and taking all of it is the norm rather than the exception, though even that has its own quiet tension: workers at firms with newly "corporatised" cultures report the opposite pressure, quietly skipping leave out of habit rather than rule.

### The Reckoning

Here's the inversion that catches people off guard: the country with the stricter, more top-down management culture is also the one where showing up to the social event is lower-stakes, because the hierarchy itself tells you where you stand without requiring you to perform belonging at a bar. In Turkey, respect is demonstrated through formal gestures — accepting food, confirming the meeting time twice, letting the senior person order — not through how convincingly you nurse a beer on a rooftop. In Australia, the hierarchy is flat enough that status has to be earned somewhere, and "somewhere" is disproportionately the pub, the sweep, and the WhatsApp thread about Friday drinks. SBS's own reporting on workplace adjustment for migrants puts it plainly: Turkish management expects "toughness from bosses and obedience from employees," while Australian offices are "highly regulated" with explicit rules against bullying and bias — yet it's the regulated, egalitarian one that quietly punishes the colleague who never comes for a drink.

The other twist is the overtime math. Turkey's 45-hour legal ceiling looks almost Scandinavian on paper; in practice, OECD figures show close to half the country blowing past it, a polite fiction colleagues have quietly agreed to outgrow. Australia's four-week leave guarantee looks identical to most of Western Europe's, and mostly gets honoured — until a company culture shift, like the one Atlassian staff described after their stock slide, starts quietly rewarding people who don't use it. Neither rulebook tells you what will actually happen to your calendar. The lunch table and the bar tab will.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — An engineer at an Australian tech firm said the company's once-strong internal culture shifted hard toward "US style corporate culture with heavy performance review" after its stock price fell, and admitted they now rarely take their annual leave even though it's worth roughly a month's salary, saying they'd rather be paid out for it than actually use it.*

> *Capital.com.tr (Turkish business press) — A foreign general manager running a multinational's Turkish operations noted "traces of family company culture" surviving inside otherwise modern firms, observing that leadership there had "still not moved beyond the process of issuing directives" and recommending newcomers build their own open-communication habits rather than wait for one to be modeled for them.*

> *SBS Language — A feature aimed at new migrants flagged that an invitation to "have a coffee" in an Australian office is a relationship gesture, not a literal beverage request, and warned that missing this signal — along with underestimating how much unwritten workplace politics shapes who's seen as "a team player" — is a common early misstep for arrivals used to more explicit cultures.*

Genuine first-person Reddit and Quora threads specifically pairing Turkey and Australia proved difficult to source for this piece — Quora's results were not independently fetchable, and Reddit's relevant threads skewed toward visa logistics rather than office life — so this section leans on expat forums, Blind, and named business journalism instead of padding with invented quotes.

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between them on culture alone: Turkey asks you to master a code of hospitality and hierarchy that, once learned, tells you exactly where you stand and exactly how to show respect — no ambiguity, just a longer dinner. Australia asks you to treat your manager like a mate, disagree with them in meetings, and then prove you meant no disrespect by turning up to the pub on Friday — a code that looks relaxed and is, in its own way, just as exacting. The real variable isn't which system is "friendlier"; it's which kind of performance you'd rather get good at. Honestly? Learn to love Turkish coffee. It's a lot easier to fake enjoying a hot drink than to fake enjoying your fourth warm beer standing up in a car park.
