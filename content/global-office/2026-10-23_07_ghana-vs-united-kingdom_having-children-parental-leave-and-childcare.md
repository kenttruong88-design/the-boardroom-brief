**Ghana Gives You Twelve Weeks and a Village; Britain Gives You Twelve Weeks and a Spreadsheet**

🇬🇭 Ghana · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Here is a statistic to sit with before you accept either relocation package: more Ghanaian women of working age are in the labour force (61.5 percent, per World Bank data) than British women (57.3 percent), despite Ghana having, by any formal measure, almost no childcare system at all. The United Kingdom, by contrast, has statutory maternity pay, a shared parental leave scheme, tax-free childcare accounts and a government "30 free hours" programme — and UNICEF still ranks it 34th out of 41 rich countries for family-friendly policy, because none of that infrastructure changes the fact that nursery costs more than many parents earn. The lesson, before you've even packed, is that generous-sounding policy and generous outcomes are not the same thing, and the gap between them is where this guide lives. Neither government is lying to you, exactly. They are just measuring different things: one measures weeks written into statute, the other measures who actually shows up to carry a toddler at 8am.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇬🇭 Ghana

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget for live-in household help from day one — full-time care in Accra runs roughly GHS 1,000 (around $65) a month, the default solution rather than a backup plan | Don't assume formal daycare exists near your office; a UN Women-reported study found organised childcare for working mothers "generally not available" outside a handful of market creches |
| Get the midwife's confinement certificate early — your 12 weeks of fully-paid leave under the Labour Act only starts running once it's on file | Don't skip comparing international-school fees against your package before signing; $3,700 a semester per child at a leading international school erases a strong cedi salary fast |
| Put paternity leave terms in writing with HR before the birth — it remains employer discretion, not law, until the pending reform passes | Don't expect paid paternity leave as a default; most employers still treat it as unpaid or simply undefined |
| Lean into the extended-family network deliberately — aunts, grandmothers and in-laws are the real childcare infrastructure, so invest in those relationships early | Don't wait on the draft labour bill (14 weeks maternity, 5 days paternity) to arrive before planning; it has sat in parliament since 2023 with no passage date |
| Get your full-pay maternity entitlement confirmed in your contract, not just assumed from the Act | Don't hire live-in help on a handshake; agree wages, hours and days off in writing, since rates swing from GHS 150 to GHS 1,000 depending on scope and everyone quotes a different figure |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| File your MATB1 maternity certificate with HR by week 25 of pregnancy to lock in the enhanced first-six-weeks pay rate | Don't assume the £194.32 statutory weekly rate covers much; it's roughly a third of average full-time weekly earnings |
| Apply for your 30 funded nursery hours the moment your child turns nine months old | Don't underestimate waiting lists; a full-time under-two place averages £140–190 a week even with funding, and city nurseries oversubscribe a year out |
| Discuss Shared Parental Leave with your partner and employer before the birth, since the SC3/SC4 paperwork is awkward to file retroactively | Don't treat unpaid stretches of shared leave as a casual default; fathers' uptake stays in single digits precisely because the pay drop is real |
| Ask at offer stage whether your employer tops up statutory pay; many professional-services and tech firms quietly pay full salary for 3–6 months | Don't assume a full-time return is automatic or even financially sensible; IFS/ONS data show mothers' earnings fall to under half of fathers' within seven years of a first birth |
| Check Tax-Free Childcare and Universal Credit's childcare element separately — they interact with household income thresholds in ways that can cancel each other out | Don't count on nearby family to plug childcare gaps; many corporate transfers have none, and the system is not built to assume otherwise |

Ghana's statutory floor is straightforward: Section 57 of the Labour Act, 2003 (Act 651) entitles women to at least 12 weeks of maternity leave at full pay and benefits, with two further weeks for multiple births or complications, and the government cannot dismiss you for taking it. What it does not entitle you to is paternity leave, childcare, or much else — a draft labour bill that would stretch maternity leave to 14 weeks and introduce five paid-or-unpaid days of paternity leave has been circulating since 2023 without a vote, and in September 2026 the Trades Union Congress was still publicly lobbying government to close the gap between Ghana's 12 weeks and the 14-week minimum under ILO Convention 183.

What fills the space where institutions don't is the extended family, plus a labour market where live-in domestic help is a routine household line item rather than a luxury. A full-time helper covering cooking, cleaning and childcare for a family with two children runs around GHS 1,000 a month by recent expatriate-forum estimates — a fraction of what formal nursery costs in most of the world. Formal daycare, by contrast, is scarce enough that one of Accra's few dedicated facilities, the Makola Market Childcare Centre, is run and funded entirely by the market traders themselves because the Ministry of Education has not taken it on. Ghana's high score for collectivism on Hofstede's cultural index (an individualism score of just 15, against the UK's 89) is not an abstraction here: it is the actual childcare system. The same research literature that documents this also flags its limits: studies of mothers returning to work in Accra describe the transition as emotionally difficult regardless of the informal safety net, and the arrangement still runs largely through women — fathers are rarely the ones rearranging their schedules around a sick child, whatever the household's income level.

Britain's statutory offer looks, on paper, like the mature version of all this: up to 52 weeks of maternity leave, 39 of them paid, two weeks of statutory paternity leave, and a Shared Parental Leave scheme letting couples divide the time. Statutory pay for 2026/27 is 90 percent of earnings for the first six weeks, then the lower of £194.32 a week or 90 percent of earnings for the rest — a rate UNICEF's cross-country analysis uses to help explain why the UK ranks 34th of 41 OECD and EU countries on paid parental leave generosity, and why UK parents were, in that same study, the most likely of 29 countries surveyed to say cost is what stops them using more formal childcare.

That cost is not abstract. Coram's 2026 childcare survey puts a part-time nursery place for an under-two at roughly £189 a week with no funding, and even a funded full-time place averages around £149 — against take-home pay that, for anyone on or near minimum wage, a Mumsnet thread of parents comparing notes shows can be lower than the nursery bill itself. The structural result shows up squarely in the numbers: ONS and Institute for Fiscal Studies analysis finds fathers' earnings barely move after a first child, while mothers' earnings drop sharply and, seven years on, average less than half of what fathers earn. The 2023 gender pay gap for full-time employees sat at 7.7 percent; among all employees, including the much higher share of part-time mothers, it was 14.3 percent.

The Reckoning, then, runs counter to instinct. Ghana offers almost nothing in statutory parental infrastructure and yet keeps more women working through parenthood than Britain does, because the support structure is relational rather than contractual — family, neighbours and affordable household staff absorb what the state does not. Britain offers a formal architecture of rights, weekly rates and funded hours, and that architecture still produces a workforce where mothers' pay collapses and many simply exit, because the formal system's actual price tag outruns what it promises on the leaflet.

Put bluntly: in Ghana you are negotiating with people — your mother-in-law, your helper, your employer's goodwill. In the UK you are negotiating with a spreadsheet — HMRC thresholds, nursery waiting lists, tax-credit cliff edges — and spreadsheets do not flex for a sick toddler the way a grandmother might. Neither system is kinder so much as differently brittle, and knowing which kind of brittle you're better equipped to manage is the actual question before you sign. It is also worth asking what happens if the relational system fails you specifically: a corporate transfer with no in-country family has none of Ghana's informal cushion to fall back on, and will be paying full Western-style nanny rates in a market built around the assumption that most households don't need to.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com (Ghana forum) — A Ghana-based expatriate adviser, asked by a family of four about cost of living, broke down a full-time live-in helper covering cleaning, cooking, babysitting and light shopping at roughly 1,000 cedis a month, noting that older forum estimates of 120–150 cedis were years out of date and no longer realistic.*

> *Expat.com (Ghana forum) — A parent with three children enrolled at an international school in Accra described a $75,000 salary as sounding generous until rent, school fees at $3,700 per child per semester, transport and health insurance were accounted for, leaving food and discretionary spending surprisingly tight.*

> *Mumsnet (UK) — A mother on minimum wage calculated that the cheapest nursery place she could find in her area cost more per week than she took home working part-time, and said she wanted to return to work but genuinely could not afford to; another commenter on the same thread had been out of the workforce for twelve years after reaching the same conclusion with three children.*

> *Hacker News — A UK father who took twelve months of leave split with his partner reported central London nursery costs of around £100 a day for an 8am–8pm slot, with public nursery places oversubscribed; a separate commenter on the same thread noted that some colleagues left the UK altogether after a second child specifically because of childcare costs.*

> *Expat.com (UK forum) — A parent recounted that after childbirth she was kept in hospital for three days and ran short of basic supplies, and that managing work, study and a young child alone, without nearby family, made flexibility "almost impossible" — a reminder that Britain's formal entitlements assume a support network the policy itself doesn't provide.*

### Conclusion

If you are weighing Ghana against the UK specifically on parenthood, the honest advice is to stop comparing statutory weeks and start comparing support networks, because that is the variable actually doing the work in both places. Ghana will give you less on paper and more in practice if you can build or buy into a family-and-household-staff system; the UK will give you more on paper and can still leave you doing the arithmetic on whether working pays for itself, because its formal system was built assuming costs that haven't kept pace with its own promises. Choose based on who, specifically, is going to be holding the baby at 2pm on a Tuesday when a meeting runs long — not based on which country's HR handbook reads better.

Either way, budget for childcare before you budget for anything else, because in both countries it is the line item that quietly rewrites everyone's career plans.
