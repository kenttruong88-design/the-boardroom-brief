## The Number Nobody Says Out Loud: Salary Talk in Dar es Salaam and London

**🇹🇿 Tanzania · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In London, your salary is a state secret you'll nonetheless anxiously benchmark against a Glassdoor thread at 2am. In Dar es Salaam, it's written plainly into your employment contract, as the law requires — and then almost never mentioned again in casual conversation. Both cities treat pay as something you don't discuss at the pub or the daladala stop. Only one of them is actively legislating its way out of the habit.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇹🇿 Tanzania

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Insist on a written contract stating remuneration, calculation method, and any in-kind benefits — it's a legal requirement | Accept verbal-only salary agreements, however friendly the employer |
| Expect a dual local/expat salary structure if you're hired internationally — local currency plus a USD top-up is common | Assume Tanzanian shilling salary figures translate directly to a comparable cost of living |
| Negotiate directly and early, before signing — renegotiation later is harder | Bring up colleagues' pay in casual office conversation |
| Research sector benchmarks (NGO, tourism, logistics pay very differently) before any offer discussion | Assume one multinational's pay scale reflects the local market broadly |
| Learn basic Swahili — it's not required at multinationals but it changes how negotiations are received | Expect fluent English alone to substitute for cultural rapport in a negotiation |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Research salary bands before interviewing — fewer than 1 in 7 job ads list a number, so you'll need outside sources | Ask a colleague directly what they earn, even one you're close with |
| Negotiate after a written or verbal offer, not before — that's the accepted window | Bring up a competing offer's exact figure — mentioning it exists is fine, the number usually isn't |
| Push back if asked for current salary history — practice is shifting and candidates increasingly decline to answer | Assume silence on salary in a job ad means the number is negotiable upward — it may just be low |
| Expect this to keep changing — UK government proposals may mandate salary ranges in ads and restrict salary-history questions | Treat a lack of listed salary as a red flag automatically — it's still the norm, not the exception |
| Use published gender pay gap reporting (mandatory for large employers) as a real negotiating data point | Assume a flat pay-transparency law already exists nationally — as of today it largely doesn't |

### Tanzania: Written Down, Rarely Spoken

Tanzanian labor law requires that employment contracts specify remuneration, the method of its calculation, and any benefits or payments in kind (WageIndicator, Rivermate) — on paper, more formal transparency than many wealthier countries require by law. But that documentation obligation sits between employer and individual employee; it does nothing to make pay visible across a team or an industry. Average gross monthly pay nationally sits around TZS 650,000–720,000, roughly USD 250–280 (Playroll, CloudPay), though this masks huge variation: expat and multinational hires in tourism, NGOs, and logistics around the Dar es Salaam port can clear several times that, with reported ranges from roughly $700 a month for entry-level roles up to $5,000-plus for senior positions at larger companies (Expat Arrivals, Quora).

Negotiation itself tends to happen once, upfront, and directly — Tanzanian employment guidance emphasizes getting expectations and terms clear during contracting rather than revisiting them (WageIndicator). What's largely absent is horizontal transparency: colleagues don't compare notes, unions are the main channel through which collective pay negotiation actually happens, and an individual employee has little external benchmark beyond word of mouth and sector-specific salary guides.

### The UK: Legally Silent, Culturally Louder Than It Looks

The UK's transparency gap is not cultural reticence alone — it's structural. Only 13% of UK job ads include a salary figure at all, meaning roughly nine in ten postings give applicants nothing to calibrate against (Jobtrain, Careerminds analysis). That's a genuine cost: 89% of candidates say they're more likely to apply when a range is listed, and 83% report a missing figure actively worsens their opinion of the employer (same analysis). Yet Britons remain famously allergic to discussing pay socially — asking a colleague directly what they earn is still widely treated as taboo, even as 62% say they'd be willing to disclose their own salary publicly if it improved equity (Careerminds, WiHTL).

The government has been circling a fix. A 2025 call for evidence on equality law floated mandatory salary ranges in job ads and restrictions on employers asking about salary history, and separately, 54% of companies surveyed say they plan to start disclosing internal pay ranges to their own staff (Croner-i, Compport). The UK's gender pay gap, meanwhile, sits at 8.3% for full-time employees per the Office for National Statistics — a figure that exists at all only because large employers are legally required to report it, one of the few places UK pay data is genuinely public.

### The Reckoning

Both countries keep salary out of daily conversation, but for opposite structural reasons. Tanzania's opacity is a market-thinness problem: benchmarks are scarce, most negotiation happens once at hiring, and there simply isn't enough public data to compare against even if colleagues wanted to talk. The UK's opacity is a policy choice dressed as etiquette — the data mostly exists (recruiters, pay-gap filings, internal HR systems), it's just been deliberately kept from candidates and employees as leverage, until regulation forces otherwise.

For someone relocating, the practical upshot inverts expectations. In Tanzania, get everything in writing early because verbal renegotiation later is genuinely harder in a thinner, less legible market. In the UK, treat the listed (or unlisted) salary as a conversational opening bid in a system that's visibly, if slowly, being forced toward more disclosure — today's taboo may be next year's mandatory line item on the job ad.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone asked whether it's ever acceptable to negotiate a UK job offer after already verbally accepting it; the consensus answer was that it happens more often than people admit, and isn't considered as rude as the stiff-upper-lip stereotype suggests, provided it's handled privately and promptly.*

> *Quora — A contributor evaluating compensation for a senior role managing a Tanzanian office for a European company noted that the real skill wasn't negotiating the shilling salary at all, but negotiating the structure — how much landed as local currency versus a hard-currency top-up, since that split determined actual purchasing power far more than the headline number.*

> *Expat.com (Dar es Salaam forum) — An Indian professional who'd received a job offer in Dar es Salaam asked fellow expats whether the offered figure was reasonable, and the most useful replies weren't about the number itself but about which benefits (housing, flights home, dependent schooling) should be negotiated as separate line items rather than folded into a single salary figure.*

> *r/AskUK — One commenter described being caught off guard when a recruiter asked for their current salary as a matter of routine, and said the discomfort wasn't the question itself but not knowing, in the moment, whether declining to answer would quietly end the process — advice they later got was that a polite refusal rarely does.*

> *Quora — A UK-based commenter pushed back hard on being asked for current salary by recruiters, arguing an offer should be based on the role and the candidate's ability to do it — a view echoed widely enough in the replies that several called it their single biggest UK job-search frustration.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two moves, the honest advice is to over-prepare for the negotiation you'll actually have, not the one the culture pretends is happening. In Tanzania, that means getting every term — currency split, benefits, housing — locked into the written contract before you sign, because there's little ambient data to fall back on afterward. In the UK, it means doing your own market research before ever seeing a number, because the ad won't tell you and your new colleagues won't either, at least not yet. The one thing both places agree on: whatever you're paid, don't mention it at the leaving drinks.
