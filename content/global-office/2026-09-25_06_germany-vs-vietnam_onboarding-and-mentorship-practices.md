**Germany vs. Vietnam: One Country Hands You a Checklist, the Other Hands You a Mentor**

**🇩🇪 Germany · 🇻🇳 Vietnam**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Show up for your first day in a German office and you will likely be handed something close to a formal onboarding schedule — a welcome day, a defined integration officer or mentor assignment, a checklist, per Make it in Germany's own guidance to employers, designed to leave as little ambiguous as possible. Show up for your first day in a Vietnamese office and the structure, per multiple firsthand accounts, is more likely to be relational than procedural: less a checklist than an assigned senior colleague who takes informal responsibility for showing you the ropes, a relationship that in Vietnam's more hierarchical, Confucian-influenced workplace culture carries real weight and real obligation on both sides. Both approaches get a new hire integrated. Only one of them puts it in writing.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect (and ask for, if it isn't offered) a structured onboarding plan — welcome day, defined milestones, an assigned point of contact | Assume informal, ask-around onboarding is standard; German companies increasingly formalize the process, especially for international hires |
| Take direct, early feedback at face value — German workplace feedback culture values clarity over cushioning, even in a mentorship context | Read directness in early feedback as personal criticism; it is a communication style applied consistently, not a signal you're struggling |
| Ask specific, well-prepared questions during onboarding — German colleagues generally respond well to demonstrated initiative and clear questions | Assume they'll proactively over-explain unprompted; German onboarding culture expects you to actively engage with the structured materials provided |
| Clarify reporting lines and decision-making authority early — German organizational structures tend to be clearly defined and worth understanding precisely | Assume flexibility in process just because a company culture seems modern; formal structure often persists beneath an informal surface |
| Treat your assigned onboarding buddy or integration contact as a genuine resource — the role is taken seriously on the company's side | Rely solely on your manager for onboarding questions; the assigned buddy/mentor role exists specifically to offload that burden |

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Build a genuine relationship with whichever senior colleague informally takes you under their wing — this relationship carries real weight, not just informational function | Treat a mentor relationship as purely transactional; in Vietnam's more relational workplace culture, mentorship often extends beyond formal working hours |
| Show appropriate deference to hierarchy and seniority, especially in your first weeks — this is read as respect, not passivity | Bypass a more senior colleague to get a faster answer from someone junior, even if more convenient; it can be read as disrespectful of the relational structure |
| Expect onboarding to be less document-driven and more observation- and relationship-driven than in a Western corporate context | Assume a lack of formal written onboarding materials means a lack of structure; the structure exists, it's just relational rather than procedural |
| Invest time in team meals and informal gatherings early — these function as an informal but genuinely important extension of onboarding | Rush past the relationship-building phase to get straight to "productive" work; doing so can slow your actual integration into the team |
| Ask questions humbly and be receptive to correction — younger and newer staff are expected to learn through guided observation from someone senior | Publicly challenge or correct a senior colleague's guidance, even if you believe it's mistaken; raise concerns privately and respectfully instead |

**Germany**

German onboarding practice, per Make it in Germany's employer-facing guidance and Eurojob Consulting's analysis of onboarding field and remote employees, leans heavily structured and procedural — many companies formally assign an "integration officer" or onboarding buddy specifically for new international hires, alongside defined welcome days and milestone check-ins. The approach reflects a broader German workplace preference, well documented across Hofstede-derived cultural analyses, for explicit process and reduced ambiguity: rather than leaving a new hire to absorb norms by observation, the system spells them out.

That structure doesn't eliminate the need for genuine mentorship, but it does change its character. A Quora thread on the "unspoken rules of German work culture that newcomers often overlook" surfaces a recurring theme: even with a formal onboarding plan in place, new hires still have to learn, mostly by direct experience, that German workplace feedback — praise and criticism alike — tends to be delivered more bluntly and with less social cushioning than many international hires expect, a cultural layer no onboarding checklist fully prepares you for.

**Vietnam**

Vietnamese workplace onboarding, per VGGate's comparison of German and Vietnamese working cultures through a Hofstede lens, operates in a higher power-distance, more collectivist and relationally structured context — mentorship functions less as an HR-designed program and more as an organically assigned, socially binding relationship between a new hire and a senior colleague. Great Place To Work Vietnam's analysis of onboarding younger, Gen Z employees notes that even as digital-native younger workers push for more modern, flexible workplace practices, the underlying relational structure of Vietnamese mentorship — deference to a senior figure who takes real responsibility for a junior colleague's development — remains largely intact.

Reeracoen Vietnam's 2026 analysis of retention in the Vietnamese labor market draws a direct line between the quality of this early mentorship relationship and whether a new hire stays past their first year, framing informal mentorship not as a soft cultural nicety but as one of the most consequential retention levers available to employers in a competitive hiring market.

**The Reckoning**

The contrast here isn't about which country onboards new employees more thoroughly — both clearly take it seriously — it's about where the thoroughness lives. Germany formalizes onboarding into process: documented, scheduled, assigned. Vietnam formalizes it into relationship: undocumented but socially binding, carrying obligations on both the mentor's and the new hire's side that a Western HR system would struggle to write into a policy document at all. A German new hire who receives excellent written onboarding materials but no real relational mentor may still feel unsupported in a way the paperwork doesn't capture; a Vietnamese new hire assigned a genuinely engaged senior mentor may navigate ambiguity far better than any checklist could account for.

For someone moving between the two systems, the practical translation matters: don't expect Vietnamese-style relational mentorship to show up on a German onboarding schedule, and don't expect German-style procedural clarity to substitute for actually building the relationship a Vietnamese workplace expects of you.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A response to "What are the unspoken rules of German work culture that newcomers often overlook?" described receiving blunt, unsoftened feedback in the first week on the job and initially assuming it signaled the role was in jeopardy, before realizing it was simply the standard register for feedback at every level, positive included.*

> *Quora — A poster asking about the general attitude of Germans toward foreign colleagues noted that formal onboarding materials were thorough and well-organized, but that genuinely being included socially by the team took months longer than the paperwork suggested it would.*

> *Formatera (personal blog, "German Work Culture: How I Adapted to the Differences") — The author described struggling initially with how procedural and document-driven their onboarding felt compared to what they were used to, before coming to appreciate that the clarity reduced the guesswork they'd experienced in less structured workplaces elsewhere.*

> *Quora — Someone describing what it's like to live and work as a foreigner in Hanoi noted that a senior colleague essentially adopted responsibility for their integration unprompted in the first week, extending well beyond work tasks into helping navigate housing and daily logistics — a level of informal support they said no job offer had led them to expect.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two on onboarding alone, the honest advice is to prepare for opposite failure modes. In Germany, the risk is a technically excellent onboarding process that leaves you feeling procedurally supported but socially adrift — so invest deliberately in the relationships the paperwork won't build for you. In Vietnam, the risk is the reverse: a warm, relationally rich integration that can leave the formal expectations of your role somewhat undefined — so ask direct questions about scope and expectations even when the relational warmth makes it feel unnecessary. Neither country's system is incomplete; they're just optimized for different things. Find out which one you're walking into before your first day, not during it.
