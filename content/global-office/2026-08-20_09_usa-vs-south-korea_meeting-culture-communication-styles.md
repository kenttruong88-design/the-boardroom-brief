# Say It Now or Read the Room: Meetings in America and South Korea

**🇺🇸 USA · 🇰🇷 South Korea**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In an American meeting, silence reads as disengagement — 87% of successful international executives cite understanding American directness as the single most important factor in their US career success, according to research cited by Foothold America. In a Korean meeting, silence often reads as respect, and the real communication happens through nunchi, the culturally essential skill of reading a room without anyone saying much at all. Both are considered "professional communication." They are close to opposites.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇺🇸 USA

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Come to meetings prepared to speak up — silence can be read as disengagement or lack of confidence | Wait to be invited to contribute; participation is assumed, not offered |
| State disagreement directly but frame it constructively ("I see it differently because...") | Assume open debate over an idea is a personal or hierarchical challenge — it's normalized |
| Expect meetings to run on a clear agenda, start on time, and move toward action items | Expect extended relationship-building small talk before getting to the point — some exists, but briefly |
| Use first names and informal address, even with senior leadership, unless told otherwise | Assume informality signals low stakes — casual tone doesn't mean casual expectations |
| Follow up in writing with clear action items after a meeting | Leave a meeting assuming verbal agreement alone is sufficient follow-through |

#### 🇰🇷 South Korea

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Practice nunchi — read tone, hesitation, and group mood as seriously as the spoken content | Rely only on literal words to gauge whether an idea has actually been accepted |
| Raise disagreement privately, one-on-one, after the meeting rather than in the room | Contradict a senior colleague directly in front of the group, even if you're confident you're right |
| Learn and use appropriate honorifics and titles — they signal you understand your position | Address someone by first name or skip titles before you're explicitly invited to |
| Expect hoesik (after-work dinners) to be where real trust and rapport actually get built | Treat hoesik as fully optional; repeated absence can quietly affect how you're perceived |
| Let silence do work — a pause isn't a gap to be filled, it's often part of the communication | Rush to fill silence in a meeting; it can come across as impatient or status-blind |

### USA: Say the Thing

American meeting culture is built on the assumption that clarity is a form of respect. Cultural Atlas and Foothold America's guides both describe US business communication as concise, solution-oriented, and comfortable with direct, even confrontational, debate over ideas — a norm rooted in an individualist culture where separating the idea from the person defending it is considered basic professional competence. Meetings tend to be structured around agendas circulated in advance, with the expectation that attendees arrive ready to contribute rather than absorb.

The flip side, well documented across guides for international hires, is that this directness has its own unwritten rules that trip up newcomers just as often as Korean indirectness trips up Americans: silence genuinely gets read as a problem, not a courtesy, and disagreement expressed too gently can be mistaken for lack of conviction rather than diplomacy. American meetings reward getting to the point quickly — but "the point" still has to be delivered inside a specific register of confident, friendly, first-name-basis informality that isn't actually as casual as it sounds.

### South Korea: Read the Room, Then Speak Carefully

Korean meeting culture inverts nearly every one of those assumptions. Edstellar's 2026 guide to Korean work culture frames hierarchy and Confucian values — respect for elders, group harmony, proper social order — as the load-bearing structure underneath everyday office communication, and honorifics aren't a courtesy, they're functionally part of the grammar: word choice itself signals where you understand yourself to sit in the hierarchy. Direct contradiction of a senior colleague in front of others isn't seen as honest engagement, it's seen as social aggression, which is why disagreement in Korean offices routinely travels through private, one-on-one conversation rather than open floor debate.

Nunchi is the skill that makes this system work without everyone constantly stating their position aloud — it's described across multiple sources as a professional competency as real as any technical skill, the ability to sense what's needed, what's being avoided, and what a group's mood actually is without relying on explicit statements. And crucially, the real relationship infrastructure that makes daytime hierarchy bearable gets built elsewhere: hoesik, the after-hours team dinner, functions as the primary mechanism for forming the trust that formal meetings then quietly draw on.

### The Reckoning

The genuine irony is that both systems are trying to solve the same problem — how do you get accurate information and real buy-in out of a group — through opposite mechanisms. America tries to get the truth into the room; Korea tries to get the truth around the room, later, more carefully. An American who reads Korean silence as agreement will walk out of meetings with false confidence about what's actually been decided. A Korean employee who reads American directness as personal hostility will misjudge disagreement that was never meant to be anything but professional.

What actually breaks down cross-culturally isn't the information exchanged, it's the confidence people have in what they think they heard. American clarity can create false certainty about buy-in that hasn't actually formed. Korean indirectness can create false uncertainty about disagreement that's actually quite settled, just not stated aloud.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who moved to Seoul from an American company described the hardest professional skill to learn as literally waiting — the American instinct to fill a pause with a clarifying question turned out to be reading, in Korean meetings, as impatience with the group's process.*

> *r/korea — A foreign hire recounted contradicting a senior manager's proposal directly in an early team meeting, intending it as normal debate, and only later understanding through a colleague that the room had read it as a serious breach that took months of quiet, careful behavior to recover from.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — An engineer transferring from a Korean tech firm to a US one said the biggest shock wasn't the openness of American meetings but how much was expected of him personally — silence, which had always signaled respect back home, was actively read by his new American manager as a lack of preparation.*

> *Internations Seoul — A Western consultant noted that skipping hoesik twice in her first month, thinking it was purely social and optional, visibly cooled how forthcoming her Korean colleagues were with her afterward — a lesson no orientation document had made explicit.*

> *r/AskAnAmerican — One respondent, comparing notes with an international colleague, said the most useful reframe was realizing American "directness" isn't actually more honest than Korean indirectness — it's just honesty delivered on a different schedule, immediate versus deferred.*

### Conclusion

If you're heading into either meeting culture, the operating question is the same: where does the real decision actually get made — in the room, or after it? In America, assume it's in the room, and speak accordingly. In Korea, assume the room is a formality layered on top of decisions and disagreements already shaped in private, and adjust your reading of silence accordingly.

What I'd tell a friend before either move: don't grade the other culture's communication as less honest than your own. American bluntness and Korean restraint are both just local dialects of the same underlying question — can I trust what I think I just heard — and neither one hands you a reliable answer until you've learned to listen for what it's actually built to say.
