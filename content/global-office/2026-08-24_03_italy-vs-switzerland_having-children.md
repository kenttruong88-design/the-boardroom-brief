# Italy Gives You Five Months Off. Switzerland Gives You a Waitlist Number.

## 🇮🇹 Italy · 🇨🇭 Switzerland

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Italy, a new mother is legally entitled to five months of leave at 80 percent of her salary and, statistically, a solid chance she never fully returns to work. In Switzerland, she gets fourteen weeks, a daycare bill that can eat a quarter of household income, and, per the OECD, one of the highest female part-time employment rates in the bloc. Neither country has solved this. They have simply chosen different ways of turning parenthood into a career event.

#### 🇮🇹 Italy

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm INPS maternity payments and any employer top-up in writing before leave starts | Assume a national daycare place is waiting — asilo nido spots are scarce and vary by comune |
| Budget for grandparental help — one in two Italian families uses nonni for regular childcare | Expect this if you're an expat family with no relatives nearby |
| Ask explicitly about the six-month, 30-percent-pay optional parental leave and who should take it | Assume taking it goes unnoticed at review time — reintegration is rarely smooth |
| Negotiate flexible hours before the birth, not after | Expect your manager to offer part-time work unprompted |
| Start researching private or company-affiliated nidi during pregnancy | Wait until the baby arrives to begin waitlist paperwork |

#### 🇨🇭 Switzerland

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for a Kita place the moment you know you're pregnant — waitlists can run over a year | Wait until the third trimester and expect a spot the year you need it |
| Check your canton and employer for top-ups — the statutory 14 weeks at 80 percent is a floor | Assume federal law is generous — Switzerland trails most of the OECD on leave length |
| Budget CHF 1,500–2,500+ per month per child, and check eligibility for the new federal childcare allowance | Expect subsidized rates without applying — public-rate places are limited and means-tested |
| Discuss reduced hours openly — part-time work post-parenthood is the cultural default, not a stigma | Assume returning at 100 percent is the expectation without asking your specific employer |
| Use the two-week paternity leave and the newer 14-week shared parental pool deliberately | Assume fathers taking leave is invisible on a CV — attitudes vary sharply by industry |

### Italy

Italy's statutory offer looks generous on paper: five months of maternity leave at 80 percent pay, per the OECD Family Database, plus up to eleven months of shareable parental leave usable until the child turns twelve. The trouble isn't the leave — it's what happens after. According to Istat data cited by CEPR's VoxEU, the employment rate for women aged 25–49 without children sits around 76.6 percent, but drops to 55.5 percent for those with a child under six — a gap more than four times the EU average. Il Sole 24 Ore reports working mothers earn roughly 40 percent less than childless women even fifteen years after giving birth, with no sign of the gap closing. Public childcare spending lags most EU peers, so the shortfall is quietly outsourced to family: The Local Italy reports one in two Italian families relies on grandparents for regular childcare, a fifth doing it daily. Warm and low-cost, if you have Italian in-laws nearby. Not a solution if you're an expat who doesn't.

### Switzerland

Switzerland's statutory leave is thinner: 14 weeks of maternity leave at 80 percent pay (capped around CHF 196/day), two weeks of paternity leave since 2021, and a shared parental leave pool added in 2024. What Switzerland does instead is make daycare the default rather than family, and price it accordingly. The OECD found Swiss families spend up to 30 percent of income on childcare, more than double the EU average; SWI swissinfo.ch notes full-time nursery places in Geneva and Zurich can run 13–20 percent of household income versus 4–6 percent in neighboring countries. The predictable result, per the Swiss Federal Statistical Office: roughly 78 percent of working mothers are part-time, one of the highest rates in the OECD, while fathers largely remain full-time earners. A federal childcare allowance passed in December 2025 (at least CHF 100 per care day) is a first attempt to soften this, but the pattern — mother goes part-time, father does not — remains the norm.

### The Reckoning

The country with the more generous statutory leave has the worse long-term employment outcome for mothers, and the country with the thinner leave has better headline participation, just heavily part-time. Italy's five months look like a policy win until you notice it functions as an off-ramp — a third of mothers who leave the workforce cite rigid scheduling, not preference, as the reason. Switzerland never invites mothers to leave outright; it just prices full-time daycare so a "voluntary" move to 60 percent becomes the path of least resistance. Both outcomes are gendered, both are expensive, both are dressed up as choice.

Hofstede Insights scores Switzerland lower on Power Distance (34 versus Italy's 50) — a flatter, more egalitarian baseline culture — yet that egalitarianism hasn't reached the household division of labor; it shows up instead as bureaucratic evenhandedness rather than generosity. Italy's higher Power Distance shows up as family, not state, absorbing the gap: nonni fill in where the comune doesn't. The honest framing: Italy trades money for family proximity, Switzerland trades family proximity for money you may not have.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A commenter recounted that their fiancée had a job offer in Switzerland rescinded after she disclosed she was pregnant, once the employer had already sent the contract; the response noted this is illegal under Swiss labour protections, though enforcement after the fact is cold comfort once you've relocated your life around the offer.*

> *Quora — An American expat parent listed the genuine upsides of raising children in Switzerland: low crime, strong public healthcare, and firm legal protection against dismissal during pregnancy and for sixteen weeks after birth, concluding the trade-off is worth it despite the daycare bill.*

> *The Local Italy — A reader described relying on nonni as the default backup plan, then realizing mid-pregnancy that as a family who'd moved cities for work, that backup simply didn't exist, forcing a scramble for a nido spot with no waitlist head start.*

> *The Local Switzerland — A family in canton Lucerne described receiving an official letter stating there weren't enough daycare places for their child that year despite registering what they believed was early, and patching together a childminder and reduced hours instead.*

> *Quora — Someone who'd worked in both Milan and Zurich advised that the real planning question isn't which country pays better maternity leave, but who in the household is willing to go part-time for a decade — because one of you probably will, whichever country you pick.*

### Conclusion

If proximity to grandparents, a longer paid leave block, and lower cash burn matter more than two full-time incomes, Italy's system plays to those strengths, provided you have family nearby or budget for private nido fees early. If you'd rather pay Swiss prices for Swiss infrastructure and a legal system that protects you on paper during pregnancy, Switzerland is the better bet, as long as you register for daycare before you've told your own parents you're expecting. Neither country has cracked full-time dual-career parenthood; they've just built two different-shaped exits from it, and pretending otherwise is the only mistake actually worth avoiding.
