# The Whole Country Goes Quiet vs. The Calendar That Moves: Leave in Croatia and Jordan

**🇭🇷 Croatia · 🇯🇴 Jordan**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Croatia, don't bother emailing company management in August — the entire country, by informal but near-universal consensus, is at the coast. In Jordan, the calendar itself moves: Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha shift roughly eleven days earlier every solar year, so the paid holidays that structure your working life this year land in a completely different month a few years from now. Both countries guarantee real time off. Neither guarantees it will look anything like what you're used to scheduling around.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇭🇷 Croatia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect the statutory 20 days (4 weeks) as a floor, not a generous perk | Assume you can negotiate this down; it's a legal minimum |
| Plan around August as effectively a national office slowdown | Schedule critical deliverables or client decisions in August |
| Use unused leave before June 30 of the following year | Assume carryover is indefinite |
| Ask early about company-wide closure dates | Wait until July to plan your own summer leave |
| Expect vacation leave eligibility to kick in after 6 months | Assume day-one full entitlement at a new job |

#### 🇯🇴 Jordan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Track Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha dates fresh each year — they shift on the Gregorian calendar | Assume public holidays land on the same dates as last year |
| Expect Friday (sometimes Friday–Saturday) as the weekend, not Saturday–Sunday | Schedule Western-style weekend plans assuming Saturday off |
| Ask about Ramadan-specific hours if you're there during it | Assume standard office hours apply unchanged during Ramadan |
| Know your leave scales with tenure — 14 days rising to 21 after 5 years | Expect the higher entitlement on arrival |
| Budget extra patience for Ramadan-adjacent scheduling shifts | Plan tight, back-to-back meetings across a Ramadan month |

---

Croatia's leave framework is straightforward on paper: as an EU member, it guarantees the bloc's floor of four weeks (20 working days) of paid annual leave, with eligibility kicking in after six months of continuous employment, per CXC Global's compliance guide. What the statute doesn't capture is the informal but powerful cultural overlay: Croatia's own business-culture resources note plainly that it's "frequently difficult to reach company management" during the July–August stretch, when the Adriatic tourist season and the national vacation instinct converge almost completely. This isn't staggered PTO usage the way many workplaces manage it — it functions closer to a de facto national shutdown, meaning a newcomer's real scheduling risk isn't the 20-day entitlement itself, but assuming business continues at a normal pace around it.

Jordan's leave structure is built on a different axis entirely: religious and lunar-calendar holidays layered onto a modest statutory baseline. CXC Global's guide puts standard annual leave at 14 days, rising to 21 after five consecutive years with the same employer — notably below Croatia's floor — but supplements it with 15 to 17 paid national and religious holidays a year, including Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha, both tied to the Islamic lunar calendar and therefore drifting roughly 10–11 days earlier on the Gregorian calendar annually. Playroll's 2026 compliance guide is a useful reminder that these dates simply cannot be assumed from a prior year's calendar and must be re-checked annually. Layered on top is the practical reality of Ramadan, during which — per multiple accounts — office environments with air conditioning make fasting considerably more manageable than manual labor, but hours and pace still shift, and a newcomer's calendar needs real flexibility built in in the corresponding month, whenever it happens to fall.

### The Reckoning

The counterintuitive divergence is which country's leave calendar is actually more predictable a decade out. Croatia's is functionally fixed — the same 20 days, the same August slowdown, year after year, tied to the fixed Gregorian summer. Jordan's headline number of paid days off is actually higher once you count religious holidays, but the calendar itself is a moving target, requiring active tracking rather than institutional memory. A Western hire used to fixed-date holidays (Christmas is always December 25) can coast on assumption in Croatia and will get burned doing the same thing in Jordan, where "the same holiday as last year" is a category error, not just an approximation.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com Amman forum — A contributor discussing working and living in Amman noted that adapting to an Arab country's rhythms — including the Friday weekend and the shifting religious calendar — was harder than expected for someone coming from a culture with fixed, Western-dated holidays, describing it as a mental recalibration that took the better part of a year.*

> *Quora — A response on Ramadan's effect on work performance noted that office-based roles with air conditioning made fasting considerably more manageable than physical or outdoor jobs, but still recommended that colleagues and managers build in real flexibility around the month rather than expecting unchanged productivity.*

> *Expat In Croatia (relocation resource, aggregated contributor notes) — A recurring theme in materials aimed at newcomers was that the informal August shutdown catches people off guard specifically because it isn't written into any policy — you simply discover, mid-project, that nobody with decision-making authority is reachable until September.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a question about accommodations employers should provide during Ramadan suggested shifted start times and shorter core hours as standard practice in Gulf and Levant offices, framing it less as an accommodation and more as the expected default for that month.*

> *Expat.com Amman forum — Another thread on finding work in Jordan noted that most expats end up in teaching or international-organization roles (UN agencies, embassies) rather than the local private sector, which indirectly shapes whose leave calendar you're actually operating under — an NGO calendar often blends Western holidays with local ones, doubling the number of observed days off compared to a purely local employer.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Croatia, plan your own major life admin — and your expectations of anyone else's responsiveness — around a hard August blackout, and use your leave before the June 30 carryover deadline rather than banking it. If you're moving to Jordan, build in a habit of checking the Islamic calendar fresh every year rather than trusting your memory of last year's dates, and expect Ramadan to reshape your month rather than merely decorate it. The honest thing I'd tell a friend over a drink: Croatia's leave is generous and utterly predictable, Jordan's is trickier to track but arguably richer once religious holidays are counted — plan for the shape of each, not the number alone.
