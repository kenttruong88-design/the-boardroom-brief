# The Nursery Gap: Saudi Arabia Legislates Its Way to Family-Friendly, Qatar Just Assumes You'll Manage

🇸🇦 Saudi Arabia · 🇶🇦 Qatar

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Two neighboring petrostates, two very different theories of what a newborn is worth in paid time off. Saudi Arabia spent 2025 rewriting its labor law to hand new mothers twelve weeks of leave and new fathers a legally guaranteed three days, funded not by employers but by the state insurance fund. Qatar, meanwhile, has left its private-sector maternity entitlement exactly where it has sat for years — fifty days, no paternity requirement at all — and is relying on employer goodwill to fill the gap. Neither country would describe itself as behind on family policy. Only one of them recently rewrote the law to make sure it wasn't.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇦 Saudi Arabia
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm GOSI (not your employer) is processing your maternity pay | Assume full pay if you've worked under a year — it drops to half |
| Book paternity leave within seven days of the birth | Wait past that window — the three days lapse if unused |
| Choose a private hospital if you want your spouse in the delivery room | Expect that at a government hospital — men aren't admitted to the maternity ward |
| Ask HR whether your workplace nursery obligation applies (50+ female staff triggers it) | Assume a small employer has any childcare obligation at all |
| Register the birth at the hospital immediately | Assume the baby gets dual citizenship unless the father is Saudi |
| Ask about the Qurrah childcare subsidy if you're a Saudi national | Count on it if you're a non-Saudi expat — it's aimed at citizens |

#### 🇶🇦 Qatar
| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read the paternity clause in your contract line by line | Assume the law guarantees any paternity leave — it's purely employer discretion |
| Plan around fifty days of maternity leave, not fifty weeks | Compare it out loud to your home country's policy in the office |
| Start touring nurseries in month two of pregnancy | Wait until after the birth — decent waitlists run for months |
| Get the medical certificate paperwork sorted early if you might need the unpaid extension | Assume the extra sixty days are automatic — they require proof you can't yet return |
| Ask about the paid one-hour daily nursing break | Assume it continues indefinitely — it's time-capped |
| Compare public- and private-sector offers before signing | Assume private-sector parity with government roles — public sector gets roughly three months |

## Saudi Arabia: The Reform Sprint

Saudi Arabia's parental leave regime has moved further, faster, than almost anywhere else in the Gulf. Under Royal Decree M/44, effective February 2025, maternity leave rose from ten weeks to twelve, with six weeks mandatory after birth, and the bill now goes to the General Organization for Social Insurance rather than the employer — a shift that, not coincidentally, removes an employer's financial incentive to avoid hiring women of childbearing age. Paternity leave, previously nonexistent in law, is now three paid days under Article 113, to be taken within a week of the birth.

The reforms sit inside a bigger project. According to Vision 2030 planning documents and coverage of the Qurrah Programme, the government is subsidizing childcare specifically to keep the cost of a nanny or nursery from eating a working mother's paycheck, and the Council of Ministers has directed authorities to expand workplace nurseries, with any employer of fifty or more women now obligated to provide one. The World Bank's female labor force participation data for Saudi Arabia shows the payoff: the female participation rate has climbed sharply over the past decade, a trend the ILO's June 2025 Care Economy Brief credits partly to exactly this kind of maternity-protection reform. It is, in effect, industrial policy delivered through a maternity ward.

## Qatar: The Quiet Status Quo

Qatar's approach looks, by comparison, almost nostalgic. Fifty days of paid maternity leave — a figure unchanged in years — covers time before and after delivery, with at least thirty-five days required post-birth, and applies only after a full year of continuous service. Paternity leave has no legal basis whatsoever; the three-to-five days most companies grant exist purely because HR departments have decided it's good practice, not because the Labour Law requires it. Public-sector mothers do considerably better, often receiving three months fully paid, which means the country runs two distinct parental-leave regimes depending on who signs your paycheck.

Childcare is a private transaction. Nurseries in Doha run from roughly QAR 2,000 to QAR 5,500 a month depending on hours and curriculum, and subsidized options are, per multiple guides, reserved for Qatari nationals. With female labor force participation in Qatar sitting around 30 to 35 percent — among the lower figures in the region, per labor market analysis — the absence of a childcare subsidy or a real parental leave floor is arguably not a footnote but a structural explanation. The ILO's framing, that maternity protection is a prerequisite for sustained female employment rather than a perk layered on top of it, reads almost like a diagnosis of Qatar's private-sector numbers specifically.

## The Reckoning

Put the two countries side by side and the more theocratically conservative one comes out, on paper, as the more generous employer. Saudi Arabia — a Hofstede Insights outlier at 95 on power distance, a society built around deference to authority — used exactly that top-down authority to mandate better leave by decree. Qatar, which markets itself internationally as the Gulf's cosmopolitan financial hub, has quietly let its statutory maternity leave lag behind Riyadh's by more than a month and skipped paternity leave altogether. The reform came from the country with the more rigid hierarchy; the stasis persists in the country that likes to be photographed as the region's most modern.

The practical consequence is that a mother's experience in these two countries now depends less on the country's overall reputation and more on the fine print of who employs her. A Saudi government-adjacent employer in 2026 offers meaningfully more paid time and a state-funded safety net; a private-sector Qatari employer offers fifty days and whatever paternity leave HR feels like extending. Neither country, notably, has solved the deeper problem the OECD keeps flagging elsewhere: leave policy only moves the needle on women's careers if returning to work doesn't quietly cost them the promotion track anyway.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering "what's the hardest thing about raising children in Saudi Arabia," one respondent noted the experience splits sharply by gender: manageable, even comfortable, for fathers, but genuinely difficult for mothers navigating logistics designed around a male default.*

> *Doha News — A British expat mother named Noor described the fifty-day Qatar maternity leave as "shocking" once she compared it to the UK's fifty-two weeks, and said the gap forced her to consider leaving her job entirely rather than returning before she or the baby were ready.*

> *Expat.com forum — A thread on giving birth in Saudi Arabia surfaced a genuine surprise: private hospital maternity care was described as more straightforward than expected, with online paperwork and English-speaking staff, though the poster noted government hospitals bar men from the maternity ward entirely, catching several new fathers off guard.*

> *ExpatWoman.com — A Qatar forum post asking how to sponsor a nanny, or find a nanny-share arrangement, underscored how much of Doha's childcare system runs on expat word-of-mouth rather than any formal placement service — new parents are largely left to solve it themselves.*

> *sarajaynesays.com — A pregnant expat blogging from Saudi Arabia wrote about learning, mid-pregnancy, that basic prenatal logistics she'd taken for granted at home — like a spouse present at every appointment — required active research into which hospitals actually allowed it.*

### Conclusion

The practical advice is almost embarrassingly simple: read your specific contract, not the country's reputation. In Saudi Arabia, the law itself has become the more generous party, thanks to a 2025 overhaul that shifted cost and obligation onto the state; in Qatar, the law is a floor, and everything above fifty days and zero mandated paternity leave is a function of which employer you happened to sign with. Both countries are chasing the same outcome — more women staying in the workforce after having children — but only one of them has recently admitted, in statute, that the previous arrangement wasn't working.

For the expat calculating where to have a baby: bring your own childcare budget either way, and check your maternity ward's visitor policy before you check the size of your signing bonus.
