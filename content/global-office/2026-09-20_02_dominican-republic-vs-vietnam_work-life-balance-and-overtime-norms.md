# The Nap Desk and the Four-by-Four: Overtime in Hanoi vs. Santo Domingo

**🇩🇴 Dominican Republic · 🇻🇳 Vietnam**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Vietnam has legally enshrined a napping culture into its workday — a 15-to-30-minute midday rest, taken at the desk or on a folding cot, that no one treats as a sign of low commitment. The Dominican Republic, meanwhile, has a labor code capping the workweek at 44 hours and a documented practice, per the U.S. State Department's own human rights reporting, of factories running mandatory "four-by-four" 12-hour shifts anyway, enforced through the threat of lost pay. Two very different relationships between what the law says and what actually happens at 6 p.m.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇩🇴 Dominican Republic

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm your actual weekly hours in writing before accepting a role, especially in manufacturing or free-trade-zone jobs | Assume the legal 44-hour cap reflects what you'll actually be scheduled |
| Expect "Dominican time" — flexible starts, long lunches — but firm deadlines regardless | Mistake relaxed scheduling for relaxed expectations on output |
| Ask directly about overtime pay structure (135% up to 68 hrs/week, 200% beyond) | Work unpaid overtime assuming it will be "made up" informally |
| Build in buffer time for bureaucracy and scheduling changes | Plan tightly-timed personal commitments around a promised end time |
| Take the big midday meal seriously as a real break | Eat at your desk through the traditional noon meal window — it reads as antisocial |

#### 🇻🇳 Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Take the midday nap if colleagues do — it's normalized, not lazy | Schedule calls or send urgent emails during the 12-1pm rest window |
| Clarify overtime caps (12 hrs/day, 40/month, 200/year) if you're in manufacturing, textiles, or electronics | Assume white-collar Hanoi hours apply to factory or seasonal-order roles |
| Expect after-hours messages to be unwelcome outside of true emergencies | Message your Vietnamese team casually at 9pm expecting a reply |
| Ask specifically about your sector — tech and hospitality skew long hours | Generalize from one friend's laid-back teaching job to the whole labor market |
| Respect the 8am start; punctuality signals respect for hierarchy | Treat flexible-seeming norms elsewhere as license to arrive late |

---

On paper, the Dominican Republic looks like the more protected worker: a 44-hour standard week, a legal cap of 80 overtime hours per quarter, and overtime pay that jumps to 200% once someone crosses 68 hours in a week. In practice, the U.S. State Department's own labor rights assessment is blunt that authorities "did not adequately enforce" wage, hours, and safety standards, and that mandatory overtime was common in factories, with the Dominican Federation of Free Trade Zone Workers documenting the "four-by-four" pattern — 12-hour shifts stacked back to back — as a real, ongoing practice rather than a legal loophole. The gap between statute and shop floor is the single most important thing a prospective hire needs to understand: the law is genuinely worker-protective, but enforcement is where it breaks down, particularly in export manufacturing.

Vietnam's overtime regime is more tightly specified and, per ILO documentation, more actively enforced at the margins — a standard 48-hour week, overtime capped at 200 hours annually (extended to 300 for seasonal industries like textiles and electronics), and a tiered premium structure that pays out 50% extra on regular days, 100% on weekends, and 200% on holidays. Manpower Vietnam's own workplace guides describe office hours as a fairly conventional 8-to-5, but note that "long working hours are common in sectors such as technology, manufacturing, and hospitality despite the official legal limits" — an admission from an employment services firm that the same enforcement gap the Dominican Republic has is present here too, just concentrated in different industries. What Vietnam adds that the Dominican Republic doesn't is a genuinely codified rest culture: the midday nap isn't a personal quirk, it's an institution, socially compulsory in the opposite direction — skipping it can read as odd, not virtuous.

### The Reckoning

The ironic divergence is which country's stated culture more accurately predicts your actual week. In the Dominican Republic, "Dominican time" — a real, widely acknowledged flexibility around punctuality and scheduling — creates an expectation of a relaxed pace that can be almost entirely absent once you're inside an export-manufacturing shift schedule; the vibe and the shift board frequently disagree. In Vietnam, the opposite pattern holds: the formal culture (hierarchy, punctuality, an 8am start) predicts a genuinely more disciplined day-to-day rhythm for white-collar roles in Hanoi or Ho Chi Minh City, even as tech and hospitality quietly run their own unofficial 996-adjacent hours. The Dominican Republic is also mid-pilot on a genuinely countercultural experiment: a voluntary four-day, 36-hour workweek trial for public and private employers, full pay retained — a policy direction almost nobody would predict from the "employers regularly abuse employees" reporting on its factory sector, and worth watching as a signal of where the country's white-collar and public-sector norms may be heading even as manufacturing lags behind.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com Dominican Republic forum — A contributor adapting to local pace wrote that "Dominican time is real and must be respected," but warned this cuts only one way: colleagues and vendors run late constantly, while your own deadlines are still due on time, regardless of how loosely the day around them is structured.*

> *Quora — Someone who taught English in Vietnam described "a decent balance between work and life," but flagged that this was specific to teaching — friends in manufacturing and tech reported a completely different, far more demanding schedule than the one they personally experienced.*

> *Expat.com Dominican Republic forum — Another poster working locally (rather than remotely or retired) pushed back on the island's laid-back reputation directly, writing that the relaxed pace applies mainly to retirees and tourists, and that anyone actually employed there should expect a demanding, non-negotiable workload.*

> *TeamBlind — In discussions of extended-hours "996-style" schedules spreading from Chinese tech firms into other Asian tech hubs, several engineers noted that the practice gets rebranded as a "growth culture" expectation at fast-moving startups rather than framed as overtime at all — a linguistic sleight of hand that makes it harder to push back on than a clearly labeled long shift.*

> *Manpower Vietnam workplace guide (secondary aggregated commentary) — A recurring observation in employer-side guidance is that younger professionals in Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City increasingly negotiate explicitly for work-life balance during hiring, a shift employers describe as generational rather than sector-wide, meaning the same job title can carry very different real hours depending on who negotiated the offer.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway is to distrust the vibe in both directions. In the Dominican Republic, don't let "Dominican time" convince you the job itself will be relaxed — ask specifically about your sector's real overtime pattern, especially if it's manufacturing, and get the pay structure for hours 44 through 68 confirmed in writing. In Vietnam, don't let the formal 8-to-5 structure convince you the stated hours are the real hours — ask current employees in your specific function, not just HR, since tech and hospitality quietly run on a different clock than the labor code implies. The honest line I'd give a friend: in both places the law is a reasonable starting document and a poor predictor of your actual Tuesday — budget your expectations around your industry, not the country's reputation.
