# Blend In or Cover Up: Dress Codes in France and Thailand

**🇫🇷 France · 🇹🇭 Thailand**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The French dress to disappear into a crowd of equally well-put-together strangers; the goal is polish so consistent it stops registering as effort. Thais dress, in the office at least, to signal respect for the room rather than for the crowd — modesty as a form of consideration rather than a fashion statement. Both cultures will silently judge a foreigner in gym shorts. Neither will tell you why.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest in a small number of well-tailored, high-quality pieces in dark or muted colors rather than a large wardrobe of cheaper items | Don't wear sportswear, yoga pants, or running shoes outside an actual gym or exercise context — it will be visibly, if silently, judged |
| Dress with the same care for a grocery run that you'd bring to a casual work meeting — the French "effortless" look is, per multiple guides, an artful effort, not an absence of one | Don't expect "Casual Friday" — it's not a widely recognized concept in French offices, and dressing down can read as a lack of professionalism |
| Keep accessories, makeup, and hairstyles more subtle and refined than flashy or logo-heavy | Don't wear anything overtly branded or logo-forward; French style leans toward understatement over visible-label status signaling |
| Aim to blend in rather than stand out — polish that doesn't call attention to itself is the actual goal, not a compromise | Don't assume formal business attire is reserved for client-facing roles only; conservative dress is the default across most French offices regardless of role |

#### 🇹🇭 Thailand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Cover shoulders and knees as standard office dress for both men and women, regardless of how hot it is outside | Don't wear sleeveless tops, shorts, or visibly revealing clothing to work, even in casual industries — modesty is close to a universal professional norm |
| Expect and match a genuinely conservative baseline: suits for men, modest dresses, blouses, or skirts for women in most traditional offices | Don't show up to a meeting in shorts even after decades in-country; one long-term expat noted never once seeing a Thai colleague do so |
| Adjust your expectations by setting — Bangkok's fashion-forward industries allow bolder choices, while rural offices and traditional sectors (banking, education) expect stricter conservatism | Don't assume Bangkok's cosmopolitan, runway-adjacent reputation extends to every workplace; regional and sectoral variation is significant |
| Lean into sun-conscious, modest dressing (light fabrics, longer hemlines) as both culturally appropriate and practically comfortable | Don't treat visible sun protection as purely a personal-comfort choice; it also happens to align with what's culturally expected, which is a useful overlap to know about |

### France: Discipline Disguised as Effortlessness

French dress culture runs on a paradox that trips up most newcomers: the look is supposed to appear unstudied while actually being the product of real discipline. CIEE's essay on the subject frames it as the central question outright — "effortless or an artful effort?" — and comes down firmly on the latter. TalkInFrench's business etiquette guide describes French professional dress as "cautious" and "highly formal," extending the same standard to correspondence and everyday interactions that most cultures reserve for client meetings.

What distinguishes France from many "formal dress code" cultures is how far down the formality extends into ordinary daily life. GaminTraveler's piece on dress-code culture shock notes an unspoken expectation of effort even for basic errands — sweatpants avoided in public, logos avoided generally, a baseline of polish that applies to "servers, students, business workers, and even children." For an American or Brit used to reserving effort for the office and relaxing everywhere else, the adjustment isn't really about the office at all — it's about recalibrating what "casual" means across the entire week.

### Thailand: Modesty as Professional Respect

Thai dress culture operates from a different first principle entirely. Where French formality signals taste and social calibration, Thai workplace modesty — shoulders and knees covered, conservative cuts, suits for men — signals respect, both for the workplace itself and, per the Thailand Foundation's description of traditional dress values, an inherited emphasis on "grace, modesty, and elegance" that long predates any given company's HR policy. Zafigo's guide for businesswomen in Thailand confirms the practical baseline is genuinely conservative by international standards: sleeveless tops and shorts are broadly discouraged regardless of the heat, which is a real ask in a tropical climate.

The regional variation matters here more than in France. Bangkok's fashion-forward industries allow for bolder, runway-adjacent choices among the young and creative-sector workforce, while banks, schools, and government-adjacent offices — along with virtually all rural and small-town workplaces — hold to a stricter standard. A foreigner who calibrates their wardrobe once, based on what they see on a Bangkok street, and applies it uniformly across the country will misjudge more conservative settings badly.

### The Reckoning

Both cultures reject the Western-casual instinct that comfort should generally win, but for opposite underlying reasons. France's formality is aesthetic and social — dressing well is a form of self-respect and social participation, disconnected from any particular moral or religious framing. Thailand's formality is more explicitly about propriety and respect for others and for the setting, with modesty functioning as a form of consideration rather than a style statement.

The genuinely ironic overlap: both cultures would look at a foreigner in gym shorts and judge them identically, for almost entirely different reasons — France reading it as a lapse in taste and self-presentation, Thailand reading it as a lapse in respect for the room. The remedy, conveniently, is the same either way: default to covered, tailored, understated, and adjust up in formality rather than down whenever you're unsure.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering "How do French people dress?", one contributor summarized the unwritten rule as dressing specifically to blend into a crowd rather than stand out from it — a philosophy they said took real unlearning for anyone arriving from a culture where personal style is meant to be individually expressive rather than quietly harmonized.*

> *Quora — A response to "What is the work culture in France like?" from an American contributor described being surprised not by clothing specifically but by the broader formality it was embedded in — noting that everyone in the office started each day shaking hands individually with coworkers, a ritual of formality that extended naturally from how people dressed to how people greeted each other.*

> *Expat.com, Bangkok forum ("Business etiquette in Thailand") — A long-term contributor with roughly 20 years in Thai industry and commerce said they had never once seen a Thai colleague wear shorts to a meeting in that entire span, regardless of heat, industry, or seniority — offered as the single most reliable rule of thumb for newcomers unsure how seriously to take the modesty guidance.*

> *Expat.com, Bangkok work-environment guide ("Understanding the work environment in Bangkok") — The guide's contributors flagged that newcomers frequently miscalibrate by assuming Bangkok's fashionable public image extends into every office, only to be caught underdressed — or over-casually dressed — at more traditional firms in banking, education, or government-adjacent sectors.*

### Conclusion

If you're packing for either posting, the safe move is the same: buy fewer, better pieces, skew conservative, and watch what your most senior local colleague wears before calibrating your own choices. In France, that means quiet, tailored, unbranded polish extended into your weekends, not just your workweek. In Thailand, it means covered shoulders and knees as a baseline you don't relax just because the thermometer says you should. Neither culture is asking you to perform their aesthetic — they're asking you to demonstrate, through the small daily discipline of getting dressed, that you understand what kind of respect the room requires. Get that right, and the rest of the wardrobe sorts itself out.
