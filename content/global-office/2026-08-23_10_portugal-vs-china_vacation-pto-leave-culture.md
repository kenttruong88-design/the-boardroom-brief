# Twenty-Two Days of Sun vs. Nine Days of Silence: What "Vacation" Means in Lisbon and Shenzhen

🇵🇹 Portugal · 🇨🇳 China

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Portugal, the entire country agrees, without ever holding a meeting about it, that August does not really happen. In China, a worker with one year of tenure is legally entitled to five paid vacation days — fewer than the number of days Lisbon spends closed for lunch in a single month. Both countries call this arrangement "annual leave." Only one of them means it.

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|

| Plan your own trip around the 13 public holidays, several falling midweek | Show up to close a deal in the first two weeks of September; everyone is still recovering |

### Portugal: The Republic of August

Portugal's labour code guarantees 22 working days of paid annual leave, on top of 13 public holidays, putting the country's statutory floor among the more generous in the OECD, alongside Austria and Spain (OECD). Eurostat's most recent figures put Portuguese full-time employees at roughly 41 hours of weekly work when they are, in fact, working, and around 1,631 hours across the year — a number that only makes sense once you account for how completely certain months disappear from the calendar (Eurostat). August is the tell: small businesses, notaries, and entire branches of the civil service close for weeks at a stretch, an informal national understanding that predates any statute and that no employer has ever successfully legislated around.

What makes Portugal's leave culture distinct isn't the day count — several EU peers match or beat it — but the total absence of guilt attached to using it. A 2021 labour law formalized the right to disconnect, making it illegal for employers to routinely contact staff outside working hours, which converts what is aspirational policy in most countries into enforceable practice here. The long midday lunch, often one to two hours, functions as a daily miniature of the same principle: work expands to fill the time allotted to it, and the Portuguese have simply allotted it less generously than most.

### China: Leave on Paper, 996 in Practice

China's Employee Paid Annual Leave Regulations set entitlements by cumulative career tenure rather than time at a given employer: five days after one year of total work experience, ten after ten years, fifteen after twenty (China National Bureau of Statistics; SCMP). On paper this looks modest but functional. In practice, the China National Bureau of Statistics has clocked average weekly working hours for enterprise employees at roughly 48 to 49 hours through 2024 and into 2025 — well above the 44-hour statutory ceiling — a gap that regulators have repeatedly acknowledged without closing (China NBS).

The '996' schedule — 9am to 9pm, six days a week — became shorthand for tech-sector overwork culture, and China's Supreme People's Court has since ruled it unlawful without additional compensation, a finding covered extensively by the South China Morning Post. Yet compliance is uneven, and Bloomberg's reporting on the domestic labour market describes an environment where economic insecurity makes workers reluctant to test the limits of an entitlement that exists more robustly in statute than in office culture. Public holidays — a genuine strength, with seven-day blocks for Chinese New Year and National Day — do the load-bearing work that annual leave is supposed to do elsewhere, concentrating rest into a few loud, crowded weeks rather than spreading it across the year.

### The Reckoning

Put the two systems side by side and the paradox sharpens: Portugal, with the smaller economy and the lower average wage, guarantees more actual rest than China does, and — more importantly — enforces the guarantee culturally as well as legally. China's leave entitlement looks reasonable in the statute book and evaporates on contact with a results-oriented, hierarchical corporate culture; Hofstede Insights scores China at 80 on power distance versus Portugal's 63, a gap that tracks fairly well with how comfortable an employee feels telling a manager they're taking Friday off (Hofstede Insights).

The counterintuitive twist is that China's public holidays, taken as a bloc, can outperform Portugal's spread-out leave for sheer volume of consecutive days off — Golden Week alone rivals a decent chunk of a Portuguese worker's annual allowance. What China does not offer is the quiet, unremarkable, un-guilty version of rest that defines the Portuguese August: a stretch of ordinary weeks where nothing is expected of you and nobody is impressed that you took it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A contributor working in a Chinese tech firm described leave entitlement as "five days on the contract, zero days anyone actually takes," noting that requesting time off outside Golden Week or Chinese New Year is technically approved but socially read as a lack of seriousness.*

> *Quora — A commenter pointed out that annual leave in China accrues by total career tenure, not by years at your current company, which surprises job-hoppers who assume — wrongly — that changing employers resets the clock back to zero.*

> *Teamblind (workingfromchina channel) — A poster advised submitting leave requests weeks in advance and pairing them with a clear, low-stakes justification, since ambiguous requests tend to get quietly deprioritized rather than denied outright.*

> *Portugal Handbook (expat blog) — A newcomer recalled an out-of-office reply reading "back in a few days" that turned out to mean three weeks, and learned the hard way that nothing involving a signature gets done in Portugal between late July and early September.*

> *lovefromportugal (Substack) — A longtime resident described the August shutdown as something you stop fighting after the first year, rescheduling any bureaucratic errand, business deal, or home repair to June or October rather than expecting movement in between.*

### Conclusion

If your priority is a leave policy you can actually rely on without negotiating it against your manager's opinion of your commitment, Portugal's combination of a generous statutory minimum and a culture that enforces it — right down to a legally protected inbox — is the safer bet, even if the trade-off is a slower-moving economy and a September that starts late. If you're weighing China, go in clear-eyed: the entitlement on your contract and the leave you will actually be able to take without professional cost are two different numbers, and the gap between them is filled, unevenly, by a handful of loud national holidays. Read the contract, but budget your year around the culture, not the clause.
