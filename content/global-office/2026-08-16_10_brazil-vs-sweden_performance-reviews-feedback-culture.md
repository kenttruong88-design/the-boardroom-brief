# "We Can Try" Means No in São Paulo. In Stockholm, They'll Just Tell You It's a Bad Idea — Gently, and With Your Consent.

**🇧🇷 Brazil · 🇸🇪 Sweden**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Brazilian offices, "We can try" typically signals obstacles the speaker has already spotted and would rather not name directly, "That's an interesting idea" often means polite disagreement, and "I'll do my best" can quietly mean the deadline is unrealistic — all documented patterns in cross-cultural feedback research, none of them stated as bluntly as they're meant. In Sweden, performance conversations happen regularly, not just annually, feedback is delivered privately but genuinely directly, and managers are trained to treat "constructive but honest" as a single, non-negotiable standard rather than a trade-off. Both countries think of themselves as considerate. Only one of them thinks considerate and vague are the same thing.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇷 Brazil

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn to decode softened phrases — "we can try," "interesting idea," "I'll do my best" all carry real, specific subtext | Take these phrases at face value and assume enthusiastic agreement — you'll miss the actual message being sent |
| Deliver criticism privately, warmly, and with visible relational investment first | Give direct, blunt criticism in front of others, even when well-intentioned — it costs more trust than the feedback is worth |
| Lead with positive framing, even in a formal review | Assume positive-heavy feedback means there's nothing to improve — Brazil emphasizes encouragement, but the constructive content is still there, just wrapped differently |
| Build the relationship before you need to deliver hard feedback within it | Treat performance reviews as a purely procedural, arms-length event — relational trust genuinely shapes how the content lands |
| Ask a trusted local colleague to help you read between the lines on a vague response | Escalate a vague "we'll see" as if it were a firm commitment — it frequently isn't one |

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect regular, ongoing feedback conversations, not just an annual review | Wait for a scheduled review to raise a performance concern — Swedish managers are expected to address issues as they arise |
| Take direct feedback as respect, not hostility — Swedish managers deliver honesty as a form of care | Read Swedish directness as coldness; it's paired with genuine collaboration and rarely delivered without context or support |
| Expect feedback delivered privately, even though it's direct | Assume directness means public callouts — open confrontation in front of the team remains rare and is actively avoided |
| Come prepared to discuss goals, workload, and development as a two-way conversation | Treat the review as one-directional — Swedish performance dialogues are explicitly framed as mutual, not top-down |
| Ask directly for more feedback if you want it — Swedish modesty can under-deliver praise by some cultural standards | Expect frequent unprompted praise — the culture favors honest, calibrated feedback over effusive positive reinforcement |

### The Republic of the Encouraging Euphemism

Brazilian feedback culture sits in an interesting middle position — genuinely more direct than the most indirect global cultures like Indonesia or Japan, but still high-context enough that a substantial amount of real information travels through softened, coded language rather than plain statements. The throughline across every account of Brazilian workplace communication is that relational investment drives professional outcomes: feedback lands differently, and honestly gets processed differently, depending on how much trust already exists between the people exchanging it, which is precisely why Brazilian managers tend to front-load positive framing — not to obscure the substance, but because the relationship has to absorb the criticism, and a relationship in good standing absorbs it far better.

The practical challenge for a foreign manager or employee is that the vocabulary of Brazilian feedback isn't ambiguous by accident — it's a functioning code, and once you learn to read "we can try" as "I see problems I'm not going to name yet" and "that's interesting" as "I disagree, carefully," Brazilian feedback stops looking vague and starts looking precise, just precise in a register that assumes the listener is paying close attention to more than the literal words.

### The Kingdom of the Scheduled Honesty

Sweden's feedback culture runs almost as a mirror inversion: direct, but delivered inside a structure specifically designed to protect the relationship anyway. Swedish workplace guidance is explicit that harsh, blunt criticism delivered in public is genuinely rare — feedback comes privately and gently in form even when its content is completely unambiguous, which lets Swedish managers maintain both honesty and harmony rather than treating them as a trade-off the way many other cultures implicitly do. Performance conversations aren't confined to an annual event either; regular, ongoing dialogue about goals, workload, and development is the expected baseline, framed explicitly as a two-way exchange rather than a top-down evaluation handed downward.

The flat, consensus-oriented hierarchy that defines Swedish management more broadly shapes this directly — managers are expected to act as facilitators rather than top-down judges, and the entire performance conversation is built on trust and equality rather than authority. The result is feedback that is unmistakably clear in content, since Swedish workplace norms simply don't route substantive information through euphemism the way Brazilian ones often do, but is nonetheless carefully, even gently, delivered in tone — honest without ever needing to be harsh to prove it's honest.

### The Reckoning

Both cultures have concluded that blunt, public criticism is bad management — they've just built opposite solutions to the same problem. Brazil protects the relationship by softening the content itself, encoding real information into gentler phrases that a well-calibrated listener learns to decode. Sweden protects the relationship by keeping the content fully direct but moving the delivery entirely into private, low-stakes, frequently-recurring conversations, so no single moment of honesty ever has to carry the full emotional weight of an annual reckoning.

The trap for someone moving between them is almost perfectly symmetrical. A Swedish manager relocating to Brazil who delivers feedback with their usual directness, even privately, risks landing far harder than intended, since the local calibration for "direct" sits at a different point on the scale. A Brazilian employee relocating to Sweden who reads a manager's calm, quiet feedback delivery as soft or non-urgent risks badly underestimating how seriously the content is meant to be taken — Swedish gentleness in tone is not, as it might be read through a Brazilian lens, a signal that the substance is optional.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — An employee navigating what they described as "mismatched values" around indirect feedback wrote that the hardest part of adjusting wasn't receiving vague feedback but learning to trust that vagueness was intentional information, not an absence of it — a distinction that took months of misreads to fully internalize.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a question about giving a boss constructive criticism noted that the safest approach in most high-context cultures was reframing the criticism as a request rather than an observation, since a request preserved the relationship in a way a direct critique, however accurate, often didn't.*

> *LinkedIn Pulse — A cross-cultural consultant who trained managers on giving feedback across regions described Brazil and Sweden as the two cultures her clients most consistently misjudged in opposite directions, over-softening feedback for Swedish colleagues who wanted it straight, and over-delivering bluntness to Brazilian colleagues who needed it wrapped.*

> *Reddit (Swedengate-adjacent discussion thread) — Commenters reflecting on Sweden's reputation for blunt social norms noted that the same directness people found startling in personal settings showed up in professional ones too, but almost always delivered one-on-one and without any apparent malice, which softened the impact once newcomers adjusted to expect it.*

> *Internations Stockholm — A Brazilian professional who relocated to a Swedish employer described her first formal review as disorienting in its plainness — direct language about a missed deadline, delivered without any of the relational cushioning she was used to — and said it took a full review cycle to realize the plainness was a form of respect, not a lack of investment in her.*

---

### Conclusion

The practical calibration: in Brazil, listen for what's underneath the encouraging language, and never deliver criticism without first investing in the relationship it has to travel through. In Sweden, take direct feedback exactly as literally as it sounds, and don't mistake its gentle delivery for low stakes — ask for more of it if you want it, since Swedish modesty can under-praise by other cultures' standards even while it over-delivers honesty. If a friend asked me over a drink: in São Paulo, learn the code before you trust the words; in Stockholm, trust the words and skip looking for a code that was never there.
