# In Taiwan, the Boss Is Always Right. In Egypt, the Boss Is Also Your Father.

## 🇹🇼 Taiwan · 🇪🇬 Egypt

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Hofstede Insights puts Taiwan's power distance score at 58 and Egypt's at 80, which sounds like a modest 22-point gap until you are the new hire deciding whether to email your director a counter-proposal or simply nod [Hofstede Insights](https://www.hofstede-insights.com/country-comparison/taiwan,egypt/). In Taipei, you will be told, with great sincerity, that the office is flat and everyone's opinion matters; you will still not interrupt your manager in a meeting. In Cairo, no one will bother with the flat-office fiction, because your manager already assumes you understand the arrangement: he outranks you, and increasingly, he expects to be consulted the way one consults a stern uncle. Neither country has abolished the org chart. Both have simply found more interesting ways to enforce it.

#### 🇹🇼 Taiwan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Learn the seniority ladder in week one and address people by title, not first name | Publicly question a manager's decision in a meeting, however gently |
| Stay a beat past when your boss logs off, even with nothing left to do | Be the first one out the door while your director is still at their desk |
| Route disagreement through a private, indirect suggestion | Mistake after-hours group-chat friendliness for a flattened hierarchy |
| Accept invitations to team dinners — they are unofficial performance reviews | Skip the ritual "acknowledged" reply in the group chat, even to a message you dislike |
| Bring tea or a small gift the first time you meet senior staff | Assume consensus-seeking means your input will change the outcome |
| Expect decisions to arrive already made, dressed up as a discussion | Push back twice on the same point once a senior colleague has spoken |

#### 🇪🇬 Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept the tea or coffee offered at the start of every meeting | Decline hospitality — it reads as a rejection of the relationship, not the drink |
| Use formal titles until a superior explicitly invites first names | Expect an official org chart to explain who actually holds influence |
| Let your manager ask about your health, family, and weekend before business starts | Rush straight into the agenda — it will backfire on trust, not just mood |
| Build the personal relationship before making a work request | Assume warmth means feedback will also be direct |
| Go through your direct manager rather than skipping levels | Be surprised if promotion criteria feel informal or relationship-driven |
| Expect your manager to mediate personal disputes, not just work ones | Treat "the boss will handle it" as a brush-off — it is often literal |

### Taiwan: Consensus Theatre

Taiwanese management culture runs on what researchers describe as a Confucian, paternalistic model: subordinates owe deference and hard work, and in return managers owe wise, protective guidance [Commisceo Global](https://commisceo-global.com/management-guides/taiwan-management-guide/). In practice this produces a workplace that performs consultation while quietly reserving the decision for whoever is most senior in the room. Meetings solicit "input," teams are asked for "thoughts," and then the person with the most tenure says what is going to happen, and everyone nods, because open disagreement with a superior is treated less as a difference of opinion and more as a breach of etiquette.

The paternalism extends to the clock. According to Taiwan's Directorate-General of Budget, Accounting and Statistics, employees in the industrial and service sectors logged 163.6 hours a month as of late 2025, with an additional 8.9 hours of overtime on top — a workload that has, in past years, been directly linked by labor authorities to a string of overwork-related deaths among younger staff. The unwritten rule is not that anyone will ask you to stay late. It is that you are simply expected to notice when your boss has not yet left, and to arrange your own departure accordingly.

### Egypt: Management as Family Business

Egyptian hierarchy runs on a different fuel: not silent deference but active relationship-building. Authority is concentrated at the top and decisions are made there too, but the process of getting a decision made is warm, personal, and unhurried — tea is served, small talk is mandatory, and rushing straight to business reads as rude rather than efficient [Commisceo Global](https://commisceo-global.com/management-guides/egypt-management-guide/). Employees frequently describe the workplace as a second family and the manager as something like a household head, one who is expected to intervene in personal problems, not just professional ones — and the sooner a manager accepts that responsibility, the sooner trust follows.

That family metaphor has a less charming corollary: connections. A 2014 survey found 55% of young Egyptian adults believed jobs were obtained "to a large extent" through wasta — personal or family influence — and World Bank researchers have flagged it as a leading obstacle young jobseekers cite ahead of skills or education [World Bank](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/arabvoices/wasta-hampering-arab-youth-chances-dignified-life). Meanwhile Gallup's 2026 State of the Global Workplace recorded Egypt's employee engagement at just 4%, tied for the lowest in the survey — a startling number given how relationally warm the day-to-day management style is reported to be. Closeness to the boss, it turns out, is not the same as engagement with the job.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the paradox sharpens: Taiwan's lower Hofstede power-distance score suggests a gentler hierarchy, yet its overtime culture and reflexive non-contradiction of superiors produce a workplace where dissent is nearly impossible to voice out loud. Egypt's higher score suggests a harsher one, yet its managers are, by most accounts, more emotionally available and personally invested in staff than their Taiwanese counterparts — the hierarchy is steeper but the relationship at the top of it is warmer.

The practical difference for a new hire is where the discomfort lands. In Taiwan, you will feel it in your calendar: long hours, late departures, meetings that agree with whoever spoke last. In Egypt, you will feel it in your network: who you know will matter more than what you know, and the manager who brings you tea in the morning may also be the reason a colleague with better connections got the promotion you wanted.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — a tech worker in Taipei asked colleagues whether "overrespecting hierarchy" was a cultural pattern or just their office, and the consensus reply was blunt: in Chinese-influenced workplaces, employees are expected to agree with managers almost by default, and managers rarely solicit real pushback even when they claim to want it.*

> *Quora — one contributor summed up their year in a Taiwanese company as "the boss is always right — even when they are not," while another expat, bracing for the same hierarchy to dominate every interaction, found the anxiety was mostly hollow: day-to-day relationships with their managers felt barely different from home.*

> *Expat.com forum — a new call-center hire in Cairo described colleagues snapping their fingers to get attention, the same gesture used on a pet, and being unnerved by it, but also noted real comradery: if a teammate ran late or struggled, others quietly covered for them without being asked, a solidarity the poster said they rarely saw in New York offices.*

> *Quora — a jobseeker asking how to compete "when nepotism is not on your side" was told, repeatedly, that in the Egyptian market this is less a hurdle to overcome than a system to join: cultivate the personal relationships before you need them, because by the time you need them it is too late.*

> *Blind — an engineer weighing an offer at a Taiwanese semiconductor firm was warned that the real onboarding advice was simple: don't ask when you're allowed to leave, watch when your manager leaves, and plan your evening around that instead of the clock.*

### Conclusion

If you are moving for work, the honest advice is to pick your discomfort. Taiwan will ask you to internalize hierarchy quietly and prove your commitment with your hours; Egypt will ask you to build a relationship with your boss before you can get anything done, and to accept that the relationship, not the org chart, is where the real authority lives. Neither is more "hierarchical" in the way that word usually gets used — they have just distributed the hierarchy differently, one into the clock, the other into the guest list.

What I would tell a friend over a drink: in Taiwan, learn to read the room and never be the first to leave it; in Egypt, learn to drink the tea and never skip the small talk — because in both places, the org chart is decorative, and the real one is unwritten.
