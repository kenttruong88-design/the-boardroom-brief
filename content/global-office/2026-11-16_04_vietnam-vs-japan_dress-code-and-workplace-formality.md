# Vietnam Wears a Tie to Teach; Japan Is Thinking About Shorts. Both Are Still Sweating

🇻🇳 Vietnam · 🇯🇵 Japan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In July 2026, a 50-year-old Tokyo government official turned up to work in shorts, at the governor's urging, and told Euronews it felt embarrassing before it felt comfortable. The governor herself, the architect of the campaign, declared shorts a step too far. Meanwhile in Ho Chi Minh City, foreign teachers debate whether to wear a tie in a classroom with no air conditioning. Two countries, one question: how much fabric does professionalism require?

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Vietnam

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress formally for first meetings, government offices, banks and large corporations; suits for men, skirts, dresses or blouses for women | Assume business casual is accepted everywhere; it is more common in Ho Chi Minh City and tech |
| Ask whether your employer has a dress code, and follow it | Wear shorts and a T-shirt to teach or present; students and parents notice |
| Choose cotton and loose fits, and keep a spare shirt | Wear dark synthetics and trust fortune |
| Introduce and hand out business cards in order of seniority, starting with the most senior person | Pocket a card without reading it |
| Raise disagreements privately, to protect face | Contradict a senior colleague in a meeting |
| Use "Anh" and "Em" or the appropriate form of address when senior staff expect it | Assume first names are universally welcome |

### In Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Wear a dark suit (black, grey or navy) with a neutral shirt for interviews and client meetings | Show up in a bright suit, busy blouse patterns or very high heels |
| Ask early whether your office is business professional or business casual | Assume the creative-industry exception applies to you |
| Keep grooming tidy: short neat hair, clean shoes that come off easily, intact socks | Wear a hat in the office |
| Cover tattoos at conservative workplaces and go light on fragrance | Treat a strong cologne as a personality |
| Follow the summer Cool Biz convention only when your office has declared it | Take off the jacket before your boss does |
| Raise disagreements with a manager privately | Contradict seniors in the meeting |

## Vietnam

Vietnam is formal on paper and negotiable in the afternoon. Vietnam Briefing says business attire remains relatively formal, especially in government offices, financial institutions and large corporations, with men in suits for formal meetings and women in professional dresses, skirts or blouses. It adds that business casual may be acceptable in some sectors, particularly in Ho Chi Minh City and technology. Vietcetera's guide draws the same regional line: Hanoi tends toward the white-collar look, Ho Chi Minh City toward business casual. Expat.com's Ho Chi Minh City guide says suit jackets and blazers are often left out day to day and many men wear a suit only for serious business, while women are advised to wear skirts or dresses reaching the knee and covering the shoulders.

The climate does much of the policy work. In an Expat.com thread on what male teachers wear, posters described classrooms with fans and open windows rather than air conditioning, and recommended cotton, loose fits and roll-on deodorant. Opinions on dress itself split. Several said trousers and a collared shirt are standard, international schools require a tie, and parents notice how teachers look, since foreigners are paid far more than local teachers and are expected to look the part. One poster recalled that their employer required a tie before the contract was signed and quietly dropped the requirement afterwards, because no other teacher wore one. Shorts divided the room: students told one poster that shorts were fine for socialising but not for studying.

Formality in Vietnam lives less in the wardrobe than in the forms of address. According to Vietnam Briefing, respect for hierarchy and seniority is highly valued, and business cards go first to the most senior person. A poster on an Expat.com etiquette thread said that formality depends on seniority, with directors and senior managers expecting strict forms of address such as "Anh" and "Em", and that cards should be exchanged with both hands and never pocketed. Hofstede Insights puts Vietnam at 70 on power distance, and the results are visible: employees are reluctant to disagree in public and concern for face is real. The nap after lunch, which one poster found uncomfortable and others considered perfectly normal, is formality's one concession to physics.

## Japan

Japan is formal in the way an exam is formal: the rules are known, applied uniformly and noticed when broken. A JobsInJapan writer who came from an American workplace found Japanese offices far more formal, with workers expected to dress conservatively, usually in suits, and colleagues remarking on clothing that looked too casual. Hacker News commenters recorded the same impression from the other direction, one noting that dressing down is discouraged and that office culture pushes back when suits are skipped, another that linen suits worn in Japan were treated by coworkers as casual clothes. A site guide from a Tokyo relocation agency advises sticking to black, grey or navy, avoiding coloured suits and high heels and keeping grooming spotless.

The official counterweight is Cool Biz. The Ministry of the Environment launched it in 2005 with a target air-conditioning setting of about 28°C; its first survey found 95.8% of respondents had heard of it and 32.7% said their offices ran their air conditioning warmer. After the 2011 earthquake, Super Cool Biz allowed polo shirts and trainers. In 2026, the Tokyo government pushed further, promoting polo shirts, T-shirts, sneakers and, depending on the job, shorts, citing electricity supply. Euronews quoted a 30-year-old marketing employee who praised the flexibility, and a 38-year-old government worker who found his commute noticeably cooler. The governor said shorts remind her of a day off.

Formality in Japan also governs speech and rank. Expat.com's Tokyo guide says communication is indirect, decisions slow and meetings frequent, colleagues are addressed by title, and at an after-work drinks event the leader takes the best seat while young trainees sit near the door. It adds that staying late is common and that leaving before your manager can be seen as breaking group harmony. Expat.com's Tokyo guide cites an average of about 50 hours a week against an official standard of 40. The OECD-based table compiled on Wikipedia gives about 1,691 annual hours per worker for Japan in 2023, against 2,160 for Vietnam.

## The Reckoning

The distinction is that Vietnam is formal toward people and Japan is formal toward everything. A Vietnamese office will forgive a loose shirt in humidity but not an overheard disagreement with the director; a Japanese office will ignore the disagreement if it is delivered privately and indirectly, but will remark on the shirt. Hofstede Insights supplies the numbers: Vietnam scores 70 on power distance and 30 on uncertainty avoidance, Japan 54 and 92. Vietnam is a hierarchy that tolerates improvisation; Japan is a rule set that tolerates hierarchy. Japan also scores 95 on masculinity, a dimension Hofstede ties to competitiveness and achievement, which may explain a great deal about the suit.

For the newcomer, the practical difference is risk. In Vietnam, getting it wrong costs you a polite look and some sweat; in Japan, it costs a conversation you will not be invited to. Gallup's 2025 report puts engagement at 18% in East Asia and 26% in Southeast Asia, against 21% globally, so neither country has made the formality pay for itself in enthusiasm. Both, however, have at least begun asking whether the jacket is the point.

One further difference is how the rules reach you. Vietnam tends to communicate its expectations by example: you notice that nobody wears a tie, and you stop. Japan communicates by correction: a colleague remarks on your clothing, and you stop. The first method is gentler and slower; the second is faster and leaves a mark. Either way, the safest strategy for your first month is to dress one notch above the room and then, observing the room, adjust downward by a single, carefully chosen button.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — A teacher in Ho Chi Minh City said they sweat through a classroom of 40 to 50 students without air conditioning, but considers the pay worth it; another advised silk shirts to wick sweat and plenty of fluids.*

> *Expat.com — A poster on a Vietnam business-etiquette thread said that senior staff expect strict forms of address, that cards must be handled with both hands, and that a physical office matters because remote work is uncommon.*

> *JobsInJapan — A teacher from the United States found a Japanese school office formal and pyramid-shaped, with every desk lined up and the vice principal at the front. Questions to a supervisor travelled through several people, and the reply often came from someone other than the boss.*

> *Hacker News — Commenters noted that in Japan dressing down is discouraged, and that a linen suit worn by one visitor was treated by coworkers as casual clothing.*

> *Euronews — A government official who wore shorts to work on a 34°C day said they felt embarrassing at first and comfortable later, while a worker in his thirties said the heat of his commute was bearable only in shorts.*

## Conclusion

If you hate dressing for the weather, neither country is your friend, but Vietnam is the more forgiving. Learn the forms of address, carry a spare shirt, and treat the company dress code as the final word. If you choose Japan, buy a dark suit before you arrive, ask early what the office expects, and wait until your boss removes his jacket. The Cool Biz season, and its more adventurous Tokyo cousin, are officially permitted and still carefully watched.

In both countries, the dress code is the written rule; the real code is whatever your senior colleague is wearing.
