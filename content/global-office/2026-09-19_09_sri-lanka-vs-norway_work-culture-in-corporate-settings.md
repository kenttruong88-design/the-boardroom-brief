**In Colombo, Wait for the Senior Person to Speak First; In Oslo, Watch the CEO Bus Their Own Lunch Tray**

🇱🇰 Sri Lanka · 🇳🇴 Norway

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Sri Lankan office will greet its most senior member first, out of genuine respect for a hierarchy everyone quietly agrees is real. A Norwegian office will have flattened that same hierarchy so thoroughly that the CEO clears their own tray in the canteen — and if you try too hard to stand out for your individual brilliance, you'll hit an invisible wall called Janteloven before lunch is over.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇱🇰 Sri Lanka

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use titles when addressing colleagues and counterparts unless explicitly invited to use first names | Address a senior colleague casually before they've offered that familiarity |
| Greet the most senior person in the room first — hierarchy by age and position is a guiding social principle | Skip straight to the most junior or most convenient person in a group greeting |
| Read indirect language, tone, and silence carefully — a flat "no" is rare even when disagreement is real | Take a non-committal or vague response as agreement; it may be a polite way of declining |
| Expect decisions to require confirmation from senior figures, even after a productive meeting with middle management | Assume a deal is done after agreement with mid-level staff who lack final authority |
| Build the relationship first — face-to-face meetings and shared meals are where real trust forms | Rush straight to business before any relationship-building has happened |

#### 🇳🇴 Norway

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use first names with everyone, including the CEO, from the start | Default to formal titles — it will read as oddly distant, not respectful |
| Present results as team outcomes and credit the group, even when your individual contribution was central | Claim individual credit prominently — overt self-promotion triggers real, if subtle, social friction |
| Expect real autonomy and trust once given a task — managers delegate and step back | Wait for constant oversight or check-ins; initiative is assumed, not requested |
| Respect the end of the workday as a genuine boundary — offices empty out on schedule | Assume staying late signals dedication; it's more likely to read as poor time management |
| Frame your own achievements through outcomes and numbers, then credit the team, if you do need visibility | Present yourself as exceptional or above the collective — Janteloven treats that as a real social error |

---

Sri Lankan corporate culture retains a formal, age-and-position-based hierarchy that, per Commisceo Global and Cultural Atlas, persists even though the country's historical caste structures no longer formally organize business life. Titles matter enough that using one incorrectly is a real etiquette lapse, not a minor slip, and greeting order in a group setting is expected to follow seniority. Communication is notably indirect and context-sensitive: a Sri Lankan counterpart will rarely deliver a flat "no," which means genuine disagreement often has to be read through tone, hesitation, and what's left unsaid rather than taken from the literal words offered. Business guides consistently flag patience as essential — meetings with middle management may feel productive without actually producing a binding decision, since final authority typically sits with senior figures who weren't necessarily in the room, and personal relationship-building, often over a shared meal, is treated as a legitimate first step rather than a delay before "real" business begins.

Norway sits close to the opposite end of the spectrum. Flat organizational structures are the cultural default, not an aspiration — first names are used with everyone including the CEO, and multiple guides describe managers delegating substantial autonomy to employees, trusting their competence rather than supervising closely. The deeper cultural mechanism underneath this flatness is Janteloven, the unwritten "Law of Jante" that discourages standing out or claiming individual superiority over the collective; in practice, that means overt self-promotion, personal credit-claiming, and executives who present themselves as exceptional all generate quiet social friction, even when the underlying performance genuinely merits recognition. Decision-making leans consensus-based, and Norway is described as holding one of the firmest work-life boundaries among comparable economies — offices tend to empty out at the end of the day regardless of what's left unfinished, a boundary treated as a cultural value, not a lack of commitment.

**The Reckoning**

The instructive irony here is that both cultures are, in their own way, deeply relationship- and trust-based — they've just built trust through opposite structural choices. Sri Lanka builds trust through visible, formalized hierarchy: knowing your place and showing deference is how relationships (and eventually results) get built. Norway builds trust through the deliberate erasure of visible hierarchy: flattening status differences is itself the trust-building mechanism, on the theory that genuine collaboration requires everyone to feel like a peer, at least on the surface.

The practical trap for a newcomer is applying the wrong instinct to the wrong country. Someone who's spent years in Norway's flat, first-name, consensus-driven culture will, if transplanted to Colombo, likely under-defer to seniority and misread the slow, hierarchy-routed pace of decisions as inefficiency rather than protocol. Someone moving the other direction, from Sri Lanka to Norway, risks the opposite error — treating the CEO with a formality that reads as oddly stiff, or hesitating to take initiative while waiting for permission that, culturally, was never going to be explicitly granted.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone answering "what is the work culture like in Sri Lanka" described the biggest early misstep as taking a colleague's polite non-answer as a soft yes — projects moved forward on assumed approval that, weeks later, turned out to have been a face-saving way of saying no all along.*

> *Quora — A respondent describing living in Sri Lanka as a foreigner noted that hierarchy respected age and position simultaneously, which occasionally meant a younger but higher-ranking foreign manager had to work visibly harder to earn the same deference automatically extended to an older, lower-ranking local colleague.*

> *lifeinnorway.net (Janteloven feature) — A cited professional account described submitting a strong individual performance review only to have a Norwegian manager gently redirect the framing toward team outcomes — not as a rebuke, but as a genuine cultural reflex neither party found tense once it was named directly.*

> *Culture Comms Consulting (Janteloven analysis) — A described case involved an American hire whose confident, achievement-forward self-presentation in meetings was well-received individually but subtly cooled team dynamics until they began explicitly crediting colleagues alongside their own results — after which the friction reportedly eased considerably.*

> *cultureflow.no (working with Norwegian colleagues) — A contributor described the adjustment to Norway's strict end-of-day boundary as harder than expected for someone used to visible late-night effort signaling ambition — colleagues who left exactly on time weren't seen as less committed, just better at the local definition of professionalism.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing between these two, ask which discomfort you'd rather sit with: deferring visibly to people above you until you've earned the standing to speak first, or suppressing the instinct to claim credit even when you know the win was yours. Sri Lanka will ask patience of you; Norway will ask humility, whether or not you feel it. My honest take, over a drink: Norway is the harder adjustment for anyone raised on individual achievement as the currency of a career — Sri Lanka's hierarchy, at least, tells you exactly where you stand.
