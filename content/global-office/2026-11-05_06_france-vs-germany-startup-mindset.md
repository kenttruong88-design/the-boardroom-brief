# France Will Give Your Startup a Union Rep. Germany Will Give It a Mittelstand Work Ethic. Neither Will Give You Equity That Matters.

🇫🇷 France · 🇩🇪 Germany

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The popular caricature has the French clocking out at five for a long lunch while the Germans grind through a disciplined, efficient workday. OECD figures say the opposite: French employees who are actually working log more annual hours per worker than German employees do. The gap people actually notice — the one that shows up in Berlin and Paris startup offices rather than in a spreadsheet — has nothing to do with laziness and everything to do with who still runs the room once the beanbags are unpacked.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect real hierarchy underneath a "flat" startup culture, even with a Chief Happiness Officer on staff | Assume a ping-pong table or a beanbag chair means the usual French chain of command has dissolved |
| Respect the contractual, legally protected workweek — French workers tend to actually use their time off | Schedule routine after-hours demands assuming a "startup hustle" culture will simply absorb them |
| Take French skepticism of imported Silicon Valley management theater seriously, not personally | Lean hard on American-style motivational language ("we're a family," "we're changing the world") without expecting pushback |
| Recognize the elite-university pipeline — many major French startup founders come from a small number of grandes écoles | Assume France's startup scene is as geographically or educationally decentralized as Germany's |
| Expect strong worker protections and labor-law awareness even inside small, young companies | Assume a startup's small size exempts it from France's broader labor-law expectations in practice |

#### 🇩🇪 Germany

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate base salary hard — equity at most German startups is a founder-level reward, rarely a real wealth path for employees | Treat a startup's stock-option offer as a meaningful substitute for a competitive base salary |
| Ask a prospective manager directly how they've handled a past over-budget project or crunch period before accepting an offer | Accept a startup role on culture-pitch alone without probing how the team actually behaves under pressure |
| Learn German if you're serious about staying — it opens a far larger, better-protected job pool | Assume an English-only career path in German tech will stay as competitive or secure long-term |
| Expect German corporate roles to be comparatively hard to be dismissed from post-probation, with clear promotion ladders | Assume a German corporate job offers the same fast movement or flat structure as its startup counterpart |
| Watch for "we are a family" framing as a possible signal of informal unpaid-overtime expectations | Assume founder enthusiasm and a tight-knit office culture are purely positive signs with no attached cost |

The hours data alone should retire the stereotype. The Local's analysis of OECD 2023 and Eurostat figures puts French workers at 1,494 annual hours per worker against Germany's 1,340 — France ahead, not behind. The per-capita gap runs the other way (France 666 hours against Germany's 724, largely because France has more retirees, more unemployment, and a higher birth rate pulling down its workforce participation), and weekly hours sit at 35.8 in France against 33.9 in Germany — again, France working more, not less, once you're actually counting the people doing the work rather than the whole population. The stereotype survives anyway because it was never really about hours; it's about what French workplaces, startup or otherwise, are willing to perform in public. Connexion France's own reporting on the country's resistance to imported startup culture describes an enduring "them and us" labor-versus-management mentality, strong hierarchy, and hard-won labor protections workers actively defend — alongside a pointed, unproven claim that French productivity still beats the UK's despite fewer average hours, the implication being that time spent at a French desk is spent working rather than performing the ping-pong-table theater of an imported management style.

Germany's startup scene, by contrast, has real scale — Germanpedia's 2025 unicorn count puts it at 32 companies, narrowly ahead of France's 29, together accounting for roughly half of all EU unicorns — but the texture of actually working inside one, per a detailed r/germany thread reachable through a mirror after direct Reddit access was blocked, is considerably rougher than the funding numbers suggest. Commenters describe founders who pitch "we're all going to make it big" equity stories that rarely pay out for anyone but the founders themselves, since few German startups go public; one poster flatly calls their own Berlin startup job "the worst" they'd had, citing a manipulative "we are a family" culture used to extract unpaid overtime covering for absent colleagues; another describes being hired at a startup that entered insolvency four months later. A more measured voice in the same thread offers a counterpoint — decent networking, reasonable hours, and pay that genuinely beat a prior job in Italy — while recommending, bluntly, that anyone serious about staying should learn German specifically to escape the brutal competition among English-only applicants for the limited roles that don't require it. Germany's startup geography also looks structurally different from France's: Speedinvest's comparative analysis finds France's biggest funding rounds dominated by founders from just five elite grandes écoles (52 percent of them), while Germany's equivalent figure is only 13 percent, spread across Berlin, Munich, and Hamburg rather than concentrated in one capital the way more than half of French startups are concentrated in Paris alone.

The geography of where each country's startup scene actually happens reinforces the same split. More than half of all French startups, per Speedinvest's analysis, are concentrated in Paris alone, a centralization that mirrors France's broader administrative tradition of routing power and prestige through the capital; Germany's scene spreads across Berlin, Munich, and Hamburg instead, with no single city holding anything like Paris's gravitational pull. That dispersion shows up in funding patterns too — French VC funding grew a striking 25 percent in a single recent year, suggesting a scene producing startups quickly, while German startups, per the same analysis, tend to scale larger once they reach later funding stages, suggesting a slower but more durable growth pattern once a company clears its early, more precarious years. For someone choosing a city rather than just a country, the practical implication is that "German startup" and "French startup" aren't just cultural categories but genuinely different economic geographies, with Paris functioning as the overwhelming center of one and no equivalent single hub anchoring the other.

### The Reckoning

The genuinely useful irony here is that France's startup scene behaves, culturally, more like an elite extension of its corporate establishment than a break from it, while Germany's startup scene behaves more like an unregulated frontier than an extension of its famously stable Mittelstand corporate culture. Hofstede's data back this up cleanly: France scores far higher on power distance (68 versus Germany's 35), meaning hierarchy persists in French offices by default, startup branding notwithstanding, while Germany's lower score and more consensus-driven management style should, in theory, make its startups flatter — except the r/germany accounts above suggest that in practice, a German startup without the Mittelstand's institutional guardrails can simply mean less protection, not more equality.

For someone choosing between the two, the honest framing is this: a French startup job will likely feel more like a fast-track corporate job with better coffee than true Silicon Valley disruption, credentialed pedigree included. A German startup job will likely feel less protected than either a German corporate role or its own funding numbers suggest, with equity that functions more as a recruiting narrative than a real financial instrument for most employees.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/germany — a commenter described their Berlin startup job as the worst they'd ever had, citing a founder culture that framed itself as "a family" specifically to pressure staff into unpaid overtime covering for colleagues, and noted a separate poster in the same thread had been hired at a startup that quietly entered insolvency four months later.*

> *r/germany — a different commenter in the same thread offered a real counterpoint, praising the networking value and pay of their own Berlin startup role (notably better than a prior job in Italy) while still recommending that anyone serious about a long-term German tech career learn German to escape the intense competition for English-only positions.*

> *Hallo Germany relocation blog — the most repeated, concrete piece of advice framed startup-versus-corporate as a personality fit question rather than a prestige question: corporates offer promotion ladders and real post-probation job security, startups offer visibility and risk, and the blog's advice was to be honest with yourself about which one you actually are before accepting either offer.*

> *Connexion France, opinion piece — the article's own framing captured a specific French skepticism toward imported perks like beer fridges and "Happiness Managers," arguing they function as management theater layered over an unchanged adversarial labor relationship rather than as evidence of a genuinely flattened hierarchy.*

> *Speedinvest, VC ecosystem analysis — the firm's own comparative data point was blunt enough to read like a verdict from the investors themselves: just 13 percent of founders behind Germany's biggest funding rounds came from the country's top five universities, against 52 percent in France, a gap the analysis frames as proof that Germany's startup culture is genuinely less credentialist, for better and for worse, than France's.*

### Conclusion

If you're choosing where to take the startup job, the question to actually ask isn't "which country is more entrepreneurial" — both clearly are, in different registers — it's "what am I actually being offered once the pitch deck language is stripped out." In France, that's likely a well-credentialed, still-hierarchical fast track dressed in startup language. In Germany, that's likely genuine risk and genuine upside concentrated almost entirely at the founder level, with employee equity functioning more as morale than as money.

I'd tell a friend taking either job the same thing: ask for the base salary you'd want even if the company fails, because in both countries, that number — not the mission statement — is the only part of the offer you can actually bank on.
