**Who Gets to Speak First: Pakistan, the UK, and the Battle of the Birth Years**

🇵🇰 Pakistan · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Pakistan's median age is 20.8. The United Kingdom's is roughly double that. Put differently, you could relocate from a country where the office junior is statistically more typical than the boss to one where the boss is statistically more typical than the junior, and your actual job description might not change at all. What changes is who you're allowed to interrupt, when you're allowed to ask for a raise, and whether "the way we've always done it" is a compliment or a complaint. Neither country has solved its generational workplace problem. They have simply organised it differently, and a transfer between them means learning an entirely new choreography of who speaks first.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet the most senior person in the room first, by title and surname, even in a two-minute hallway exchange | Correct a senior manager's figures or decision out loud in a meeting — raise it afterward, privately, as a question |
| Build slack into every deadline for the multiple sign-offs decisions typically require | Assume a foreign degree or MBA moves you up the seniority queue faster than tenure does |
| Let a senior colleague speak first in group settings, then add your point after, not instead | Skip the chain of command to email someone two levels up, even when it's objectively faster |
| Treat staying visibly late in your first weeks as a signal your new team is watching for | Expect quick praise or feedback — only 4% of Pakistani employees report being engaged at work, so silence is the baseline, not a verdict on you |
| Learn the names and informal standing of your boss's deputies; warmth operates alongside hierarchy, not instead of it | Raise work-from-home flexibility in week one; earn the social capital for that conversation first |

#### 🇬🇧 United Kingdom

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use first names with your manager, and often the CEO, from day one unless told otherwise | Assume grey hair signals either seniority or comfort with the tech stack — over a third of younger UK employees name older colleagues' tech unfamiliarity as their top office friction |
| Put objections in writing or raise them directly in the meeting — unspoken disagreement reads as agreement | Skip the small talk before business; weather, commute and football are not filler, they're the warm-up |
| Take a proper lunch break away from your desk | Push visibly for rapid promotion in your first months — it's the single most-cited generational flashpoint UK employers report |
| Ask about flexible or hybrid terms at offer stage, not after you've started | Vanish for lunch or after hours without a word — "just popping out" is a courtesy, not optional |
| Say no to extra work when you're at capacity; boundary-setting reads as competence, not defiance | Wait for an annual review to discuss your trajectory — only a third of workers over 50 report doing any workplace learning at all, so don't assume a plan exists unless you ask |

Pakistan's workplace generational story is really a story about proportions. Pew Research Center data cited by columnist Andleeb Abbas in *Business Recorder* put Baby Boomers at roughly 24% of the Pakistani office workforce, Generation X at 33%, Millennials at 35%, and Gen Z at a mere 6%, though climbing fast. That 6% nonetheless runs disproportionately through the country's IT and services export sector, meaning the generation with the least formal authority often has the most technical leverage. The arrangement sits inside a Hofstede cultural profile that helps explain why authority still concentrates at the top regardless: Pakistan scores 55 on power distance and a notably low 14 on individualism, a combination that rewards deference to seniority over individual initiative, by Hofstede Insights' own measures.

The practical result shows up in the numbers nobody wants to put on a recruitment brochure. Gallup's 2025 State of the Global Workplace puts Pakistani employee engagement at just 4%, against a South Asia regional average of 21% and a global average of 20%, with 68% of employees "not engaged" and a further 28% "actively disengaged." The Express Tribune has reported that a 2016 study found nearly half of Pakistani youth did not want to remain in the country at all, and more than 3.7 million Pakistanis, overwhelmingly aged 18 to 25, left within six years. That exodus is less a rejection of Pakistan than a rejection of the specific bargain younger employees are offered inside it: rigid hierarchy, slow progression, and limited public dissent, regardless of how fluent the junior employee is in the tools the business actually runs on.

Business Recorder's generational breakdown adds a second layer worth knowing before you accept an offer letter. Among Millennials, who make up the largest single cohort at 35% of the office workforce, only 30% report being engaged, 55% describe themselves as not engaged, and 21% say they switched jobs within the past year, with 62% open to leaving for the right offer. Generation X, at 33% of the workforce, is described in the same reporting as caught between demanding seniors above and increasingly vocal juniors below, with technology comfort limited mostly to email. If you arrive as a mid-career foreign hire, you will likely be slotted somewhere near that Generation X layer by default, regardless of your actual age, simply because you are neither the newest junior nor the most senior figure in the room.

The United Kingdom runs the opposite demographic experiment with a similarly uneven outcome. Workers aged 50 and over made up 28% of the UK workforce in 2011 and 33% by 2021, and managers aged 50-plus rose from 31% to roughly 35% over the same window, according to ONS data cited in the Chartered Management Institute's "Age in the Workplace" paper and the CII's "Talented Generations" report. Hofstede's figures help explain why the resulting friction is loud rather than silent: the UK scores 89 on individualism against Pakistan's 14, and only 35 on power distance against Pakistan's 55, meaning disagreement is not just tolerated but structurally expected to be voiced.

And voiced it is. The CMI paper finds Millennials most often cite older colleagues' unfamiliarity with new technology (34%) as their chief source of workplace conflict, while Generation X (34%) and Baby Boomers (24%) cite younger workers' impatience for rapid progression, a complaint 69% of employers agree is the dominant cause of tension. A 2025 Kahoot! survey reported that 44% of UK Gen Z office workers had faced direct criticism from older colleagues over generational stereotyping, and 57% said they had personally had to teach their manager a basic digital task. None of this stops the UK workplace from functioning; it simply means the friction is explicit, documented, and frequently the subject of its own HR training module, which is a very British way of handling an argument.

The learning-and-development gap compounds the problem quietly rather than loudly. CMI's polling found 74% of employees in their thirties had a personal development plan, falling to 59% among those 50 and older, and access to reskilling specifically fell from 67% for under-30s to 45% for the over-50s. Fewer than one in five managers reported that their organisation gave any age-diversity training at all. So while the generational arguments in a UK office tend to be audible, the structural disadvantage running underneath them, who actually gets invested in going forward, is the part nobody argues about out loud, which is usually the more durable problem.

Put the two systems side by side and the symmetry turns almost comic. Pakistan's youngest cohort holds disproportionate technical skill and almost no formal standing to act on it; the UK's youngest cohort holds roughly the opposite deal, formal standing to speak up and comparatively little seniority to back it with. And the supposedly predictable alignments invert on contact: a 2025 Epassi survey found UK employees under 30 were markedly less likely than Millennials or Generation X to resist a return to the office, upending the assumption that youth automatically means a preference for remote work. If you arrive in either country expecting the stereotype you packed, budget time to unpack a different one.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hacker News — a commenter recounting friction inside a Western-style newsroom-adjacent startup described colleagues under 26 as routinely voicing blunt opinions in internal chat that they would never say aloud in a meeting, with little regard for who outranked whom; Boomer and Gen X staff, by contrast, expected formal deference and reacted badly when they felt overruled in public, a split the commenter expected to widen rather than close.*

> *Expat.com forum (England sub-forum) — an American arrival described a young supervisor who expected informal, almost chaotic office behaviour (changing clothes at a desk, no fixed break structure) while simultaneously drawing a hard line on any pushback from newer staff; the poster also noted that cigarette breaks, not lunch, were where real workplace bonding and information-sharing happened, leaving non-smokers quietly excluded from decisions made outside formal meetings.*

> *Expat.com forum (Pakistan sub-forum) — a Pakistani-origin professional who had spent roughly a decade working in Norway described returning to Pakistan and hitting what they called a "huge mind gap" between returnees used to flatter, faster workplace norms and locals operating inside the layered, seniority-first system that never changed while they were away; their advice to anyone making the same move was to rent before buying and expect a long adjustment, not a short one.*

> *Pakistaniat.com forum — a commenter working in Pakistani IT and services firms described a specific, recurring tell: leaving the office at the official closing time once drew visible staring from managers who had stayed later, and advised newcomers that leaving on time once or twice a week was tolerated, but doing it as a consistent habit quietly marked you as uncommitted, regardless of output.*

### Conclusion

The honest advice for anyone weighing these two postings is to stop asking which country "respects" its workforce and start asking which currency it trades in. Pakistan's hierarchy is explicit and durable, so your leverage comes from patience, visible deference, and letting competence accumulate as tenure; the payoff, when it comes, is real but slow, and the 4% engagement figure is less an indictment of individual managers than a structural feature you are choosing to opt into. The UK's hierarchy is informal and contested, so your leverage comes from stating your case early, in writing, and expecting it to be argued with rather than silently overruled; the cost is that nobody, at any age, gets to assume good faith is implied rather than spoken.

Neither system is kinder. Pakistan asks the young to wait their turn in a country where they are the overwhelming majority; the UK asks everyone to negotiate their turn out loud in a country where no single generation holds a clear majority at all. Pack accordingly, and if a colleague twenty years your senior tells you how it's "always been done," ask yourself honestly which country you're in before you decide whether that's wisdom or an opening bid.
