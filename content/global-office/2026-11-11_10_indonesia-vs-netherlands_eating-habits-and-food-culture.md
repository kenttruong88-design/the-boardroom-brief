# Indonesia Will Deliver Your Lunch to Your Desk; the Netherlands Will Judge the Bread

🇮🇩 Indonesia · 🇳🇱 Netherlands

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In March 2024, according to BPS-Statistics Indonesia, half of the average Indonesian's monthly spending, about 50.1 per cent, went on food. In the Netherlands, where the working week is the shortest in the European Union at 32.2 hours according to Eurostat, much of the nation's lunch consists of a slice of bread, a slice of cheese and thirty minutes of silent efficiency. One country has a food culture that organises its day, and the other has a food culture that is organised around the day. For a professional deciding where to relocate, the difference will show up at noon, every working day, for as long as the contract lasts.

## Do's & Don'ts

### In Indonesia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Install Gojek and Grab before your first day and tip the driver | Expect a lunch hour to be quiet or solitary |
| Start with mall restaurants and busy, well-attended warungs, then work towards the street | Eat raw vegetables or street ice on the first week |
| Drink bottled water and keep rehydration salts in your desk | Assume every pharmacy stocks your usual stomach remedy |
| Ask colleagues where they eat and follow them | Order pork-heavy dishes without checking the setting |
| Learn the names of three local staples, such as nasi goreng, gado-gado and soto | Dismiss instant noodles as beneath a serious diet; locals do not |
| Accept invitations to eat, since meals are where work relationships form | Turn down food from a colleague without a polite reason |

### In the Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Bring a packed lunch and eat it at the usual hour, around noon | Reheat strongly smelling fish in the shared microwave |
| Learn the vocabulary of the sandwich: beleg, hagelslag, broodje | Order a delivery to your desk and expect approval |
| Try a broodje kroket at least once | Eat while typing, which is read as a symptom of poor work-life balance |
| Join the colleagues who sit together at midday, even if briefly | Disappear for an hour and a half to dine |
| Accept coffee, and take the biscuit offered with it | Arrive late back from lunch and hope no one notices |
| Plan a proper hot meal for the evening | Expect the canteen to serve a hot lunch at a modest price |

## Indonesia

For Indonesian professionals food is a social institution first and a nutritional matter second, and the arithmetic reinforces that. BPS data reported by Databoks Katadata show that in March 2024 Indonesians spent about Rp1.5 million per person per month, of which Rp751,790, or 50.10 per cent, went to food. BPS itself notes that a higher food share tends to indicate lower welfare, so this is the context in which the country's cheap, abundant cooking should be read: food is a major household cost, and prices are sensitive. The Jakarta Post's own columnist, a recent arrival, singled out Indomie instant noodles as the best-value meal in the city, praised rendang and bubur ayam, and then, with the honesty of the newly resident, advised keeping a stock of noodles for sick days.

The office meal is increasingly delivered. Momentum Works, cited by The Jakarta Post, estimates Indonesia's food delivery market at about US$6.4 billion in 2025, the largest in Southeast Asia, having grown roughly 18 per cent and added about a billion dollars in a year. The same columnist observed that hundreds of thousands of Jakarta office workers use Gojek for their morning coffee or lunch each day. A newcomer will find that anything, from coffee to boba, can be brought to the building, and that the apps are close to a precondition of daily life. The delivery driver is, in effect, a member of the extended office, and the one whose punctuality is more reliable than most meeting start times. For the expatriate this removes a classic relocation anxiety: nobody needs to find the good restaurant, since the restaurant will find the desk. What the apps cannot remove is the expectation to be present for the part of lunch that is not about eating, which is the conversation.

Hofstede puts Indonesia at 78 on power distance and 5 on individualism, one of the most collective scores recorded. That is visible at the table: meals are shared, offers are made repeatedly, and eating alone is an event that requires explanation. The practical risks are less cultural than gastrointestinal. Expat.com contributors recommend bottled water, avoiding ice from small vendors, and building up to street food gradually, although some eat it daily without incident. The same index records uncertainty avoidance of 48 for Indonesia, a middling figure that fits a food culture comfortable with improvisation: the stall changes, the menu shifts and nobody consults a hygiene certificate. Pork is less widely available in a predominantly Muslim city, and cheese and other dairy are expensive, according to an American resident reporting to Talesmag in 2025.

## The Netherlands

The Netherlands treats lunch as an administrative interval. Eurostat reports that in 2023 the Dutch worked an average of 32.2 hours a week, against 36.1 in the EU as a whole, and the midday break is correspondingly brisk. IamExpat describes a lunch of thirty minutes or less, eaten at the desk, in the canteen or on a bench, usually cold, on the reasoning that light food avoids the afternoon slump and leaves people free to go home earlier. The same article concedes that experts recommend 45 to 60 minutes and that the rushed version leaves less time for colleagues to socialise. DutchReview, in a deliberately comic piece, makes the point that a home-made sandwich is at the top of the lunch hierarchy because it signals planning and frugality, while delivery sits at the bottom.

The writer's tone is humorous, but the hierarchy is accurate enough. The Leiden International Centre says that warm lunches exist "usually only on special days", with the broodje kroket, a fried meat ragout in a bun, as the revered exception. DutchReview's German contributor adds that Dutch lunch breaks are typically unpaid, which may account for the speed. IamExpat lists the usual alternatives, among them a kroket roll, smoked sausage with toast or instant soup, washed down with milk or juice, which gives some idea of the ambition level. DutchReview adds that offices tend to empty at about noon, that early returners attract suspicion, that working through lunch draws concern rather than admiration, and that salad eaters are respected for their health and slightly pitied for their virtue. A newcomer who arrives with a hot meal from home may find that it marks them as, in the article's phrase, different and possibly foreign.

Colleagues will, however, share cake and coffee with enthusiasm at the proper moments, and the Leiden International Centre recommends using the sandwich itself as a conversation starter, since asking about cheese versus hagelslag is a low-risk way to practise Dutch. The scores explain the manners. The Netherlands registers 38 on power distance, 80 on individualism and 14 on masculinity, according to the reproduction of the same data at Cyborlink, which is to say a flat, independent and unassertive office. Nobody will insist you eat with the group, and nobody will be hurt if you do not, but a modest sandwich at the same hour as everyone else is read as agreement with the national religion of not making a fuss.

## The Reckoning

The two countries answer the same question differently: who is responsible for lunch. In Indonesia, the answer is everyone, and the meal is a collective enterprise supported by a small army of cooks, vendors and riders, with the cost to the worker being time, digestion and a degree of social obligation. In the Netherlands, the answer is you, preferably the night before, and the cost is a certain monotony and the loss of a hot meal in the middle of the day.

For the relocating professional, the Indonesian system offers variety and inclusion but demands a resilient stomach and a willingness to be sociable on someone else's schedule. The Dutch system offers predictability, an early finish and almost no social friction, provided one adopts the bread. Neither system is hostile to outsiders; both simply assume the outsider has read the rules. A person who values company at the table and flavour will be happier in Jakarta, and a person who values the hours between five o'clock and bedtime will be happier in Amsterdam, with the bread.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Reddit r/Netherlands — a newcomer asked whether the noon bread-and-cheese lunch was universal, and replies confirmed it largely was, with ham, hagelslag or peanut butter as variations; one commenter said a warm lunch generally means bringing it from home, and that affordable restaurants serving a hot midday meal are uncommon.*

> *Expat.com — in a thread on eating in Jakarta, one poster recommended bottled water and avoiding spicy food at first, another suggested working up from decent restaurants through food courts to the street, and a third joked that pizza and beer had caused more stomach trouble than Indonesian cooking.*

> *Expat.com — in an older thread on street food, one resident ate it nearly every day with local friends without trouble, another said some stalls were excellent and others cost days of digestive misery, and several advised following locals to busy stalls and keeping Imodium to hand.*

> *Talesmag — an American resident wrote that fresh produce is cheap and plentiful, that dining out costs less than at home, that cheese is dear and pork scarce, and that almost everything can be delivered by Gojek or Grab.*

> *The Jakarta Post — a columnist new to the city said delivery apps make office ordering effortless, urged readers to install Gojek and Grab and tip drivers, and warned that carts selling fried snacks crowd the pavements.*

## Conclusion

If you are moving to Indonesia, budget for the delivery apps, pace yourself on street food and accept that lunch is a social occasion that will not be hurried. If you are moving to the Netherlands, learn to like bread, bring it at noon and be seated before anyone has questions. In both countries, ask a colleague where and what they eat in the first week, because the answer is more useful than any official guide.

The honest advice is to pack antacids for Jakarta and patience for Amsterdam, and to accept that either way you will be eating something that someone else has already decided you should.
