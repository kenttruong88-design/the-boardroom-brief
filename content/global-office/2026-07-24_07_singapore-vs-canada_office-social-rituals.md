# Lunch Is Not Optional, Drinks Are Negotiable: Office Rituals in Singapore and Canada

**🇸🇬 Singapore · 🇨🇦 Canada**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Singapore, skipping the team lunch is the socially loaded move. In Canada, it's skipping the after-work drinks — and increasingly, Canadian employees are pushing back on being asked to attend those drinks at all. Both countries bond over food and social time; they've just decided, culturally, which meal of the day is mandatory and which one is up for negotiation.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇸🇬 Singapore

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Join the team lunch regularly — it's a genuine daily bonding ritual, not an optional extra | Consistently eat alone at your desk — it reads as social distance, not just a scheduling choice |
| Expect after-work team dinners and drinks tied to deals, milestones, or simply team cohesion | Assume declining every dinner invitation carries no professional cost |
| Use hawker centres and kopi runs as genuine informal networking — plenty gets discussed there | Treat lunch as "just food" — Singaporean colleagues often build trust there before the meeting room |
| Accept that meritocratic, competitive culture extends into how visibly social you are at work | Expect team bonding to be confined neatly to work hours — much happens on personal time |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Attend social events at least briefly to build relationships — full absence is noticed over time | Feel obligated to drink alcohol — non-alcoholic options are completely normal and accepted |
| Say no to optional after-hours events without much explanation — declining is broadly acceptable | Assume opting out has zero relationship cost — some informal bonding still happens there |
| Push, if you can, for team bonding scheduled during work hours — 81% of surveyed employees want more of this | Assume your employer will proactively offer this — many still default to after-hours events |
| Treat "personal time is protected" as a real cultural value, not just a policy line | Overcommit socially early to "prove" fit — steady, moderate participation reads better long-term |

### Singapore: The Lunch Table Is the Real Meeting Room

Singaporean office culture treats the daily team lunch as close to structural, not optional. Offices empty out at midday toward hawker centres and communal eating spots, and the ritual functions as an ongoing, low-stakes trust-building exercise — informal conversations over kopi often surface team friction before it reaches a formal meeting, according to multiple Singapore workplace culture guides including Edstellar's. Team dinners and drinks carry real business weight too: deals get discussed, relationships get built, and — per commentary from outlets like RICE Media — the sheer consistency of the ritual can become genuinely exhausting for employees who'd rather eat lunch alone occasionally without it reading as a social withdrawal.

This sits inside a broader meritocratic, competitive workplace culture where visibility matters. Singapore's work environment is frequently described, including in first-person accounts, as demanding in a way that treats the office as something close to a second home — long hours are common, and social participation functions as an extension of professional presence rather than a separate, optional layer of office life.

### Canada: Social, But Increasingly on Its Own Terms

Canadian office social culture leans toward holiday parties, team lunches, sports leagues, and charity events, with mentorship and newcomer integration frequently woven into these gatherings, per WelcomeAide's newcomer guide to Canadian workplace culture. The traditional after-work drinks model, however, is visibly eroding: HRD Canada's 2026 reporting found 81% of surveyed employees wished their employers offered more bonding activities during work hours rather than after, while 64% said they still feel some obligation to attend after-hours events despite that preference — a genuine gap between stated preference and lived pressure.

What's culturally distinct is how socially acceptable it is to protect personal time. Canadians are widely described as fiercely guarding time outside work, and declining an optional after-hours social event carries less professional risk than in many other markets — non-drinkers face essentially no stigma for choosing a non-alcoholic option at whatever events they do attend.

### The Reckoning

The inversion here is about which ritual is treated as mandatory. In Singapore, lunch — squarely inside the workday — is the non-negotiable bonding event, while evening socializing, though frequent, is a layer added on top. In Canada, lunch is often genuinely optional and personal, while after-work events, though increasingly resented, remain the default venue for team bonding that employees haven't yet succeeded in moving into work hours.

Hofstede Insights data helps explain the difference in stakes: Singapore's strongly collectivist culture (scoring around 20 on Individualism) makes group participation — including at meals — a meaningful marker of belonging, while Canada's high individualism supports a norm where personal time is legitimately defensible, even against a manager's implicit expectations. Neither country lacks social ritual; they've just placed the "optional" label on opposite halves of the day.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A respondent comparing Singaporean and Tokyo work cultures, writing as someone exhausted by Tokyo's pace, noted that Singapore's team lunch culture felt intense by Western standards but genuinely less punishing than Japan's, since declining an occasional lunch in Singapore didn't carry the same social cost that skipping equivalent rituals did in Tokyo.*

> *Quora — Someone describing Singaporean corporate culture broadly said the office functions as a "second home" for many employees, with team meals and after-work gatherings blending into the same continuous expectation of visible presence rather than reading as clearly separate professional and social spheres.*

> *teamblind.com — In a thread asking why "frequent team events" are marketed as an employee benefit, several posters pushed back, arguing that mandatory-feeling social events after hours function more as an unpaid extension of the workday than a genuine perk — a sentiment that recurred across multiple company-specific Blind channels, including Canadian offices.*

> *teamblind.com — A separate discussion on drinking with coworkers found opinions split: some described after-work drinks as genuinely optional and low-pressure at their Canadian employer, while others said avoiding them entirely, over time, subtly affected how included they felt on the team, even without any explicit consequence.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Singapore, budget social energy for lunch as a daily, near-mandatory ritual, and treat evening team dinners as periodically important rather than constant. If you're moving to Canada, know that declining after-work drinks costs you less than you'd expect, but don't disappear from every optional event either — occasional presence is what keeps the relationship credit balance from running dry. The line I'd give a friend: in Singapore, they'll notice if you skip lunch; in Canada, they'll barely notice if you skip the bar, but they will notice if you never show up anywhere at all.
