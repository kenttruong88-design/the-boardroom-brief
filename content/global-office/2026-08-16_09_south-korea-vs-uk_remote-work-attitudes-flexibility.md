# South Korea Works From Home Half a Day a Week. The UK Just Made It Nearly Illegal to Say No.

**🇰🇷 South Korea · 🇬🇧 United Kingdom**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

South Korean employees average just 0.5 days of remote work per week — among the lowest figures recorded anywhere — despite 72% of APAC professionals telling researchers they actively want hybrid arrangements, against only 46% who currently have access to one. In the UK, the Employment Rights Act 2025 now requires employers to prove that refusing a flexible working request was actually reasonable, not just that they followed the correct process to deny it — a legal bar that has quietly shifted the default from "convince us" to "convince us you're wrong." One country has a workforce begging for remote flexibility it mostly can't get. The other just wrote the begging into statute.

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |

| Push for structured hybrid arrangements where labor market pressure allows it — tight tech talent markets are slowly forcing more formal policies | Assume asking for remote flexibility will be well received at a traditional large firm — cultural resistance remains the norm outside specific sectors |
| Recognize that a request may face resistance on stated "efficiency" grounds | Take low remote-work efficiency claims fully at face value — Korea's own OECD ranking shows the country underperforms on output-per-hour even with high in-office hours logged |
| Leave when your team leaves, if working in a traditional office culture | Assume this cultural pressure is disappearing quickly — remote work adoption dropped further post-COVID rather than continuing to rise |
| Target industries actively offering hybrid as a hiring differentiator | Expect a blanket national shift toward flexibility — change is happening firm by firm, not sector-wide |

### The Republic of the Full Office

South Korea's remote work resistance isn't primarily a policy failure — it's a structural and cultural one, compounded by a workplace management style that research consistently describes as bureaucratic, hierarchical, and heavy on documentation and meetings rather than delegation. Dense city layouts, an entrenched in-office presence culture, and a workforce still accustomed to six-day, high-intensity weeks all work against remote adoption even where the technology and roles would clearly support it. The result is a genuine mismatch: 72% of professionals across the APAC region want hybrid arrangements, but only 46% actually have access, and South Korea sits toward the more restrictive end of that gap even among regional peers.

What's forcing incremental change isn't cultural conversion — it's labor economics. Tight competition for tech and analytics talent is pushing a growing share of Korean firms toward codified hybrid policies with structured autonomy, since younger, in-demand workers in those specific sectors are increasingly able to select employers on flexibility grounds. Everywhere else, the presumption remains firmly in-office, and the justification offered — that remote work drops efficiency — sits awkwardly against Korea's own middling OECD productivity ranking, a country that logs long hours and comparatively low output per hour even with nearly everyone physically present.

### The Kingdom of the Justified Refusal

Britain's shift has run through legislation rather than labor scarcity. The Employment Relations (Flexible Working) Act 2023 first made requesting flexible work a day-one right for every employee, doubling the number of annual requests permitted and shortening the employer's response window. The Employment Rights Act 2025 went further, raising the actual bar for denial: employers must now demonstrate the refusal itself was reasonable, not merely that they followed the correct procedural steps in reaching it — a meaningful legal shift from process-compliance to substantive justification.

The numbers show real but uneven progress: roughly a quarter of British workers now operate in some hybrid arrangement and 13% work fully remote, with 74% of UK organizations offering some form of hybrid setup. But the access gap by income is stark — only 29% of workers earning under £20,000 have remote options against 80% of those earning over £50,000 — and momentum has genuinely started reversing at the margins, with 65% of hybrid-offering employers already mandating minimum office attendance and 14% actively planning to increase those requirements over the coming year.

### The Reckoning

The two countries are, in a sense, converging from opposite directions toward the same modest middle ground — Korea inching toward flexibility through sector-specific talent pressure, Britain inching back toward the office through employer-driven mandate creep even as its legal framework keeps strengthening workers' right to ask. Neither country has actually delivered universal remote access; they've just built different mechanisms for rationing it. Korea rations by industry and seniority within a culture that still treats presence as the default signal of commitment. Britain rations by income and negotiating power within a legal system that increasingly requires employers to justify saying no, but still lets them say it.

The irony worth sitting with: Britain's legal right to request flexible work is now genuinely strong on paper, yet the practical direction at many employers is toward more mandated office time, not less. Korea has almost no legal framework pushing toward flexibility at all, yet market pressure in specific high-demand sectors is quietly doing more to shift actual behavior than British legislation has managed for lower-income workers still stuck at 29% access.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Blind — An employee at a Seoul-based tech firm known for atypically flexible policies described colleagues at more traditional Korean companies reacting with open disbelief on hearing about monthly work-from-home reimbursements, treating it less as a perk than as evidence the company must not be a serious one.*

> *Medium — A writer comparing Korean office presence culture to its productivity data argued that the two were causally linked in the wrong direction from what management assumed: long in-office hours were substituting for, rather than producing, efficient output, since so much of the visible time logged was spent in extended meetings and layered documentation rather than actual work.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a question about UK flexible working rights explained that the practical value of the 2025 legal changes wasn't that requests always got approved, but that employers now had to put a real, defensible reason on paper if they refused — a small change that made a surprising number of previously automatic rejections quietly disappear.*

> *Internations London — A relocating professional described discovering that her new UK employer's "hybrid" policy technically meant three mandatory office days that had crept up from two within her first year, and said the lesson was to ask not just about current policy but about its direction of travel before accepting an offer.*

> *r/expats-adjacent forum — A foreign employee at a Korean company's international division described being one of the only people in the office who left when the workday officially ended, and said it took nearly a year of quiet observation to understand that colleagues staying late weren't necessarily still working, but were reluctant to be the first to leave.*

### Conclusion

If flexibility matters to your decision, read past the headline policy in both countries. In South Korea, target the specific sector and firm — tech, marketing, digital services, and companies competing hard for scarce talent — rather than assuming national culture will bend for you. In the UK, ask directly about the trajectory of any hybrid policy, not just its current terms, since the legal right to request flexibility is strengthening even as many employers quietly pull the actual offer back. I'd tell a friend: in Seoul, watch what your future colleagues do at 6pm before you sign; in London, ask your future employer what their hybrid policy looked like eighteen months ago, not just today.
