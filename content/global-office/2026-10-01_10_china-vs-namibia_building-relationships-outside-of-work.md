# In China, Friendship Is Built One Baijiu Shot at a Time. In Namibia, It's Built Around a Fire, and Nobody's Impressed by the Fire Anymore.

**🇨🇳 China · 🇳🇦 Namibia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

A Chinese data analyst drinks until her boss is satisfied because her career depends on it. A Namibian newspaper columnist complains that his country's wealthiest citizens have nothing more imaginative to offer their friends than a braai, again, for the thousandth time. Both countries build real relationships outside the office. Neither one, it turns out, finds the process entirely comfortable — just for opposite reasons.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇨🇳 China

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat the business dinner as where real decisions actually happen — formal meetings often just confirm what dinner already settled. | Rush to business talk during the meal itself; contract discussions typically follow the meal, not during it. |
| Wait to be shown your seat; placement (facing the door for the honored guest, host directly opposite) carries real meaning. | Pick your own seat at a formal dinner — it can read as a hierarchy misstep. |
| Pace yourself during baijiu toasts and touch your glass below a senior colleague's as a sign of respect. | Feel obligated to empty your glass on every "gānbēi" — pacing is increasingly accepted, especially among younger professionals pushing back on the culture. |
| Recognize guanxi-building as a genuine, long-term investment, not a one-off networking event. | Expect to skip it without consequence; declining repeatedly is documented to affect bonuses, project assignments, and advancement. |

#### 🇳🇦 Namibia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Accept a braai invitation warmly — it's the default, most common form of genuine social bonding, not a brush-off. | Expect a sophisticated restaurant or gallery scene to be where meaningful relationship-building happens, even among wealthier Namibians. |
| Show up and actively mingle; Namibians are widely described by newcomers as unusually welcoming to foreigners. | Assume the social scene lacks depth just because the format (home, farm, fire, meat) repeats constantly. |
| Seek out specific cultural venues (theatres, music rooms) when you want a different register of socializing. | Expect those venues to be the norm — one columnist bluntly called the elite's reliance on braai culture over urban cultural investment "a sad indictment." |
| Use weekend trips to nearby towns as a genuine, common way locals extend and deepen a new relationship. | Mistake Namibia's small social world for a closed one — accounts from newcomers consistently describe it as easy to enter. |

---

**China**

Relationship-building in Chinese professional life runs through guanxi — a network of personal connection and mutual obligation — and its most visible ritual is the business dinner, where, as one guide for foreign professionals puts it, "the real negotiations happen later, once rapport is built and mutual respect is established." The dinner has its own grammar: the most honored guest sits facing the door, the host sits directly opposite, and guests wait to be seated rather than choosing for themselves. Baijiu, the potent traditional liquor, anchors the ritual through toasting — touching one's glass below a senior colleague's as a mark of respect, and navigating the literal meaning of "gānbēi" (dry the glass) against the practical need to still be standing afterward.

The obligation underneath the ritual is real, and increasingly contested. A 26-year-old data analyst, Zhang Ke, described attending mandatory monthly drinking sessions despite having no client-facing role, including a banquet where a client held her female colleague's hand while her own boss made her visibly uncomfortable — and said plainly of the pressure to drink: "my only choice is to drink up, or it would affect my career." A 27-year-old manager, Zhou Wenkai, attending roughly three banquets a month despite a weak stomach and genuine distaste for the practice, tried spitting alcohol into towels and then secretly diluting red wine with watermelon juice — until he was caught, and told by a supervisor to "get rid of your bookish demeanor and make more toasts." A 2021 survey found 84% of young professionals expressing outright disgust with the culture, while 14% said they still participated under direct coercion — a gap between genuine preference and professional necessity that defines the whole institution.

**Namibia**

Namibian social life outside work runs through a format so dominant it's become a subject of domestic self-criticism: the braai (barbecue), typically at a home, farm, or lodge, with meat, fire, and loud music as the near-universal constants. A Namibian opinion columnist described the country's professional and wealthy class as locked into exactly this script — "'Home.' 'Braai.' 'Something cold.' Music loud enough for the neighbours to hear" — arguing that even Namibia's elite, despite real accumulated wealth and education, has failed to develop a parallel "urban culture of sophistication," instead building, in his words, "a larger, more expensive version of the village from which we supposedly escaped." He framed this less as simple preference than as a form of "cultural insecurity," urging investment in restaurants, galleries, and performance venues as alternative, deliberately chosen relationship-building spaces, rather than defaulting endlessly to the fire pit.

For newcomers, though, the same format that draws domestic criticism tends to register as a genuine asset rather than a limitation. One foreign student's account of settling into Windhoek described making friends there as "extremely easy," explicitly denying encountering any discrimination as a foreigner and calling Namibians "very friendly" — while also pointing newcomers toward specific cultural venues (a local theatre, a music venue) and nearby coastal weekend trips as additional, genuinely available ways to deepen new relationships beyond the default braai circuit.

**The Reckoning**

China's relationship-building operates through structured, ritualized obligation with real professional stakes attached — refusing the baijiu toast isn't a personal boundary, it's a documented career risk, which helps explain why younger Chinese professionals are now visibly, if cautiously, pushing back against a system they widely find exhausting rather than quietly accepting it as tradition. Namibia (not individually scored in Hofstede's original study, though broader Southern African patterns skew collectivist and relationship-dense, similar to other sub-Saharan clusters) runs on comparatively low-stakes, low-ritual informality — nobody's career depends on attending a specific braai, which is precisely what frees domestic critics to complain that the format has gotten culturally stale, a complaint that itself would be a strange luxury in the Chinese context, where the problem isn't boredom with the ritual, it's the ritual's enforceability.

The shared thread, oddly, is that both countries' relationship-building happens almost entirely outside anything resembling a structured networking event — around a dinner table in one case, around an open fire in the other — which means a newcomer evaluating either country by its formal professional-development culture alone will miss where the actual relationships, and in China's case actual career outcomes, get built.

### Conclusion

If you're building a career in China, understand upfront that declining the baijiu circuit isn't a neutral personal choice — it's a documented, quantifiable career cost, however unfair that feels to a newcomer used to treating after-work socializing as optional. If you're building a life in Namibia, show up to the braai without cynicism; it really is the entry point locals use to welcome outsiders in, even if homegrown critics wish the national social imagination stretched a little further past the fire pit.

The honest version: in China, you'll know you've arrived when your liver starts negotiating on your behalf. In Namibia, you'll know you've arrived when someone hands you a plate of meat and stops treating you like a guest.
