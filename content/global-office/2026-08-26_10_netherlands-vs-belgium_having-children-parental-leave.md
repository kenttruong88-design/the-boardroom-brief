# The Netherlands vs. Belgium: Where Having a Baby Comes With the Best Paperwork

## 🇳🇱 Netherlands · 🇧🇪 Belgium

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Somewhere in The Hague, a government spreadsheet quietly decided that the average Dutch mother should work 0.7 of a job, forever. Somewhere in Brussels, a set of parents registered their unborn child for daycare in the fourth month of pregnancy, on the theory that a fetus with a waitlist number has a fighting chance. Both countries will tell you, with a straight face, that they have solved parenthood. According to the OECD, roughly 73 percent of Dutch women work part-time, nearly three times the OECD average — a statistic the Netherlands presents as liberation and everyone else quietly files under "gilded cage." Belgium, meanwhile, offers a more American-style bargain: generous leave on paper, brutal scarcity in practice, and a childcare system so oversubscribed that ambition is now measured in weeks of gestation. Neither country is lying about being family-friendly. They are simply friendly to family in the way a maze is friendly to a mouse — technically navigable, occasionally rewarding, and designed by someone who has never personally had to find the cheese.

#### 🇳🇱 Netherlands

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register for kinderopvang (daycare) the moment the pregnancy test is positive; waitlists in Amsterdam and Utrecht run 6–18 months | Assume a spot will materialize after the baby arrives — by then you're behind parents who registered at eight weeks |
| Claim kinderopvangtoeslag; combined incomes under roughly €56,412 can get up to 96% of costs refunded in 2026 | Pay full daycare sticker price without applying — providers won't remind you, and the subsidy isn't retroactive indefinitely |
| Expect your partner to take 1 week of fully paid geboorteverlof plus up to 5 more weeks at 70% pay via UWV | Assume "paternity leave" means what it does back home — the Dutch version is generous but UWV-administered, not employer-optional |
| Ask about a 4-day work week after birth — it's the cultural default, not a favor | Push for full-time return and expect it to read as ambition rather than an odd flex |
| Use your legal right (children under 8) to request reduced hours — employers can only refuse for real business reasons | Negotiate this informally and hope goodwill covers it; put the request in writing |
| Book kraamzorg (a maternity nurse who visits daily for the first week); it's covered by basic insurance | Skip it thinking you'll manage solo — most expats without local family call it non-negotiable |

#### 🇧🇪 Belgium

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register with multiple crèches from the fourth month of pregnancy — priority goes to full-time working parents | Register with just one preferred nursery; backup applications are standard practice, not paranoia |
| Know that each parent gets an individual right to 4 months' parental leave, usable in flexible chunks until the child turns 12 | Confuse "entitled to leave" with "easy to schedule" — most of it is unpaid or low-paid and taken in reduced-hours form instead |
| Consider a private (non-subsidized) crèche if the public waitlist is long — spots open faster, at a real cost premium | Assume private and public daycare cost the same; budget €400–600/month for private options |
| Expect statutory maternity leave (15 weeks) and 2 weeks of fully paid paternity/birth leave, plus additional lower-paid weeks | Expect Belgian norms on returning to full-time work quickly — informal pressure to extend leave or go part-time is real |
| Talk to HR before leave about your return date and hours | Let leave "informally extend" without a plan — research links this pattern to steeper long-term earnings losses |
| Lean on ONE (Wallonia/Brussels) or Kind & Gezin (Flanders) case workers for subsidized placement questions | Expect the system to explain itself in English; bring a Dutch- or French-speaking friend to bureaucratic appointments |

### The Netherlands: A Nation Optimized for the 0.8 FTE Parent

The Dutch parental leave system is, on paper, one of the better-funded in Western Europe: 16 weeks of fully paid maternity leave, up to six weeks of birth leave for partners, and 26 weeks of parental leave per parent (the first nine paid at 70 percent) usable any time before the child turns eight. What the brochure omits is what happens next, which the OECD has helpfully quantified: the Netherlands has the highest part-time employment rate among mothers in the OECD, with about 73 percent of Dutch women working part-time versus 28 percent of men. This is not incidental drift — it is the system working as designed. Dutch law grants parents of children under eight a statutory right to request reduced hours, and employers can only refuse for compelling business reasons, which has quietly turned the four-day week into a default rather than a negotiation. The upside is real: nobody in Amsterdam looks at you strangely for picking up your kid on a Wednesday. The less-discussed cost, per the OECD's own analysis, is that this "part-time and partly equal" arrangement widens the gender gap in earnings, pensions, and promotion into management — a trade-off the Netherlands has made so thoroughly normal that most Dutch parents genuinely don't experience it as a trade-off at all.

### Belgium: Leave That's Generous Until You Try to Use It

Belgium's statutory offer looks respectable next to its neighbor: 15 weeks of maternity leave, a fully paid two-week paternity slice, and a genuinely flexible four-month individual parental leave entitlement per parent, extendable in reduced-hours installments until the child turns 12. The catch is capacity, not policy. Subsidized crèche places under ONE (Wallonia/Brussels) or Kind & Gezin (Flanders) are scarce enough that parents are formally advised to apply while pregnant, and priority skews toward full-time dual-earner households — a quiet penalty for anyone easing back part-time. Belgian academic research from ULB's DULBEA institute has tracked what happens when the system's informal slack absorbs the gap: female earnings can fall by as much as 43 percent in the years following childbirth, driven partly by a documented rise in mothers taking extended leave via sickness absence rather than a negotiated return-to-work plan. Belgium, in other words, has built a leave system generous enough to look progressive in a spreadsheet and porous enough that the actual burden of shortfall still lands, disproportionately, on mothers.

### The Reckoning

Put the two systems side by side and the irony sharpens: the Netherlands solves the childcare capacity problem by making working less the default, while Belgium keeps the traditional full-time expectation and simply runs short on the childcare to support it. Both outcomes produce the same headline — mothers doing more of the unpaid adjustment — but the Dutch version is codified and comfortable, while the Belgian version is improvised and occasionally frantic. Hofstede Insights' country comparison notes that both nations score as highly individualist, yet the Netherlands pairs that individualism with unusually low power distance, which helps explain why a Dutch employee can simply request a four-day week and expect it granted, while a Belgian employee's more hierarchical workplace culture leaves the same request feeling like a favor rather than a right.

The counterintuitive finding is that "family-friendly" rankings, which both countries win regularly, measure leave generosity and childcare spending — not what happens to a career afterward. On that second metric, the Netherlands quietly caps women's earning trajectory by design, while Belgium caps it by attrition. Neither is obviously better; they are simply different flavors of the same asterisk.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *British Expats forum — A mother who'd just relocated to Enschede described being bounced for weeks between a kindergarten willing to take her daughter and a job-agency caseworker who refused to authorize subsidized care until she'd started an integration course — which itself couldn't start until the childcare was sorted, a bureaucratic loop she only escaped by threatening to pay full price herself.*

> *British Expats forum — A longer-term resident advised a struggling newcomer to look into a gastouder (home-based guest parent) instead of a formal daycare centre, noting it was far more flexible on hours and days, and added the specific tip that Wednesday and Friday slots are easiest to secure since Dutch schools run half-days and many parents are already off work then.*

> *Expat.com Brussels forum — A parent recounted registering for a crèche place at six weeks pregnant, calling it standard practice in Brussels and noting that simply landing a spot, subsidized or not, felt like a genuine achievement rather than an administrative formality.*

> *Personal expat essay (Scary Mommy) — A mother far from her extended family described the Dutch kraamzorg nurse who visited daily during the first postpartum week as a "lifeline," handling feeding and bathing support that she hadn't expected any healthcare system to simply provide as standard.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A London-based tech worker weighing a move to Amsterdam for the sake of young kids noted that the Netherlands' 30 percent tax ruling sweetens the numbers, but isn't permanent, and that the calculation ultimately came down to trading marginally lower headline pay for a genuinely better environment for children and easier access to flexible schedules.*

### The Conclusion

If the question is "which country makes it administratively survivable to have a baby while employed," the honest answer is that both do, and both extract a price for it. The Netherlands will let you keep your job and your sanity by quietly expecting you to work less forever, a bargain that suits people who value time over title. Belgium will let you keep full-time ambitions intact on paper, provided you're willing to spend a pregnancy playing daycare roulette and absorb the occasional bureaucratic ambush. Choose the Netherlands if your career goals were already comfortable with a four-day week; choose Belgium if you'd rather fight a waitlist than a culture. Either way, budget more energy for the paperwork than for the parenting — the paperwork, unlike the toddler, does not eventually learn to sleep through the night.
