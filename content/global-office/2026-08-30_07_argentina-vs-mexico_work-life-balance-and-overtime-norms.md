# Argentina Clocks Out for Dinner at 10 PM. Mexico Never Really Clocks Out.

🇦🇷 Argentina · 🇲🇽 Mexico

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Mexico logs more hours on the job than any other country tracked by the OECD — 2,207 a year, well clear of the OECD average of 1,683 — and yet scores 97 out of 100 on Hofstede's indulgence index, a measure of how freely a culture lets people enjoy life. Argentina, 35 points less indulgent by the same measure, nonetheless treats a 9 PM dinner reservation as premature. Both countries passed sweeping labor reforms in 2026. Neither promises anyone gets to leave earlier.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇷 Argentina

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Book client dinners for 9 PM or later — earlier reads as a business-hours snack | Schedule a "quick" 6 PM call and expect it to end quickly |
| Budget for the aguinaldo — a mandatory 13th-month salary paid in two installments | Assume a stated meeting end-time is the actual end-time |
| Expect 14 to 35 paid vacation days by law, scaled to seniority under Ley 20744 | Treat the 2026 "banco de horas" reform as fewer hours — it just reschedules them |
| Build 15-30 minutes of slack into every meeting start time | Confuse relationship-building small talk for a delay tactic |
| Confirm overtime tiers: 50% weekday surcharge, 100% after 1 PM Saturday, Sundays, holidays | Plan anything requiring focus for the hour after Friday lunch |

#### 🇲🇽 Mexico

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Clear 2 to 4 PM for la comida — a work ritual conducted at a restaurant | Count comida toward your eight legal hours; it's usually unpaid |
| Expect the unofficial workday to run 9 AM to 7 or 8 PM regardless of the legal cap | Assume the 40-hour workweek is already in effect — it phases in through 2030 |
| Track hours electronically once the mandate starts in January 2027 | Exceed the weekly overtime cap — excess now costs a 200% wage penalty |
| Use your "Vacaciones Dignas" entitlement: 12 days minimum after year one, plus a 25% bonus | Mistake desk presence for productivity — the culture often does, the arithmetic doesn't |
| Ask directly whether "flexible hours" means flexible start time or flexible everything | Sign anything advertising work-life balance without asking the actual clock-out time |

## Argentina

Argentina's labor law sets a standard workweek of 48 hours across six days, with overtime paid at a 50% weekday surcharge and 100% after 1 PM on Saturdays, Sundays, and holidays — figures the 2026 Ley de Modernización Laboral left untouched even as it rewired everything around them ([Perfil](https://www.perfil.com), [Diario de Cuyo](https://www.diariodecuyo.com.ar)). The reform's real addition is the *banco de horas*: employers and workers can bank extra hours worked on a heavy day against lighter hours later, up to 30 hours monthly and 200 annually without prior sign-off. It is presented as flexibility. It functions as deferral — the hours still get worked, just not paid as overtime the week they happen.

What the law protects generously is time off. Under Article 150 of the Ley de Contrato de Trabajo, vacation scales with tenure: 14 calendar days under five years' seniority, rising to 21, 28, and 35 days past twenty — calendar days, so weekends count toward the total ([argentina.gob.ar](https://www.argentina.gob.ar)). Layered on top is the aguinaldo, a mandatory thirteenth-month salary paid in two installments.

None of this explains why the Argentine workday feels endless despite ending at a reasonable hour. The culprit is dinner. Buenos Aires restaurants don't fill until after 9 PM, and the entire evening — drinks, client dinners, catch-ups — shifts accordingly, so "end of the workday" and "actually going home" sit several loosely professional hours apart.

## Mexico

Mexico holds a distinction no employer brochure leads with: the longest average working hours among OECD member countries, at roughly 2,207 a year, against an OECD average of 1,683 ([Katadata](https://databoks.katadata.co.id)). The legal maximum stood at 48 hours weekly, but enforcement was historically loose enough that the cap functioned as a suggestion — a gap the government's May 2026 constitutional reform, signed by President Claudia Sheinbaum, now targets directly ([Littler](https://www.littler.com), [Norton Rose Fulbright](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com)).

The reform phases the standard workweek down from 48 to 40 hours by 2030, with overtime caps rising gradually from 9 hours weekly in 2026 to 12 by 2030, distributed across no more than four days. Exceed the cap and the penalty jumps to 200% of ordinary wages. From January 2027, employers must maintain electronic time records for every worker, with fines up to 5,000 UMAs per employee for noncompliance ([DLA Piper](https://knowledge.dlapiper.com)). The reform explicitly bars any accompanying pay cut — worth noting given how often "reduced hours" reforms elsewhere have quietly meant reduced pay too.

What the reform doesn't yet touch is la comida, the two-to-four-hour midday meal that functions as business infrastructure, unpaid and uncounted toward the daily total. A nominal eight-hour shift routinely becomes a ten-hour commitment once travel, the meal, and the return are factored in — and skipping it isn't really optional.

## The Reckoning

Line the two countries up and the paradox sharpens. Mexico scores 97 on Hofstede's indulgence index — about as high as the measure goes — while logging the most working hours in the OECD. Argentina scores a comparatively restrained 62, yet nobody in Buenos Aires would call the culture joyless; they'd simply note that joy, like dinner, starts around 9 PM. Indulgence measures attitude toward pleasure, not the hours standing between a worker and it.

The 2026 reforms diverge in direction as sharply as the cultures do. Argentina's law deregulates — it hands employers and workers a flexible instrument, the hour bank, and trusts them to use it fairly. Mexico's reform does the opposite: it compresses the legal week, raises the cost of exceeding it, and backs it with mandatory electronic tracking. One country is loosening its rules on when overtime happens; the other is tightening its rules on whether it should happen at all.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A commenter explaining Mexico's long-hours reputation described how the unpaid, unofficial comida block turns a contractual eight-hour day into a ten-hour one before anyone's clocked any overtime, and how visible desk time still outweighs measured output in a lot of offices.*

> *Quora — An expat weighing the pros and cons of working in Argentina noted that professional relationships there run on personal trust and loyalty to individuals rather than the company on the letterhead, and that skipping small talk to "get to business" reads as rude rather than efficient.*

> *Buenos Aires Expats Community (baexpats.org) — A newer arrival asked, half-serious, when locals actually eat dinner, having shown up to a restaurant at 7 PM to find it empty; regulars explained the whole evening simply runs two to three hours later than they were used to.*

> *Blind (teamblind.com) — A reviewer of a Mexico City "Success Engineer" role, advertised as flexible and client-facing, described the actual job as an always-on support desk in disguise — a warning that titles promising autonomy are worth checking against the ticket queue first.*

> *Hacker News (Ask HN thread on unpaid overtime) — A commenter argued that where extra hours go unenforced by law, "voluntary" overtime calcifies into an unspoken norm within a year, and the only real defense is negotiating explicit hour boundaries before signing, not after starting.*

## Conclusion

Managing across both markets means accepting the legal workday and the real one are, for now, different documents. In Mexico, that gap closes on a fixed schedule through 2030, backed by wage penalties and mandatory time-tracking — plan accordingly, and don't book anything urgent for the comida window. In Argentina, the gap isn't closing so much as being formally acknowledged and rescheduled, one banked hour at a time, while the actual social day simply keeps ending later.

Neither country has produced a workplace where the clock and calendar agree, which is either a Latin American problem or, more likely, the same problem everywhere else, with better dinner reservations.
