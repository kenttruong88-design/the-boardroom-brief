# The Corner Office Has No Corners: A Boss-Employee Survival Guide for Egypt and Sweden

🇪🇬 Egypt · 🇸🇪 Sweden

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Cairo, an employee who contradicts the boss in a meeting is committing a small social offense. In Stockholm, an employee who *doesn't* contradict the boss in a meeting is committing a small social offense — silence reads as disengagement, not deference. Hofstede Insights scores Egypt at 70 on power distance versus Sweden's 31, one of the widest gaps the index records between two trading partners, and nothing captures it better than watching a newly arrived manager try to run the same meeting format in both places and fail identically, for opposite reasons.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇪🇬 Egypt

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address managers by title plus surname until invited otherwise | Use first names with a senior figure on day one |
| Let the most senior person speak first and make the final call | Publicly contradict your boss in front of the team |
| Build a personal rapport before pushing a business ask | Treat the relationship as purely transactional |
| Expect decisions to be made by one person, quickly, after consultation | Assume a group discussion means a group decision |
| Dress sharply and arrive on time, even if the room doesn't | Interpret formality as coldness — it's usually warmth in a suit |
| Route disagreements privately, after the meeting | Send a bluntly worded email challenging a decision |

#### 🇸🇪 Sweden

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use first names with everyone, including your CEO | Ask for a corner office or a reserved parking spot |
| Speak up in meetings — silence is read as disengagement | Wait to be told what to do; take ownership unprompted |
| Leave on time and take fika breaks | Stay late to "show commitment" — nobody is impressed |
| Expect consensus-building to take longer than you'd like | Push for a fast, top-down decision to save time |
| Stay home when sick, fully | Come in "for a few hours" while contagious |
| Raise concerns about a manager's idea directly, calmly | Assume raising a concern is insubordination |

Egypt's management culture runs on a logic that predates the modern office by several millennia and has, if anything, hardened under it. According to Hofstede Insights, Egypt's power distance score of 70 places it firmly among societies where hierarchy is viewed as natural rather than negotiable — subordinates expect to be told what to do, and bosses expect to be the ones telling. Commisceo Global's management guide to Egypt describes the dominant style as "consultative authority": a manager may solicit opinions widely, sometimes at length, but the decision and the accountability for it remain his alone, and no one in the room mistakes the consultation for a vote.

That authority is inseparable from relationship. Business etiquette guides consistently note that Egyptian professional life runs on trust built face-to-face, over tea, before any contract is discussed — a tea-maker is a fixture in most Cairo offices, not an eccentricity. The World Bank's Enterprise Surveys data shows small firms make up 89 percent of Egyptian businesses, and CAPMAS reports the private sector generated 98 percent of new job vacancies in 2023, though only about 24 percent of those private-sector jobs were classified as permanent. Put those two facts together and the hierarchy makes structural sense: in a labor market this informal, a strong, visible boss is often the only stable institution an employee has.

Sweden built the opposite system on purpose. The country's power distance score of 31, per Hofstede, sits among the lowest in the world, and the OECD's Better Life Index backs the lived experience with numbers: only 1 percent of Swedish employees work very long hours, against an OECD average of 11 percent, and 77 percent of working-age Swedes hold a paid job. Pernilla Petrelius Karlberg of the Stockholm School of Economics, interviewed by The Local, describes the Swedish manager's job less as directing and more as "calling on co-workers to take ownership" — the near-opposite of micromanagement, and a style she traces to Sweden's history as a small, export-dependent economy that "had to collaborate" because it was "too small to quarrel."

The friction is that consensus takes time, and time is not universally regarded as a renewable resource. Harvard Business Review's coverage of cross-cultural authority notes that decision-making styles vary less by industry than by national default settings for who is allowed to hold the pen. In Sweden, everyone technically holds the pen, which produces excellent buy-in and, per Karlberg's own account, meetings where outsiders genuinely cannot identify the moment a decision was made — a problem serious enough that it strained flat organizations during the shift to remote work, when the informal cues that signaled "we've decided" disappeared along with the shared physical room.

**The Reckoning:** Move an Egyptian employee to Stockholm and the disorientation is rarely the informality — Swedes turning up in jeans is easy enough to accept. It's the demand for opinion. A newcomer trained to wait respectfully for direction reads a Swedish manager's open-ended question as either a test or an abdication of duty, when it is neither; it is simply how the system extracts commitment. Move a Swedish manager to Cairo and the failure mode inverts: she convenes a consultative meeting expecting the room to co-author the outcome, and instead gets polite silence followed by everyone waiting for her, the senior figure, to just decide — because in that room, that's her job.

Neither system is a flaw awaiting correction by the other's virtues. Egypt's clear chain of command allows for fast execution once a decision is made and a level of personal loyalty that Western HR departments spend fortunes trying to manufacture through engagement surveys. Sweden's flat structure produces the buy-in and low attrition that show up reliably in its OECD well-being rankings, at the cost of a decision-making tempo that can be maddening for anyone used to a single accountable owner. The honest takeaway is that "good management" is not a fixed target; it's a local dialect.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — A commenter on a thread about Middle Eastern management noted that bosses in the region often run stricter, more top-down operations than their European counterparts, and attributed it less to personality than to a system where authority substitutes for the institutional stability many workplaces lack.*

> *Quora — Someone describing their time as an expat employee in Sweden mentioned that colleagues were unfailingly friendly and helpful on a personal level, but that anything requiring a group decision moved at a pace that took real getting used to.*

> *The Local Sweden — A long-time columnist warned newcomers that staying late "to show commitment" backfires badly with a Swedish boss, who is more likely to conclude you can't manage your workload than to be impressed by the extra hour.*

> *The Local Sweden — In an interview, a Stockholm School of Economics researcher recounted how a Finnish team under a Swedish manager grew frustrated when the manager kept asking questions instead of giving direction — a mismatch of expectations about what leadership is supposed to sound like.*

> *Expat blog (JustLanded) — A foreign employee in Cairo wrote about being genuinely surprised by the near-universal presence of a dedicated office tea-maker, and by how much weight was placed on addressing colleagues by their formal title, long after the work itself had become routine.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing Cairo against Stockholm for the org chart alone, the practical advice is unglamorous: in Egypt, invest early in the relationship with whoever signs off on things, because that relationship *is* the process; in Sweden, invest early in having opinions ready to share, because withholding them will be read as a lack of ownership, not modesty. Neither adjustment is about becoming a different person — it's about recognizing that "respecting your boss" and "respecting your team" mean structurally different things depending on which side of the Mediterranean you've landed.

The honest wry line is this: Sweden will teach you that a good manager asks more questions than they answer, and Egypt will teach you that a good manager answers before you finish asking — pick your discomfort accordingly.
