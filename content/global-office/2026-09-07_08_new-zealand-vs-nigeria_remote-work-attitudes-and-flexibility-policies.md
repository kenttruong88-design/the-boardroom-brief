# New Zealand Gave Workers the Right to Disconnect. Nigeria Gave Them a Generator.

🇳🇿 New Zealand · 🇳🇬 Nigeria

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

On 1 July 2025, New Zealand enacted a law entitling employees to ignore work messages after hours without fear of reprisal — a legislative first for the country, and arguably the most Kiwi statute imaginable, since it formalizes a boundary most cultures merely assume. Roughly 8,500 miles northwest, in Lagos, the operative flexibility question is rarely whether you should answer that 11pm Slack message, but whether the power grid will still be running when you try. New Zealand debates the right to disconnect. Nigeria debates the right to stay connected at all. Both countries call this "remote work." Only one of them means it literally.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇳🇿 New Zealand

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Enjoy being unreachable after hours — it's a legal right since the July 2025 Right to Disconnect law | Assume "flexible" means "always on"; constant online status reads as a boundary problem, not diligence |
| Ask about your team's hybrid cadence upfront; arrangements vary widely between employers | Show up five days a week uninvited; you'll read as desperate or slightly suspicious |
| Budget four weeks of leave into a normal year, and expect colleagues to actually take it | Schedule anything urgent for late December through January; the country runs on summer-holiday time |
| Get your "communication windows" written into your onboarding paperwork | Read a slow email reply as a bad sign; it usually just means someone's at the beach |
| Notice that hierarchy still quietly shapes who gets flexibility approved | Mistake casual dress and first-name bosses for the absence of a chain of command |

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Buy a UPS or small inverter before week one — power, not discipline, is the real risk | Assume your employer's idea of "reliable connectivity" matches a generator-free flat |
| Run backup data plans on two networks; connectivity drops are routine | Book back-to-back video calls with no buffer; a dropped call mid-sentence is normal |
| Get time-zone and after-hours expectations in writing; "always on" is a cultural default in many sectors | Assume evenings and weekends are automatically yours; being reachable reads as commitment |
| Expect flexibility to flow from your manager's goodwill more than from formal policy | Trust a written flexible-work policy to mean much day to day |
| Treat foreign-client roles as the stable path, given the fast-growing ICT sector | Rely on domestic job boards alone; only ~17% of Nigerian jobs are remote |

### New Zealand

New Zealand's approach to remote work reads less like corporate policy and more like a settled cultural fact that Parliament recently got around to writing down. According to Stats NZ, roughly 34% of employed people work from home at least part-time, a share that has held steady since 2022 rather than receding post-pandemic. In the public service, 42% of staff work from home at least one day a week, and Public Service Commission surveys find 49% of managers believe the arrangement improves productivity, against just 9% who think it hurts. Employers have gotten the memo too: more than 70% now have some form of flexible or remote policy, per proHR.

Then, in July 2025, the sentiment got legal teeth. The Employment Relations Amendment Act gave workers the right to decline out-of-hours calls, texts, and emails without risking their jobs, with penalizing employers facing fines up to NZ$10,000. A separate four-day-week trial run by 4 Day Week Global reported an 8.2-out-of-10 satisfaction score, alongside a 44% drop in absenteeism and a 9% drop in resignations — evidence that Kiwi employers experimenting with less office time keep arriving at the same conclusion.

### Nigeria

Nigeria's flexibility conversation runs on a different axis entirely: less about whether you're allowed to work from home, more about whether the infrastructure will let you. Only about 17% of Nigerian jobs are remote — well below the global average of 28% — yet demand comfortably outstrips supply, with 55% of job seekers prioritizing hybrid or remote roles, per MyJobMag's 2026 data. The underlying economy is genuinely booming: ICT contributed 11.31% of real GDP in Q1 2026, up from 10.59% a year earlier, and Nigerians make up a large share of the roughly 17.5 million online freelancers working across Nigeria, Ghana, and South Africa combined.

But "remote" in Lagos or Abuja carries an asterisk that doesn't exist in Wellington. Power outages are routine enough that seasoned freelancers treat a UPS or small generator as a basic tool of the trade, not a luxury. Hofstede's cultural-dimensions research offers a structural explanation for why flexibility, where it exists, tends to be discretionary rather than codified: Nigeria scores roughly 80 on power distance against New Zealand's 12, meaning flexibility is granted by a manager's goodwill more often than guaranteed by policy. The result is a workforce that has become remarkably resourceful around its constraints — a politer way of saying the constraints haven't gone anywhere.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the irony sharpens: New Zealand needed a law to protect a boundary its culture already assumed, while Nigeria's workers are chasing a flexibility their infrastructure hasn't yet caught up to enabling. That 55% of Nigerian job seekers rank hybrid or remote work a top priority is, proportionally, a stronger signal of appetite than anything New Zealand's surveys turn up, where flexibility is common enough to be unremarkable. Wanting something badly is a reasonable response to not quite having it.

The more counterintuitive question is who ends up better prepared for a genuinely borderless job. A New Zealand hire arrives well-rested, well-boundaried, and accustomed to being taken at their word — excellent, assuming the wifi holds. A Nigerian hire arrives having already solved, on their own dime, problems most Western employers have never considered: unreliable power, patchy connectivity, a boss one hierarchy away. One pool has been protected from remote work's failure modes by policy; the other drilled in them by necessity. Employers hiring for resilience, not convenience, may be looking in the wrong hemisphere.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — a Nigeria-based professional asking about remote job options noted that most of the well-paying opportunities actually available locally required a foreign client or an existing international network; domestic remote listings paid a fraction of what freelance platforms offered in foreign currency.*

> *Medium — a Lagos-based writer described remote work as viable "only when I have electricity and internet," recalling video calls that froze mid-sentence and a single 1GB file that took thirty minutes to download.*

> *Medium — a Nigerian freelancer's advice to newcomers was blunt: budget for a UPS or small inverter before your first week, since power outages, not client demands, were what actually put deadlines at risk.*

> *Expatsblog.com — a Canadian expat interviewed about her move to New Zealand said the biggest adjustment was a pleasant one: colleagues genuinely disconnected after hours, with none of the reflexive pressure to stay reachable that she'd grown used to back home.*

> *Immigration New Zealand settler profile — a sonographer relocating from Chicago expected full-time hours to be the professional default, and was surprised to find nearly all her new colleagues worked part-time or reduced schedules as a matter of course, not an exception.*

### Conclusion

The practical calculus for anyone weighing these two moves isn't "which country is more remote-friendly," but "which kind of friction you'd rather manage." New Zealand offers a legally protected, culturally reinforced boundary between work and life, at the cost of a small, isolated labour market and a pace that can feel, to some arrivals, like retirement with meetings. Nigeria offers scale, hustle, and a genuinely fast-growing digital economy, at the cost of infrastructure you will need to personally engineer around — where a UPS isn't optional gear, it's onboarding.

Neither country is misrepresenting what it offers. New Zealand's flexibility is real and increasingly codified; Nigeria's flexibility is real and increasingly improvised. Choose New Zealand if you want your right to disconnect written into law. Choose Nigeria if you'd rather learn to disconnect the hard way — usually around 6pm, without warning, and not by choice.
