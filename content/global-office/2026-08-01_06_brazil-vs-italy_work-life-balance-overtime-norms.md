# The Ranking Lied: Italy Tops the Charts, Brazil Just Lives It

**🇧🇷 Brazil · 🇮🇹 Italy**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

The OECD says Italy has the best work-life balance in the developed world — a 9.4 out of 10, only 3% of employees working very long hours, practically a case study. InterNations expats living there rank it 47th out of 53 countries for the same thing. Brazil, meanwhile, has no such headline ranking to its name, a genuine overtime culture on the books, and somehow still manages to produce workers who report enjoying their lives more than the statistics predict. One country's paperwork is lying to you. The other one just never bothered to write the good version down.

### Brazil: No Ranking, Real Rhythm

Brazil doesn't top international work-life balance rankings, and its labor statistics are genuinely mixed: the legal work week is capped at 44 hours, but multiple sources, including The Brazil Business, describe actual hours frequently running 50 to 54 hours in practice, driven by what one analysis bluntly calls "a true culture of overtime" — part financial incentive, part fear of unemployment, part cultural expectation that visible extra effort signals commitment. OECD data complicates the picture further: only around 6% of Brazilian employees report working very long hours, below the OECD average of 10%, and full-time workers there spend roughly the OECD-average 15 hours a day on personal care and leisure.

What actually seems to protect Brazilian work-life balance isn't policy so much as culture: long lunch breaks averaging close to an hour, genuine use of vacation time rather than accrual-and-guilt, and a well-documented cultural emphasis on relationship-building and enjoying life alongside work. Remitly's global work-life balance research lists Brazil among strong performers on this front, and InterNations survey data shows the majority of expats there feeling fairly paid and reasonably balanced — a country whose actual lived experience outperforms its formal labor statistics, rather than the other way around.

### Italy: The Ranking That Doesn't Survive Contact

Italy's official numbers are genuinely striking: OECD's Better Life Index put it at the top of the entire ranking in 2020, with just 3% of employees working over 50 hours a week against a 10% OECD average, and 16.5 daily hours devoted to non-work activities. Ferragosto, the August 15 holiday, still functions as a real cultural checkpoint — many smaller and family-run businesses shut down for weeks around it, a genuine, calendar-wide permission to disconnect that few countries maintain at scale.

But The Local Italy's own reporting punctures the headline: Italy ranked 47th out of 53 countries in InterNations' 2023 Expat Insider survey for work-life balance, and no Italian city made Forbes' 2023 worldwide work-life balance index. The gap is explained by what the OECD's methodology doesn't fully capture — long, often multi-hour commutes into major cities, and a documented pattern of unpaid or under-compensated overtime in sectors without strong contract protections, with Quora accounts describing workers formally hired for 40 hours effectively delivering 60, the last 20 functionally unpaid. Hofstede's data situates Italy among cultures with an indulgence orientation — a stated cultural preference for leisure and enjoyment — but the lived experience for many employees, especially in fast-moving northern cities like Milan, diverges sharply from both the OECD ranking and the indulgent-Mediterranean stereotype.

### The Reckoning

Here's the trap for anyone reading rankings instead of talking to actual workers: Italy looks, on paper, like the platonic ideal of work-life balance, and Brazil looks, on paper, like a country with a real overtime problem. Reverse the framing and it holds up just as well — Brazil's culture actively protects leisure time in ways its labor statistics undersell, while Italy's statistics describe a labor reality that a meaningful share of its own workforce, and nearly half the expats surveyed about it, don't recognize as their actual experience. The honest read is that both countries have a real gap between formal number and lived texture — Brazil's gap runs more favorable than the data suggests, Italy's runs less favorable than the headline suggests.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *The Local Italy — Citing InterNations' own Expat Insider data, the outlet noted that Italy's 47th-place ranking out of 53 countries for work-life balance sat awkwardly beside the OECD's claim that Italy topped the world — a contradiction the piece argued was mostly explained by long commutes and unpredictable, unpaid overtime that formal statistics don't capture.*

> *Quora — Someone describing their experience working in Italy said they were formally contracted for 40 hours a week but regularly worked 60, with the final 20 hours essentially unpaid, and warned that this arrangement was more common than job postings ever admitted.*

> *Quora — A Brazilian professional pushed back on the stereotype that Brazilians don't work hard, pointing out that a standard 40-hour week is still the norm on paper, but adding, only half-joking, that Brazilians simply refuse to let overtime culture cancel their weekend plans the way it does elsewhere.*

> *Reddit — A worker in a Brazilian corporate job described real cultural pressure to stay visibly late even when the actual workload didn't require it, framing it as a performance of commitment aimed at bosses rather than a genuine productivity requirement.*

> *InterNations — A respondent to the Expat Insider survey highlighted Italy's August shutdown as a genuine, structurally protected break — unlike the "unlimited PTO nobody uses" pattern familiar from other countries — even while describing the rest of the year as far less relaxed than the OECD ranking implied.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving to Brazil, don't let the lack of a flashy ranking fool you — ask about real overtime norms in your specific sector, but expect your evenings and vacation days to actually be yours. If you're moving to Italy, don't plan your life around the OECD headline — ask pointed questions about unpaid overtime and commute expectations before you sign, and treat August as the one guarantee that's actually reliable. The honest version for a friend: trust the InterNations expats who are actually living it over the institutional ranking that's measuring something slightly different than your actual Tuesday.
