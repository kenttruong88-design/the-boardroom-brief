# In Zambia, a Proverb Delivers the Bad News. In Cambodia, a Smile Does.

**🇿🇲 Zambia · 🇰🇭 Cambodia**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Zambia's power distance score sits at 64 — a workplace where seniority is respected reflexively and negotiations stretch out through long, patient deliberation. Cambodia doesn't even have an official Hofstede score; researchers describe its cultural profile as skewed hard toward extremes on some dimensions and only slightly off-center on others, which is a very academic way of saying: good luck predicting exactly how indirect a "no" is going to be until you've been in the room for one.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇿🇲 Zambia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress formally for meetings — suits for men, modest formal wear for women, without exception | Show up underdressed assuming a relaxed regional climate means relaxed dress norms |
| Expect long, deliberate negotiations with extended discussion before any decision | Push for a fast yes/no; patience is read as respect, urgency as pressure |
| Listen for proverbs and softened language when a position is being pushed back on | Take a proverb-laden response as small talk rather than an actual answer |
| Show consistent deference to seniority — hierarchy is respected at all times, not just formally | Assume friendliness from a senior colleague loosens the expected hierarchy |
| Treat indirect pushback as a real signal, not indecision | Interpret a lack of direct disagreement as full agreement |
| Build in real time for relationship-based trust before expecting fast collaboration | Expect Western-paced deal cycles from a first or second meeting |

#### 🇰🇭 Cambodia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Read silence as potential disagreement — it often replaces an awkward "no" | Assume silence means agreement or that the topic has been resolved |
| Treat "yes" as sometimes meaning "I'm listening," not necessarily "I agree" | Take every "yes" at face value and act on it as a firm commitment |
| Soften eye contact periodically during conversation | Hold intense, sustained eye contact — it can read as a challenge |
| Speak softly, calmly, and avoid confrontational tones even under pressure | Raise your voice or show visible frustration — it damages trust immediately and durably |
| Respect local customs and hierarchy visibly, even in informal settings | Dismiss cultural norms as "just business" — locals notice and remember disrespect |
| Give space for a graceful non-answer rather than forcing a direct refusal | Corner a Cambodian counterpart into an explicit "no" in front of others |

### Zambia: Hierarchy, Patience, and the Proverb as Diplomatic Tool

Zambian meeting culture runs on visible respect for structure. [LinkedIn's guide to Zambian work culture](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/work-culture-etiquette-zambia-gnanissarie-wickramasinghe) and [REAB's business traditions overview](https://reab.pro/en/info/business-traditions-and-mentality/zambian-business-culture) both describe workplaces built on hierarchy and power, where respect for seniority is expected continuously, not just performed in formal settings. Meetings and negotiations tend to run long, with extended discussion and deliberation the norm rather than the exception — a pace that rewards patience and can frustrate newcomers used to faster deal cycles.

What makes Zambian communication distinct is its specific technique for softening disagreement: rather than a flat refusal, [research on cross-cultural business etiquette](https://acemoneytransfer.com/it/blog/understanding-workplace-customs-and-etiquette-abroad-a-guide-for-zambian-expat-workers) notes Zambians often use proverbs or indirect phrasing to convey pushback, avoiding direct confrontation while still communicating a real position. Formality extends to appearance too — suits for men, modest formal attire for women — signaling that respect for organizational structure starts before anyone speaks.

### Cambodia: Face, Silence, and the Ambiguous Yes

Cambodian communication is built almost entirely around preserving face for everyone in the room. [Cultural Atlas](https://culturalatlas.sbs.com.au/cambodian-culture/cambodian-culture-communication) describes an indirect style where negative topics get expressed in deliberately vague terms, and outright refusals are avoided in favor of smiling, nodding, and generally amenable-seeming responses that don't actually commit to anything. Disagreement often shows up as silence rather than a spoken objection — a Cambodian colleague who stays quiet in a meeting may be signaling real reservations, not simply having nothing to add.

The word "yes" itself carries this ambiguity: per [Cultural Atlas](https://culturalatlas.sbs.com.au/cambodian-culture/cambodian-culture-communication), it can mean genuine agreement, or it can function like a nod — an acknowledgment that someone is listening and understands, without endorsing the content. Non-verbal cues matter just as much: sustained direct eye contact can register as a challenge, so softening or diverting gaze periodically is part of maintaining a comfortable, non-confrontational exchange. [ClickUp's business etiquette guide](https://clickup.com/p/business-etiquette/cambodia) is blunt about the cost of getting this wrong: raised voices or visible frustration can permanently damage a working relationship in a way that's difficult to repair.

### The Reckoning

Both cultures avoid direct refusal — but the mechanism each uses is almost opposite in texture. Zambia's proverb-softened pushback is still, ultimately, a form of speech: something is being said, just indirectly, and a patient listener can usually parse the actual position being communicated. Cambodia's silence-and-smile approach often withholds a position altogether, leaving a foreign counterpart to infer disagreement from what wasn't said rather than from what was.

This means the exact skill that works well in one culture can actively mislead in the other. A newcomer trained to "listen for the proverb" in Zambia might keep waiting for equivalent verbal cues in a Cambodian meeting that simply never arrive — the silence itself is the message, and treating it as an absence of information rather than the information itself is the single most common cross-cultural misread between these two meeting styles.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Someone who had organized a business gathering in Cambodia and found no one showed up, despite earlier apparent interest, asked in frustration why Cambodians seemed to "not welcome" foreign collaboration — the more experienced responses reframed it gently, explaining that a lack of direct commitment during the invitation stage was very likely a soft decline that got missed for what it was.*

> *Quora — A respondent offering tips on doing business in Cambodia stated plainly that raising your voice or showing visible frustration with staff would get a foreign manager nowhere, and that the relationship, once damaged that way, rarely recovered to its prior footing.*

> *Quora — Someone asking about life and work in Lusaka as a foreigner was advised, memorably, to "be a citizen" rather than behave like an outsider — the responder described Zambians as fundamentally friendly and willing to help, but noted that visibly respecting local hierarchy and pace, rather than importing a faster foreign work style, was what actually earned trust.*

> *Expat.com, Lusaka forum — A contributor new to the city described their first several business meetings as unexpectedly long, with discussion looping back over settled points repeatedly, and came to understand this as thoroughness and relationship-testing rather than inefficiency once a local colleague explained the pattern.*

> *Quora — A poster explaining direct versus indirect communication in professional settings noted that misreading an indirect "no" as an open question was one of the most common and costly mistakes foreign businesspeople made across multiple Southeast Asian markets, Cambodia included, often leading to wasted follow-up effort chasing a decision that had effectively already been made.*

### Conclusion

If you're heading into meetings in either country, the operating principle is the same even though the specific tell is different: assume disagreement is being communicated somehow, and go looking for it rather than waiting for it to be stated outright. In Zambia, listen past the proverb. In Cambodia, listen to the silence and watch the eyes.

Priya's honest take, over a drink: in Lusaka, if the answer takes three sentences and a saying to arrive, it's probably a no wearing a very polite hat. In Phnom Penh, if the answer is just a smile, it's probably the same hat, worn even more quietly.
