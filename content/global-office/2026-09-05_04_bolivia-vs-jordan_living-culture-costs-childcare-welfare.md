# Bolivia vs. Jordan: Cheap Rent, Pricey Peace of Mind

🇧🇴 Bolivia · 🇯🇴 Jordan
*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

In Bolivia you can rent a furnished one-bedroom apartment for less than a typical Amman security deposit, and in Jordan that same landlord will still ask for a year of rent up front, in cash, before handing over a key. Both countries market themselves as affordable places to relocate. Neither mentions that "affordable" and "administratively frictionless" are unrelated variables, and that the real cost of living abroad shows up not in the grocery bill but in the paperwork, the daycare shortage, and the fine print of who is legally allowed to fall ill for free.

## Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇴 Bolivia

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Budget for altitude before you budget for rent — La Paz sits above 3,600 meters | Assume a daycare waitlist exists; only three in ten families with young children use any childcare service at all |
| Bring patience for the Ministry of Foreign Affairs land-title permit if buying | Buy land within 50 kilometers of a border — not on offer to foreigners |
| Register with the Social Security Fund if formally employed locally | Skip private insurance assuming the public system covers you — coverage for salaried expats is inconsistent |
| Ask which tenancy law applies — most leases still run on the 1959 Tenancy Law | Expect a mortgage; Bolivians overwhelmingly buy in cash |
| Price public daycare in La Paz first — $7–12 a month versus roughly $100 private | Be surprised when imported goods spike during a currency squeeze |

#### 🇯🇴 Jordan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Negotiate the rent schedule before signing — landlords routinely expect quarterly or annual lump sums | Expect the public health scheme to cover you; foreigners are never eligible, regardless of years resident |
| Buy private health insurance before your visa is finalized — a hard requirement for most residency categories | Plan to own more than one property; non-Arabs are capped at a single unit with a resale lock-in |
| Budget 20–30% of income for housing in West Amman | Bank on formal childcare; fewer than 3% of under-fives attend any registered facility |
| Confirm whether Social Security registration is included — it covers pension and injury, not health | Try to buy in a border, historic, or archaeological zone — off-limits by law |
| Get every verbal promise about utilities into the written lease | Assume a deposit is refundable without a fight |

## Bolivia: Cheap, Communal, and Administratively Improvised

Bolivia is, by nearly every consumer price measure, one of the least expensive places in the hemisphere to live. According to Numbeo's 2026 data, overall costs run roughly 74% below the United States and rent about 85% lower, putting a single person's non-rent monthly budget near $320 and a one-bedroom apartment anywhere from $300 to $700. Cheap does not mean simple: the World Bank notes the health system is a patchwork of a new public Unified Health System (SUS), which nominally covers the roughly 70% of Bolivians who previously had no insurance, alongside an employer-linked Social Security Fund and a private sector most foreigners quietly default to for anything beyond a routine consultation.

Childcare follows the same pattern of low cost, low institutional presence. Only three in ten Bolivian families with young children use any formal childcare, a fact rooted less in unavailability than in a cultural expectation that family, not the state, absorbs the caregiving load. Where public daycare exists, it is a genuine bargain at $7–12 a month in La Paz against roughly $100 for private alternatives — but the low uptake tracks a broader labor pattern: Bolivian women earn about 64% of what men earn and face double the unemployment rate. Property ownership for foreigners is permit-gated through the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, with land within 50 kilometers of any border entirely off-limits, while renting sits on comparatively even footing, with foreigners holding the same tenancy rights as citizens under a law that predates the moon landing.

## Jordan: Orderly, Formal, and Not Cheap Where It Counts

Jordan's cost profile inverts Bolivia's: daily expenses and rent in Amman run meaningfully higher, with a city-center one-bedroom averaging around JOD 238 (about $336) a month and premium West Amman units pushing well past $1,000. What Jordan sells instead of rock-bottom prices is order — low violent crime, a well-worn expat infrastructure of NGOs, embassies, and international schools, and a housing stock that, per multiple relocation guides, arrives furnished and company-arranged more often than not. That formality extends hard to healthcare: the public Civil Insurance Programme is closed to non-Jordanians permanently, and the Social Security Corporation, while mandatorily enrolling all workers regardless of nationality for pension, injury, and maternity benefits, has never built a standalone private-sector health benefit. Private insurance is consequently load-bearing rather than optional, and a formal visa requirement for work, investor, and family residency.

Childcare is scarcer still — fewer than 3% of children under five attend any registered facility, a gap the IFC's Care Arabia initiative estimates is suppressing female workforce participation by more than 7 percentage points. Property ownership carries its own bureaucracy: non-Arab foreigners may own exactly one property, subject to a three-year resale lock-in, prior ministerial approval, and an outright ban in border, archaeological, and historic zones. Even renting comes with a distinctly Jordanian formality, since landlords frequently expect rent in quarterly, biannual, or full-year lump sums rather than the monthly cadence most Western tenants assume is universal.

## The Reckoning

Line the two up and a paradox emerges: the country where things cost less asks less of you administratively, while the country that runs like clockwork extracts a premium for that order — in insurance you must privately arrange, in rent paid a year at a time, in property law that treats foreign buyers as a supervised category rather than customers. Hofstede Insights scores Bolivia at 78 on power distance with a strongly collectivist 21 on individualism, while the "Arab World" cluster it uses for Jordan sits even higher on power distance (80) and considerably higher on uncertainty avoidance (68) — a statistical way of saying Jordanian institutions are built to minimize ambiguity, which is precisely why a lease reads like a contract and a visa checklist reads like an audit.

The counterintuitive part is that neither welfare state was built with the expat in mind, and both essentially route around themselves for anyone without local citizenship: Bolivia through informal family and community care, Jordan through mandatory private insurance and employer-sponsored housing. You are, in either country, largely on your own — Bolivia just charges you less for the privilege.

## The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — One respondent describing life as an English-speaking expat in Bolivia noted that visa extensions, residence permits, and bank accounts exist in what they called a "slow-motion process."*

> *Quora — An account of daily life in Amman described most international staff as living in company-arranged accommodation in West Amman, spacious by design, so new arrivals wouldn't have to navigate the local rental market alone in their first year.*

> *Expat.com forum (Jordan) — A contributor on the site's cost-of-living thread flagged that landlords routinely ask for rent in full six-month or annual installments rather than monthly payments, a scheduling quirk that catches newcomers off guard.*

> *livinginjordanasexpat.com — A blog post on renting practicalities warned that deposits and advance rent, often six to twelve months' worth, are notoriously difficult to recover in full, and advised getting every apartment condition documented before handing over cash.*

> *expatsblog.com — An American photojournalist interviewed about her years in Bolivia observed that the country's low overall cost of living is deceptive: healthcare, education, and privacy, she said, become "extremely expensive if you want good quality."*

## Conclusion

The practical math: Bolivia rewards anyone comfortable improvising around thin institutions — cash purchases, family-based childcare, a private insurance top-up for peace of mind — in exchange for a cost of living that makes almost any salary stretch. Jordan rewards anyone who prefers formality and can absorb the upfront cash flow hit — a year of rent, a private health policy, a single approved property — in exchange for order, safety, and an established international community to land in. Neither system was designed with you, specifically, in mind, which is really the only honest thing either country's relocation brochure could say, and doesn't.
