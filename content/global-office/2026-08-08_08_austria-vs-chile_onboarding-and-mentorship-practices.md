**The Apprentice and the Pituto: How You Actually Get Shown the Ropes in Vienna and Santiago**

🇦🇹 Austria · 🇨🇱 Chile

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Austria runs one of Europe's most formalized vocational systems — programs like "AusBildung bis 18" and the "Überbetriebliche Lehrausbildung" ensure that structured mentorship and apprenticeship are treated as a matter of public policy, not employer generosity. Chile runs on something almost opposite in spirit: "pituto," a word with no clean English translation, referring to the personal connections and referrals that quietly determine who gets shown the ropes at all — and, per Chilean labor-market research, a significant share of mid-to-senior roles are filled through referral before they're ever publicly posted. In Austria, mentorship is a system you're enrolled in. In Chile, it's a relationship you have to already have, or quickly build.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇦🇹 Austria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Expect a structured onboarding process — policies, labor-law briefings, and a formal introduction to your team | Assume onboarding is informal or self-directed — it's typically systematic |
| Take a formally assigned mentor seriously as your primary cultural translator | Rely solely on your direct supervisor for cultural onboarding — mentors often exist separately |
| Learn workplace German basics even in English-speaking teams — it signals real investment | Assume English fluency alone is enough for full cultural integration |
| Respect precision and process from day one — first impressions on rigor matter | Treat early mistakes casually — Austrian colleagues notice attention to detail immediately |
| Ask about your specific performance metrics and career-path structure early | Wait passively for career guidance to be offered — ask directly, it's expected |

#### 🇨🇱 Chile

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Actively build a personal network (your "pitutos") — it shapes access to real opportunities | Assume merit alone will surface your best opportunities — relationships open doors first |
| Invest time in informal relationship-building — coffee, lunch, small talk — before expecting real mentorship | Expect a formal, HR-run onboarding program at every company — many are informal, especially at smaller firms |
| Show warmth and personal interest in colleagues early | Stay strictly transactional in early workplace interactions — it reads as distant |
| Identify who in the office is genuinely well-connected and cultivate that relationship respectfully | Assume your assigned "mentor" (if any) is your only source of institutional knowledge |
| Be patient — trust and real onboarding often happen gradually, through repeated informal contact | Expect full inclusion in informal decision-making circles within your first weeks |

---

Austria's approach to onboarding and mentorship is inseparable from its broader vocational education tradition — a system that trains a large share of the workforce through structured apprenticeships long before they enter a corporate job, and that habit of formalized skill transfer carries directly into how companies onboard new professional hires. Austrian workplace culture, per cross-cultural business guides, places heavy weight on precision, clear communication, and respectful hierarchy; new hires are typically walked through explicit documentation covering labor law entitlements, benefits, performance metrics, and reporting lines, often paired with a formally assigned mentor whose job is specifically to accelerate cultural assimilation. The system assumes that leaving onboarding to chance would be a process failure, not a personality quirk of a particular manager.

Chile inverts this almost entirely. While large multinational subsidiaries in Santiago increasingly run Western-style formal onboarding, much of the broader labor market — and certainly most small and mid-sized Chilean firms — still operates on relationship-driven access. The concept of "pituto," documented by researchers studying informal economies, describes the personal connections and favors that determine who hears about an opportunity, who gets introduced to the right people, and who gets genuinely mentored versus formally processed. This isn't corruption in the way an outsider might initially assume — it's a deeply normalized social operating system, and Chilean professionals build and maintain their pitutos deliberately, the way an Austrian professional might build formal credentials. The professional cost of skipping this — staying purely transactional, avoiding the coffee and small talk that build the relationship — is real: you can be technically excellent and still find yourself excluded from the informal channels where actual mentorship and opportunity flow.

### The Reckoning

The irony is that both systems are, in their own way, deeply relationship-based — Austria simply formalizes the relationship (a mentor is assigned, a curriculum exists), while Chile leaves it emergent and personal (a mentor is earned, through sustained informal contact). An Austrian professional moving to Santiago who waits for HR to assign a mentor may wait indefinitely; a Chilean professional moving to Vienna who assumes they need to charm their way into informal favor may be surprised to find a formal structure already doing that work for them, no charm required. Neither system is more meritocratic than the other — Austria's formal structure can flatten out real talent differences behind process, while Chile's informal one can quietly reward proximity over competence — but a newcomer needs to know, walking in, which game they're actually playing.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *r/expats — A German engineer who moved to a Chilean subsidiary of his firm described struggling for months before a colleague finally explained that his lack of informal invitations to lunch, not his technical work, was the reason he felt perpetually out of the loop — after that, he made a point of accepting every casual coffee invite.*

> *Quora — Someone who onboarded at an Austrian firm after working in Latin America wrote that the most disorienting part wasn't the formality but the completeness of it — every question she had was already answered in a document before she thought to ask, which felt oddly impersonal after years of learning things through relationships instead.*

> *InterNations Santiago — A Canadian expat noted that her Chilean colleagues were unfailingly warm in daily interactions but that "warm" and "included in real decisions" turned out to be two separate thresholds, and crossing the second one took nearly a year of consistent, patient relationship-building.*

> *Expat.com Chile guide (reader comments) — A contributor advised that finding a job in Chile without any local contacts was significantly harder than the qualifications alone would suggest, and recommended new arrivals prioritize networking events and expat community groups specifically to generate a first "pituto" before job-hunting seriously.*

> *HackerNews — A software engineer who worked in both Vienna and Santiago offices of the same company observed that the Vienna office's formal mentorship program produced faster technical ramp-up, while the Santiago office's informal networks produced faster social integration — and that neither location's approach transferred well to the other.*

---

### Conclusion

If you move to Austria, trust the system — a mentor will likely be assigned, the documentation will likely be thorough, and your job is mainly to engage with what's already been built for you. If you move to Chile, build the system yourself — attend the coffee, accept the lunch, invest in the relationships that will eventually become your real onboarding, because no org chart will do it for you. The honest advice for a friend headed to Santiago: your first "pituto" is worth more than your first month of stellar performance reviews, and that's not cynicism, it's just how the office actually works.
