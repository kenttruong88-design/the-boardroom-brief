**No One Will Show You Where the Bathroom Is: Onboarding and Mentorship in Bangladesh and Pakistan**

🇧🇩 Bangladesh · 🇵🇰 Pakistan

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Somewhere between the offer letter and the first team lunch, both countries quietly agree on one thing: there is no onboarding binder. Instead you get a manager who expects you to defer, absorb, and eventually — once you're patient and useful enough — get let in on how things work. Bangladesh does this through hierarchy that rarely explains itself; Pakistan through relationships that take weeks to earn. Neither hands you a map on day one; both expect you to draw your own and get corrected as you go.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇩 Bangladesh

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Address seniors with appropriate honorifics (Bhai, Apa, or the formal title) until told otherwise | Don't skip the chain of command to ask a senior manager a question your direct supervisor could answer |
| Watch how disagreements are raised — usually privately, after a meeting, not during it | Don't publicly correct or contradict your manager in a group setting, even if you're right |
| Expect your first two weeks to be observation-heavy; ask for reading material and shadow calls | Don't assume silence from your manager means disapproval — it often means "figure it out, then check in" |
| Build rapport over tea breaks; a surprising share of real onboarding happens there, not in meetings | Don't expect a written 30-60-90 day plan; few firms outside MNCs formalize one |
| Treat your assigned buddy or senior colleague's informal tips as the real manual | Don't bring up compensation or leave policy in your first week unless HR raises it first |
| Show up early and stay a little late in your first month — it registers | Don't leave exactly at closing time on day one, even if your contract says you can |

#### 🇵🇰 Pakistan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Invest time in small talk and personal rapport before expecting real mentorship | Don't expect a mentor to be assigned; you'll likely have to identify and cultivate one yourself |
| Defer visibly to senior staff in meetings, even ones younger than you in tenure elsewhere | Don't interrupt a senior colleague's point to make yours, however efficient that feels |
| Accept that your manager may ask about your family or personal life — it's relationship-building, not overreach | Don't treat friendliness from leadership as informality; the hierarchy is still very much intact |
| Be patient with meetings that run long, get interrupted, or start late | Don't schedule your first weeks assuming punctual, single-topic meetings |
| Lean on whoever in the office has "adopted" you informally — it's often more useful than official training | Don't assume merit alone will fast-track your integration; who introduces you matters as much as what you know |
| Dress conservatively and formally until you've read the room | Don't assume dress-down Fridays exist just because the office feels warm and hospitable |

### Bangladesh: Hierarchy as Onboarding Manual

Bangladesh's workplaces run on a power-distance script that Hofstede Insights' comparative scoring places among the more hierarchical in the world: decisions flow down, rarely up, and new hires are expected to absorb rather than question. According to workplace culture research from PiHR and Global Squirrels, Bangladeshi firms — particularly family-owned ones — often route conflict resolution straight to the owner or senior executive, who arbitrates personally rather than delegating to HR. That paternalism shows up in onboarding too: your manager may ask sincerely about your commute or your health, and expect the same closeness to extend to how you're managed. It is warmth wrapped around a clear chain of command.

The Daily Star's opinion pages have argued the corporate sector needs a "cultural reset," citing fear-based environments where junior staff hesitate to flag problems upward. That tracks with the ILO's labour market picture: ILOSTAT puts roughly 85 percent of Bangladeshi employment in the informal sector, meaning most workers never encounter a formal HR process — norms transmit person to person, not through a handbook. Even formally, structured mentorship remains the exception; the assumption is that juniors learn by proximity to seniors, over tea, over shared deadlines, over months.

### Pakistan: Mentorship as Something You Earn, Not Receive

Pakistan's management style, per Commisceo Global's cross-cultural guide, tends toward the autocratic and paternalistic: decisions are made at the top and handed down, with the boss expected to function as something between a supervisor and a family elder. That paternalism is real, but it is not structured mentorship. Doing business — and being managed well — depends on personal trust built over time, so the useful onboarding rarely happens in an orientation session; it happens once a senior colleague decides you're worth the investment.

World Bank and Pakistan Bureau of Statistics data underline why this matters: labour force participation sits around 52 percent, well below the global average near 61 percent, and youth unemployment keeps ticking upward even as the workforce expands. In a tight, relationship-dependent market, mentorship functions less like an HR benefit and more like informal insurance — who vouches for you can matter more than your CV. It's also unevenly distributed: well-connected hires get folded in quickly; everyone else spends months reading the room from outside.

### The Reckoning

Here is the counterintuitive part: Bangladesh's steeper hierarchy often produces faster clarity, if not faster warmth — you know exactly who to ask, even if the answer is "wait." Pakistan's paternalistic, relationship-driven style produces the opposite: enormous warmth up front, but a slower, murkier path to actually knowing how things work, because that knowledge is guarded by whoever has chosen to like you. One system is transparent about its opacity; the other is generous about hiding it.

The practical upshot for a foreign hire: in Bangladesh, invest early in understanding the org chart and never skip a rung. In Pakistan, invest early in people — the org chart matters less than who has decided to walk you through it personally.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — a former employee describing the banking sector in Dhaka recalled the job as high-stress from day one: demanding managers, brief prayer and lunch breaks, and long unpaid hours that were simply the norm rather than an exception to flag.*

> *Quora — someone asking how to find a mentor in Karachi got answers pointing almost entirely to personal networks and cold outreach, not company programs, underscoring that structured mentorship is something you assemble yourself rather than something HR assigns.*

> *Blind — an engineer on a team split between Chinese, Indian, and Pakistani colleagues described being let go over a vague "cultural fit" issue, tracing it back to mismatched expectations around how directly feedback should be given and received.*

> *Quora — several respondents to a thread on what surprised them about Pakistan converged on the same theme: the sheer speed and sincerity of hospitality extended to newcomers, often exceeding what they'd expected from a purely professional relationship.*

> *Expat Arrivals — a contributor's guide to working in Pakistan advised new arrivals to expect meetings that run long, get interrupted, and rarely stay on a single topic, and to treat that not as disorganization but as a sign that relationship-building was happening alongside the agenda.*

### Conclusion

If you need to know where you stand quickly, Bangladesh will tell you — bluntly, without much cushioning, but you'll rarely be confused about who's in charge. If you'd rather be embraced first and informed gradually, Pakistan will do that instead, on the understanding that information arrives once the relationship has matured, not before. Neither hands a newcomer a real onboarding manual; both hand you a person, and expect you to figure out how much of their goodwill converts into actual guidance.

The thing I'd tell a friend over a drink: in Bangladesh, learn the org chart before you learn anyone's name; in Pakistan, learn everyone's name before you dare ask for the org chart.
