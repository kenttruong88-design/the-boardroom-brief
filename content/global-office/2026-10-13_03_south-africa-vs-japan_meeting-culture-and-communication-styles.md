**The Silence Will Not Be Explained to You**

🇿🇦 South Africa · 🇯🇵 Japan

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In a Johannesburg meeting, someone will tell you exactly what they think of your proposal, loudly, with a rugby anecdote attached, inside the first ten minutes. In a Tokyo meeting, you may never find out what anyone thinks of your proposal at all — because the real meeting, the one where opinions actually form and decisions actually get made, happened in a series of quiet one-on-one conversations days before you were in the room.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇿🇦 South Africa

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Come with statistics, case studies, and charts — decisions run on evidence, not gut feel | Pitch on vision and intuition alone and expect it to land |
| Expect meetings to open with real small talk — sport, food, travel — before business starts | Skip straight to the agenda; it reads as cold |
| Take directness at face value — if someone disagrees, they'll usually just say so | Assume bluntness equals hostility; it's usually just honesty |
| Build in real relationship time before expecting full openness | Expect first-meeting candor on sensitive topics — that comes with trust, over time |
| Avoid race relations, local politics, and city-vs-city comparisons as small talk | Bring up any of the above assuming it's neutral icebreaker territory |

#### 🇯🇵 Japan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Do your real lobbying — *nemawashi* — in private, one-on-one, before the group meeting | Expect the group meeting itself to be where minds get changed |
| Invite quiet colleagues in directly by name if you need their input in the room | Assume silence means agreement, or that no objection means approval |
| Slow your English down and avoid idiom and slang in mixed-language meetings | Talk at native-speaker pace and wonder why half the room has disengaged |
| Treat a smile plus silence as a probable "no" | Take a non-committal "I'll consider it" as a yes in progress |
| Handle business cards with both hands and leave them visible on the table during the meeting | Pocket a business card immediately or write on it in front of its owner |

---

South Africa's meeting style runs on a genuinely useful contradiction: people are direct, often loud, and quick to say what they think — Executive Support Magazine's regional guide describes South Africans as "direct and often loud" while "highly aware of how their words land" — but decisions themselves are evidence-driven almost to a fault. Proposals succeed or fail on statistics, case studies, and data, not charisma, and large corporates layer in enough governance and sign-off that few calls rest with one person alone. The result is a room that feels confrontational to a newcomer but is actually just comfortable with disagreement as a normal, low-stakes part of getting to a decision.

Japan's meetings run on the opposite assumption: that disagreement voiced in the room, in front of the group, is itself the problem to avoid. The real decision-shaping happens beforehand, through *nemawashi* — literally "root-binding" — a process of informal, individual consultations that builds consensus quietly so the formal meeting can ratify something everyone already privately agreed to. Cross-cultural trainers at Japan Intercultural Consulting note that foreign participants often misread this as inefficiency, when it's actually risk management: nobody is surprised, nobody loses face, and nobody has to publicly back down from a position taken in front of colleagues.

The communication gap compounds the decision-making gap. Japanese professionals frequently stay quiet in mixed-language meetings not from disengagement but from a specific trio of anxieties documented by workplace trainers: fear that a question will sound unpolished, difficulty finding a natural opening in fast English conversation, and simply losing the thread when slang or jargon picks up speed. Hofstede Insights scores Japan at 92 on Uncertainty Avoidance — among the highest of any country measured — which tracks: a culture this averse to ambiguity has built elaborate social protocols specifically to avoid the open-ended risk of unscripted disagreement in a room.

**The Reckoning**

The trap for a South African manager newly transplanted to Tokyo is assuming silence in a meeting is either agreement or disengagement — it is usually neither. It's a decision that was already made elsewhere, being performed for the room's benefit. The trap running the other direction — a Japanese manager newly arrived in Johannesburg — is assuming the loud disagreement in the room is personal or hostile, when it's closer to South Africans doing their *nemawashi* out loud, in real time, as the meeting itself.

Both systems get to a decision. They just disagree, fundamentally, about whether the meeting is where the deciding happens or merely where it gets announced.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *JREF forum (Japan Reference) — One longtime poster warned newcomers that Japanese colleagues "avoid saying no" far more consistently than foreigners expect, so a polite, noncommittal answer is often a refusal in disguise — something the poster said most newcomers "do not understand" until they've been burned by it waiting on a favour that was never coming. A second poster added a practical tip: handle a business card with both hands, actually look at it, and leave it on the table during the meeting rather than pocketing it.*

> *Japan Intercultural Consulting (cross-cultural training case files) — Trainers working with mixed Japanese-foreign teams repeatedly documented the same three reasons Japanese staff go quiet in English-language meetings: fear of sounding foolish, the difficulty of finding a natural break in fast conversation, and simply losing the thread when jargon or idiom picks up pace — not disengagement, as foreign managers often assume.*

> *Executive Support Magazine (regional business guide) — A profile of South African meeting etiquette noted that gift-giving and entertaining are tightly controlled at JSE-listed firms, to the point that employees must often formally declare anything offered or accepted — a level of institutional caution that sits oddly against the stereotype of South African business as relationship-first and informal.*

> *Rudlin Consulting (Japan business-culture notes) — Their guidance to foreign managers stressed treating the absence of objection in a meeting as inconclusive, not as a green light, since real buy-in in Japanese organisations is confirmed through the private *nemawashi* process, not through what is or isn't said in the room.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing a transfer between these two, the practical question isn't "which culture communicates better" — both communicate constantly, just through different channels. South Africa tells you everything out loud, in the room, often with a joke attached. Japan tells you everything, just never in the room, and never to you first unless someone has already quietly checked that you're ready to hear it. Priya's actual advice: in Johannesburg, say what you mean. In Tokyo, find out who you're actually supposed to be saying it to — privately, well before the meeting where it supposedly gets decided.
