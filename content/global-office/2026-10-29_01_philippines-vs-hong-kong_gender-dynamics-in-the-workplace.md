# In Manila, She's Already the Boss. In Hong Kong, She's Still Proving It at 9pm.

**🇵🇭 Philippines · 🇭🇰 Hong Kong**

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

The Philippines ranks fourth out of 80 countries for women in management, according to the International Labour Organization, and is one of the few economies on earth where women earn more than men on average — 10.3 percent more, per the ILO's 2018/19 Global Wage Report. Hong Kong, by contrast, pays its 20-year-old women more than its 20-year-old men, then spends the next four decades quietly reversing the favor, until a 60-year-old woman earns half what her male peer does, according to Willis Towers Watson. Both facts are true. Neither tells you what it's actually like to run a meeting in either city.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇭 Philippines

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Ask about a colleague's family before getting to business — it signals respect, not nosiness. | Schedule anything through the midday lunch block; it's treated as sacred, regardless of who's asking. |
| Expect female department heads to be the norm in HR, finance, and marketing. | Assume parity in mid-management means parity in the boardroom — the C-suite still skews male. |
| Use "Ma'am" or "Sir" plus first name for any manager, regardless of age or gender. | Offer the "matriarchal society" line as a compliment — many Filipina executives hear it as a way to credit culture instead of competence. |
| Expect maternity leave plans to be raised openly and early, often by the employee herself. | Treat a colleague's live-in household help as unusual — it's standard infrastructure for most dual-income homes. |
| Read assertiveness from a female manager as simple competence. | Assume a flexible daytime schedule means an equal one after hours — unpaid care work still lands disproportionately on women. |

#### 🇭🇰 Hong Kong

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Treat visible hours — still at your desk past 7pm — as a loyalty signal that applies to everyone equally. | Request a remote or compressed week in year one; it can quietly read as an exit signal, whoever asks. |
| Expect junior and mid-level finance and banking teams to be majority female. | Assume that holds at the top — senior leadership stays male-heavy, and the pay gap widens, not narrows, with seniority. |
| Budget for a live-in domestic helper if you're a dual-income household with kids — it's the default, not a luxury. | Moralize about the helper system to a local colleague; it's the unspoken backbone of the city's two-income economy. |
| Notice when a male colleague takes extended parental leave — it's still rare enough to be remarked on. | Ask him "why," even lightly — the stigma hasn't caught up with the policy. |
| Take a quiet "sure" in a meeting at face value. | Push back loudly in the room if you disagree — raise it one-on-one afterward instead. |

### The Philippines

The headline statistic is real and frequently cited by Manila's own HR consultancies: Filipino women out-earn Filipino men on an hourly basis, the Philippine Statistics Authority has recorded it for years (₱414.29 average daily basic pay for women against ₱392.82 for men in 2016), and the ILO has ranked the country near the top globally for the share of management roles held by women. Local commentary has a name for this — a "matriarchal" business culture, inherited from a pre-colonial tradition of women controlling household finances and extended into the modern office.

The trouble, per a 2017 study from the Philippine Institute for Development Studies, is that the number holds up mainly in aggregate and at the top of the pay scale; women still earn less than men in lower-level roles like clerks and shop assistants, and the same researchers found the culture still frames women as homemakers "by default," which makes the credit for female leadership land oddly — assigned to culture rather than to the women doing the work. Jo-Ann Capuz, a senior recruitment consultant at Monroe Consulting in Manila, told CIPD researchers she sees no meaningful pay gap and considers maternity leave the only real cost difference between hiring men and women — a confident, common view among employers that sits uneasily next to academics calling the same workplace's "unconscious gender issues" still very much felt.

Female labor force participation, meanwhile, has sat flat for years around 63 percent, well below men's, with household duties cited as the main reason women who want to work still don't.

### Hong Kong

Hong Kong does not pretend to have solved this. Willis Towers Watson's pay analysis shows women out-earning men at 20, trailing by 11 percent at 30, by 25 percent at 50, and by half at 60 — a gap that opens with seniority rather than closing with experience, the reverse of what most equality campaigns are built to fix. HSBC's own figures put women in 30 percent of its senior Hong Kong roles, which the bank's Inwha Huh notes beats most Western offices still in the 20s; her stated problem isn't hiring women, it's keeping them once the roles get senior enough to be worth leaving over.

Underneath both numbers sits the same piece of infrastructure: a live-in domestic-helper system, overwhelmingly staffed by migrant women from the Philippines and Indonesia, that makes a dual-career household workable in one of the world's most expensive cities. It is also, by design, a tiered one — a helper's visa is tied to living with her employer, a structural dependency that barely comes up in conversations about Hong Kong's "progress" on gender, because the progress in question usually belongs to someone else's household staff. On the office floor itself, flexibility requests cut the opposite way from how they're marketed: one Hong Kong banker who took four years off to raise his children told researchers almost no other men approached him for advice afterward, and a Singapore-based colleague's request for a four-day week was read by her own family as the first sign she was being managed out.

### The Reckoning

Put side by side, the irony inverts the obvious story. The Philippines looks like the gender-equality success case on paper — ahead on pay, ahead on management representation — while a confident HR consultant in Manila will tell you, sincerely, that there's no bias left to discuss, even as her own country's researchers document exactly that bias shaping who gets hired into which rung. Hong Kong looks like the laggard on paper, with a pay gap that widens visibly with age, while quietly running more women into senior banking roles than plenty of Western head offices manage — its real failure isn't the glass ceiling, it's a retention problem once women get close enough to see it.

The subtler contrast is in what each city's apparent progress is actually resting on. Manila's is cultural: a "matriarchal" story that assigns women authority by inherited custom, which can just as easily be used to wave off a harder conversation about hiring panels and promotion committees. Hong Kong's is structural: a two-income urban economy held together by migrant household labor that rarely shows up in the pay-gap chart it's quietly subsidizing. On Hofstede's cultural-dimensions framework, the Philippines scores high on power distance (94) and low on individualism (32); Hong Kong scores lower on power distance (68) but still firmly collectivist (25) — both cultures expect deference upward, they simply disagree on who gets handed it as a default.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Hong Kong Discussion Group forum (via a Human Resources Online roundup) — One recurring post collected lines employees said their bosses had actually used, among them "I pay you a salary" and a flat "Get out!" aimed at someone who'd declined a late-night overtime request — less a gender story than a reminder that in Hong Kong, hierarchy outranks everything else, including the clock.*

> *InterNations Expat Insider survey, reported via the South China Morning Post — Marcy LaRont of the American Women's Association in Hong Kong traced some of the city's low work-life-balance scores among expat women to "trailing spouses" under financial pressure to find work fast, compounded by husbands who travel constantly, "adding extra burden to the balance of family life, especially if there are kids."*

> *GTReview banking-industry interviews — Maaike Steinebach of CBA Hong Kong said the bigger surprise wasn't women asking for flexibility, it was how rarely men did, and how it's read when they do: "asking for help can make men look weak," with "a lot of unconscious bias" still shaping how career gaps get judged afterward.*

> *GTReview banking-industry interviews — Ginnie Chin of Cullum Capital offered the piece of advice she wishes more newcomers heard before requesting a lighter schedule: flexibility in Asia can be misread as an exit sign, "people think you're trying to make them redundant" rather than simply adjusting your hours.*

> *Monroe Consulting Philippines recruitment survey — Jo-Ann Capuz, a senior consultant in Manila, was the pleasant surprise and the cautionary tale in one answer: she reported roughly half her own firm's consultants are women with management split evenly too, while maintaining there's no pay gap left to address — a confidence her own country's labor researchers don't fully share.*

### Conclusion

If you're moving from Hong Kong to Manila, expect your authority to be assumed rather than negotiated for — Filipino offices default to taking a woman's seniority at face value, which is a genuine relief after years of proving it. Just don't mistake the "matriarchal" compliment for the whole picture; it can excuse a harder conversation as easily as it can celebrate one. If you're moving the other way, expect your competence to go unquestioned at 28 and your trajectory to get a lot more scrutinized by 48 — and sort out your childcare and household logistics before you sort out your office wardrobe, because in Hong Kong, that's the actual infrastructure decision.

Either way, keep a calendar that can hold a 7am call with New York and a 9pm one with your kids' bedtime. Nobody in either city has figured out how to avoid needing both — they've just divided up who's expected to.
