**98 Days Guaranteed, 48 Weeks Taken, Neither One Free**

🇵🇪 Peru · 🇨🇦 Canada

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

In Peru, the law guarantees a new mother 98 days of leave — but only if she has a formal contract, and the subsidy that pays for it comes from the state, not her employer, who may find creative ways to claw the time back anyway. In Canada, a new mother can take up to a full year off, paid partly through a public insurance scheme — and research suggests that very generosity is what quietly stalls her career for a decade after she returns. Neither country has solved the problem of having a child and staying on a career track. They've just failed at it in opposite directions.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇵🇪 Peru

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Confirm your EsSalud contribution status well before you're pregnant — benefits depend on it | Assume maternity leave pay is automatic just because you have a job |
| Know the real split: 98 days total, 49 pre- and 49 post-natal, with unused prenatal days rolling forward | Assume your leave is a flat block starting the day you give birth |
| Expect paternity leave to be short — 10 days standard, 20 for complications — but employer-paid in full | Expect anything close to maternity-length leave for the other parent |
| Budget for informal daycare quality varying wildly if you're not a salaried, contracted employee | Assume affordable, quality childcare is a given outside the formal sector |
| Push for lactation accommodations explicitly — they aren't universally provided | Assume a workplace will have a lactation room without asking first |

#### 🇨🇦 Canada

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Understand EI parental benefits replace only up to 55%, capped near $668 a week — not your full salary | Assume government benefits alone will cover your household budget during leave |
| Ask your employer directly whether they top up EI — nearly 40% of working mothers report getting none | Assume a "generous leave policy" means your employer is paying the difference |
| Consider splitting leave with a partner if extended benefits (up to 69 weeks combined) make sense for your household | Assume the standard-vs-extended choice is obvious; it changes your weekly payment significantly |
| Plan the daycare search a year or more ahead — wait times are a real, documented source of parental stress | Assume a spot will be available when your leave ends |
| Weigh shorter, more frequent returns to work carefully — research shows multiple short absences cost more than one long one | Assume breaking up your leave into pieces is a safe way to "ease back in" |

Peru's system is generous on paper and precarious underneath it. The law provides 98 days of maternity leave — 49 before birth, 49 after, with unused prenatal days rolling into the postnatal period — paid not by the employer but by EsSalud, the national health insurer, based on prior contributions. That protects women with formal contracts reasonably well. It does nothing for everyone else. A widely shared first-person account from Enmita Marin, a Peruvian doctor writing for World Pulse, described being paid for "productivity" rather than salary at a Ministry of Health hospital — a classification that excluded her from maternity protections entirely. After a Cesarean section, she got just 15 days of leave and was told she'd have to repay it by working eight-hour Saturdays for two months: "we've already paid you for the entire month, so you'll have to pay back in hours the work you didn't do." Paternity leave is far smaller — a mandatory four days under the original 2009 law, some guides now describe 10 standard days (20 for complications), paid directly by the employer. Hofstede Insights scores Peru at 87 on Uncertainty Avoidance, one of the highest readings in the Americas, which tracks with a system built on rigid, rule-bound entitlements — generous exactly as written, and unforgiving to anyone outside the formal categories the rules were written for.

Canada's system looks like the opposite problem: genuinely generous, available to nearly everyone with insurable employment, and still quietly costly to the people who use it. Employment Insurance replaces up to 55% of salary (capped around $668 a week) for the first 15 weeks after birth, then splits up to 40 more weeks of shared parental benefits, extendable to a combined 69 weeks at a lower 33% rate. Roughly 90% of new Canadian mothers take leave, averaging 48 weeks — but a 2010 TD Economics study found time away from work accounts for as much as half of the 20% lifetime wage gap between men and women, with each year away costing about 3% of earnings in a penalty that persists after return. One cited example: a woman earning $64,000 who took three years off could lose roughly $325,000 in lifetime earnings. The penalty is worse for women who take several short absences instead of one long one. Hofstede scores Canada's Individualism at 80, nearly five times Peru's 16, fitting a system built around individual choice rather than Peru's rigid categories — but flexibility, the data suggests, is exactly what lets a career cost accumulate quietly, one choice at a time.

**The Reckoning**

Peru's failure is a failure of coverage: the 98 days are real, but only for women inside the formal economy, and the system offers no protection — sometimes active punishment — for everyone else, including a doctor paid by "productivity" rather than salary. Canada's failure is a failure of design: the leave is available to nearly everyone, generously extended, and still produces a measurable, lasting wage penalty that falls disproportionately on the people who use it most, which in practice means mothers. A Peruvian policymaker could look at Canada's numbers and reasonably ask whether more generous leave is actually the fix. A Canadian policymaker could look at Peru's and see exactly how bad things get without any formal safety net at all. Both are right, and neither country's system, as it stands, resolves the actual tension between having a child and keeping a career moving.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *World Pulse / TIME — Enmita Marin, a Peruvian doctor paid by "productivity" rather than salary, was told after 15 days of post-Cesarean leave: "Dr. Marin, we've already paid you for the entire month, so you'll have to pay back in hours the work you didn't do." She worked through her pregnancy with no sick leave or time off for prenatal appointments, and gave up one of her three jobs after her workplace provided no lactation room.*

> *The same account — Marin described visiting a free daycare with dirt floors, no clean water, and no toys, caring for about 15 children aged two to four, and asked directly: "Why is it that in Peru a woman has to choose between being a mother or a professional?"*

> *Macleans — Reporting on Canada's "motherhood gap," the piece cited a 2010 TD Economics finding that the wage penalty for multiple short leave absences runs three times higher than for one long absence of the same total length — a result that complicates the common advice to "ease back in" gradually.*

> *Global News — An Angus Reid poll cited in coverage of Canada's record-low fertility rate found 41% of adults who'd delayed having children cited job market and financial security concerns, and fully 4 in 10 respondents said they will "definitely" not have or raise a child at all.*

### Conclusion

If you're weighing these two for family planning, the honest framing is "which failure can you personally absorb." In Peru, the real risk is falling outside the formal system entirely — informal work, productivity-based pay, gig contracts — where the generous-looking 98 days simply don't apply to you. In Canada, the real risk is that the system works exactly as designed and you still pay for it, one percentage point of lifetime earnings at a time, for choosing a longer or more broken-up leave. Priya's actual advice: in Lima, nail down your formal employment status and EsSalud contributions before you're pregnant, not after. In Toronto or Vancouver, read the TD Economics numbers before you decide how to split your leave — the "ease back in gradually" instinct is, statistically, the more expensive choice.
