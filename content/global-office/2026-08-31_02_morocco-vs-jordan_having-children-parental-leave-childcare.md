# Congratulations, It's a Career Setback: Morocco and Jordan on the Economics of Parenthood

🇲🇦 Morocco · 🇯🇴 Jordan

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Morocco pays new mothers their full salary for fourteen weeks, funded by the state, no negotiation required. Jordan, not to be outdone in generosity on paper, obliges any employer with enough infants on its books to build them a nursery. And yet across both countries, a mother who actually claims these benefits belongs to a shrinking minority: fewer than one in five working-age Moroccan women, and roughly one in seven working-age Jordanian women, are in the labor force at all. The leave, it turns out, was never the hard part.

#### 🇲🇦 Morocco

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Register the birth at the état civil within 30 days — everything downstream depends on it | Don't assume your home insurer will settle a private-clinic bill without pre-authorization |
| Confirm your CNSS contributions are current before the due date — the 100%-salary benefit depends on it | Don't expect your partner to get anywhere near equal leave — 3 paid days is standard |
| Start the nursery search in the first trimester in Casablanca or Rabat | Don't count on employer-provided daycare — it exists at only a handful of large firms |
| Budget for extended family showing up to help, fed and housed, for weeks | Don't treat every relative's feeding advice as clinical guidance, however forcefully delivered |
| Learn that the nanny market runs on word-of-mouth trust networks | Don't post a stranger job ad and skip the reference checks — nobody else will do it for you |
| Ask if your employer is public or private — the new 15-day paternity leave only covers one of them | Don't assume the "longest paternity leave in the region" headline applies to you |

#### 🇯🇴 Jordan

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Check whether your employer has 15+ children under 4 years 8 months on payroll — that legally triggers a free nursery | Don't assume the threshold is actually enforced — compliance is inconsistent |
| Bank your one-hour daily nursing break — it's protected for nine months after you return | Don't expect it to extend a day past that window |
| Research private maternity hospitals in Amman early; the clinical care is genuinely strong | Don't wait until your due month to book a private suite — good ones fill up |
| Plan around daycare pickup at 3 or 3:30pm against your own office hours | Don't assume daycare includes meals — pack breakfast and lunch, every day |
| Look into the new National Childcare Policy pilots if you're near one | Don't expect them to have scaled yet — under 3% of children under five are in formal childcare nationally |
| Know that up to a year of unpaid leave is legally available | Don't assume your seniority survives the year untouched — re-entry frictions are informal but real |

Morocco's maternity offer is, by regional and even global standards, unusually generous: fourteen weeks of leave at full pay, split evenly before and after birth, administered through the CNSS social security fund according to guidance from [Upsilon Consulting](https://www.upsilon-consulting.com/en/maternity-leave-morocco/) and [Skuad](https://www.skuad.io/leave-policy/morocco). Fathers have historically received three paid days, but a new bill extends that to fifteen consecutive paid days — for now limited to public-sector employees, with private-sector coverage still pending, a distinction that [The New Arab](https://www.newarab.com/news/moroccos-parliament-approves-15-days-paid-paternity-leave) reports would make it the longest paternity leave in the MENA region, asterisk included. None of this generosity has moved the underlying number that matters: according to the [World Bank](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SL.TLF.CACT.FE.NE.ZS?locations=MA), female labor force participation has fallen from 30.4% in 1999 to roughly 19% today, a decline the Bank attributes not to a lack of education — Moroccan women have made real gains there — but to a shortage of quality jobs, inadequate public transport, and childcare that simply isn't there when the leave runs out.

Jordan's package looks less generous on paper — maternity leave was recently extended to 90 days, with at least six weeks required after birth, and a one-hour daily paid nursing break for nine months upon return, per reporting from the [ILO](https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/extended-paternity-leave-urged-support-parenting-jordan). Paternity leave sits at three days, unchanged. But Jordan has a policy nobody else in this pairing has bothered with: employers with fifteen or more children under 4 years 8 months among their staff's dependents are legally required to provide an on-site, professionally staffed nursery at no cost. It is a clever mandate that runs into a familiar wall — enforcement. Formal childcare enrollment for children under five sits below 3%, and female labor force participation, at around 14%, is among the lowest in the world against a global average above 51%, according to figures cited by [the IMF](https://www.imf.org/-/media/Files/Countries/MCD/JOR/employment-and-labor-force-participation-in-jordan-for-posting.ashx) and [World Bank](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/SL.TLF.CACT.FE.ZS?locations=JO)-sourced analysis. The government's newly launched National Childcare Policy, backed by [the IRC](https://www.rescue.org/press-release/jordan-introduces-landmark-national-childcare-policy-empower-families-and-boost), and an IFC-supported pilot programme spanning Jordan and Lebanon are explicit attempts to close that gap — evidence, at least, that the diagnosis has finally landed.

Put the two side by side and the counterintuitive part isn't that either country is stingy — it's that generosity and participation barely correlate. Morocco offers nearly five times the paid maternity leave of Jordan and still can't crack 20% participation; Jordan builds a legal nursery mandate into its labor code and still can't crack 15%. Both economies are, in effect, running on a childcare system that was never legislated at all: the extended family, and specifically its grandmothers, who [Hofstede Insights](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/267722435_Replicating_Hofstede_in_Jordan_Ungeneralized_Reevaluating_the_Jordanian_Culture) data would predict, given Jordan's low individualism score of 27.7 (against a regional Arab average of 38) and Morocco's similarly collectivist profile — the two societies are simply built to absorb care work informally, unpaid, off any ministry's books.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering "what's the hardest thing about raising children in Morocco," one respondent pointed less to logistics than to social friction: navigating extended-family expectations around discipline and schooling, especially when your own approach diverges from what grandparents consider settled wisdom.*

> *Hacker News, on an "Ask HN: software devs who went to work in the Middle East" thread — a commenter noted that once children entered the picture, the calculus stopped being about salary and shifted entirely to who could realistically pick a child up from school at 3pm — proximity to grandparents ended up mattering more than the compensation package.*

> *Expat.com, Amman forum — a newly arrived working mother asked for help covering the gap between daycare pickup at 3:30pm and her own workday ending at 5pm, only to learn that daycares don't provide meals either, so the packed lunch became one more line item in an already tight morning.*

> *MarocMama blog — the writer described her postpartum weeks in Morocco as surprisingly well provided for, with extended family cooking, cleaning, and caring for the baby without being asked. The same relatives, she noted, also had opinions on everything from what she ate to whether she was dressed warmly enough, delivered with equal generosity.*

> *Expat Exchange — an expat mother recounted giving birth at a private hospital in Amman and being startled by the quality: a large private suite, attentive nursing staff, and a level of clinical care that exceeded what she'd expected walking in, with Farah Hospital specifically recommended by her doctors.*

### Conclusion

Neither country has a childcare problem in the sense of missing legislation — Morocco's maternity pay and Jordan's nursery mandate are both, on paper, more ambitious than what many wealthier economies offer. What both are missing is the unglamorous machinery that turns a law into a functioning system: enforced daycare hours that match office hours, subsidized formal childcare at scale, and a paternity leave long enough to make caregiving look like something other than a maternal assignment. Until then, the honest advice for a new parent in either country is the same: read the labor code for what your rights are, then go find out where your nearest grandmother lives, because she is doing more of the actual work than the ministry.
