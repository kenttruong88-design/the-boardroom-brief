# Heels in Sofia, Hoodies Nowhere: A Dress-Code Survival Guide to Bulgaria and France

🇧🇬 Bulgaria · 🇫🇷 France

***By Priya Mehta, The Global Office***

Here is the statistic that ought to embarrass two nations with such strong opinions about your shoes: according to Eurostat's household budget survey, both Bulgaria and France spend a smaller share of household income on clothing and footwear than the EU average of 4.9 percent — Bulgaria hovers around 3.2 to 3.5 percent, France around 3.3 to 3.8 percent. Neither country, in other words, is buying more clothes than its neighbors. They are simply judging you more precisely in the ones they already own. A new arrival who assumes that low spending means low standards will be corrected within the first commute.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇧🇬 Bulgaria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Dress up even for routine errands — public appearance is taken seriously off the clock | Wear gym clothes or sportswear outside the gym |
| Invest in a few well-pressed pieces rather than a large wardrobe | Assume "casual Friday" applies — many offices still expect suits or smart attire daily |
| Double-check sizing in person; labels run inconsistently and smaller than expected | Trust the tag over the mirror when shopping |
| Groom deliberately — clean hair, polished shoes, tidy nails | Wear eccentric jewelry or anything visibly cheap |
| Wear heels, tailoring, or bold pieces with confidence | Expect judgment on shorter hemlines — norms on skin are looser than the formality suggests |

#### 🇫🇷 France

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Keep one structured, neutral-toned outfit ready for unplanned invitations | Wear leggings, sweatpants, or trainers as everyday errand-wear |
| Invest in a proper coat or blazer — outerwear is a statement, not an afterthought | Wear flip-flops, logo-heavy gear, or team merchandise in the city |
| Default to smart casual, even at a startup | Expect "casual Friday" laxity — chinos and city sneakers are the ceiling, not hoodies |
| Keep makeup and accessories understated | Overdress with flashy jewelry or bright patterns |
| Choose sleek, minimal sneakers or loafers | Wear bulky running shoes outside actual exercise |
| Dress for the specific occasion, every time | Reuse one sightseeing outfit for dinner, a meeting, and a museum |

### Bulgaria: formal by default, freer than it looks

Bulgarian workplace culture treats clothing as a visible marker of respect, and the country's Hofstede profile explains why: a Power Distance score of 70 and Uncertainty Avoidance of 85 point to a society comfortable with hierarchy and allergic to ambiguity, and dress is one of the clearest, cheapest signals of both. According to Commisceo Global's country guide, the default is still standard suit-and-tie for men and comparably formal attire for women, with revealing or provocative clothing considered unacceptable in professional settings — though, notably, the line on "revealing" sits differently than the formality would suggest. Businessculture.org notes that smart-casual exists as an option in some industries, but sportswear, bright colors, and scuffed shoes are non-starters regardless of seniority.

What official guides underplay is the sheer effort involved. Bulgarians, per multiple etiquette sources, tend to leave the house looking deliberately put-together even for non-work occasions, and grooming — clean hair, manicured nails, a well-kept beard — is treated as part of the same package as the outfit itself. Given that Bulgaria's household clothing spend is among the lowest recorded in the EU, this adds up to a culture that gets more visible mileage out of fewer garments: pressed, matched, and worn with intent, rather than accumulated and rotated carelessly. Secondhand shopping has been rising for exactly this reason — fewer pieces, chosen with more care.

### France: minimalist, but not casual

France's Hofstede numbers tell a more contradictory story: Power Distance of 68 and Uncertainty Avoidance of 86 suggest a similarly hierarchical, rule-bound culture, but Individualism scores a comparatively high 71 — a combination that produces dress codes with real personal expression squeezed inside genuinely rigid boundaries. According to Expatica and Snippets of Paris, the office default leans "smart casual" rather than boardroom-formal, but casual Friday, in the American or British sense, barely exists; even at startups, business casual means chinos and clean sneakers, not the hoodie-and-joggers uniform common in Silicon Valley. Quality over volume is the operating principle: FashionUnited and Statista data show French households spending less on clothing per capita than the EU average — roughly €430 annually as of 2020, below the EU-27's €490 — while 62 percent of consumers say they'll pay more for pieces they consider sustainable, and secondhand now accounts for about 12 percent of fashion, footwear, and leather goods sales.

The reputation for effortless French style, in other words, rests on buying less and policing it harder. Business of Fashion and Vogue Business coverage of the French luxury sector consistently frames Paris's fashion authority as inherited and curatorial rather than aspirational — the point isn't to own more, it's to know precisely what not to wear, an instinct outsiders tend to acquire only after several public corrections.

### The Reckoning

Put the two side by side and the irony sharpens: Bulgaria looks more formal on paper — suits, ties, a stated intolerance for sportswear — yet turns out to be relatively unbothered by short skirts, bold colors, or a well-executed low-cut top. France looks more relaxed on paper — no ties required, "smart casual" as the house style — yet enforces some of the strictest unwritten rules in Europe about logos, gym wear, and flip-flops, treating a hoodie in a business-casual office as roughly equivalent to showing up barefoot.

Neither country, meanwhile, is spending disproportionately on clothes to achieve any of this. Both sit below the EU average on fashion spending, which means the judgment you'll encounter in Sofia or Paris isn't a function of wealth on display — it's a function of knowing the rules cold. A new arrival with a generous clothing budget and no grasp of local norms will be read instantly as exactly that: someone with money and no idea what to do with it.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Expat.com — An American who relocated to Bulgaria found her personal style relaxing by comparison: local women showed up daily with full hair, makeup, and heels, a standard she gave up trying to match, though she appreciated that nobody blinked at short skirts or bold outfits either.*

> *Expat.com — Another long-term expat in Bulgaria described a shopping trip gone sideways, told by a shop assistant that a garment labeled several sizes too large still didn't fit — a mismatch between labeled and actual sizing that caught more than one newcomer off guard.*

> *Quora — A poster warned that sports clothing worn outside actual exercise — yoga pants, shorts, plain tees, running shoes — gets visibly, immediately judged in France, regardless of how normal that combination reads back home.*

> *Gamintraveler (blog, substituting for unavailable Internations/TheLocal threads on this topic) — A travel writer advised keeping one polished, structured outfit on hand at all times in France, since even a casual picnic or errand can turn into an unplanned invitation, and flip-flops mark a newcomer as a tourist within seconds.*

> *Snippets of Paris — Someone who worked corporate jobs in both France and North America noted that even startups on "casual Friday" stop at chinos and clean city sneakers — no hoodies, ever — and that makeup and jewelry stay deliberately understated no matter how relaxed the office culture otherwise feels.*

### Conclusion

The practical takeaway travels lighter than the stereotypes suggest. For Sofia, pack fewer, sharper pieces — a couple of well-tailored outfits, good shoes, and the willingness to make an effort on an ordinary Tuesday — and don't over-worry about hemlines. For Paris, buy one excellent coat, keep the palette neutral, retire the sneakers-as-daily-uniform habit, and accept that "smart casual" is doing more work than the phrase implies. Neither country is asking you to spend more; both are asking you to spend more carefully.

The honest summary: Bulgaria will forgive what you wear as long as you clearly tried, and France will forgive that you didn't spend much as long as it doesn't show.
