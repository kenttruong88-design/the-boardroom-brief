**Two Hierarchies, Two Very Different Ways of Never Disagreeing With Your Boss**

**🇮🇳 India · 🇳🇬 Nigeria**

*By Priya Mehta, The Global Office*

Both India and Nigeria will tell you, in their own way, that the boss is right — the difference is in the choreography. In an Indian office, disagreement is dissolved into deference before it's ever spoken aloud, wrapped in formal titles and indirect phrasing designed to preserve everyone's face at once. In a Nigerian office, deference is louder, more physical, and more personal — greetings, titles, and public acknowledgment of seniority function almost as their own language. Hofstede Insights puts Nigeria's Power Distance score at 78 against India's 65, both well above the global average of 55, which is a polite way of saying: pick your hierarchy, because you're getting one either way.

### Do's & Don'ts

#### 🇮🇳 India

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Use formal titles and honorifics with senior colleagues until invited to drop them | Address a manager by first name unprompted on day one |
| Wait for your manager's explicit sign-off before acting on ambiguous instructions | Assume silence means agreement — it may mean deference |
| Expect decisions to flow top-down, even in flatter-sounding org charts | Push for a peer-style debate with a senior in a group setting |
| Build informal rapport — chai, small talk — alongside formal hierarchy | Treat socializing with colleagues as separate from getting work done |
| Phrase disagreement indirectly, in private, framed as a question | Openly contradict a superior's decision in a meeting |
| Notice that MNCs often run flatter than domestic firms — calibrate per employer | Apply one hierarchy playbook to every Indian company you encounter |

#### 🇳🇬 Nigeria

| ✅ Do | ❌ Don't |
|---|---|
| Greet senior colleagues properly and use full titles and surnames | Skip greetings to "get to business" faster |
| Show visible, public respect to elders and superiors in meetings | Interrupt or contradict a senior colleague in front of others |
| Expect final decisions to rest with senior management even after consultation | Assume input-gathering means the decision is now collective |
| Build personal relationships — hierarchy in Nigeria is often described as personal, not just structural | Keep interactions purely transactional and skip relationship-building |
| Put your phone away fully during conversations with elders or superiors | Check your phone or take calls while a senior colleague is speaking |
| Budget extra time and staff for the working day — teams often run lean and long | Expect Western-style staffing ratios or a strict 9-to-5 |

**India**

India's corporate hierarchy is dense but often quiet — a system where, per Safeguard Global and multiple workplace-culture guides, respect for seniority is less a workplace policy than a cultural value employees absorb well before their first job. Decisions travel top-down; employees, per multiple accounts of Indian corporate norms, follow direction "out of respect for seniority" more than out of any formal reporting requirement, and open disagreement with a superior — especially in a group setting — is something handled indirectly, if at all, to preserve face on both sides. A 2024 comparative study on Nigerian and Indian workers' cultural values, published via RSIS International, found both nations scoring high on Hofstede's power-distance and collectivism measures relative to global norms, reinforcing that group harmony and hierarchical deference aren't just anecdotal impressions — they show up in the data.

Where India complicates the stereotype is scale and sector variance: multinational and tech-sector employers in India increasingly run flatter, more Western-style structures, while traditional and government-adjacent firms remain steeply hierarchical. A Quora thread comparing Indian and international work culture noted a dynamic some contributors described bluntly as a "master-slave" relationship with one's direct boss — an exaggeration, but a telling one — versus workplaces abroad where even senior leaders are expected to own their mistakes publicly. The throughline, across sector and company size, is that authority in India is rarely challenged in the room; it's negotiated afterward, privately, in softer language.

**Nigeria**

Nigeria's hierarchy is, if anything, more explicit and more embodied. Commisceo Global's business-culture guide and multiple accounts of Nigerian office norms describe hierarchy as "not just structural — it's personal": titles, correct forms of address, and visible deference to age and seniority aren't formalities layered on top of the real work, they are read as part of the real work. Modern breaches of respect, per cultural guides aimed at foreign professionals, include things that would barely register elsewhere — answering a phone call while a senior colleague is mid-sentence, or prioritizing a Slack message over a face-to-face greeting.

Hofstede's 78 Power Distance score for Nigeria — among the higher scores globally — pairs with a notably high Indulgence score (84%), a dimension capturing how freely a culture permits enjoyment and impulse gratification, which shows up in office life as warmth and sociability layered directly on top of firm hierarchy rather than instead of it. Practically, this means Nigerian offices can feel simultaneously stricter about who has final say and warmer, louder, and more personally engaged day to day than their formality might suggest. Expat guides also flag a harder operational reality: because staffing costs and security overhead run high, employers frequently keep headcounts lean, which translates into long hours and broad job scopes even at a professional level — hierarchy governs who decides, not how much any one person is asked to do.

**The Reckoning**

The paradox is that Nigeria's higher formal Power Distance score produces a workplace that can feel more emotionally present than India's — respect is performed loudly and specifically (titles, greetings, physical deference), which means once it's paid, interactions often relax into real warmth. India's hierarchy operates more by omission: the respect is in what doesn't get said, which can leave newcomers unsure where they actually stand, since disagreement, doubt, and even enthusiasm all get filtered through the same indirect register.

The other divergence is where the hierarchy bends. In India, hierarchy softens noticeably inside multinational and tech employers, so your experience of "Indian corporate culture" depends heavily on which India you land in. In Nigeria, the personal, relationship-driven nature of hierarchy means it bends less by company type and more by how well you, individually, build rapport with the specific people above you — the org chart matters less than whether the person at the top of it actually likes you.

### The Part the Brochure Left Out

> *Quora — Answering how office culture in India actually feels day to day, one contributor described a pattern where even a two-person task tends to produce one person quietly positioning themselves as the manager of the other — a reflex toward informal hierarchy that shows up even without a title to justify it.*

> *Quora — A response comparing Indian and international workplaces noted that bosses in India rarely admit fault openly, in contrast to workplaces abroad where even senior directors are expected to apologize for mistakes in front of the team — a difference the poster framed as one of the harder adjustments for anyone used to flatter cultures.*

> *Quora — Someone answering a question specifically about the Indian expatriate experience in Lagos wrote that Indian professionals moving to Nigeria tend to take real time to settle in, but lean heavily on an established community of fellow Indian expats already working there to navigate the transition.*

> *Quora — A response on what it's like to work in Nigeria as a foreigner advised that success hinges less on formal onboarding and more on actively involving yourself in the culture, framing the foreign employee's role explicitly as "a guest in someone else's land" rather than simply a colleague among equals.*

### Conclusion

The practical difference for someone relocating is this: in India, you'll need to learn to read what isn't being said, because the hierarchy operates through omission and indirection, and your safest early move is formality until someone explicitly grants you informality. In Nigeria, you'll need to learn to perform respect actively and specifically — titles, greetings, undivided attention — because hierarchy there is personal and visible, and it rewards effort you can actually see yourself making. Neither system is asking you to be a pushover; both are asking you to notice who outranks you and act accordingly, just in almost opposite registers. The line I'd give a friend: in India, silence is often the loudest disagreement you'll hear; in Nigeria, disagreement is fine, as long as you never let it look like disrespect.
